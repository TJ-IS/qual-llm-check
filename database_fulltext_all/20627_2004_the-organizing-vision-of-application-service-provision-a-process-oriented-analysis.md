# The organizing vision of application service provision: a process-oriented analysis

Wendy L. Currie

Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AB, UK

Received 30 January 2004; received in revised form 12 January 2004; accepted 19 July 2004

## Abstract

The processes by which information systems (IS) innovations become institutionalized are the subjects of much debate within the field of organization theory. Yet few empirical studies exist which examine how IS innovations come to be adopted and difused across organizations. This paper draws from neo-institutional theory by extending the work of Swanson and Ramiller, using their concept of the organizing vision as a lens for observing and analysing an emerging information systems innovation targeted at small and medium enterprises (SMEs), which came to be known as application services provisioning (ASP). We observe that over time, the initial euphoria and hype- which surrounded the organizing vision of ASP was replaced by scepticism and distrust, as powerful institutional interests in the form of leading technology firms, industry analysts and IT consultancies were ultimately unsuccessful in their attempts to disseminate ASP throughout the wider business and not-for profit IS user communities. The lessons from this research suggest that a processes-oriented analysis of how organizing visions are interpreted, legitimised and mobilized is critical for understanding and explaining how underdevelopment of an organizing vision at an early stage, may inhibit its later adoption and institutionalization.  2004 Elsevier Ltd. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

The management and information systems literature is replete with information systems (IS) innovation and change programs that claim to ofer a novel approach to enhancing business value and performance. Over the past three decades, total quality management (TQM), just-in-time production management (JIT), business process re-engineering (BPR) and, more recently, knowledge management (KM) have emerged, in one form or another, as solutions for transforming business and technical processes within organizations. Whereas some have become embedded into management discourse and organizational processes, methods, and practices, others have been disregarded or have simply faded from memory, as past ideas are replaced with new ones (Abrahamson & Rosenkopf, 1993; Currie & Galliers, 1999).

IS innovations may be depicted as management fashions (Abrahamson & Fairchild, 1999). The management discourse surrounding these fashions has largely focused upon how new methods, tools and techniques can be used by managers to enhance eficiency and productivity. Part of the interest is to observe how managers use discourse about IS innovations to communicate to others that their organizations conform to institutional norms mandating the use of these techniques- (Abrahamson & Fairchild, 1999, p. 708; Meyer & Rowan, 1977). An equally important focus is to examine how management discourse facilitates the adoption and difusion of IS innovations across a range of diverse organizations. Moreover, it achieves this by reinforcing the belief that these organizations are similar in ways that would cause them to benefit equally from adopting a management technique- (Abrahamson & Fairchild, 1999, p. 708).

The widespread adoption and difusion of IS innovation and change programs under normative pressure may lead to their institutionalization, as societal expectations of appropriate organizational form and behaviour come to take on rule-like status in social thought and action- (Covaleski & Dirsmith, 1988, p. 562; Meyer & Rowan, 1991). In this context, institutionalist theory examines how social choices are shaped, mediated, and channelled by institutional arrangements (Powell & Dimaggio, 1991) and how actors and their interests are institutionally constructed (Zucker, 1991). The unit of analysis is above the individual level (i.e. community or societal), with the purpose of generating cognitive or cultural explanations of the phenomena under scrutiny, and not merely at the level of the organization, department or individual.

Institutional theorists have argued that early adoption of an IS innovation is based on local, rational organizational choice, whereas later adoption is institutionalized and taken-for-granted (Tolbert & Zucker, 1983). Others argue that institutional processes are engaged from the outset (Jepperson, 1991). Similarly, the management fashion literature examines the management discourse surrounding IS innovations, and it promotes their adoption and difusion across diverse organizations (Abrahamson & Fairchild, 1999).

Against the backdrop of this body of literature, others have observed how a diverse inter-organizational community creates and employs an organizing vision (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997) of an IS innovation that is central to its early, as well as later, difusion. In this context, an organizing vision is a focal community idea for the application of information technology in organizations- (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, p. 460). This vision serves key functions in interpretation, legitimation, and the organization and mobilization of economic roles and exchanges. Complex institutional forces therefore determine the development, influence and decline of an organizing vision.

This paper places the organizing vision at center stage in our discussion of a recent IS innovation that came to be known as application service provision (ASP). ASP emerged in the latter half of the 1990s, as a new IS innovation for business computing, where firms could access their software applications remotely over the Internet or using a wide area network (WAN) (Currie, Desai, & Khan, 2004). Promoted by powerful institutional interests in the form of leading technology sector firms, government bodies, academia and the media, ASP became one of the most popular acronyms of the early dot.com era. Echoing the management discourse surrounding past and present information and communications technologies, ASP became a new IT silver bullet or panacea to enhance business performance (Susarla, Barua, & Whinston, 2003). Similar to the service bureaus of past decades, ASP was depicted as a modern form of pay-as-you-go business computing, which would reduce total cost of ownership of IT, provide 24 · 7 software availability and enable a firm to become more agile and flexible (IDC, 2000). In its simplest form, ASP would allow firms to remotely access their software applications over the Internet or via a virtual private network.

Like other organizing visions (i.e. CASE tools and BPR) (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997), rapid market growth was predicted for the ASP market particularly in the light of the tens of millions of small and medium businesses anticipated to adopt ASP. Notwithstanding the hyperbole that surrounded ASP, with all its expectations for market growth and perceived opportunities for small and medium size firms to reduce IT costs, the career dynamics- of ASP replicated past organizing visions or management fashions, where its initial popularity and hype gave way to its equally rapid demise within the space of only a few years. This process is inevitable, however, as an IS innovation is seen to commence its difusion transparently (since it is largely unrecognised), but will conclude it equally transparently, as the vision behind it is discredited and forgotten-, or becomes important and embedded- across the wider community (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). The process by which an organizing vision is ultimately collectively forgotten, will therefore occur irrespective of whether the outcome is abandonment or institutionalization (Ramiller & Swanson, 2003).

In this paper, we adopt a process-oriented analysis of the ASP organizing vision. In agreement with Swanson and Ramiller (1997), we believe that process-oriented, historical studies of specific organizing visions produce more than a rudimentary insight, since they are designed to observe the phenomenon over an extended period. Our interest is the larger community terrain over which the ASP organizing vision has ranged, rather than the adoption practices of specific organizations. We argue that, instead of the ASP organizing vision becoming institutionalized through its widespread difusion across the community, it failed to penetrate much beyond its intended market of small and medium size firms. A process analysis suggests that, as opposed to the supporting rhetoric of ASP losing salience and fading away, technology vendors recognising that ASP was ostensibly a flawed IS innovation (Hagel, 2002) sought to re-position themselves as Web Services firms, albeit ofering the same hosted software solutions to customers under another label. So rather than moving from one IS innovation to another in the way that fashions reach their ascendancy before moving into a state of decendancy (Ramiller & Swanson, 2003), our analysis suggests a more complex picture in that management discourse evolved to embrace facets of a new IS innovation in the form of Web Services, while at the same time undergoing a re-labelling exercise for ASP.

The paper is structured as follows. We begin with a brief overview of institutional theory and proceed by introducing the notion of the organizing vision. Next, we discuss the research method. By adopting a process-oriented analysis to investigate the ASP story using a range of data collection and analysis methods and techniques, our purpose is to ofer rich and diverse insights into how organizing visions evolve, fade, and change over time. We then discuss the ASP organizing vision using the six key themes of Swanson and Ramiller-s (1997) model: community discourse; community structure and commerce; the IS practitioner subculture; the business problematic; core technology; and innovation, adoption and difusion. We conclude the paper by observing how poor adoption of ASP has led to it undergoing a re-labelling exercise, with many ASP firms now describing themselves as Web Service vendors. Finally, we suggest ideas for future research on institutionalist perspectives on IS innovation and change.

## 2. The organizing vision

The intellectual antecedents of the organizing vision are derived from institutionalist theory that examines how social choices are shaped, mediated, and channelled by institutional arrangements (Powell & Dimaggio, 1991). Institutionalization occurs at the sectoral or societal levels, with the emphasis upon inter-organizational change. The focal lens is on how organizational forms, structural components, and rules become institutionalized, and not on specific organizations, departments or individuals. Institutionalization is viewed both as a phenomenological process where social relationships and actions come to be taken for granted, and a state of afairs in which shared cognitions determine what actions are possible and what has meaning (Zucker, 1983, p. 2). An institutional perspective confines irrationality to the formal structure, and not at the level of the individual. It attributes the difusion of departments and operating procedures to inter-organizational influences, conformity, and the persuasiveness of cultural accounts, rather than to the functions they are intended to perform- (Powell & Dimaggio, 1991, p. 13).

There are several dimensions to shaping institutional environments. Powerful organizations force their immediate relational networks to adapt to their structures and relations. For example, large firms in the technology sector create demand for particular products and services (Carr, 2003). Other firms, particularly small and medium businesses, may have to adapt to these changes (Hayes & Wheelright, 1984), especially insofar as coping with rapid technological obsolescence. Coupled with this, powerful organizations attempt to build their goals and procedures directly into society as institutional rules. This is witnessed where industry leaders help to create the rules, which govern best practice, such as technology standards (Porter, 1985). This creates a barrier to entry as rivals must then compete both in social networks or markets and in contexts of institutional rules which are defined by extant organizations. In this fashion, given organizational forms perpetuate themselves by becoming institutionalized rules- (Meyer & Rowan, 1991, p. 41 or 49).

Prior work on institutional theory has suggested that early adoption of innovations stems from local, rational organizational choice, while later adoption is institutionalized and based upon taken-for-granted assumptions about what constitutes proper practice (Tolbert & Zucker, 1983). More recently, writers on institutional theory question this view by asserting that institutional processes are present at the outset of an innovation-s difusion (Jepperson, 1991), and that rational or nonrational choice (Scott, 1994) is exercised within the context of, and with reference to, those processes (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). This view asserts that institutional processes may reduce the perceived uncertainty surrounding the innovation and, hence, in making local rationality, or informed choice, possible. These processes are fundamentally cognitive and exercised at the relatively under-explored level of the organizational field (Scott, 2001).

This concept is relevant to our discussion since it encapsulates those organizations that, in the aggregate, constitute a recognized area of institutional life- (Powell & Dimaggio, 1991, p. 143). This includes technology suppliers, customers, government agencies, the business and technology media and other relevant organizations. The cohesiveness of organizational fields is held together by shared cultural-cognitive or normative frameworks or by a common regulatory system that constitutes an identifiable area of institutional life (Scott, 2001). The notion of a field suggests the existence of a community of organizations imbued with a shared meaning where its participants interact more frequently and fatefully- with each another than outside the field (Scott, 1994, p. 207–8).

The IT industry may be conceptualised as an organizational field since it comprises a heterogeneous network of parties with a variety of tangible and intangible interests. Within this context, its members may collectively create and employ an organizing vision (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997) of an IS innovation that is fundamental to the decisions and actions afecting its adoption and difusion. Such an organizing vision is the product of the eforts of the members within the community to make sense (Weick, 1995) of the innovation as an organizational opportunity that, in turn, helps to define and create it. Thus, CASE tools, client server computing, Intranet technologies, and other IS-related innovations, while focused upon a narrower organizational application, are also part of the sense-making of the broader organizational field (Dimaggio & Powell, 1983).

As a contribution to institutional theory, the organizing vision is a useful theoretical lens for undertaking a process-orientated analysis of an IS innovation which came to be known as application service provision, or ASP. Unlike many organizational studies examining innovations that are well difused, we concur that institutional theories are relevant for observing the career dynamics of an IS innovation. That is, from their early adoption (Teo, Wei, & Benbasat, 2003) to their later difusion and eventual fading away from community discourse, a process described as collective forgetting- occurs (Grint & Willcocks, 1995).

The organizing vision concept by Swanson and Ramiller (1997) supports a process-oriented view since it observes how a focal community idea- for an IS innovation changes over time. It is vision for organizing in a way that embeds and utilizes information technology in organizational structures and processes- (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, p. 460). The authors assert that organizing visions arise because they fulfil certain basic functions in the development and difusion of IS innovations. They present three concepts that are critical for exploiting the opportunities presented by an emerging IS innovation. They are interpretation, legitimation, and mobilization. These are not linear processes, but instead constitute important features pertaining to the life-cycle or career dynamics of the organizing vision. We will briefly discuss each one.

## 2.1. Interpretation

First, the interpretation of an emerging IS innovation is not readily understood within and outside the IS practitioner community. Descriptive accounts of an IS innovation may vary between interested parties, thus creating confusion and misunderstanding. Past examples are JIT manufacturing methods that became popular during the 1980s and BPR following in the 1990s. In the case of JIT, interpretations varied both within and across firms and countries. North American and European interpretations tended to perceive the benefits from JIT more narrowly, focusing upon the eficiency gains within the manufacturing process (Schonberger, 1986) whereas case research conducted in Japan suggested that JIT encapsulated a wider array of activities, such as production management methods, TQM (including quality circles) and total preventive maintenance methods (TPM) (Currie & Seddon, 1992). Similarly BPR generated intensive interest, with interpretations varying from those that emphasised the technical and operational benefits from re-engineering the corporation (Hammer & Champy, 1993), to others that concentrated on the organizational and individual learning dimension (Grint & Willcocks, 1995). Yet difering interpretations about an emerging IS innovation are not necessarily harmful to its later adoption. Rather, the various interpretations represent the attempt by the community to develop a social account (Jepperson, 1991) that explicates the IS innovation within the broader social, technical and economic domain (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). An interesting observation about emerging IS innovations is how they are often interpreted as ofering something new across a range of dissimilar organizations (Abrahamson, 1991). Further, they are inextricably linked to notions of progress and prosperity (Peters, 1990). IS innovations have all been described in the language of having a revolutionary impact on work organization (Currie, 1999). More recently, e-commerce, e-business, and the world-wide-web (WWW) were all presented as critical to the survival of firms until the dot.com crash (Cassidy, 2002), which saw these organizing visions becoming tainted with stories of disappointment and failure. Further, IS innovations are invariably couched in the language of eras within business computing.

For example, the mainframe era, the client server era and the Internet era, each suggest that IS innovation follows a linear path. The implication for firms is therefore to keep pace with the latest IS innovation, or risk the fate of becoming uncompetitive.

## 2.2. Legitimation

Second, legitimation is where the underlying rationale for the IS innovation is developed, tested, refined, and propagated as part of the organizing vision itself- (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, p. 461). Would-be adopters question the merits and de-merits of adopting the IS innovation, and this may be influenced by observing the behavior of others. In the example of BPR and IT, (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997) show how Hammer and Champy (1993) linked this organizing vision to the end of the industrial era. In this regard, BPR was depicted as an organization-wide innovation, with IT playing a major role in the change process. More recently, legitimation of e-business has been fuelled by powerful institutional interests from government and leading technology sector firms using discourse that emphasises the importance of moving to the networked society or digital economy and the potential threat from emerging economies (Department of Commerce, 1999). The legitimation processes for an IS innovation is therefore supported by the reputation and authority of those who promulgate it, and through the identities of those who undertake the innovation (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, p. 461).

## 2.3. Mobilization

Third, the organizing vision further serves the dynamic function of helping to activate, motivate, and structure the entrepreneurial and market forces that emerge to support the material realization of the innovation- (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, p. 461). The IS innovation gains traction in the marketplace as interested parties mobilize resources to further generate interest. For example, during the height of the dot.com boom, there were countless conferences, trade fairs and exhibitions on various aspects of e-business, from supply-chain management (SCM), e-procurement, sector-specific software applications (i.e. for health and manufacturing) and many others. Leading industry players, business analyst firms and governments often sponsored these events. During this period, numerous start-up e-business firms emerged, having secured first round venture capital funding (Cassidy, 2002). In conjunction with the proliferation of industry events, the business and technology media produced numerous publications espousing the benefits from e-business. This generated a feedback loop that further reinforced the perception that large and small firms should embrace e-business or risk losing out to their more innovative competitors (DTI, 1999).

From this brief overview of some of the key concepts in institutional theory coupled with the notion of how IS innovations may be conceptualized as organizing visions, we develop three broad research questions: How do institutional arrangements shape the nature of collective action in the formation of an IS organizing vision? What is the role of powerful institutional groups in building and maintaining an IS organizing vision? How are IS innovations adopted and difused across the wider community? To answer these questions, a process-oriented research study was conducted into how the emerging IS innovation labelled as ASP was adopted and difused.

## 3. Research method

The research began in 1999 at the height of the dot.com boom where a proliferation of e-business start-ups had entered the market place. The initial research interest was to investigate an emerging e-business phenomenon labelled by the IS practitioner community as application services provision, or ASP. Why ASP? As an emerging IS innovation, ASP became one of the most popular buzzwords or acronyms of the early dot.com era. The description of ASP as a buzzword is not meant to downgrade its significance as potentially a major development in business computing, since we concur with Swanson and Ramiller (1997) that buzzwords play an important role in the formation and difusion of organizing visions.

This research study investigated the adoption and difusion of ASP from both a customer and supply-side perspective. Three literature streams informed the study. They were, (1) the e-business literature, with particular emphasis upon the growth of e-business and its impact upon contemporary firms (Timmers, 1999; Weill & Vitale, 2001), (2) the IT management literature, to understand how e-business may bring about changes in strategic IS practices, such as outsourcing, planning, evaluation and cost control (Brown & Eisenhardt, 1998; Willcocks & Lacity, 1998); and (3) the organization theory literature, to provide a theoretical backdrop on how socio-economic and technical factors influence and change work organization (Orlikowski & Barley, 2001; Orlikowski, Walsham, Jones, & DeGross, 1995).

The duration of the research program lasted four years where a range of qualitative and quantitative research methods was used. Initially, a research study was planned over a two-year period, beginning in 1999, using mainly an exploratory-descriptive approach for data collection and analysis. However, research funding was awarded for a further two years of investigation. This enabled a program of research to be undertaken (comprising a number of focused studies on ASP) rather than the much shorter study originally planned. The advantage was the opportunity to conduct longitudinal, process-oriented analysis of the ASP phenomenon.

While it is outside the scope of this paper to discuss the results from the entire research program, we present an overview of the various methods and techniques of data collection and analysis. In this paper, we concentrate our attention mainly on the results derived from exploratory-case studies with IT vendors and potential and existing ASP customers. Diferentiating between potential and existing ASP customers was important, since it was critical to gain an understanding of the adoption strategies of firms that were already using an ASP solution, and those that were not.

In addition to primary data collection, we used secondary data from a range of sources listed below. This research study primarily used an inductive research method rather than one designed to test a particular theory from the outset. The intention was to collect and analyze a range of material from many diferent sources, rather than to build a conceptual model and hypotheses. Essentially, the aim was to make sense of what was going on in this fast moving, dynamic and confusing market space.

## 3.1. Data collection and analysis

Three approaches to data collection were adopted: (1) the creation of an ASP database, (2) visits to trade fairs and exhibitions, (3) semi-structured interviews with ASPs and customers. First, an ASP database was set up to capture all those firms describing themselves as ASPs. Over 700 hundred firms were identified. This exercise was useful since it became apparent that ASPs comprised a heterogeneous group of firms, mostly from the technology sector. For example, large and well-established firms in telecommunications and computer hardware developed ASP enablement services, such as data storage, co-location and security. Other firms focused entirely upon the delivery of application services in three broad market/product segments: vertical (industry-specific); horizontal (business-wide); and enterprise (companywide) software applications (Currie & Seltsikas, 2001). Many others were pure-plays, having received first round venture capital funding to ofer hosted software applications, mainly through partnering with independent software vendors. Data on ASP firms was then categorised according to Type of ASP Provider-. Further analysis of this data produced a taxonomy of ASPs: (1) ASP Enabler, (2) Enterprise ASP, (3) Vertical ASP, (4) Horizontal ASP, (5) Pure-Play ASP. This exercise was useful for gaining an understanding of the technology industry structure in which ASP firms were positioned. From the taxonomy of ASPs, further categories were developed to diferentiate the Product and Service Oferings of ASPs-. Five key performance indicators were identified to delineate ASP oferings: (1) delivery of service, (2) management and operations, (3) integration of applications, (4) business transformation, (5) client/vendor relationships. The ASP database was a valuable tool, which acted as a source of ASP intelligence from which firms could be selected for in-depth case research.

Second, data was obtained through extensive visits to trade fairs, exhibitions and other technology sector events. Between 1999 and 2000, twelve visits were made to various ASP events in Europe and the US. These visits were critical for gaining an overview of the scale and scope of the emerging ASP market. Much of the data collected was in the form of company brochures and marketing literature, sample software, press and media releases, white papers written by ASPs or their management consultant representatives, power-point presentations from CEOs, CIOs and other personnel at industry events, web sites (i.e. of ASP firms, industry/business analysts, IT industry special interest groups or SIGs), and from numerous informal conversations with a range of participants. These informal conversations were critical for gaining an intuitive feel for how participants perceived the ASP market would develop, i.e. whether it would change the business computing landscape or become just another buzzword-. While it was not appropriate to record these conversations taking place, many of these comments were entered into notebooks as they were considered as important as secondary data from company brochures and reports.

Third, the principal source of data collection was from semi-structured interviews with executives, managers and technical personnel at ASP vendors and customer firms. All interviews were tape recorded and transcribed. Access to ASPs was obtained by attending ASP events and from the ASP database. ASPs were selected for their diversity to provide an overview of the look and feel of the ASP industry, rather than simply to compare and contrast their diferent service oferings. Customers were identified by trawling through ASP vendor brochures and from the business computing trade press. ASP customers were interviewed in a range of industry sectors, such as financial services, retailing, manufacturing, and sports and leisure. Identifying ASP customers was not easy as most ASPs had very few customer reference sites. Even where ASPs claimed to have thousands of customers, these were mainly confined to a few firms that were using an ASP for commodity (hosted) applications such as email for a large number of their employees. The data from these interviews produced a wealth of material. Careful attention was exercised to divide the semistructured questionnaire into broad subject and topic areas (see Table 1).

Having conducted the first phase of the research (September 1999 to March 2000), two research grants <sup>1</sup> were obtained to fund extensive ASP industry-analysis studies. This enabled three cross-national visits to the US (Silicon Valley) to provide a rich picture of how ASP industry stakeholders defined, speculated, and evaluated this new IS innovation for business computing. In addition to the above methods of data collection and analysis, two questionnaire surveys were conducted on both customer and supply-side themes and issues, although it is outside the scope of this paper to discuss these findings in detail. The results from this survey have been published (Currie et al., 2004). In total, 174 interviews were carried out with ASP firms, industry analysts and customers.

## 3.2. Deriving theory from data

This research program did not set out to test a particular theory since it was primarily concerned to investigate an emerging IS innovation within the technology sector using an exploratory-descriptive approach to data collection and analysis (Yin, 1984). The initial step was to collect data, which could then be analysed through a process of comparing and contrasting its content. The next step was the conceptual ordering of the data into classifications, taxonomies, constructs and concepts to form an overarching explanatory scheme- (Strauss & Corbin, 1998, p. 25) rather than to demonstrate their relationships or inter-dependencies. Following a lengthy process of data collection and conceptual ordering, the next challenge was to systematically integrate the various explicitly stated dimensions through statements of relationship (Strauss & Corbin, 1998, p. 25). This process is known as deriving theory from the data.

In undertaking this study, a range of theories were examined such as the resourcebased view (RVB), transaction cost theory (TCT) and resource dependency theory (RDT). Whilst each of these theories provided a lens through which part of the ASP story could be analysed, the data collection and conceptual ordering phases pointed to the need for a wider societal and process-oriented analysis. For example, RBV ofered rich insights on how ASP vendors strategically positioned themselves by attempting to develop dynamic assets, resources and capabilities to compete against rival firms. Yet RBV ofered few insights beyond a firm-level analysis. This was also the case with TCT and RDT. However, our interest was to explore the discourse or rhetoric- emanating from the technology sector, as well as how an emerging IS innovation was adopted and difused across the IS user community. This necessitated the search for a theory to explain broader concerns of how powerful groups, such as leading technology vendors, government and industry analysts, help to create and promote new IS innovations. The theory would also ofer insights into the factors pertaining to the growth and possible decline of ASP, particularly in the light of the ascendancy and decendancy of past innovations (Ramiller & Swanson, 2003).

## 3.3. A process-oriented analysis

The fact that a particular theoretical model did not underpin this research study from the outset was unproblematic, as a process-oriented approach to ASP adoption and difusion was considered more fruitful than a comparative analysis of specific case studies on ASP vendors and customers. Given the plethora of case-based research on IS innovations, this research was conducted at the time of intense activity and turbulence within the technology sector. Between 1998 and 2001, numerous Internet start-ups were created and abandoned, despite extensive venture capital funding coupled with the optimistic messages about their potential for contributing to economic growth (Cassidy, 2002). Since this research was conducted at a period in time that encapsulated the rise and decline of numerous dot.coms, it seemed appropriate to seek a theoretical explanation for the ASP story that included a processoriented analysis of significant events and changes.

Recognising that there were very few process-orientated studies on why management fashions in the form of IS innovations rise and decline (Abrahamson & Fairchild, 1999), attempts were made to seek a theoretical explanation within the organizational theory literature. This was found in the seminal work of Swanson and Ramiller (1997) in their conceptualisation of the organizing vision, with its theoretical antecedents in the area of institutional theory. Having developed a model of the organizing vision, these authors call for process-orientated, historical studies on specific organizing visions....if we are to achieve more than the present rudimentary insight- (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, p. 471).

## 3.4. The organizing vision as a theoretical lens for analysing ASP

This research deploys the organizing vision model as a theoretical lens for undertaking a process-oriented analysis of ASP. In agreement with Swanson and Ramiller (1997) this endeavour is best served by focusing upon the evolving text and institutional presence of the ASP organizing vision rather than on the adoption practices of specific firms. The organizing vision proposed by Swanson and Ramiller (1997) is presented as a model with six features. They are (1) community discourse; (2) community structure and commerce; (3) the IS practitioner subculture; (4) the business problematic; (5) core technology; and (6) innovation and difusion. The model presented by Swanson and Ramiller (1997) is reproduced in Fig. 1.

These features appeared to overlap our own conceptual ordering of ASP. For example, community discourse points to the rhetoric, language and descriptions about an IS innovation. Similarly, our classification ASP model- (Table 1) more broadly captured the various descriptions of ASP.

By conceptualising ASP as an organizing vision, we use this model as a framework for presenting our data. Each of the six features is presented sequentially, though this does not reflect a linear path through which the ASP journey proceeded. Rather, it is simply to provide a structure to our discussion whilst at the same time, remaining faithful to the Swanson and Ramiller (1997) model. We do, however, highlight areas where the model and the terminology used to elucidate it may not quite fit with this version of the ASP story. Notwithstanding this caveat, conceptualising the ASP phenomenon as an IS organizing vision ofers rich insights into broader debates within the e-business, IT management and organization theory literature, particularly insofar as neo-institutional theory adopts a wider (i.e. societal and inter-organizational) unit of analysis rather than one which simply conveys examples of best practice at the organizational or individual levels.

## 4. ASP as an organizing vision

Application service provision or ASP emerged towards the end of the 1990s in parallel with numerous academic and practitioner material (books, articles, company brochures, etc.) about the possibilities and opportunities arising from the commercialisation of the Internet and World Wide Web (WWW). New terminology emerged in the form of e-commerce (Timmers, 1999), e-business models (Weill & Vitale, 2001), the networked society (Castells, 1996) the Internet galaxy (Castells, 2001) and the digital economy (US Department of Commerce, 1999). In common with past IS innovations, ASP was expected to enhance business value and performance, particularly for small and medium size firms, which, hitherto, had been unable to aford enterprise-wide applications such as enterprise resource planning (ERP).

Numerous interpretations emerged describing ASP as, The new IT business model- (ITAA, 2000), and the Net-s next killer app- (Dean & Gilchrist, 2000). ASP was further described as a new variant of information systems outsourcing using the name of AppSourcing- (IDC, 2000). Other interpretations suggested the ASP movement- was a market moving phenomenon- emerging from the late 1990s onwards and maturing in Internet time- (Cooper & Moore, 2000).

Powerful institutional interests generally promulgated these messages. Among them were, international government organizations which envisioned new opportunities for economic growth and employment from the Internet; large firms in the technology sector aiming to develop new Internet-enabled products and services; industry/business analysts working with, or independently from, client firms, to generate hype about the Internet, with the aim of furthering their own business opportunities; and also, academic institutions through developing new postgraduate and undergraduate degree courses/modules aimed to attract new students and to be seen to be keeping up-to-date with the latest trends, ideas and thinking on business computing.

In parallel with other forms of e-business (i.e. business-to-business) institutional support for ASP was fuelled by many groups. Large technology sector firms mobilized themselves to set up various special interest groups and membership organizations. The ASP Industry Consortium (ASPic) was established in May 1999 by twenty-five <sup>2</sup> technology sector firms. Its mission was to promote the ASP industry by sponsoring research, fostering standards, and articulating the measurable benefits of this evolving delivery model-. ASPic members defined an ASP as a firm that, manages and delivers application capabilities to multiple entities from data centers across a wide area network. An ASP may be a commercial entity, providing a paid service to customers or, conversely, a not-for-profit or government organization supporting endusers-.

The founding members of the ASPic were large technology vendors, and their target customer market for ASP was primarily small and medium size firms. Within a year, the membership base of ASPic grew to over 600 firms, boosted by a range of self styled ASPs. Against a backdrop of large institutional interests, all of whom perceived the ASP model as a way to generate new business opportunities for their firms, one industry analyst commented that, . . .serious opportunities still exist for start-up and emerging players- (Durlacher, 1999). This encouraged the formation of numerous small start-up ASP, often seeking financial, business and technical support from the larger players.

Other outlets to promote ASP emerged in the form of Internet reference or news sites. For example, WebHarbor.com described itself as, The ASP Industry Portal-. Others were aspnews.com and aspindustrynews.com, with the mission to, cover the ASP marketplace-. All these sites served to educate- potential customers by providing market analysis on the latest software-as-a-service product oferings, markets trends and surveys, mergers and acquisitions between industry players, ASP customer reference sites, gossip and industry/business analysis.

As the ASP model won the backing of various groups, those with vested interests in the technology sector began to convey cautionary messages highlighting the growing relevance of ASP. These messages amounted to a call for firms to mobilize resources to embrace this new IS innovation in business computing. An often cited quote made around 1999 was from the Chief Executive Oficer of Sun Microsystems:

Five years from now, if you-re a CIO with a head for business, you won-t be buying computers anymore. You won-t be buying software either. You will rent all your resources from an Application Service Provider- (Source: Scott McNealy, CEO, Sun Microsystems).

This message later appeared on the brochures and marketing material of firms, many of which were in the process of developing their own ASP business. The message was repeated as a caution to chief information oficers (CIOs) and others, responsible for business computing in their organizations, to adopt the ASP model. Powerful messages were further articulated in the form of emerging trends and new waves in business computing, for the purpose of demonstrating the threats posed by the rapid and relentless pace of technical change. Implicit in these messages was the warning that, unless managers and IT professionals keep pace with these changes, they will lose out to their competitors. Past warnings from the technology sector to Get innovative or get dead- (Peters, 1990) were replaced by modern interpretations, If you-re not an e-business, you-re out of business!-. The notion of the relentless pace of Internet time, was also linked to Moore-s Law, <sup>3</sup> originally used to describe changes in the semi-conductor industry, in that, every 18 months, for the foreseeable future, chip density (and hence computing power) would double while cost remained constant, thus creating ever more powerful computing devices without raising their price (Downes & Mui, 2000).

## 4.1. ASP community discourse, structure and commerce

Against a backdrop of optimism surrounding the possibilities of e-commerce from government, the technology sector, industry analysts and academia, ASP became part of the community discourse among these members, thus reinforcing its legitimacy as an IS innovation supported by powerful institutional interests. Discourse is defined as a language or system of representation that has developed socially in order to make and circulate a coherent set of meanings about an important topic area (Fiske, 1987, p. 14). Throughout the year 2000, the acronym, ASP was claimed by ASPic to be the most popular and commonly used within business computing. This was reinforced by the widespread coverage and interest in ASP with the claim that, ASPs enter the mainstream- within business computing.

Intensive activities were centred around international conferences, trade fairs, the computer press (and some aspects of the business literature), newspaper articles, the emergence of ASP news sites, Internet business and technical publications, company websites (i.e. their product and service portfolios, white papers and case studies) and in academic research. ASP could not simply be dismissed as the latest buzzword, not least because serious financial investment from large established firms, coupled with venture capital funding for new ASP start-ups, signalled a belief that software-as-aservice represented a new opportunity for firms to make money, and for customers to benefit from a new value proposition from the technology sector. Indeed, the existence and development of ASP was facilitated by the development and repetition of the ASP buzzword, particularly as the concept of software-as-a-service was not as original or striking. To this end, a buzzword gives a title, so to speak, to the public account, the story, which the organizing vision represents- (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, p. 463). The buzzword gains further legitimacy as the discourse creates or redefines other forms of language, including, business or technical concepts, phrases and themes, etc, all of which comprise a legitimated vocabulary- (Meyer & Rowan, 1977).

Yet discourse surrounding ASP reflected diferences in priorities, scope, and perceptions among community members since it evolved through the dynamic layering of texts-, each building upon, and responding to, previous texts (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, p. 463). Large firms identifying fresh business opportunities by endorsing the shift from software-as-a-product to software-as-a-service, each interpreted ASP according to their own business problematic. Echoing these diferent interpretations was a proliferation of large and small firms, each embracing the ASP label, yet ofering potential customers their own version of a viable ASP solution. Whilst these firms shared the view that utility computing using the ASP model was compatible with business imperatives to deploy information technology to enhance eficiency and reduce costs, examples of how this would be achieved may be found in viewing ASP as a taxonomy, <sup>4</sup> where diferent ASP models are deconstructed into their component parts. The ASP marketing literature pointed to a variety of oferings from diferent technology vendors. Such product and service diferentiation among suppliers suggested that ASP was not a one-size-fits-all IS innovation.

Telecommunications firms, independent software vendors, and start-ups all claimed to ofer an ASP service, even though their backgrounds were dissimilar. Other ASPs positioned themselves to serve niche or vertical markets. A view reflected in the ASP community discourse was that product/service diferentiation was an important goal of ASPs, as potential customers were more likely to outsource their software applications to technology vendors who understood their business. Numerous other ASPs peddled similar messages. For example, one described itself as an industry-specific ASP- while another claimed it was, Europe-s leading ASP-.

## 4.2. Technology change and the business problematic

Community discourse about ASP was inextricably linked to a wider business problematic, beyond the narrow concerns of technology management per se, to embrace current best practice thinking in business and management more generally. Technology vendors depicted ASP as ofering numerous benefits, not simply to business organizations, but to society as a whole. At the organizational level, ASP was described as an IS innovation which would tackle the productivity-paradox (Strassmann, 1985) problem, where the costs of technology outweigh the return on investment. ASP would be inexpensive to implement, it would enable firms to access their software applications on a 24 · 7 basis, and it would ofer extensive advantages to enhance flexibility (IDC, 2000). Further, ASP was a wake up call to senior managers about the consequences of not keeping pace with technology – in this case, the Internet. By failing to embrace the ASP model, small and medium size firms were likely to face economic meltdown.

At the wider societal level, government actively promoted ASP in their wider ebusiness campaigns to get firms connected- (DTI, 2002). Numerous publications were produced giving examples of how government agencies were embracing e-business across all services, from schools, hospitals and administration. Calls for rapid action to deploy software application capabilities via ASPs were further repeated throughout the technology sector.

The notion of the Internet as a mechanism for enhancing business agility and flexibility, in addition to allowing small firms to appear larger, <sup>5</sup> gained some ground, particularly as start-up ASPs attempted to disguise their lack of physical assets, with some even proclaiming that outsourcing all their non-core services enabled them to better serve their customers. ASP start-ups recognised that partnering with leading technology players was essential, particularly as many of them had few assets and resources other than a marketing plan to develop an ASP brand. A common partnership was between ASP start-ups/ISVs and telecommunications firms. The latter perceived lucrative opportunities for revenue generation by selling data centre and storage services to customers via ISVs and/or ASPs, premised on the notion that Internet access to data was a growing business opportunity.

## 4.3. Extending ASP within and beyond the IS practitioner community

The flurry of activity surrounding ASP was at its peak in 2000. To some commentators, ASP was little more than a solution in search of a problem, developed and promoted by the technology sector, yet remaining poorly difused across the IS practitioner community. <sup>6</sup> and beyond. Critics, even those from technology firms, described ASP as another technology push initiative, no diferent from the service bureaus of previous decades. By 2001, the initial optimism about the Internet revolution and all its possibilities for large and small firms to compete more efectively in global markets gave way to the pessimistic realisation that few ASPs were economically viable. This contributed to the wider dot.com shakeout, with numerous ASP going out of business by the end of 2001. Within the IS practitioner community, several explanations were put forward to explain the failures. In the case of one ISV, which abandoned its strategy of operating directly as an ASP in favour of working through channel partners, business analysts described this as a move indicating an immaturity in the ASP market as well as a rethink on the part of software suppliers- (Computer Weekly, April, 2000).

The notion of an immature market is interesting since it implies that markets move in linear progression: from immaturity to maturity to decline. By this logic, ASP could be depicted as being ahead of its time, where the reluctance of firms to adopt an ASP solution could be explained in terms of a lack of cultural, social, organizational and technical readiness. One view was that potential customers did not fully understand the ASP model, leading an industry analyst to assert that, many of those who do not understand the ASP idea simply confuse things, those that fear it tarnish the concept and this all adds to a high level of uncertainty relating to ASP (Fawcett, 2000).

Failure to understand- the ASP organizing vision was repeated across the technology sector. For example, technology vendors tended to define ASP to match their own business objectives. This resulted in numerous interpretations and definitions of how ASP could create value for the customer. Similarly, business analysts gave little explanation to support their highly optimistic forecasts on the ASP market, nor did they ofer detailed case study examples on successful ASP implementations. Management consultants also saw opportunities to join the ASP bandwagon. This group saw the solution to the poor adoption and difusion of ASP as a need for more education-. Interviews with ASP customers during this period pointed up some interesting observations about the sales and marketing tactics of ASP firms. One ASP customer, an IT manager in the leisure industry commented.

We used an ASP to host Microsoft Ofice and Exchange for us. We didn-t want the hassle of running these applications in-house so we decided to outsource the work. We under-estimated two potential problems. The first was that the company went out of business within three months. If this wasn-t bad enough, we then lost a lot of our data. After that, we brought the application back inhouse. I don-t think we will ever use an ASP again!-

Problems of this nature were not isolated incidents, but became more commonplace as successive ASP firms went out of business. While large technology vendors had spent many millions of dollars promoting ASP, the lack of customer reference sites and growing disaster stories of poor service and failed ASPs fuelled caution and distrust about ASP. A further issue was the relationship between ASP and core technology.

## 4.4. Core technology and the problem of legacy systems

Like the situation with BPR (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997) the relationship between ASP and core technology was poorly defined. Many vendors actively sought to dismiss customer concerns about how ASP would be integrated with other technologies, i.e legacy systems. Interviews with ASP vendors suggested they ring-fenced ASP as a stand-alone, out-of-the-box- solution, giving customers the advantage of accessing their software applications on a subscription pricing model. The evangelical promotion of ASP downplayed any reference to past investment in core technology. One ASP suggested that, ‘‘With ASP, you don-t have to invest in the hardware, software and staf needed to support mission-critical business applications’’.

Whilst the pay-as-you-go model was viable for single software applications (i.e. email) the situation was more complex in terms of mission-critical or enterprise-wide applications (i.e. CRM). The failure on the part of many ASPs to address important issues of integration with existing systems and the organizational procedures and practices behind these technologies, meant that potential customer interest was only likely to be generated for commodity-based (of-the-shelf) software applications.

Another problem concerned customisation and how ASP could be embedded into single or multiple business processes. Recognising that customisation could become a thorny issue for ASPs, one commentator observed that, Although a big challenge for ASPs, application customisation may prove inevitable in the future- (Bloss, 2000, p. 1). Since the revenues from most commodity software were likely to be small (i.e. \$20 per user per month for e-mail) many leading software vendors aligned with ASPs to provide outsourced CRM to small and medium size firms.

Although the market to ofer one-to-many hosted vanilla-ERP (i.e. non-customised) software applications to small and medium size firms looked promising, both large and small potential ASP adopters questioned its viability. This model of software applications delivery was seemingly at odds with perceptions of how ASP would fit with a customer-s core technology. Examples from the financial services sector found that implementation of large scale packaged ERP systems usually required more than 90% customisation. Since ASP was a one-size-fits-all ofering, it was not considered suitable for this environment. This did not bode well for the ASP model, as IT directors and managers saw little value in adopting ASP, unless it was a stand-alone application for a specific, albeit, narrow business requirement.

Firms with large-scale ERP systems saw few benefits in adopting a pay-as-you-go ERP system as an adjunct to their existing systems. One IT director at a large chemical firm was approached by his ERP vendor with an option to use a hosted ERP service. The ISV requested that additional software licenses were purchased at a discounted rate provided that each license had a named user. But as employees were allocated to use the ERP system on an as needed- basis, this option would have been expensive to implement. This led the IT director to conclude, The economics of ASP are not yet right’’.

The relationship between core technology and ASP proved to be a significant impediment to further developing the ASP organizing vision. Technology vendors underestimated the relationship between, (a) ASP and legacy systems, and (b) how ASP would fit with existing organizational policies, procedures, roles and responsibilities. A significant shortcoming of many ASPs was their failure to understand these relationships. Large firms, in particular, were unlikely to adopt ASP unless they could integrate disparate systems across business processes and reduce costs. Most ASP firms underestimated the importance of integration as an important customer requirement for large firms.

Interviews with potential adopters of ASP confirmed Swanson and Ramiller-s thesis (1997, p. 467), in that, technology at some points may constrain and obstruct the building of an organizing vision-. In the context of ASP, a mis-match was identified between the optimistic messages about ASP from technology vendors, and the lukewarm response from potential customers.

## 4.5. IS innovation, adoption and difusion

The processes of IS innovation, adoption and difusion are highly complex and not easy to disentangle (Zucker, 1987). The ASP organizing vision suggested a wide gulf between rhetoric and the reality, particularly between ASP firms and customers. At international conferences and trade fairs, spearheaded by leading technology vendors and industry analysts, the promoters of ASP far outnumbered existing and potential adopters. Throughout 2000, adoption rates of ASP remained low. Many ASP vendors had fewer than five paying customers. Yet customer reference sites were critical in the legitimation process to further develop the ASP organizing vision. Would-be ASP adopters wanted to see customer reference sites at reputable firms to reassure them that ASP was a viable option for their own firm. As 2001 approached, stories of ASP failures were reported. One industry analyst observed that, 60 per cent of current ASPs will not survive beyond 2001- (Terdiman, 2000). This projection was largely confirmed as ASPs were going out of business at a rapid rate. As the ASP market seemingly imploded with numerous ASP vendors ceasing to trade, it became apparent that surviving ASPs were beginning to question the ASP label to define their business activities. During this time, a new term appeared in the technology sector: Web Services.

## 4.6. From ASP to Web Services: a new organizing vision?

Following the dot.com downturn of 2001, most ASPs did not survive. Those that did largely abandoned the ASP acronym. ASPs that had previously described themselves as, Europe-s leading ASP- or Europe-s first ASP to specialise in the delivery of high-end, business critical front ofice applications-, began to perceive ASP as synonymous with IT failure. An interesting observation during 2002 was that many selfstyled ASPs started to re-label themselves Web Services firms. New Internet sites appeared where technology firms, having positioned themselves as ASPs, suddenly became Web Services Providers- or Web Service Enablers-. So what are Web Services? And, are Web Services any diferent from ASP?

These questions are not easily answered in the light of the emerging management discourse about Web Services largely because this IS innovation is still in its early stages. Current definitions suggest a business problematic that emphasises either organization-wide or technology imperatives. For example, Web Services are,

loosely coupled software components delivered over Internet standard technologies. A Web Service represents a business function or business service and can be accessed by another application...over public networks using generally available protocols.- (Source: Gartner Group, 2002), or a collection of business functions or capabilities taken from a single or multiple software applications that, when bundled together, can be published to a network using standard XML-based protocols for use by other applications. Each Web Service is a bundling block that enables the sharing of software functionality with other applications residing outside of the Web Service-s native IT environment-(Triple Tree, 2002).

The rhetoric emanating from the technology sector suggests that, Web Services address many of the shortcomings inherent in the flawed ASP model- (Hagel, 2002). In particular, the failure of many ASP firms to respond to customer requirements for integration and customization of software applications. Whereas ASP firms claimed that software-as-a-service using the rental model would revolutionize business computing, Web Services firms downplay this claim, by advising customers that, having already made a substantial investment in technology infrastructure, they should exploit this investment by deploying the integration capabilities of Web Services. So while management discourse on ASP has become tarnished as a result of business failure and dissatisfied customers, leading technology vendors are now jumping on the Web Services bandwagon.

Technology sector discourse on Web Services suggests that, where ASP failed, Web Services will succeed. Whereas ASPs over-emphasized the importance of 24 · 7 service delivery, pay-as-you-go software, and cost reduction of IT as the key benefits, Web Services firms believe such priorities are less important than those designed to maximize investment from existing technology. As the hyperbole surrounding Web Services continues to grow, ASPs have largely abandoned this label to define their own activities and increasingly slap the label Web Services onto their marketing literature. Such re-packaging and re-positioning is a major observation from this research study and one that has important implications for our understanding and analysis of IS organizing visions.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

In this section, we apply some of the key concepts of neo-institutional theory to our process-oriented analysis of the ASP organizing vision. We also ofer suggestions for further research on the organizing vision concept.

Tracking the development and subsequent decline of ASP over a four year period suggests the adoption and difusion of organizing visions across and beyond the IS practitioner community is a highly complex and dynamic process. Whilst we do not claim to understand this process in all its entirety, our discussion will concentrate on answering the three research questions which were posed at the outset of this study: How do institutional arrangements shape the nature of collective action in the formation of an IS organizing vision? What is the role of powerful institutional groups in building and maintaining an IS organizing vision? How are IS innovations adopted and difused across the wider community? The following sections revisit these questions in the light of the research findings.

## 5.1. The formation of an IS organizing vision

Like previous IS innovations, ASP emerged as an organizing vision which was part of a focal community idea- for the delivery and application of an IS innovation. The creation of organizing visions arise, because they serve certain basic functions in the creation and promulgation of IS innovations- (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, p. 460). The ASP organizing vision pointed up many similarities with past IS innovations, particularly insofar as the management discourse surrounding it produced contradictory statements, with some supporting its capacity to improve business computing, and others rejecting it as being no diferent from service bureau computing of past decades. But while management discourse about a new management fashion may comprise of conflicting statements (Ramiller, 2001), this also contributes to the formation of the substantive content of the organizing vision that may lead to its institutionalization.

ASP became imbued with a variety of definitions, labels and interpretations which were not readily grasped or understood both within and outside the IS practitioner community. This contributed to rhetorical confusion where technology vendors defined the benefits from ASP according to their own business agenda, rather than adhering to what the customer required as a software solution. Mixed messages across the IS practitioner community, and the proliferation of ASP events only served to reinforce this confusion, as ASP discourse had a misguiding efect where it distracted users- sense-making rather than increasing their understanding of how ASP would benefit their business.

As with past IS innovations, ASP in the rational sense was linked with notions of economic prosperity and technological progress. Like BPR (Hammer & Champy, 1993), ASP was promoted to enable firms to align their business objectives with technical advances. Yet its relationship with core technology was poorly defined. This constrained and obstructed the formation of the organizing vision since primary development of the organizing vision takes place during the innovations earliest difusion- (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, p. 458).

The multi-faceted and conflicting ASP management discourse produced contradictory expectations. At its most superficial level, ASP was a commodity-based ofering with the potential to contribute to wider societal aspirations emanating from the networked society- or digital economy- to get people connected. At a more complex level, ASP was depicted as an innovation that would resolve serious issues in the business problematic, such as poor productivity from technology and low return on investment. Mixed messages of this kind only served to confuse stakeholders. Over time, the substantive content of the ASP organizing vision remained underdeveloped, as management discourse was imbued with the notion that ASP had something for everybody. But what that consisted of for potential customers was not well articulated by technology sector firms whose contribution to the management discourse was derived from their own vantage point. The failure to develop a clear message about ASP led many IS practitioners not engaged in developing an ASP business to draw an early conclusion that ASP was nothing more than technology sector hype. Poor communication and mixed messages did not therefore bode well for the further development and maintenance of the ASP organizing vision both across and beyond the IS practitioner community.

## 5.2. Building and maintaining an IS organizing vision

Powerful institutional groups spearheaded the ASP organizing vision, particularly leading technology vendors, business analysts and government agencies. Whilst these groups helped to define, interpret and provide legitimacy to ASP, their role in building and maintaining this IS innovation was sporadic and often contradictory. Small and medium firms keenly observed the degree of commitment to ASP from large firms prior to taking their own technology adoption decisions. If large firms used ASP, this provided legitimacy for smaller firms to do the same, despite their difering technology requirements. Yet the failure of large firms to adopt ASP acted as a deterrent to small firms even though ASP was marketed to this group. By failing to adopt this IS innovation large firms were unwittingly encouraging their smaller counterparts to question ASP more closely.

On closer examination, large technology vendors invariably sent contradictory messages to the technology sector about their commitment and support to ASP. Even where they claimed to support ASP, these messages were often more rhetorical than substantive. Even though leading technology vendors set up subsidiaries to ofer ASP, these were quickly abandoned when profits were slow in coming. This did not bode well for building and maintaining the ASP organizing vision as The hesitant early majority among the prospective adopters relies on this development in its eforts to make sense of the innovation (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, p. 458).

To the extent that ASP was depicted as a solution for small and medium firms, lack of adoption by large firms tended to undermine the belief that organizations are similar in ways that would cause them to benefit equally from adopting a management technique- (Strang & Meyer, 1994). This led small and medium firms to ask, If large firms don-t use ASP, why should we?- This research therefore points to serious problems in adopting a one-size-sits-all marketing message (usually designed by and for large customers). In the case of ASP, a one-dimensional message simply produced a misguiding efect as small firms become increasingly confused by

ASP rhetoric which was at odds with their own interpretation of the business problematic.

On the surface, ASP ofered customers an attractive value proposition in that it would enable them to develop their e-business initiatives. Yet ASP was largely a commodity-based ofering with few prospects of being integrated with existing technology. At a more complex level, ASP was marketed as a significant IS innovation within business computing which would revolutionize the way firms deployed, hosted and managed their enterprise software applications. Both scenarios were problematic to the institutionalisation of the ASP organizing vision. Paradoxically, the simplicity of ASP oferings led many potential customers to ask, Why bother?- with ASP, particularly as they were reluctant to replace one commodity ofering such as accounting packages with another. But claims that ASP was a revolution in business computing were largely unsubstantiated as managers engaged in rational choices about the potential benefits and risks from ASP while also dismissing the hyperbole surrounding it.

Over a period of two years, mixed messages from the technology sector about the potential benefits of ASP further reinforced confusion among potential and existing customers. As a precursor to adopting an IS innovation, an organizing vision must form expectations for the future that reduce the perceived uncertainty associated with planning, decision making and action- (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, p. 459). Contrary to achieving this, ASP promised an uncertain future for both technology vendors and customers alike, particularly as web-enabled technologies emanated from an immature, volatile and uncertain market. As the dot.com market imploded, uncertainty about ASP further accompanied the economic downturn. As such, the building out and maintenance of the ASP organizing vision became tarnished with stories of failure. This afected the prospects for the adoption and difusion of ASP.

## 5.3. Adoption and difusion of an IS innovation

Institutional theory suggests that two aspects for the adoption and difusion of an IS innovation are related to institutionalization. The first is the relationship between the innovation and organizational reputation (Zucker, 1986). The second aspect is where independent evaluation of an IS innovation is replaced with uncritical acceptance based on its legitimacy. We discuss these two ideas in relation to the adoption and difusion of ASP.

The reputational efects of an IS innovation are linked to its institutionalisation within and beyond the organization (Zucker, 1987, p. 453). This research suggests that, whilst the technology sector promoted ASP as a solution tailor-made for small and medium size firms, widespread adoption and difusion was unlikely in the absence of large reputable firms ofering examples of how ASP was deployed, hosted and implemented in their own firms. Customer reference sites are an important ingredient for customer adoption of an IS innovation. While technology vendors were aware of this, they tended to believe they could convince potential customers to adopt ASP by using complicated technology cost of ownership models (which were designed for large investments in IT) and outside the business problematic of small firms. Clearly, this message mis-fired with small and medium firms, many of whom asked, What are the real benefits from ASP?- Where are the examples of best practice?- The lack of reputational efects further inhibited the institutionalisation of ASP as the intervention of large technology vendors served to confuse small and medium firms making them reluctant to adopt ASP. While the organizing vision concept says little about the influence of large firms over small ones, this research substantiates the importance of reputational efects in developing IS innovations.

The second aspect is where independent evaluation of an IS innovation is replaced with uncritical acceptance based on its legitimacy (Tolbert & Zucker, 1983). For IS innovations, the link between reputational efects and evaluation is critical, particularly as many innovations are correlated, not causally related, to high performance-(Zucker, 1987, p. 453). Once an IS innovation becomes institutionalised, it is less likely to be formally evaluated.

Presented as an exciting and new IS innovation, technology vendors claimed ASP would ofer a new way of evaluating and procuring software applications, priced on a pay-as-you-go model. This would be a major shift from the traditional licensing and maintenance contracts for pricing software applications. While the rhetoric supported a change in the evaluation techniques, the reality was somewhat diferent suggesting that institutions are shaped by historical factors that restrict the range of options available to decision makers- (North, 1990). An impediment to ASP adoption was that decision makers were unlikely to abandon existing methods and techniques for evaluating technology irrespective of whether an IS innovation is correlated or causally related to enhanced performance. In this study, individual behaviour was shaped and constrained by institutional practices where calls from industry to adopt ASP were met with increasing caution as sense makers were confused about the relationship between ASP and core technology. Such confusion was counter-productive as firms sought more rather than less measures and metrics to evaluate ASP.

The above points to the notion that, Where the organizing vision remains underdeveloped after early adoption, later difusion and institutionalisation of the innovation is likely to be retarded- (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, p. 458). While early adopters of an IS innovation ofer a template for change (Hinings, Greenwood, Ranson, & Walsh, 1986) the supporting rhetoric of an organizing vision is likely to lose salience and fade over time, either because the IS innovation has been abandoned or become institutionalized (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). Initially, adoption may be predicted on a rational basis as an important and much needed organizational or technical change. But as difusion continues, the explanatory power of the variables decreases significantly (Zucker, 1987).

With ASP, confusing and contradictory management discourse adversely afected its adoption and difusion across the IS practitioner community. As the substantive content of the management discourse remained under-developed, the future prospects for ASP looked increasingly bleak. Correspondingly, the ASP organization vision lost salience and faded over time, not because it became institutionalised, but because it failed to develop much beyond the rhetorical stage. Coinciding with the economic downturn and as part of the wider dot.com crash, ASP simply failed to gain ground as an IS innovation with compelling links to the business problematic. For a brief period, ASP became a fashionable marketing exercise led by technology firms. Yet for a fashion to become adopted, it needs the support of its target audience. ASP failed to achieve this status and gradually faded from the list of hot innovations-.

But rather than disappear completely, ASP was re-packaged by technology vendors in the form of Web Services. Where ASP failed to deliver web-enabled software-as-a-service due to a complex array of business and technical shortcomings, Web Services was expected to resolve the problems of lack of integration with existing technology. So where ASP was largely sold as a standalone product and service ofering, Web Services would enable firms to integrate old and new technology. Whilst it is outside the scope of the present paper to discuss the intricacies of Web Services, our interest here is that by undertaking a process-oriented analysis, this study extends research on organizing visions by observing how their career dynamics- evolve, subsequently being replaced by a new management fashion.

## 5.4. Concluding remarks and recommendations for further research

This paper has sought to further our understanding of how IS innovations are adopted and difused using a process-oriented analysis of the ASP organizing vision. Framing our discussion using Swanson and Ramiller-s (1997) model to analyse the career trajectory of ASP, in addition to drawing upon wider contributions from the literature on neo-institutional theory (Scott, 2001), this paper shows how the ASP organizing vision was both facilitated and constrained by powerful institutional interests within the technology sector, subsequently failing to be adopted by the wider IS practitioner community and beyond.

The six feature model on the organizing vision ofers a useful framework for analysing how management discourse on ASP was imbued with mixed messages about the business problematic faced by small and medium firms, particularly insofar as new IS innovations interface with core technology. This study ofers insights into the dynamics pertaining to these factors. Yet further research could explore these relationships in greater depth. While the organizing vision conceptualises IS innovations by their ascendancy and decendancy (Ramiller & Swanson, 2003), this study ofers fresh insights on how the ASP organizing vision did not simply fade away, but was re-invented under the name of Web Services. This suggests that organizing visions, as opposed to being substantively distinctive, blend past ideas with new ones. An analysis of the literature on management discourse about prior IS innovations highlights this tendency to mix and match the terminology (Currie, 1999). Yet additional research may seek to delineate the distinctive features of new IS innovations more fully from the hyperbole which accompanies them (Ramiller, 2001).

Using institutional theory as a theoretical lens to examine the ASP organizing vision ofers a rich picture of how an emerging IS innovation is adopted and difused. Furthermore, our aim has been to elucidate some of the key factors underpinning its failure to become institutionalised across the wider IS practitioner community and beyond. Having observed the ASP story from its initial inception to its apparent demise, future research may focus more closely on how IS innovations evolve over time and become embedded into the discourse on emerging management fashions (Abrahamson, 1996).

While this research did not set out to test the organizing vision concept from the outset, future research may be more closely linked to institutional theories through the development of hypotheses and propositions. Additional research may also explore how management discourse on an IS innovation may evolve over time. In particular, it may observe how diferent stakeholders contribute to the building and maintenance of the organizing vision. As we have seen in the case of ASP and Web Services, a management fashion that subsequently fails to become institutionalized does not simply fade away through a process of collective forgetting. Instead, it may became inextricably linked with an emerging IS innovation. Future research may therefore seek to explain what aspects of an organizing vision become institutionalized and those that do not. Such an approach may ultimately help to dispel notions that every IS innovation represents a step change from the past and, by so doing, identify the processes in how past ideas become embedded in new IS innovations.

More generally, an institutional analysis may extend the literature on e-business by examining business models (Timmers, 1999; Weill & Vitale, 2001) from a wider societal and inter-organizational context, and not simply at the level of the firm, business unit or decision-making practices of individuals. Through observing the ASP organizing vision in conjunction with wider contributions within institutional theory, we aim to avoid the tendency of the technology literature to downplay the societal and organizational issues, and the shortcomings of the organizational theory literature that often treats IT as a black box- (Orlikowski & Barley, 2001). Finally, further research may seek to explain how an institutionalized organizing vision may become de-institutionalized over time. Within the MIS field, this work may explore how accepted practices of developing, implementing and managing new technology give way to new IS innovations; which was in fact, the original goal of ASP.
