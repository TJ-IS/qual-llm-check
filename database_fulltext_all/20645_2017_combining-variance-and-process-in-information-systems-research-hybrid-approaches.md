# Combining variance and process in information systems research: Hybrid approaches

Ana Ortiz de Guinea<sup>a,b,⁎</sup>, Jane Webster<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Deusto Business School, Universidad de Deusto, Calle Hermanos Aguirre, 2, Bilbao, Basque Country 48014, Spain

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Variance Process Hybrid Paradigm Theory Method Research Guidelines Methodology Design Approach

## A B S T R A C T

For the most part, research in Information Systems has been conducted following either a process or a variance approach. Some argue that the two approaches should be kept separate because they have di<sup>f</sup>erent underlying assumptions. Others disagree. Through problematization, we review the distinctions between process and variance approaches and examine such distinctions to show that these approaches sometimes overlap and can be combined. We then present a new set of assumptions for hybrid approaches and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally distinguish between three types of combinations or hybrids, parallel, weakly blended, and strongly blended. We then introduce a toolbox for modeling hybrid approaches in a coherent way followed by an example on how to use the toolbox. We end the article by providing reasons for considering hybrid approaches, possible areas of research, and a set of guidelines for authors and review teams.

We do not have to accept the integrity of extant paradigms because their assumptions are always limited and, therefore, the subject of critique… Rather we need to develop new paradigms, with their own assumptions and commitments, which draw on the strengths and weaknesses of the current ones, recognizing the plurality and diversity of the world.

(Mingers, 2001, p. 243)

## 1. Introduction

Scientists use theories to help them explain what goes on in the real world. Such theories need to be thought of as abstract re: presentations that depend on theorists' interests, not necessarily corresponding to real-world phenomena in a one-to-one way. In information systems (IS), we typically construe these phenomena using variance (e.g., Davis, 1989) or process (e.g., Lapointe & Rivard, 2005) approaches (Gregor, 2006; Paré, Bourdeau, Marsans, Nach, & Shuraida, 2008; Rivard, 2014). Since the explicit distinction be tween process and variance by Markus and Robey (1988) was <sup>fi</sup>rst introduced to the <sup>fi</sup>eld, several researchers have argued for the necessity of keeping these approaches<sup>1</sup> separate (Newman & Robey, 1992; Seddon, 1997). They argue that the two approaches have di<sup>f</sup>erent ontological assumptions, di<sup>f</sup>erent types of causality, and di<sup>f</sup>erent theoretical explanations. To this day, reviewers continue to point out issues when they are not kept separate; for instance, we recently received these comments on a journal submission:

I am struck by … the apparent disconnect between your framing of the current study as a ‘process’ theory and some of the presentation and structure as a ‘variance’ theory. Perhaps it is not either/or … however, the danger with such a hybrid approach is that it comes across as a weaker alternative than the predominant or alternative paradigms … The paper comes across as a little bit schizophrenic and creates confusion in the mind of the reader about what you are actually trying to accomplish.

At the same time, others propose that separating variance and process approaches can have detrimental e<sup>f</sup>ects: it unnecessarily limits the variety of theories constructed and research conducted (Langley, 1999). Thus, some have called for a combination of the two approaches (e.g., Burton-Jones, McLean, & Monod, 2015; Sabherwal & Robey, 1995). These researchers argue that social phenomena are intertwined and that variance and process models should be combined to better explain these phenomena (e.g., Langley, 1999; Peterson, 1998). They argue that the best theories are often hybrids that combine variance and process theorizing (DiMaggio, 1995).

In this paper, we argue that the distinctions between the two approaches are more arti<sup>fi</sup>cial than a real re<sup>fl</sup>ection of research practices in our <sup>fi</sup>eld, and demonstrate how they already overlap to some extent in our theorizing and empirical research. Further, we propose that even more overlap should be encouraged because hybrid approaches are in a privileged position to address some of the toughest research questions of our <sup>fi</sup>eld. However, one of the challenges in doing so concerns representing hybrids in coherent ways: instead, we often use the same symbols (such as arrows) to represent di<sup>f</sup>erent things in process and variance models, causing confusion (Seddon, 1997). Thus, we develop and illustrate the use of a toolbox to present hybrid models.

Before doing so, however, it is important to acknowledge two facts. First, each approach, variance and process, has made substantial contributions to the IS discipline. This paper's objective is not to undermine any of these approaches or their contributions. For example, variance approaches have helped greatly in identifying the factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the acceptance of IS by users in organizations (e.g., Davis, 1989). Likewise, process approaches, although less common (Paré et al., 2008), have provided theoretically sound and empirically grounded explanations of how resistance to IS develops longitudinally in a bottom-up process involving individuals and groups (e.g., Lapointe & Rivard, 2005) or how socio-technical systems change both incrementally and in a punctuated way across di<sup>f</sup>erent levels (e.g., Lyytinen & Newman, 2008). Thus, each approach has complemented the other and helped greatly in building rigorous cumulative scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge about the many di<sup>f</sup>erent topics that make up the IS discipline.

Second, our paper builds upon others who have called for more hybrid theorizing (Burton-Jones et al., 2015). For example, Burton-Jones et al. (2015) suggest several ways of combining process and variance approaches, such as variables being a<sup>f</sup>ected by events and events being a<sup>f</sup>ected by variables. We go beyond this by <sup>fi</sup>rst, introducing a new set of assumptions regarding hybrids, second, providing a <sup>fl</sup>exible toolbox for building hybrid models, and third, proposing research ideas that can be best addressed from a hybrid approach.

We develop the paper through problematization (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011). Problematization, or the active challenging of as sumptions (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011), is a powerful motivation for theoretical development (Rivard, 2014). In doing so, we provide a succinct review of past assumptions underlying typical process and variance distinctions. We then show that much of IS research already demonstrates elements of both, suggesting that researchers value hybrid approaches rather than always keeping them separate. Third, we distinguish between three types of combinations, parallel, weakly blended, and strongly blended, and then present a toolbox that can enable hybrid modeling, demonstrating how hybrid approaches can be carried out. Finally, we provide arguments for why more hybrid approaches should be adopted, discuss important research ideas for the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld that can be addressed with a hybrid approach, and suggest how editorial review teams can become more developmental in their assessments of hybrid approaches.

## 2. Variance, process, and hybrid approaches

In order to better explain the di<sup>f</sup>erences between process and variance approaches, we initially present sharp contrasts between them, but then argue that both approaches are more similar than <sup>fi</sup>rst thought. We do not have the objective to set up such di<sup>f</sup>erences as ‘straw men’ arguments, but to help in the comprehension of the characteristics of each approach in the way that such characteristics have often been explained and portraved in the past (Mohr. 1982). By pitting variance and process against each other. we do not imply that all researchers in IS concur with the di<sup>f</sup>erences originally portrayed by Mohr. Instead, we want to bring attention to three issues. First, we believe that we have put too much reliance on Mohr's (1982) earlier work, when in fact there is confusion in his arguments (Shaw & Jarvenpaa, 1997), which he has since acknowledged (Mohr, 1996). Second, most IS papers do not enter into discussions of whether di<sup>f</sup>erences between the two approaches make sense (e.g., Gregor, 2006; Paré et al., 2008). Third, in the rare occasions in which this issue is explicitly discussed, one opinion is that variance and process should be kept separate (e.g., Newman & Robey, 1992; Seddon, 1997), although more recently others have suggested they be combined (e.g., Burton-Jones et al., 2015). We concur with this latter perspective and explain why hybrid approaches are sometimes appropriate.

Before continuing, it is important to note that there is much diversity and variations within each approach, especially within process approaches. Whereas variance approaches are generally positivist, process approaches can be built with di<sup>f</sup>erent strategies such as grounded theory, visual mapping, and narratives (Langley, 1999) and may be positivist or interpretivist (Dubé & Paré, 2003). Along a similar vein, it is important to note that some would argue that systems dynamics is a process approach, while others have categorized it as a third approach, independent of variance and process (Burton-Jones et al., 2015).<sup>2</sup> Thus, it should be noted that our portrayals of process and variance approaches may not cover all the variations that exist.

The distinctions between variance and process research were <sup>fi</sup>rst described to IS researchers by Markus and Robey (1988) who drew on Mohr (1982) to explain their di<sup>f</sup>erences. That is, researchers generally use variance theories when making precise predictions (about nouns such as organizations) that will be tested, while those drawing on process theories are concerned with explaining how and why situations and events unfold and develop (with verbs such as ‘organizing’) over time (Van de Ven & Poole, 2005). In general, process and variance approaches have been characterized in six main ways, as outlined in Table 1. As we wil explain next, variance and process approaches present de<sup>fi</sup>ning characteristics in terms of ontology (variables vs. events), causality (push type vs. pull type), time ordering of entities (immaterial vs. critical), meaning of entities over time (same meaning vs. change in meaning possible), epistemology (quantitative methods vs. qualitative methods), and type of explanation (what vs. how and why). For each of the six characteristics in Table 1, we demonstrate that overlap often exists between the two approaches, creating a start toward a hybrid approach. That is, we propose that pure variance and process approaches might share certain characteristics and some IS research has elements of both.

## 2.1. Theory elements (ontology)

In the past, espoused di<sup>f</sup>erences between variance and process approaches have included ontology, or the nature of being and things. In this case, ontology refers to the theory elements employed in each approach. While variance approaches deal with <sup>fi</sup>xed entities with varying attributes (e.g., variables), process approaches consider entities that participate in events (Mohr, 1982). That is, variance approaches deal with variables describing states while process approaches deal with events and processes that unfold over time (Van de Ven, 1992). Moreover, for the process perspective, events might be incremental, have punctuations, be discrete or ongoing.

We will look at two examples of ontology di<sup>f</sup>erences. First, let us examine the process and variance models drawn from Liang and Xue's (2009, p. 47) article on technology threats. The paper proposes a technology threat avoidance theory (TTAT) based on coping theory, and this theory is instantiated both with a variance model (see Fig. 1a) and with a process model (see Fig. 1b).

The variance instantiation of the theory is composed of variables describing states, such as perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, and perceived threat. These variables describe states that are usually quantitative levels or amounts (Mohr, 1982), such as the degree to which someone perceives the susceptibility of a threat. In contrast, the process model is composed of entities and events such as the emergence of malicious IT, threat appraisal (the action of assessing the threat), coping appraisal (the action of evaluating the coping strategies), and the coping strategy selected (the action of engaging in one speci<sup>fi</sup>c coping strategy).

Other examples of variance and process approaches can be found in the literature of (de)escalation of commitment in IS projects (see Fig. 2a and b). For example, the article by Keil et al. (2000) depicts a variance model composed of variables that describe states such as risk perception that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the willingness to continue a project (see Fig. 2a). On the other hand, Montealegre and Kei (2000) present a process model composed of four phases that depict events such as recognition of a problem and re-examination of a prior course of action that lead to low commitment to the project (see Fig. 2b).

## 2.1.1. Hybrid approaches to theory elements

In contrast to separate variance and process theory elements, we suggest that hybrid approaches are possible. As we saw with the TTAT example (Liang & Xue, 2009), the same theory can be instantiated from a variance or a process perspective. Although process approaches use events and processes whereas variance approaches employ variables, a potential hybrid approach could build realit based on events, processes, and variables. That is, if we can construct theory around events and processes with a process approach – two di<sup>f</sup>erent elements – then a hybrid approach could also incorporate additional elements such as variables. As a result, we suggest that terms such as processes, events, and variables are more a matter of semantics and operationalizations of higher-order constructs than fundamentally separate phenomena. For example, Mohr (1982, p. 39), when explaining the variance approach, admits that X and Y can be values (variance) or events (process): it is “best to think of X and Y as de<sup>fi</sup>ned states, events, or values (scores, quantities) and of not-X and not-Y as unde<sup>fi</sup>ned states – simply as the absence or negation or complements of X and Y”. He also states that “if X, then Y” is equivalent to saying “if more X, more Y” thus translating at that moment X and Y into quanti<sup>fi</sup>able variables (Mohr, 1982, p. 39).

Based on this logic, events and processes have properties that can be operationalized in variables that have di<sup>f</sup>erent levels. For a very simple example, the occurrence of events and processes can be operationalized as ‘have happened’ or ‘not have happened’ states into dummy (0/1) variables, as it is done in experimental research. Events and processes can be characterized, as well, in more complex ways, such as into scales with di<sup>f</sup>erent numerical points so as to provide a useful range of variation. As Poole et al. (2000, p. 17) put it:

the second and most frequently used meaning of process is as a category of concepts of individual and organizational actions … In this usage, process refers to a category of concepts that can be distinguished from other categories of concepts…. Like these other categories, process concepts are operationalized as constructs and measured as fixed entities (variables), the attributes of which can vary along numerical scales from low to high.

## 2.2. Causality

In the past, one of the core de<sup>fi</sup>ning characteristics of variance and process approaches was their causality. That is, for the variance approach, explanation was based on establishing necessary and su<sup>fi</sup>cient causality: If more X, then more Y (Mohr, 1982, p. 39). According to Mohr (1982, p. 41) one can think of this as a push-type causality' in which each independent variable is a necessary and su<sup>fi</sup>cient condition for the outcome. In other words, the basis for explanation is e<sup>fi</sup>cient causality (Mohr, 1982; Poole et al., 2000) or as Mohr (1982, p. 41) puts it. a variance approach “deals with efficient causes". The obiective is to get to “the few critical factors that are necessary and su<sup>fi</sup>cient conditions for the e<sup>f</sup>ect” (Poole et al., 2000, p. 33).

In contrast, for the process approach, explanation was based on establishing necessary causality: “If not-X, then not-Y”; that is, the precursor (X) is a necessary condition for the outcome (Y) (Mohr, 1982, p. 45). According to Mohr (1982, p. 59) one can think of this as a ‘pull-type causality’ in which X does not imply Y, but in contrast, the occurrence of Y implies X. In other words, the basis for explanation is necessary causality (Mohr, 1982; Poole et al., 2000) or as Mohr (1982, p. 59) puts it, a process approach “deals with a <sup>fi</sup>nal cause”. The objective is to “focus on critical events and conjunctions of events to explain development and change, and hence […] hinge on necessary causality” (Poole et al., 2000, p. 33). That is, the basis of the explanation is path dependence.

Based on these assumptions for the TTAT variance case, the arrows indicate the type of causality and they allow us to identify several factors that are necessary and su<sup>fi</sup>cient for the di<sup>f</sup>erent coping behaviors as well as for the mediating variables in the mode (see Fig. 1a). For example, one can say that the more users believe that they are susceptible to malicious IT, the stronger their threat perception” (Liang & Xue, 2009, p. 81). In this case, a change in an exogenous variable implies a change in the endogenous variables to which it relates. Thus, this provides for a push-type causality.

In contrast, the arrows in the process model of TTAT imply that coping would not take place if coping appraisal and threat appraisal do not take place. Moreover, threat appraisal and coping appraisal are not su<sup>fi</sup>cient to cause coping. That is, there can be cases in which both threat appraisal and coping appraisal take place yet there is no coping. Thus, this provides a pull-type causality.

The (de)escalation of commitment examples also illustrate this point. The variance approach by Keil et al. (2000) allows us to say that a change in level of sunk costs implies a change in both risk perception and willingness to continue a project. The same occurs with risk propensity (see Fig. 2a). The idea is to get a set of variables or factors that are necessary and su<sup>fi</sup>cient for the endogenous variables to vary. The results also inform us concerning causality by indicating the strength of the relation between the factors and endogenous variables.

In the process approach to commitment (Montealegre & Keil, 2000), however, we can say that that there would be no de-escalation of commitment to the project if the previous four phases and antecedent condition do not take place. Additionally, we can state that the antecedent condition and the four phases are not su<sup>fi</sup>cient to cause (de)escalation of commitment (see Fig. 2b). That is, there can be cases in which the antecedent condition is met as well as the four phases, yet there is no (de)escalation of commitment.

## 2.2.1. Hybrid approaches to causality

Rather than basing causality on Mohr's (1982) original assumptions concerning necessary and su<sup>fi</sup>cient conditions, we propose that hybrid approaches to causality should take a di<sup>f</sup>erent view. Similarly, Mohr (1996) now argues that “causation, which is certainly relevant in physics, is not so relevant in social science” (p. 8) because its requirements are “impossible to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll, that is, it is inherently impossible to specify the circumstances under which a particular behavior will always be determined by a given set of variables or factors” (p. 9). Shaw and Jarvenpaa (1997), p. 73) also argue that Mohr's (1982) earlier “ideas are based on assumptions of causality that are both abstract and philosophically debatable … we contend that the logical relationship between concepts as described by Mohr does not contribute signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to our understanding” (please see Mohr, 1996 for a discussion of causalit problems).

Causality is rarely discussed in IS research (Avgerou, 2013), and thus the assumptions of causality embedded in variance and process explanations have been taken as a face value (e.g., Gregor, 2006). However, one can argue that although articles generally do not discuss issues of causality, they implicitly assume the types of explanations given by Mohr (1982). In contrast, like Mackay (1974), we argue that most, if not all, social science deals with what can be considered INUS conditions: an insu<sup>fi</sup>cient but nonredundant part of an unnecessary but su<sup>fi</sup>cient condition. That is, for each e<sup>f</sup>ect, there is a “plurality of causes” (Mackay, 1974, p 61). A certain e<sup>f</sup>ect can be brought about by a number of distinct clusters of factors: each of the clusters is su<sup>fi</sup>cient to bring about the e<sup>f</sup>ect, but none of them is necessary. As experimentalists note, this explains “why a given causal relationship will occur under some conditions but not universally across time, space, human populations, or other kinds of treatments and outcomes that are more or less related to those studied” (Shadish, Cook, & Campbell, 2002, p. 5). Chance and random events can play a role and outcomes may or may not occur (even when favoring conditions are present). Thus, we conclude that the earlier causality distinctions between variance and process approaches could be replaced by INUS conditions because INUS conditions can better deal with the problems of causality of social science research, namely, redundancy, collateral e<sup>f</sup>ects, the distinction between ‘make-happen’ and ‘let-happen causes, and circularity (please see Mohr, 1996 for further discussion of this).

The examples of TTAT and (de)escalation of commitment presented earlier meet the requirements of INUS conditions. Each step in the process or each variable can be seen as insu<sup>fi</sup>cient and non-redundant parts of unnecessary but su<sup>fi</sup>cient conditions. That is, the variables in the variance approach and the events in the process approach are part of a cluster of factors in which each cluster may be su<sup>fi</sup>cient to have an e<sup>f</sup>ect but none of its parts is necessary. For (de)-escalation of commitment (see Fig. 2a), the level of sunk cost could be seen as part of a set of factors that together is su<sup>fi</sup>cient for in<sup>fl</sup>uencing willingness to continue a project but that alone cannot always bring about the e<sup>f</sup>ect. We propose that this could account for potential interactions between the level of sunk costs and other variables. The same occurs with the process model of Fig. 2b: the events can be seen as part of a cluster that is again necessary but not su<sup>fi</sup>cient alone. This explains why some people going through the same steps will not end up having a low commitment to the project at hand.

## 2.3. Time ordering of entities

The time ordering of entities represents the next characteristic for which researchers have espoused variance and process differences. It is strongly related to issues of causality. According to Mohr (1982), the time ordering of variables is not important for the variance approach, but is critical for the process approach. The variance approach often o<sup>f</sup>ers a snapshot of a situation while the process approach often o<sup>f</sup>ers a dynamic view of a situation that unfolds over time. That is, in the variance approach the temporal sequences in which independent variables in<sup>fl</sup>uence dependent variables are often viewed as immaterial, while the temporal sequence of the theory elements (events and processes) is critical for explaining the outcome in the process approach (Poole et al., 2000).

In the TTAT case (Fig. 1a), the variance model does not appear to imply any temporal sequence between independent and dependent variables. As such, the relation between perceived susceptibility and perceived threat can be summarized as a correlation type, immaterial to time. That is, both perceived susceptibility and perceived threat ‘vary’ together – upwards or downwards – which means that at any point of time, there is a relation between them

In contrast, the process model (Fig. 1b) implies a temporal sequence between the theory elements: there is a threat appraisal, after which there is coping appraisal, after which the coping process starts (either emotion-focused or problem-focused); after each type of coping either another threat appraisal occurs or there is an impact on the environment. This temporal sequence is also intrinsic to the statement of propositions for the process approach: “users appraise how to cope with malicious IT only after they appraise the threat of malicious IT (Liang & Xue, 2009, p. 78).

The same occurs with the examples from the (de)escalation of commitment literature (Fig. 2a and b). The variance model implies no time sequence: there is an “inverse relationship between risk perception and decision makers' willingness to continue a project” (Keil et al., 2000, p. 305). That is, the relation does not imply that risk perception comes before the decision makers' willingness to continue a project; instead, it implies a relation at a single point in time between the two variables. In contrast, the process model depicts the actions that lead to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c outcome of de-escalation. Those actions occur in a particular sequence: before the re examination of a prior course of action takes place, a recognition of the problem <sup>fi</sup>rst needs to occur.

## 2.3.1. Hybrid approaches to time ordering of entities

Time is undeniably an essential component of process approaches. However, we propose that time is often relevant for the variance approach. Frequently, logical arguments assume that X needs to precede Y to be a cause of Y. That is, variance approaches often implicitly assume a certain time order for the independent and dependent variables.<sup>3</sup> For example, Liang and Xue (2009) in their variance approach state the following for justifying perceived susceptibility and perceived severity as antecedents of perceived threat (see Fig. 1a): “Users develop a perception of threat after assessing potential dangers in their computing environment…Only when users believe that they are vulnerable to malicious IT and that the consequence of being attacked is serious will they perceive a threat” (Liang & Xue, 2009, p. 80; emphasis added).

There are other situations in which time is of critical importance for the variance approach. For instance, a variance approach can capture time ordering with repeated measures (for an example, see D'Aubeterre, Singh, & Iyer, 2008). As another example, experiments – aimed at observing the e<sup>f</sup>ects of a given treatment (or manipulation) – contain elements that emphasize time. That is, the very essence of experimental design is to demonstrate that the treatment or manipulation (actually an event often operationalized as a variable, in event vs. no-event conditions) has an e<sup>f</sup>ect on an outcome variable; in order to do so, time is critical: the measurement of the e<sup>f</sup>ect needs to be done after the treatment has taken place (Shadish et al., 2002). This is the case for the variance example of (de)escalation of commitment by Keil et al. (2000) where level of sunk cost is <sup>fi</sup>rst manipulated and after the manipulation occurs, the dependent variable is captured (i.e., willingness to continue a project). In this case, timing of the intervention is part of the study design that enables the proposed variance model to be tested.

Finally, another situation in which time is of implicit importance occurs in variance approaches containing mediators. Mediators are variables aimed at capturing the underlying process by which an independent variable in<sup>fl</sup>uences an outcome: “the central idea in this model is that e<sup>f</sup>ects of stimuli on behavior are mediated by various processes internal to the organism … Theories as diverse as Hull, Tolman, and Lewin shared a belief in the importance of postulating entities or processes that intervene between input and output” (Baron & Kenny, 1986, p. 1176). Thus, time has appeared in both process and variance approaches, suggesting another type of overlap between the two approaches.

## 2.4. Meaning of entities over time

The next characteristic that has been espoused is the change of meaning of entities over time (Mohr, 1982). In the variance approach, it has been argued that entities and their attributes have the same meaning over time (Mohr, 1982; Poole et al., 2000). That is, variables (or entities) have the same meaning at any point in time (e.g., points 1 and 2): their values might change but their meaning does not. As an example, consider the study of IT use continuance. Variables measured at multiple points in time, such as perceived usefulness, have the same meaning at times 1 and 2 (Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004). That is, their values might change as people change their beliefs, but the meaning of the usefulness variable stays the same.

In contrast, it has been argued that events and other entities might change in meaning over time in the process approach (Poole et al., 2000). That is, an entity such an event may change in ‘essential’ meaning as the event develops and transforms over time (Poole et al., 2000). Such change in meaning goes beyond “di<sup>f</sup>erent interpretations of the same” entity, and thus represent “fundamentally di<sup>f</sup>erent meanings for the entity itself (Poole et al., 2000, p. 47). The example that comes to mind is an enterprise that it is fundamentally transformed into a di<sup>f</sup>erent form of enterprise over the course of time, such a small startup changing into a multinational <sup>fi</sup>rm (Poole et al., 2000). Such an enterprise would have changed in a fundamental way in which its meaning also changes. This is because the process approach often addresses questions of why and how processes or events or outcomes come to be and change over time (Langley, 1999) and thus the fundamental transformation of those entities can change their meaning as a result of time (Poole et al., 2000).

## 2.4.1. Hybrid approach to the meaning of entities over time

We assume that it is possible to characterize change in meaning as static variables. That is, in contrast to the process approach described above, we suggest that changing meaning of entities can be modeled either as new variables or as di<sup>f</sup>ering levels of current variables for positivist approaches.

As an example of meaning changes modeled as new variables, consider the (de)escalation of commitment process example (Montealegre & Keil, 2000). We suggest that the meaning of the project did not change: what changed were perceptions of the project (such as recognizing problems in the project) throughout the process. And this change in participants' interpretations of meaning could have been measured with di<sup>f</sup>erent variables that contained references to di<sup>f</sup>erent relevant dimensions. As another example, consider Lapointe and Rivard's (2005) process model of resistance: they conclude that “resistance has a wide variety of antecedents and manifestations that can evolve and change in nature” (p. 484). They suggest that their antecedent, ‘object of resistance’, sometimes changes over time: from system features to system signi<sup>fi</sup>cance to system advocates. However, using more positivistic language, we might restate this as: the independent variables are – system features (t0), system signi<sup>fi</sup>cance (t1), and system advocates (t2). That is, we are saying that the meaning of variables (such as system features) remains the same over time – it is which variables are in<sup>fl</sup>uential at particular points in time that might change (they come in or out of the model). In this example, ‘object of resistance represents a label or category that encompasses the three system variables

As an example of changing levels within one variable (e.g., from low to high) over time, consider Lapointe and Rivard's (2005) process paper again. They categorize resistance behaviors along a continuum from more passive to more active and argue that they organize “these behaviors according to their intensity” (p. 482). We contend that the variable (resistance) hasn't changed in meaning, it has changed in level (intensity). In sum, for positivist approaches, we suggest that any meaning changes could be modeled as new variables or by levels within variables, thus making the development of hybrid approaches more straightforward.

## 2.5. Method (epistemology)

The next characteristic that has been discussed with respect to variance versus process is epistemology, or how to acquire knowledge. In this regard, many variance approaches have adopted quantitative methods in a cross-sectional way while many process approaches have employed qualitative ones with a longitudinal orientation (Mohr, 1982). Variance approaches often incorporate larger sample sizes, utilizing experiments or surveys administered at one point in time made up of items measuring the variables of interest, and then employ statistical analysis to determine results (Mohr, 1982). In contrast, process approaches often employ smaller sample sizes with case studies or ethnographies, using a variety of qualitative analytic techniques to explain how certain sequences of events happen (Mohr, 1982).

The examples of (de)escalation of commitment introduced earlier serve to illustrate this point. From a variance approach, Keil et al. (2000) utilized an experiment to test the proposed variance model. They presented participants with a scenario in which the level of sunk cost of the project in relation to the total budget was manipulated to have four di<sup>f</sup>erent levels. Participants were told to read the scenario and to note the probability that they would be willing to continue with the project. After this, they were asked to <sup>fi</sup>l out a questionnaire that measured the rest of variables in the model (see Fig. 2a) and demographics. The data were then analyzed through structural equation modeling (SEM) via PLS. This provided the means by which to decide whether the relations depicted in the model were supported.

In contrast, from a process approach, Montealegre and Keil (2000) utilized an in-depth longitudinal case study of the baggage handling system at Denver International Airport to develop a model to explain the process by which troubled projects can be turned around. The data gathering consisted of interviews and other public documents about the project being studied. Data analysis was conducted following a theory-building approach. That is, a theory of how projects (de)escalate over time was generated and involved one antecedent condition, four main decisions, and four main processes: (1) problem recognition, (2) re-examination of prior course of action, (3) search for alternative course of action, and (4) implementing an exit strategy (see Fig. 2b).

## 2.5.1. Hybrid approaches to method

Hybrid approaches can use a variety of methods. The traditional view notes that the variance approach uses mostly quantitative methods, while the process approach mostly employs qualitative methods. However, a one-to-one correspondence between theoretical approach and method does not exist (Burton-Jones et al., 2015): There is no reason why variance theories could not be tested with qualitative methods or process theories tested or built with quantitative methods. For example, longitudinal research and repeated measures are just a few examples of how to test change and processes with quantitative tools (for examples, see D'Aubeterre et al. (2008) and Bhattacherjee and Premkumar (2004)). In fact, it has been argued that using process data this way leads to more parsimonious theories that have greater potential for generalizability (Langley, 1999)

We agree that it is possible to detach methods from the approaches within which they have been traditionally employed and use them within other approaches that may make di<sup>f</sup>erent assumptions (Mingers, 2001). For example, when studying innovation, Van de Ven and Poole (1990) gathered in-depth qualitative data that was then systematically listed and coded into pre-agreed characteristics that were later translated into binary codes (0 or 1). These codes were used for analysis with statistical methods of time series in order to <sup>fi</sup>nd patterns in the data. Similarly, Sabherwal and Robey (1993) developed a taxonomy of IS implementation processes based on the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of ‘typical sequences of events’ during implementation across di<sup>f</sup>erent cases: a) they identi<sup>fi</sup>ed sequences from qualitative cases, b) they estimated inter-sequence distances using optimal matching algorithms (like those used in DNA research), and c) they identi<sup>fi</sup>ed event-sequence typologies based on the previous distances through cluster analysis. To sum up, variance, process, and hybrid perspectives can use a variety of methods.

## 2.6. Type of explanation

The <sup>fi</sup>nal characteristic that has been proposed in the past is the type of explanation provided by each approach. It has been argued that variance approaches emphasize the what or the changes that independent variables make on dependent ones (Mohr, 1982; Van de Ven & Poole, 2005). In contrast, process approaches explain the how and the why; that is, they provide an explanation of how and why events occur over time to produce an outcome (Mohr, 1982; Van de Ven & Poole, 2005).

Taking the example of escalation of commitment variance model (Keil et al., 2000), the type of explanation that is given is the ‘what’. That is, the model is concerned with the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of factors (e.g., risk propensity, level of sunk cost, risk perception) that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the outcome of escalation of commitment (e.g., willingness to continue a project).

In contrast, the how and why provided by process approaches are often found in the explanations given by authors based on qualitative data. Thus, Montealegre and Keil (2000) explain how de-escalation of commitment builds upon a process in which project managers recognize that there is a problem, revise their past actions, search for alternatives, and implement an exit strategy, therefore, lowering their commitment to the previous path of action.

## 2.6.1. Hybrid approaches to type of explanation

We suggest that both variance and process approaches use similar types of explanations. This is because it is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to meet the theoretical demands of explaining the critical 'why' of a theory (Sutton & Staw, 1995; Whetten, 1989) with a pure variance approach. As the variance approach requires a theoretical explanation beyond ‘what’, it resorts to ‘process thinking’ for theoretically justifying the relationships between the variables (Poole et al., 2000). That is, although variance models are constructed by specifying relations between sets of variables, they rely upon ‘process logic’ dynamics to explain and justify such relations (please see Table 2 for some examples). Likewise, because process models are constructed by specifying how and why an outcome occurs, they also resort to ‘process logic’ for explaining and justifying the ordering of events that lead to an outcome. Again, we see overlap between the two approaches

## 2.7. Characterizing past hybrid research

Although we demonstrated that variance and process approaches can overlap, few studies combine them to any great extent. In general, we propose that those combining them have generally taken one of three approaches, parallel, weakly blended, or strongly blended (see Table 3) and that such combinations have respected the new set of hybrid assumptions presented earlier in Table 1.

In the parallel hybrid approach, both variance and process approaches are used independently in the same or di<sup>f</sup>erent studies and their results are compared and discussed. (e.g., Liang & Xue, 2009; Sabherwal & Robey, 1995). That is, the two approaches are separate in terms of models and analyses (and often studies), but their results are compared or integrated into a discussion. As described earlier, Liang and Xue (2009) built two theoretical models one variance and one process concerning how users avoid IT threats. The objective was for the process and variance approaches to mutually inform each other. Likewise, Trauth and Jessup (2000)<sup>4</sup> used a variance approach and then a process approach to study computer-mediated discussions, followed by a discussion of how each approach conveyed something new that the other could not. The idea of this parallel hybrid approach is to use both a variance and a process approach to analyze and theorize about the same phenomenon: both sets of results are presented in order to obtain a more complete picture of the phenomenon under investigation (Sabherwal & Robey, 1995).

In a ‘blended’ hybrid approach, researchers combine both variance and process approaches in the same study. This approach builds one model that combines both variance and process elements to theorize about a phenomenon in a coherent way. This hybrid combination may be more or less blended.

We suggest that much of the present research that blurs the lines between variance and process could be viewed as a weakly blended approach. The weakly blended approach is characterized by a combination that incorporates many elements of one approach with very few elements from the other approach. The most common examples are studies that use variables and relations from the variance approach and the time element from the process approach, that is, longitudinal studies that test relations between variables at di<sup>f</sup>erent points in time. For example, Bhattacherjee and Premkumar (2004) studied changes in beliefs and attitudes longitudinally in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts (computer training software usage and rapid application development tool usage). They stated that usefulness and attitudes at t1 would in<sup>fl</sup>uence discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation, satisfaction, usefulness, attitudes, and intention to use at t2. Although they use theoretical elements that belong to variance entities and relations, they also incorporate time, and time becomes important in the ordering of the di<sup>f</sup>erent variables in the model. The entities in their model do not change in meaning over time. Their causality approach meets the requirements for INUS conditions. The method used to test their model is quantitative: a longitudinal survey. And the type of explanation provided in the text follows a process logic. This less blended approach is quite common (for another example, see Limayem, Hirt, & Cheung, 2007). In this regard, it is important to acknowledge that some researchers might characterize this ‘weak hybrid approach’ as just longitudinal research taken from a variance standpoint; whereas others might categorize it as a process approach. We take this ambivalence of categorization as a further evidence concerning how the two approaches are blurred, and therefore we categorize this as a weakly blended hybrid approach.

In a strongly blended hybrid approach, however, additional elements from both approaches are employed. That is, a set of elements from both approaches is present. As an example, Sabherwal and Robey (1993) used events of IS implementation processes and their sequences as theoretical elements in order to build typical sequences of events across cases. Although their theoretical elements contain only those from the process approach (e.g., sequences of events), they operationalized them into variables: these theoretical elements were actions of various types that either occurred or did not occur, being characterized as dummy variables. For example, time had a critical role to play in building the typical sequences of events: the sequences were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from employing qualitative methods (i.e., cases of IS implementation processes). The entities in their model (events) did not change in meaning over time: it is the events that might change in time ordering for di<sup>f</sup>erent cases, but their meaning stays the same. With respect to causality, their study <sup>fi</sup>ts nicely with the requirements for INUS conditions: each sequence of events for each case might be di<sup>f</sup>erent. Furthermore, both qualitative and quantitative methods were used: optimal matching algorithms were used to identify event-sequence typologies based on identi-<sup>fi</sup>cation of sequences from qualitative case studies. Finally, the explanations addressing the typologies followed a process logic.

The distinctions between hybrid models serve to illustrate the fact that most hybrid approaches have been conducted from a parallel or a weakly blended approach where combination is kept to a minimum. This characterization of past research is useful because it points to the di<sup>fi</sup>culty in building strongly blended approaches. One reason for such di<sup>fi</sup>culty is the lack of modeling tools that allow us to integrate elements of both variance and process in a coherent way. The next section addresses this issue.

## 3. How can variance and process be combined into a coherent model? A toolbox for modeling hybrids

If one agrees that variance and process approaches can be combined in hybrids, there is still one major issue to resolve: how do we draw the models that represent them in a coherent and non-confusing way? Indeed, process and variance models often utilize the same icons to draw di<sup>f</sup>erent entities and causal relations. Attempts to create hybrid models have contained the same elements (e.g., an arrow) representing di<sup>f</sup>erent things (e.g., push-type causality, time relation, pull-type causality), thus leading to confusion. For example, the original IS success model (DeLone & McLean, 1992) contained elements of both variance and process leading to interpretation problems (Seddon, 1997). Recent papers suggesting hybrid approaches face the same issue; that is, representing di<sup>f</sup>erent elements of variance and process approaches with the same icons (e.g., Burton-Jones et al., 2015). This often leads to confusion on the part of readers, reviewers, and editors (Rivard, 2016: personal communication). Seddon (1997) nicely explains such confusion:

Unfortunately, combining variance and process models is exactly what D & M [Delone and McLean] attempted to do. The result is that many of the boxes and arrows in D & M's model (Fig. 1a) can, and do, have both a variance and an event-in-a-process interpretation. The problem is that the same human perceptual limitations that enabled Dutch artist, M.C. Escher (1982) to produce paradoxical drawings of stairways that seem at one moment to be going up, and at another to be going down (because our minds can only make sense of small parts of his drawings at a time) mean that when a reader looks at D & M's model, his/her eforts to make sense of diferent parts of the model will frequently cause slippage from one meaning for a box or arrow to another. The result is a level of muddled thinking that is likely to be counter-productive for future IS research (p. 242) … [I]t has become apparent that the inclusion of both variance and process interpretations in their model leads to so many potentially confusing meanings that the value of the model is diminished (p. 240).

In order to build coherent hybrid models that convey a parsimonious understanding, we suggest the use of a toolbox with standard icons for di<sup>f</sup>erent theoretical elements. Drawing on symbols used in past variance and process studies, we provide a starting point for future researchers on such a hybrid toolbox (see Table 4). It proposes icons to use for di<sup>f</sup>erent theoretical elements: events, processes, variables, time, relations, and decisions. Events can be further divided into discrete (that is, they happen at a distinct point of time with a well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned beginning and end) or continuous (they persist over time) ones. Processes are illustrated by a continuous line that, if <sup>fl</sup>at, indicates no change over time, and, if <sup>fl</sup>uctuating, indicates change over time. Variables are represented by an oval form for latent variables and by a rectangle for manifest variables. Time is represented by a dotted arrow that indicates the direction of time in the research model. Relations, or the linking between variables, are illustrated by straight arrows that indicate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of one variable on another variable. Finally, a possible decision is summarized by a diamond icon.

We will now illustrate the application of the toolbox with an example of information technology (IT) use research. We draw on two papers regarding how users deal with discrepant and expected events during their utilization of systems (Ortiz de Guinea, 2016; Ortiz de Guinea & Webster, 2013). Discrepant events occur when an interaction with an IT does not match one's expectations (i.e., there is a problem or a situation); expected events, in contrast, occur when everything goes according to plan (i.e., no problems). We <sup>fi</sup>rst diagram variance and process models without using the toolbox. Then, we provide a hybrid model using the toolbox that incorporates elements of both variance and process, respecting the new assumptions laid out in the previous section.

## 3.1. Variance model of discrepant vs. expected events on IT use

The variance model depicts the impact of discrepant vs. expected events on the emotions, cognitions, and behaviors of users (see Fig. 3a). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the model addresses the following research questions: what are the impacts of discrepant vs. expected events on the emotions, cognitions, and behaviors of users during IT use? What are the impacts of users' behaviors on task performance? The model proposes that more discrepant events (i.e., fewer expected events) result in more negative a<sup>f</sup>ect and more physiological arousal, or in other words, fewer discrepant events (i.e., more expected events) result in more positive a<sup>f</sup>ect and less physiological arousal. Thus an event can be discrepant or expected; in this example, it is dichotomous. The model also shows a positive relation between discrepant events (vs. expected ones) and computer-related thoughts, and a negative one to non-computer related thoughts; that is, the more discrepant events one su<sup>f</sup>ers, the more one would think about the computer (i.e., software) and the less about other things (i.e., the task). Furthermore, the model shows that more discrepant events result in more adaptive behaviors (i.e., changing or adapting to the software at hand) and less exploitive behaviors (i.e., working on a task). Finally, there is a positive relation between exploitive behaviors and task performance.

## 3.2. Process model of discrepant vs. expected events on IT use

The process model addresses the following research question: how do users deal with different IT events (i.e., discrepant ys, ex: pected) during IT use? The model in Fig. 3b proposes that when a discrepant IT event occurs, people take on engagement coping characterized by a focus on the problem at hand: people experience negative emotional processes and engage in computer-related cognitive processes and adaptive behavioral processes aimed at resolving the situation. When the event is not resolved and there are available resources to face the discrepant IT event, users continue to take on engagement coping. However, after a while through the passage of time – either because there are no resources available or because the discrepant IT event is solved – people engage in disengagement coping, a form of coping that also occurs when there are expected IT events and everything goes according to plan. Such engagement is characterized by positive emotional processes, non-computer-related cognitive processes and exploitive behavioral processes (e.g., cognitions and behaviors aimed at dealing with the task at hand and no longer with the computer-related problem).

## 3.3. Hybrid model of discrepant vs. expected events on IT use

We can also pose a third set of research questions that combines the elements of the previous two and enriches them: how do IT events trigger di<sup>f</sup>erent IT use patterns composed of emotional, cognitive, and behavioral processes? How do users transition from one IT use pattern to another over time? How do the behavioral processes of IT use patterns in<sup>fl</sup>uence task performance? To theorize and model the response we built a hybrid model using the toolbox as shown in Fig. 4. In this model, di<sup>f</sup>erent IT events are characterized by di<sup>f</sup>erent IT use patterns composed of emotional, cognitive, and behavioral processes. Fig. 4 portrays this: on the horizontal axis, one can see how events (expected or discrepant) may occur over time, with their resulting IT use patterns composed of emotional, cognitive, and behavioral processes. The automatic use pattern results from expected IT events or happens as time passes since the discrepant IT event occurred (again as a result of the discrepant IT event being solved or a lack of resources to solve it): it is characterized by positive emotional processes, high physiological arousal, non-computer related thoughts and exploitive behaviors. The adjusting IT use pattern occurs after a discrepant IT event and is characterized by negative emotions related to the event, computer-related thoughts and adaptive behaviors. Finally, one can see how exploitive behaviors lead to short-term task performance.

This example follows the assumptions for a hybrid approach. The theoretical elements of both process and variance approaches – processes, events, relations, decisions are blended and combined into a coherent theory and then into a research model (see Fig. 4). Furthermore, time has a critical role in theory, in terms of ordering the di<sup>f</sup>erent elements into the research model, their empirica measurement, and the calculation of their e<sup>f</sup>ects. The entities in the model do not necessarily change in meaning over time; that is, the cognitive, emotional, and behavioral processes, as well as the remaining events and variables, might change in value but not in meaning over time. The implicit causality in the model (and in their theory throughout their article) meets the requirements for INUS conditions; that is the events are seen as INUS conditions that bring di<sup>f</sup>erent IS use patterns into life. Furthermore, both qualitative and quantitative methods are intertwined in order to test the model, in which think-aloud techniques, pop-up questions, heart-rate monitoring, and videos were employed and coded in order to operationalize cognitive, emotional, and behavioral processes over time. Hypotheses regarding events triggering di<sup>f</sup>erent patterns as well as patterns fading away over time were tested via overall trend analyses through repeated measures ANOVA. Furthermore, the relation between behaviors and performance was tested through path analysis. Finally, the explanation addressing the why behind the hypotheses followed a process logic.

## 4. Discussion and conclusion

In this paper, we have provided a new set of assumptions for hybrid approaches, presented how hybrids have been conducted in the past, and illustrated a toolbox to coherently draw hybrids. It is important to note, however, that our toolbox represents a <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt at creating a set of icons to build hybrid models. As such, we encourage authors to make it more comprehensive. For example, events could be further distinguished in terms of their strength, such as being incremental or punctuated.<sup>5</sup>

Another limitation concerns mainly focusing on examples from more positivistic research. We did not delineate all the variations and perspectives that exist within process and variance approaches, given the fact that each approach can span di<sup>f</sup>erent scienti<sup>fi</sup>c paradigms. Instead, we see hybrid approaches as more appropriate and straightforward to apply for positivist, rather than interpretive research. However, researchers have proposed hybrid interpretive approaches (Burton-Jones et al., 2015) and we encourage researchers to explore this possibility further. Nonetheless, if variance and process approaches are to be combined into a hybrid, care needs to be put into assessing whether the assumptions behind both approaches <sup>fi</sup>t within the same scienti<sup>fi</sup>c assumptions. Such careful consideration is necessary given the fact that hybrid approaches can become complex and thus might not be applicable for al types of research. A <sup>fi</sup>nal limitation concerns what some might view as process elements reduced to actions that are ultimately operationalized into variables: they may categorize our hybrid approach as a ‘weak’ view of (organizational) change (Tsoukas, 2005; Van de Ven & Poole, 2005). That is, in contrast to pure process approaches, our hybrid approach may not allow for the same level of richness and detail.

Next, we discuss reasons for conducting hybrid approaches, suggest possible areas in which hybrids could be advantageous, and present guidelines for authors and editorial review teams.

## 4.1. Reasons for creating hybrids

We believe that there are several compelling reasons for combining process and variance approaches. As described earlier, researchers construct theories to help explain phenomena observed in the ‘real’ world. That is, the real world is not made of variables, processes, or constructs; the world is as it is, with or without our theories. When we develop theories we make choices about how to represent the real world: some choose to represent it as variables if interested in predicting an outcome, or to construe it as a process if interested in questions about change. Either way, reality is the same regardless of the approach adopted.<sup>6</sup> Thus, an important reason for utilizing hybrids may be that we can obtain a more complete picture of what we are trying to understand or explain.

Another reason for combining approaches is that the phenomena we want to understand and explain in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld – such as IS use – fundamentally share the characteristics of both approaches. As we demonstrated above, the approaches are not clear-cut, and in practice phenomena of di<sup>f</sup>erent kinds are intertwined (Langley, 1999). For example, IS use can be thought of as a process or a pattern that occurs over time from the moment at which a system is introduced in the workplace until its lifecycle ends, or as a variable that captures the breadth or frequency of use of a given technology. Many factors can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the decision to <sup>fi</sup>rst use a system (Davis, 1989) and various events can trigger di<sup>f</sup>erent ways or patterns in which individuals use a system over time (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005: Ortiz de Guinea & Webster, 2013). In turn, system use can enable other processes such as speedy production of different products and the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and e<sup>fi</sup>cacy for which work is carried out by individuals and groups. Thus, we can see how IS use – one of the most important subject areas in our <sup>fi</sup>eld - contains both process and variance characteristics.

As Peterson (1998, p. 27) states: “Process and variance theories are inherently interwoven when things are viewed as chunks of process.” Accordingly, exclusively pursuing research that uses one approach or the other could have detrimental e<sup>f</sup>ects for the advancement of science: it unnecessarily limits the variety of theories constructed and research conducted. As Langley (1999, p. 693) puts it:

it may be important to understand the efect of events on the state of an entity (a variable) or to identify the efect of a contextual variable on the state of an entity on the evolution of events. Process research may also deal with the evolution of relationships between people or with the cognitions and emotions of individuals as they interpret and react to events.

Eminent theorists agree and explain that the best theories are often hybrids between variance and process (DiMaggio, 1995). Accordingly, if the phenomenon of interest has elements of both variance and process, it follows that great value could be gained by combining them. One advantage of hybrids is their “ability to address di<sup>f</sup>erent, and more complex, research questions than those posed earlier” (Sabherwal & Robey, 1995, p. 38). The promise is that these research questions (by containing elements of both approaches) would be more practically relevant because they are closer to the phenomenon under study. If so, hybrids could also help lessen the existing gap between IS-related practices and academic research (Benbasat & Zmud, 1999).

Another reason for combining both approaches is that such combinations can bring collaboration to IS research across di<sup>f</sup>erent approaches and researchers. Such research would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from participation from variance and quantitative methodologists as well as from process and qualitative experts. Recent advances in methods and tools available to IS researchers may necessitate hybrid approaches. For instance, the introduction of easy to use data mining tools provide opportunities for hybrid theorizing and research on big data and social networks which often deal with trace datasets that capture individuals' behavior over time within a given context (Whelan, Teigland, Vaast, & Butler, 2016). Hybrid approaches are in a privileged position to answer recent calls for research on how social ties affect user behavior and the resulting networks over time by combining quantitative and qualitative research methods (Whelan et al., 2016). Similarly, recent advances in and introduction of new neuroscienti<sup>fi</sup>c tools to the <sup>fi</sup>eld necessitate hybrid approaches. Most neuroscienti<sup>fi</sup>c tools – such as electroencephalograms (EEG), electrocardiograms (EKG), galvanic skin response (GSR). facial expression recognition software, and eve-tracking – have the capability of capturing phenomena (ex., neural activity within the cerebral cortex) over time in the range of milliseconds (Dimoka et al., 2012). With this type of research, we use tools that measure physical (e.g., Wastell & Newman, 1996) and neural (e.g., Ortiz de Guinea, Titah, & Léger, 2014) processes in order to look for changes that might a<sup>f</sup>ect an outcome variable (e.g., performance) (Riedl, Davis, & Hevner, 2014). It is this type of research, dealing with changes in processes that a<sup>f</sup>ect outcomes, that can bene<sup>fi</sup>t greatly from hybrid approaches.

Finally, the <sup>fl</sup>exibility of such hybrid approaches combining di<sup>f</sup>erent sources of data and methods has an additional bene<sup>fi</sup>t for research: the reduction of the likelihood of mono-method bias, priming, and consistency artifacts (Podsako<sup>f</sup>, MacKenzie, Lee, & Podsako<sup>f</sup>, 2003; Salancik, 1984). Furthermore, the combination of di<sup>f</sup>erent methods has synergistic properties: di<sup>f</sup>erent methods complement each other by compensating for the weakness of one with the strengths of the other (Jick, 1979).

All in all, a main reason for considering hybrid approaches is that phenomena we study have properties of both process and variance. Given the almost exclusive pursuit of solely process or variance approaches, our <sup>fi</sup>eld could bene<sup>fi</sup>t and complement current thinking with hybrid approaches.

## 4.2. Suggested applications for hybrid approaches

We have explained the reasons behind adopting blended hybrid approaches; however, an approach is only useful if it is capable of valuably addressing important research questions. Given the fact that phenomena of interest in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld have characteristics of both variance and process approaches, we believe that hybrid approaches hold promise for addressing important and complex research questions in our <sup>fi</sup>eld.

Hybrids are particularly suited for addressing research questions that tackle notions of change (Van de Ven & Poole, 2005) – a property of process – and notions of outcomes or impacts – a property of variance approaches. That is, hybrids can address research questions that include the study of processes over time along with their impacts on a given outcome variable. Or, e<sup>f</sup>orts can be aimed at investigating events that trigger certain changes in states or outcomes. For instance, researchers can employ time series where states are seen as processes that change as a result of particular interventions. Or, they can focus on explaining the processes that are involved in transforming antecedents into outcomes. Overall, research questions that include time, as well as elements of antecedents, impacts, events, and processes, can be well approached from a hybrid perspective.

Many recent calls for research arising from di<sup>f</sup>erent domains of IS contain fundamentally hybrid ideas. At the individual level, researchers have urged studies on how users attain e<sup>f</sup>ective use of a system over time and how such evolution a<sup>f</sup>ects their workrelated task performance (Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013). Others have suggested that e<sup>f</sup>orts are needed to investigate how and when users employ IT in re<sup>fl</sup>ective and non-re<sup>fl</sup>ective ways as well as the situations and events capable of triggering periods of re<sup>fl</sup>ective use that disrupt non-re<sup>fl</sup>ective use states (Jasperson, Carter, & Zmud, 2005; Ortiz de Guinea & Webster, 2013). Researchers have also encouraged studies of hidden or unconscious processes that relate to system adoption and use, including the evolution of emotional states and habits (Dimoka, Pavlou, & Davis, 2011; Dimoka et al., 2012; Ortiz de Guinea & Markus, 2009). Others have proposed investigating how technological events (e.g., receiving emails, use of new features) trigger di<sup>f</sup>erent neural processes (Léger et al., 2014).

With respect to the group level, there are numerous avenues for hybrid research concerning virtual teams. For example, there have been calls for research into the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of socio-economic and task-related processes involved in mediating virtual teams inputs into outputs (Martins, 2004; Powell, Piccoli, & Ives, 2004). Research is also needed to study how geographic dispersion - conceived as a multidimensional construct - impacts coordination processes in virtual teams (O'Leary & Cummings, 2007). Research has also highlighted the importance of time in virtual teams' development and outcomes (Ortiz de Guinea, Webster, & Staples, 2012), with calls for research focusing on investigating the impact of knowledge coordination processes on virtual teams' performance over time (Kanawattanachai & Yoo, 2008).

At the organizational level, research questions could be addressed on a number of di<sup>f</sup>erent topics. In the strategy area, research could focus on how technological and business characteristics in<sup>fl</sup>uence the process of value creation (Pagani, 2013). With respect to governance, researchers have called for more investigations on the new organization logic that comes with pervasive digitization (Yoo, Henfridsson, & Lyytinen, 2010). For example, researchers could study how <sup>fi</sup>rms strategically control their digital products, how such controls evolve over time, and whether such controls are e<sup>f</sup>ective (Yoo et al., 2010). In terms of the emerging and critical area of green IS (Mines, 2008), hybrid approaches are in a privileged position to advance our knowledge on how information systems [can] be used to change social norms to increase energy e<sup>fi</sup>ciency” (Watson, Boudreau, & Chen, 2010, p. 30) and how strategies can initiate the process of adopting and implementing more green IS (Jenkin, Webster, & McShane, 2011).

Hybrid approaches can also be useful in addressing research questions implying a mixed-level approach. One promising area of study is the linking of micro processes that occur for individual-level IT use and macro outcomes at the organizational leve (Jasperson et al., 2005). Such research would address a long-suspected issue for the productivity paradox (Brynjofolfsson, 1993): whether low-level processes, such as employees' everyday behaviors with IT, are critical in linking IS investments with organizational performance (Barua, Kriebel, & Mukhopadhyay, 1995; Barua & Mukhopadhyay, 2000). Nevertheless, unlike others (Burton-Jones et al., 2015), one of the di<sup>fi</sup>culties we can foresee with mixed levels is that the hybrid approach may become more di<sup>fi</sup>cult to apply. For instance, when researching group concepts we may need to specify their compositional or compilational characteristics in order to di<sup>f</sup>erentiate them from lower level entities and concepts (Rousseau, 1985). As more high-level elements become part of a multilevel research study, causality can become more complex and the operationalizations of entities become more challenging (Klein, Danserau, & Hall, 1994). Thus, we do not propose that hybrid approaches will be suitable for all types of research.

## 4.3. Guidelines for authors and review teams

As with any unfamiliar approach, authors, reviewers, and editors may need advice when <sup>fi</sup>rst utilizing hybrids. Thus, we suggest a starting point for assisting with developmental reviews (see Table 5).

First and foremost, we believe that authors and reviewers should be open to hybrid approaches. Unfortunately, authors are often told by editorial review teams to pursue either a variance or a process approach. At the same time, authors often prefer to take the safe way and pursue only one of these approaches. We invite authors and editors to be open to hybrid possibilities. Second, authors need to carefully think about the appropriateness of a hybrid approach; that is, it is important that they assess whether their theoretical background has elements of both process and variance so that a hybrid approach is warranted. Editors and reviewers should also consider suggesting a hybrid approach when they believe a paper incorporates elements that belong to both process and variance approaches and could bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a hybrid approach. Third, hybrid approaches are complex: we encourage authors and editors to embrace this complexity. For authors, complexity should not be viewed as discouraging; instead, we invite them to be determined to deal with such complexities and further develop their ideas into more parsimonious ones. With respect to editors, we ask them to be open to the complexity and provide developmental reviews to help authors improve their ideas. Fourth, authors and review teams should carefully evaluate the research against the assumptions for hybrids presented here so they can ensure that such assumptions are respected. Fifth, we encourage the use of the toolbox (or another coherent set of symbols) for modeling hybrids in order to avoid confusion. Authors should use the toolbox to build models summarizing their hypotheses (or research outputs/propositions). We see one main advantage of doing this early on: it could help them in thinking further ahead to clarify the empirical portions of their research projects (i.e., operationalization of entities and methods). We encourage editors to suggest that authors use the toolbox to help them in the development of their hybrid thinking. Finally, we invite authors to discuss the advantages of their hybrid approach: this could help justify the employment of this approach in their papers. Editors can help authors by suggesting they discuss the advantages that a hybrid approach can provide that neither variance nor process approaches can do on their own.

## 4.4. Conclusions

In conclusion, although some researchers advocate for the separation of variance and process approaches for IS research, this paper provides arguments for why variance and process are more similar than might be expected. Some may view our initial comparison of process and variance approaches as a straw man: nevertheless, we felt that it was important to make this comparison as it has been espoused in the literature so that the <sup>fi</sup>eld can move beyond Mohr's (1982) early conceptualizations.<sup>7</sup> Furthermore, we complement our critique by providing new assumptions and a toolbox for hybrid approaches. It is important to note that this toolbox can also be used to draw variance models and process models. We encourage future hybrid researchers to enrich our toolbox, guidelines, and directions to authors and editors willing to take on hybrid approaches.

We do not advocate for the exclusive application of hybrid approaches. Rather, authors' research questions should drive which approach to adopt within Burton-Jones et al.’s (2015) guiding concepts of conceptual <sup>fi</sup>t and <sup>fl</sup>exibility. Our objectives are humble: a) to point to the fact that variance and process approaches share certain similarities, presenting a new set of assumptions for hybrid approaches, b) to provide a <sup>fi</sup>rst toolbox to start building coherent blended hybrid models, and c) to call attention to hybrid approaches that hold promise to address some critical research questions that each approach alone (variance or process) cannot. All in all, we hope that this commentary sparks new and exciting research from a hybrid standpoint.
