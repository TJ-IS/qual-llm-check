# The research-practice gap as a pragmatic knowledge boundary

Samuel Makin

Selwyn College, University of Cambridge, Grange Rd, Cambridge, CB3 9DQ, United Kingdom

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Research-practice gap Research utilisation Knowledge boundaries Boundary objects Interactive expertise Cross-disciplinary collaboration

## A B S T R A C T

Existing discourse on the research-practice gap underplays the role of practitioners and assumes that the existence of the gap is due primarily to deficiencies in theory. This conceptual paper problematizes this assumption and explores in practice why practitioners have not been able to harness and apply insights from organisation theory (OT) and information systems (IS) research . Drawing on the concept of knowledge boundaries, several key arguments are made. Firstly, a pragmatic knowledge boundary divides academics and practitioners. Secondly, the novelty of OT and IS research at this knowledge boundary hinders practitioners’ ability to constructively assess it, which results in contradictions and discourages its application in practice. Finally, when ac ademics and practitioners collaborate to promote deeper engagement, and when effective boundary-spanning objects are used, there are signs that the research-practice gap can be over come. These arguments are illustrated through a case-study of employees in a medium-sized IT consulting company.

## 1. Introduction

Several advancements have been made in recent years that have offered potential for bridging the research-practice gap, a gap which has troubled scholars of organisation theory (OT) and information systems (IS) research for decades (Bartunek, 2007: Bartunek & Rynes, 2014). Firstly, several scholars have observed that the polarization of academic rigour and pragmatic relevance is an artificia one (Gulati, 2007), and that research can, and should, synthesize rigour and relevance (Hodgkinson & Rousseau, 2009; Vermeulen, 2005). This observation has enabled researchers to focus on how to produce work that is high in both academic rigour and pragmatic relevance, without any need to sacrifice theory (Hambrick, 2007). In this vein, a variety of suggestions have sought to help overcome the gap. including Susman and Evered's (1978) call for action research. Corley and Gioia's (2011) proposed focus on prescience. Van de Ven’s (Van de Ven, 2007; Van de Ven, 2018; Van de Ven & Johnson’s, 2006) call for engaged scholarship, and Barrett and Oborn’s (2018) framework for combining different levels of expertise with varying forms of academic-practitioner collaboration to develop impactful research.

Most of this work has focused on the shortcomings of academic theory (Hambrick, 1994; Hambrick, 2007), while responsibility for bridging the gap has been allocated almost exclusively to the academics, a proclivity exemplified by Vermeulen’s (2007) claim that “the buck stops with us. If we cannot explain to practitioners how our research is relevant to them, how can we expect others to be able to do it?” (p. 759). Academics certainly have more to do, and should be encouraged towards engaged scholarship and prescient research. Yet, as argued by Weick (2001), the research-practice gap is “as much a product of practitioners wedded to gurus and fads as it is of academics wedded to abstractions and fundamentals” (S71). Greater knowledge of OT and IS research would result in a range of positive outcomes (Christensen & Raynor, 2003), and enable practitioners to transcend performance barriers (Gulati, 2007, p. 781) Unfortunately, the evidence suggests that managers are not great readers of academic material and that learning is problematic when it challenges “accepted modes of practice” (Starkey & Madan, 2001, S11).

Equally striking is that the vast majority of articles on the relevance gap are essays consisting primarily of “normative opinion statements” (Bartunek & Rynes, 2014, p. 1183), most of which are based on “personal predilections and anecdotal evidence” rather than being grounded in empirical research (Rynes, Bartunek, & Daft, 2001, p. 341). Ironically, there is a concerning absence of practitioner input in current work (for an exception, see Bartunek, 2007), which has resulted in a comparative lack of practitioner voice in the conversation (Bartunek & Rynes, 2014, p. 1188). Relatedly, while there is a growing backlog of prescriptions on how to bridge the research-practice gap. there is a dearth of documented attempts to do so (for exceptions, see Bansal. Bertels. Ewart MacConnachie, & O’Brien, 2012 & Mohrman, Gibson, & Mohrman Jr, 2001).

Finally, partly because of the dominant assumption that the existence of the research-practice gap is due to deficiencies in theory, most efforts to close the gap have focused on inventing brand new forms of theory, produced through radically different practices that it is hoped will be more relevant to practitioners. Engaged scholarship (Van de Ven & Johnson, 2006) and prescience (Corley & Gioia, 2011) are both examples of this, as are calls for “changes to our academic system” (Vermeulen, 2005, p. 978). Focusing on existing theory and why practitioners do not readily apply it in practical situations is far less fashionable. To paraphrase Mintzberg et al. (2009, p. 8). there is a terrible tendency in today's management literature to prefer what is new and ‘hot' to what is older, even if it's not as good. This is equally true in discourse on the research-practice gap, but also unfortunate, because there are a range of ways in which existing management theory can be valuable to “hard-nosed executives” (Christensen & Raynor, 2003), something which holds true for OT and IS research. In other words, discourse on the research-practice gap may have too hastily written off the plethora of very good theory that already exists.

Together, these shortcomings have resulted in an incomplete and biased understanding of the issues underpinning the researchpractice gap. Without a solid understanding of how theory is received by practitioners and the factors inhibiting research uti lisation (Beyer, 1997; Beyer & Trice, 1982), bridging the gap will be difficult. I seek to assuage this by problematizing (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011; Sandberg & Alvesson, 2011) the assumption that the existence of the gap is due exclusively to deficiencies in existing theory. The primary objective is to examine how these issues unfold in practice and ask a previously under-explored question: why do managers and organisations struggle to constructively harness and apply the insights contained within OT and IS research?

This investigation will be informed by research on knowledge boundaries (Carlile, 2002, 2004). Corroborating claims made by Van de Ven (2007), I demonstrate that because practitioner knowledge is localised, embedded and invested in practice, a pragmatic knowledge boundary exists at the interface of research and practice. Building on this further, I demonstrate in practice how the novelty of OT and IS research as it crosses this boundary hampers the ability of practitioners to constructively assess, evaluate and harness this knowledge, resulting in counterintuitive and contradictory assessments that discourage its application. Finally, I demonstrate that combining effective boundary obiects (Star &. Griesemer. 1989) with various collaborative practices described by Barrett and Oborn (2018) can be effective in attenuating these issues. This argument will be illustrated through a series of vignettes based on a case-study of employees, including myself, in a medium-sized IT consulting company. This case is based on an 11 month period of full-time engagement with the organisation and draws on a variety of sources of data, including interviews, focus groups, presentations and email data.

This paper will make several contributions to theory, while simultaneously capturing and reflecting on implications for practice. As noted above, existing work on the research-practice gap underplays the role of practitioners and is overly opinion-based. This paper will ameliorate these issues by examining why practitioners struggle to harness OT and IS research. Rather than offering an opinionbased normative account, this paper is based on the insights of established organisational theory, which will enable a deeper un derstanding of the forces inhibiting the practical application of theory and research. Perhaps the most significant contribution is that it documents genuine attempts to apply OT and IS research in practice. Bridging the research-practice gap requires both actions and words. Yet, there have been few documented efforts to do this. This paper will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the gap while also enabling practical recommendations for how it can be overcome.

The remainder of this paper will proceed as follows. Section 2 will provide a brief overview of the debate surrounding the researchpractice gap in organisation, management and IS research. Section 3 will introduce knowledge boundaries as the theoretical lens used to address the issues. Section 4 will introduce the case-study that is used to illustrate the paper’s arguments. Section 5 will then delineate in detail the role of knowledge boundaries and boundary objects in academic-practitioner relations.

## 2. The research-practice gap

The research-practice gap, also referred to as the relevance gap. the rigour-relevance debate. and the academic-practitioner divide. denotes an observed gap between organisational research findings and actual management practices. There is little evidence that business research has an influence on management practice (Pfeffer & Fong, 2002) and considerable evidence that executives do not typically turn to management theory when developing management strategies (Rynes et al., 2001; p. 340). Similar observations have been made in OT and IS research.

For the research-practice gap to be bridged, practitioners must be able to apply OT and IS research to drive positive organisational outcomes. There are numerous ways in which research can be used in practice (Beyer, 1997; Beyer & Trice, 1982). Beyer (1997, p. 17) suggests three such ways: 1) instrumental use of research, which involves applying research in specific and direct ways; 2) conceptual use, which involves using research to develop general enlightenment; 3) symbolic use, which involves using academic research to legitimate and sustain predetermined positions.

Multiple factors have been proposed for why research utilisation does not occur. For instance, Van de Ven and Johnson (2006) noted that the research-practice gap can be viewed as either a knowledge transfer problem or a knowledge production problem, while Shapiro, Kirkman, and Courtney (2007) described theory as being “lost in translation” and “lost before translation.” Similarly, severa solutions have been proposed, including engaged scholarship (Van de Ven & Johnson, 2006), action research (Susman & Evered, 1978, and prescient research (Corley & Gioia, 2011).

More recently, Barrett and Oborn (2018) combined different types of expertise with varving levels of academic-practitioner collaboration to suggest several strategies for developing impactful research. Firstly, academics can maintain critical distance by pur suing a loosely-coupled form of practitioner collaboration and seeking to gain interactional expertise (being familiar or fluent with the language of a domain) in another domain. Secondly, through more tightly-coupled practitioner collaboration, academics developing interactional expertise might promote deeper engagement, whereby they seek practical utility through co-creation with practitioners. Finally, if academics can gain contributory expertise (having a high level of proficiency and tacit understanding of a domain) in a new domain, they might seek to develop prescient research or develop hybrid practices.

Notwithstanding the prevalence of this topic in academic discourse, we still have a poor understanding of how the transfer of knowledge between theory and practice actually works (Jarzabkowski, Mohrman, & Scherer, 2010). This is at least partly because existing work underplays the role of practitioners and seldom investigates how these issues unfold in practice.

## 3. Knowledge boundaries and academic-practitioner knowledge transfer

Knowledge has long been recognised within organisation studies as being vital as both a source and a barrier to organisational innovation (Cook & Brown, 1999; Nonaka, 1994; Carlile, 2002). For example, a firm’s ability to recognize the value of new, external information, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends – known as its absorptive capacity – is critical to its innovative capabilities, and also principally a function of its prior related knowledge, which includes knowledge of prevailing scientific or technologica advances in a given field (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). But while a firm’s knowledge can enhance its capacity for innovation, it can also act as critical barrier.

## 3.1. Syntactic, semantic and pragmatic knowledge boundaries

The existence of complex knowledge boundaries within and between organisations can constrain innovation (Carlile. 2002, 2004) and collaboration (Barrett & Oborn, 2010; Nicolini, Mengis, & Swan, 2012). A knowledge boundary can emerge when there are differences in knowledge between two or more groups or communities, either within or between organisations. Knowledge at such a boundary is characterised by difference, dependence and novelty (Carlile, 2002, 2004).

Carlile (2002, 2004) identified three progressively complex types of knowledge boundary: syntactic, semantic and pragmatic These boundary types differ in terms of the novelty of knowledge (Carlile, 2002). Generally, there is more novelty at a pragmatic boundary than there is at a semantic boundary, and more at a semantic boundary than there is at a syntactic boundary.

At a syntactic boundary, differences between actors’ knowledge are known, and a common lexicon is typically sufficient for knowledge sharing (Carlile, 2002, 2004). At a semantic boundary, increased novelty makes meanings ambiguous and unclear. Even if a common syntax is established, interpretive differences make communication and collaboration difficult (Carlile, 2002, 2004). For instance, Dougherty (1992) found that differences among thought worlds within various departments of an organisation prevent innovators from synthesizing their expertise, which inhibits the development of the knowledge required for innovation. Finally, a pragmatic boundary arises when novelty results in different interests among actors (Carlile, 2004). Pragmatic boundaries are possible because knowledge is localised, embedded and invested in practice. More specifically, knowledge is: 1) localised around specific problems faced in a practice; 2) embedded in the technologies, methods, and techniques used in a practice; and 3) invested in the ways of doing things and successes that demonstrate its value to a practice (Carlile, 2002).

Knowledge boundaries can engender several suboptimal outcomes. Firstly, as differences in domain-specific knowledge increases between actors, it becomes increasingly difficult for them to assess and evaluate novel knowledge (Carlile, 2004). This can occur at both semantic and pragmatic boundaries, which are both characterised by novelty. Secondly, the specialisation of knowledge that emerges when knowledge is embedded and localised in practice, while beneficial within a practice, becomes problematic when working across practices (Carlile, 2002). Individuals are committed to and invested in their hard-won knowledge, which is “at stake” and costly to replace. Consequently, people are reluctant to change their knowledge (Carlile. 2002)

## 3.1.1. Knowledge boundaries in discourse on the research-practice gap

Scholars investigating the causes of the research-practice gap frequently cite the importance of knowledge transfer (Hodgkinson & Rousseau, 2009; Jarzabkowski et al., 2010; Kieser & Leiner, 2009; Shapiro et al., 2007) and boundary spanning (Bansal et al., 2012; Barrett & Oborn, 2018: Bartunek, 2007: Gulati, 2007: Polzer, Gulati, Khurana, & Tushman, 2009: Rynes et al., 2001: Starkey & Madan 2001). Within this discourse, anecdotal evidence demonstrates how the novelty of academic knowledge can inflame academicpractitioner relations. For example, Corley and Gioia (2011, p. 21) tell the story of an engagement between academics and practi tioners in which practitioners were confronted with some theoretical idea: “One of the executives thought it over for a few pregnant seconds, however, and then said simply, ‘You people talk funny.’”(Corley & Gioia, 2011, p. 21).

Van de Ven (2007) argued that there is a pragmatic knowledge boundary between academics and practitioners, noting that because practitioner audiences have different pragmatic interests to scientific communities, traditional forms of scientific explanation need to be supplemented with different sorts of research reports (Van de Ven, 2007, p. 250). Van de Ven went on to discuss in detail various implications of a pragmatic knowledge boundary on academic-practitioner relations. By taking a practitioner perspective and exploring these issues empirically, I build on Van de Ven’s claims and demonstrate how this pragmatic knowledge boundary exac erbates the research-practice gap, hamstrings practitioners’ ability to evaluate research, and discourages its uptake and application.

## 3.2. Boundary objects and knowledge transformation

Boundary objects can be used to overcome the challenges faced at a knowledge boundary. A boundary object is an information object that can be shared across different contexts (Star & Griesemer, 1989) and provides a way of representing, learning about, and transforming knowledge to resolve the negative consequences of a boundary (Carlile, 2002; Star & Griesemer, 1989).

Effective boundary objects motivate collaboration, allow participants to work across knowledge boundaries and provide an infrastructure for doing this (Nicolini et al., 2012). However, boundary objects are not always effective: an effective object in one setting can be equally ineffective when deployed in another (Carlile, 2002). Furthermore, what is required of a boundary object in creases with the complexity of the boundary faced.

According to Carlile, if a syntactic boundary is faced, a boundary object must establish a shared syntax or language for individuals to transfer and represent their domain-specific knowledge. At a semantic boundary, a boundary object must facilitate a process of learning about and translating knowledge. This establishes common meanings that enable actors to share and assess new knowledge. At a pragmatic boundary, a boundary object must aid the transformation of knowledge, whereby, in addition to the above, individuals alter their domain-specific knowledge in order to create new knowledge and establish shared interests (Carlile, 2002, 2004).

Boundary objects come in many different guises. For example, Nicolini et al. (2012) list repositories, sketches and drawings, workflow matrices, physical and IT objects, prototypes, metaphors and narratives as among a multitude of objects that might have boundary spanning capabilities. In their own study. Nicolini et al. (2012) found that PowerPoint representations of experiment results acted as boundary objects. In the practice of software development, requirement specifications and project management methodol ogies, such as Gantt charts, bridge knowledge boundaries between those with technical knowledge and those with domain specific knowledge (Barrett & Oborn. 2010). “Requirement specifications are at the centre of the software development process as diverse groups use this to stipulate the functionality required of the system in developing code” (Barrett & Oborn, 2010, p. 1201), while Gantt charts can be used to visually manage resources while also promising closure to an inherently uncertain endeavour.

## 3.3. Using boundary objects to support cross-disciplinary collaboration

Whatever form a boundary object takes, the distinguishing characteristic uniting such objects is their capacity to serve as bridges between intersecting social and cultural worlds (Nicolini et al., 2012), thereby facilitating collaboration across knowledge boundaries (Barrett & Oborn, 2010). In other words, boundary objects make cross-disciplinary collaboration possible by acting as translation and transformation devices across various thought worlds (Nicolini et al., 2012). Existing discourse on the research-practice gap recognises collaboration as critical to overcoming it. Barrett and Oborn’s (2018) RICK Framework combines different forms of collaboration with different levels of expertise to outline various strategies for increasing the impact of research. They note that “practitioners, by developing their interactional expertise can enhance their tacit power in sense reading theoretical contributions” (Barrett & Oborn, 2018, p. 45), leading to practical utility of research findings, and that interac tional expertise can be developed through different levels of academic-practitioner relationships.

What remains to be seen is whether boundary objects can facilitate academic-practitioner collaboration in a way that enables this. In other words, can boundary objects improve academic-practitioner collaboration such that practitioners can gain interactional expertise in understanding and applying theoretical contributions? Although academic discourse on the research-practice gap rec ognises the importance of collaboration, and while it has been suggested that a pragmatic knowledge boundary might divide aca demics and practitioners (Van de Ven, 2007), we do not know whether boundary objects could be effectively used to help bridge the research-practice gap. This paper explores how boundary objects can facilitate certain collaborative practices, such as deep engage ment (Barrett & Oborn, 2018), while simultaneously enabling practitioners to develop interactional expertise.

## 5. The research-practice knowledge boundary

This section has three obiectives. Section 5.1 describes the key features of the research-practice knowledge boundary and corroborates Van de Ven’s (2007) argument that this boundary is a pragmatic one. Section 5.2 demonstrates how this knowledge boundary hinders practitioner’s assessments of OT and IS research and ultimately discourages its practical application. Section 5.3 demonstrates that a combination of boundary objects and collaboration can be effective in attenuating these problems.

## 5.1. Describing the research-practice knowledge boundary

There are sharp differences between academic knowledge and practitioner knowledge. The two communities can be said to occupy different thought worlds (Dougherty, 1992). Whereas scientists are fundamentally concerned with truth, practitioners are interested in sales, profits and liquidity (Kieser & Leiner, 2009). According to Rynes et al. (2001, p. 340-1):

Academics and practitioners have fundamentally different frames of reference with respect to such things as the types of in formation believed to constitute valid bases for action, the ways in which information is ordered and arranged for “sensemaking,” the past experiences used to evaluate the validity of knowledge claims, and the metaphors used to symbolically construct the world in meaningful ways. Similarly … there are notable differences between academics and practitioners with respect to the goals they seek to influence, the social systems in which they operate, the variables they attempt to manipulate, and acceptable time frames for addressing problems.

That academics and practitioners occupy different thought worlds suggests that, at a minimum, a semantic knowledge boundary exists between them. But practitioner knowledge is also localised, embedded and invested in a different set of practices than academic knowledge. The following vignette from the IT consulting company demonstrates how consultants’ knowledge is largely embedded in the use of industry-standard tools and techniques. Their knowledge is not embedded in academic theory, and there are strikingly less positive assessments of ’business theory’.

Vignette 1: During their induction at the company, all new consultants are required to complete an intensive five-day programme that teaches the skills that are deemed to be essential for consultants. Module one is a full-day course that teaches the con sultants how to use a variety of problem solving tools and techniques, including McKinsey’s 7-S framework, BCG’s positioning matrix, fish-bone diagrams and process mapping. Several slides are dedicated to business theory, one of which has the sub-title “Why Bother?” While the slide acknowledges that business theories can be useful for considering a problem in a structured way, consultants are warned not to ‘name-drop’ business theories into a client conversation to look like an expert. Consultants are advised that business theories date quickly and that it is unlikely that a single theory can solve a client’s problems. They are also warned that business theories are aimed at helping businesses boost profits and are less suitable for clients in the public sector.

Here. knowledge is localised around client problems that consultants face. and embedded and invested in practices, particularly the use of certain problem-solving tools and methods. It is knowledge of these tools, but also knowing how to get things done with them in practice (Orlikowski, 2002), that is deemed so important that it is the first thing the organisation teaches its new consultants. Practices involving the use of ‘business theory’ are far less established, and are viewed more sceptically.

That practitioner knowledge is embedded, localised and invested in practices so distinct from academia suggests that the knowledge boundary at the interface of academia and practice is a pragmatic one, which supports claims previously made by Van de Ven (2007). Hence, we would expect this boundary to be characterised by high levels of novelty and difference (Carlile, 2004), which has significant implications for knowledge sharing. A challenge at pragmatic boundaries is that individuals must be willing to modify their own knowledge so they can collaborate with individuals from other groups. But because individuals are committed to and invested in their knowledge as hard-won, they are often reluctant to do this (Carlile. 2002). How this principle manifests itself at the interface of research and practice can be seen in the following vignette:

Vignette 2: Jack is a senior manager in the business consulting practice, specialising in change management. At the time. he was working full time on a client assignment. When asked whether Tripsas’s theory was relevant to his client work, he responded: “So organisational identity is massively strong here. The whole organisation is built around historic silos. With that comes some cultural identity issues. We are trying to help change how they perceive themselves and you could take some of the concepts outlined in the paper and take that through into something that is actually relevant in the context of the client and tell a really good compelling story.” However, when asked how his clients would respond if he started talking about organisational identity in front of them, he was less optimistic: “I think it would be a challenge. Firstly, taking it to the customer as it stands would be terrible, and it would land very poorly. They would respond in one of two ways. One, they would be terrified by their inability to penetrate it and would say as much, i.e. ‘I don’t understand what you’re showing me there’. Or they can react emotionally where they are made nervous by being presented with something that they cannot personally penetrate. And rather than admit that they don’t know what it means, they could respond quite emotionally because of the discomfort that it makes them feel. When you are presented with something that you cannot get your head round you either go ‘I don’t get that can you explain it differently please’, or you reach an entrenched emotional position of: ‘because I can’t understand it, it must be nonsense’, and people can actually respond quite angrily or emotionally.”

Interestingly, Jack himself, who is experienced in change management, has little difficulty in recognising the relevance of the theory, and the value that could come from its application. His experience of managing organisational change in technological contexts means that the concept of organisational identity does not differ drastically from his domain-specific knowledge. But he expects that his clients, who are less equipped to understand the theory, would react negatively to it. His clients would be reluctant to replace their own hard-won knowledge with something novel that they don’t understand.

Several conclusions can be drawn from this discussion: first. there exists a knowledge boundary at the interface of research and practice: second, this boundary possesses the characteristics of both semantic and pragmatic boundaries: third, this has adverse im: plications for both how practitioners judge OT and IS research (vignette 1) and how they expect others to respond to OT and IS research (vignette 2). We see directly in both vignettes how the resulting assessment of OT and IS research discourages its application in practice. The next section will show further how the novelty of OT and IS research hinders practitioners' assessments of it.

## 5.2. Novelty and practitioner assessments of OT and IS research

To reiterate Carlile (2004, p. 556), as differences in the type of domain-specific knowledge increases between actors, it becomes increasingly difficult for them to evaluate and assess novel knowledge. Consistent with this principle, the novelty of OT and IS research hinders practitioners’ ability to assess it, but here it results in counterintuitive, contradictory and even illogical assessments of theory and research. Contradictory and counterintuitive stances are a common feature of organisational life and regularly surface in quali tative interview data (El-Sawad, Arnold, & Cohen, 2004). But actors employ methods (‘doublethink’) of “containing contradiction such that it is neither acknowledged as contradiction nor experienced as uncomfortable” (p.1179): that is, they appear oblivious to their contradictory and illogical stances (El-Sawad et al, 2004).

Several examples of this contradiction arise in academic-practitioner relations, each of which are detrimental to the uptake of OT and IS research in practical settings. Firstly, while practitioners and academics both claim to be interested in widespread and important organisational problems, they have very conflicting and contradictory ideas of what this means. Even prescient OT is judged by practitioners as failing to address “pervasive problems”. Secondly, practitioners counterintuitively judge theory as being low in reliability and factuality, exemplified in statements that theory is not “cold hard fact”, “tried and tested” or “grounded in experience”. Finally, practitioners lean towards an instrumental view of research utilisation (Beyer, 1997), valuing theory primarily to the extent that it can be productised, while simultaneously judging it negatively against this criteria. Less instrumental ways in which theory could be used are seldom imagined or considered.

## 5.2.1. Contradictory views on relevance

The best theories, according to Vermeulen (2005, p. 979–980), share a common zest for tackling real practical issues. Similarly, Corley and Gioia (2011, p. 22) argue that scholars should not endeavour to solve practitioners’ specifications of mundane managerial problems unless they are manifestations of wider or deeper issues. This observation forms the basis of their call for theoretical pre science, whereby scholars focus on incipient and significant organisational, managerial and societal issues. Yet, even when presented with prescient theory, practitioners often do not recognize its relevance, and academics and practitioners have sharply different views of what constitutes relevance.

In the IT consulting company, practitioners were presented with a summary of Tripsas (2009), who invokes the notion of organisational identity to explain why organisations struggle to adopt new technology. Because adopting new technology is a ubiq uitous challenge faced by practically all contemporary organisations, Tripsas’s work certainly meets the criteria for prescient research. Nevertheless, practitioners were less convinced of this, as can be seen in the following passage:

Vignette 3: John and Steve lead a team tasked with developing innovative solutions to client problems. John, as head of new ventures, leads the team. Steve is a senior engineer. After an initial discussion about Tripsas's theory, John mused: “There's an important thing for us about finding problems to go after is that they need to be pervasive problems. So why would we put a million quid into developing a product unless we could convince ourselves that there was a pervasive problem in the market place, and that we can develop something once and that pretty much the standard modules that we develop would apply time and time again?...In different domains in the customer problem space, there are different degrees of pervasiveness. Solving and protecting an organisation from cyber-attack: you can imagine there is quite a lot of common pervasive problems because organisations have a network. You could productise that. How to change an organisation?" After John and Steve debated this question for a while, John concluded: “I just worry that we are not in the world of productisable ‘apply-this-theory’ and you wil unlock change…It’s not a repeatable pervasive product.” Steve had similar concerns: “theory can come across as the solution, saying, if you do this you will be successful.” He added that theory is only valuable if you know which theory to look at, to which John agreed: “Well this is the worry. Is [Tripsas’s] theory going to be the theory that unlocks change for our business? No exactly.” Again, after some more discussion, John continued: “Unless it’s something like ‘get ready for GDPR’. There you are basically do and repeat…[Tripsas’s] theory is less suited to that because it’s a generic theory of change resistance, rather than, there’s a problem happening in the world around data protection.”

Here, John at once claims to be interested in “pervasive” and “repeatable" problems that will apply “time and time again”. while simultaneously criticising Tripsas’s theory for being too “generic”, all the while remaining unaware of any potential contradiction.

That academics and practitioners have different views as to what constitutes relevance is highly significant to those interested in bridging the academic-practitioner divide: it suggests that prescient research, even if necessary, may not be sufficient, because even when academics produce it, its relevance may not be appreciated by practitioners

## 5.2.2. Contradictory views on reliability

Academics and practitioners hold sharply different views about what constitutes reliability. Counterintuitively, practitioners judge OT as being low in reliability and factuality, exemplified in statements that theory is not “cold hard fact". “tried and tested” or “grounded in experience”. Because of this, its use is regarded as being risky. This is evident in the following passage:

Vignette 4: Sarah, a principal consultant in the business analysis practice, was partaking in a group discussion on Tripsas’s (2009) paper, along with two junior business consultants. Reflecting on why organisations don’t invest more time in understanding theory, Sarah came to the following conclusion: “It’s almost like the luxury of using something like that is something we don’t have, because there isn’t enough time, there isn’t enough resource and actually everyone is focused on delivering what they think they need to deliver. And that doesn’t mean it’s right, but I think there is a perception that stuff that isn’t cold hard fact, or tried and tested tools, is risky.”

Sarah suggests here that practitioners are sceptical about both the factuality (theory “isn’t cold hard fact”) and the reliability (theory isn’t “tried and tested”) of theory, which makes its application risky. Sarah implicitly confirms that practitioners are interested in “cold hard fact”, but that theory does not meet her criteria for this. The following vignette shows a similar trend:

Vignette 5: In an attempt to boost the profile of the business consulting practice, several business consultants, including the practice lead, initiated a programme to establish the practice as a centre for thought leadership. As part of this process, several junior consultants sought the advice of the company’s head of knowledge management. In an email, he advised the consultants to look closely at existing models of consulting thought leadership and then “reverse-engineer from the features that make them work to develop your own model”. The knowledge manager shared with them an article from Deloitte’s website, adding: “What makes this [article] so good? Topical? – check. Grounded in experience more than theory? – check. Direct in style and short? – check. About something relevant to the reader and also about which you might deal with Deloitte? – check. Actionable? - check”.

Here, the knowledge manager makes a point of polarising experience and theory. Reliable knowledge is deemed to be that which is embedded and localised in a specific set of practices. Because Deloitte’s knowledge is embedded and localised in similar practices, it is viewed as being more reliable than theory, even though theory emerges through, and is rigorously tested against, observation of empirical evidence.

While it may not surprise readers to find that practitioners and academics have different ideas on relevance, the lack of confidence that practitioners have in the reliability of OT and IS research is more striking. OT and IS research, like all academic theory, prides itself on scientific rigour. It is precisely this rigour that ensures reliability, differentiates theory from fiction (Morse, Barrett, Mayan, Olson, & Spiers. 2002). and ultimately grants the right for scholars to make truth claims. Indeed, it is a supposed over-obsession with scientific rigour that discourse on the research-practice gap has long assumed to be the problem, the argument being that it has obfuscated the relevance of OT and IS research. In actuality, these efforts to ensure reliability are lost on practitioners. For them, the status of “cold hard fact” or “tried and tested” is granted only to knowledge that is embedded in familiar practices.

## 5.2.3. Contradictory views on research utilisation

As noted above, Beyer (1997) identified three ways that academic research might be applied in practical contexts: instrumental, conceptual, and symbolic, noting that if academics search only for instrumental traces of their research in managerial actions, they will be “doomed to disappointment" (p. 18). However, practitioners tend not imagine or envisage that theory might have conceptual or symbolic value. Instead, they tend to judge theory by its instrumental value. For instance, practitioners judge OT and IS research by its ability to be productised and repeatedly sold to clients. Oddly, practitioners simultaneously recognise that instrumental application is not something that is easy to do with theory. In other words, practitioners at once score theory low in instrumental value, but, unable to imagine other uses for it, continue to assess it against this criteria anyway.

This tendency was apparent when John (vignette 3) assumed that for a theory to be valuable, it would have to be “productisable”, while simultaneously arguing that Tripsas’s (2009) theory could not be productised to “unlock change”. A similar trend can be seen in the following:

Vignette 6: In an email to the company’s lead consultant, I suggested that the consulting capability might benefit from a greater appreciation of the knowledge and insights emerging from OT and IS research. While not entirely against the idea, the senior consultant responded: “You do need to think about what business problems you’re seeking to solve and why clients would talk to us about them. I don’t think this would be a ‘door opening sale’ for us as we don’t have brand recognition for doing this type of work; however, there are potentially second/third order sales opportunities. However we would need to think about how we would position ourselves and why a client would consider us.” In a follow up discussion, the senior consultant also made the argument that the use of theory was tantamount to starting with a solution and finding a problem to apply it to, whereas their approach should be problem driven

Upon hearing the suggestion that greater appreciation of OT and IS research might be beneficial to the consulting capability, the consultant makes an assumption that such benefit could only be realised if the firm could use theory to increase client sales, a very instrumental view of theory utilisation. At the same time, he is not optimistic that it would be possible to realise this benefit. The lead consultant is also wary that the application of theory is inconsistent with a problem driven approach, despite there being little dif ference between this and the application of the consulting tools and techniques to client problems. However, he does not acknowledge or find uncomfortable this contradiction.

The key conclusion to draw from this section is that the novelty of OT and IS research as it crosses the research-practice knowledge boundary reduces practitioners’ ability to evaluate it, resulting in counterintuitive and contradictory assessments. This ultimately discourages its application. When scaled up to the organisational level, it is easy to imagine how this would constrain the absorptive capacity of organisations that might otherwise be fertile grounds on which OT and IS research could influence management practice.

There is a conception among practitioners that theory is abstract and isolated from experience of organisations, that practitioners conjure theory from thin air rather than it emerging from intensive observation of organisations and management. That OT and IS research emerges from empirical observation of organisations seems to count for little in practitioners’ assessments of it, even though their concern for reliability and factuality is implied through references to “cold hard fact”.

## 5.3. Boundary objects, collaborative practices and interactive expertise

Barrett and Oborn (2018) drew attention to the benefits that emerge when academics gain interactional expertise in a practical domain. However, in order for research utilisation to occur, it is equally important that practitioners can gain interactional expertise in a relevant academic domain. In this section, I demonstrate how a combination of effective boundary objects and tightly coupled academic-practitioner collaboration can aid the practical application of OT and IS research.

Firstly, boundary objects are essential for transforming knowledge and overcoming the difficulties associated with knowledge boundaries (Carlile, 2002). But boundary objects are not always employed in academic-practitioner relations. When they are, their effectiveness is, unsurprisingly, relative to the recipient or audience. Secondly, the importance of collaboration in academicpractitioner relations has been well documented. According to Barrett and Oborn (2018), academics maintain critical distance through loosely coupled collaboration with practitioners, or they can “promote deeper engagement through a more tightly coupled mode of collaboration” (p. 47).

The variation in mode of collaboration and the effectiveness of boundary objects in attempts to transform existing theory into usable forms leads to several discernibly distinct scenarios. From the perspective of bridging the research-practice gap, there are important differences between these scenarios.

## 5.3.1. Maintaining critical distance, no boundary object

Where neither collaboration is fostered, nor an adequate boundary object supplied, it is unlikely that practitioners will find ways to apply OT and IS research. In this scenario, there is no way to translate or transform research findings, nor is there any opportunity to promote deeper engagement. Under these circumstances, it is highly unlikely that practitioners will gain the interactional expertise required for them to apply research findings. In many ways, this scenario is the default position: it is fairly uncontroversial to say that the average manager is unlikely to be a frequent reader of Academy of Management Review (AMR), Administrative Science Quarterly (ASQ), Organization Science (OS) or Management Information Systems Quarterly (MISQ) – though, admittedly, bridge journals such as Harvard Business Review (HBR) still have a wide readership.

Vignettes 1 and 5, both from the early phases of my fieldwork, where critical distance was maintained and no boundary objects were used, exemplify this scenario. While unprompted, practitioners still occasionally make references to the use of business theory. But these references carry a sting of negativity, are marred in contradiction – e.g. the knowledge manager who made a point of polarising theory and experience – and even actively discourage the use of theory – e.g. the course instructor who warned his pupils not to ‘name-drop’ business theories into a client conversation. Where this scenario persists, the research-practice gap is likely to remain problematic.

## 5.3.2. Promoting deeper engagement, no boundary object

Academics might seek to promote deeper engagement through collaborative efforts without formally using any sort of boundary object. While such engagements could be highly successful, the absence of an effective method for spanning the research-practice boundary might prove problematic. Effective boundary objects establish a common meaning for individuals to share their knowl edge and learn new knowledge (Carlile, 2002). In academic-practitioner relations, a boundary object might enable practitioners to learn about how theoretical ideas apply to the problems they face. In the absence of this, theory can be perceived as abstract and irrelevant.

Vignette 6 was an example of this scenario. Here, deeper engagement was promoted in my attempts to explore, in collaboration with the lead consultant, where OT and IS research might be applied to good effect in the organisation. But the absence of a functioning boundary object in which to ground this discussion prompted the consultant to say: “You do need to think about what business problems you’re seeking to solve”.

## 5.3.3. Promoting deeper engagement, boundary object ineffective

There is no guarantee that boundary objects will effectively serve the purpose they are designed for. A boundary object that seeks to translate an existing academic theory into something more amenable to practitioner uptake might still be ineffective, especially when a practitioner’s domain-specific knowledge is sharply differently to the constructs used in the theory, under which circumstances novelty would be heightened. When collaborative efforts to promote deeper engagement are coupled with the use of ineffective boundary objects, then these efforts are likely to succumb to the same pitfalls as in the scenarios already discussed.

In my field work, the summary of Tripsas (2009) was an inadequate boundary object when supplied to practitioners who were not well-versed in change management. Vignettes 3 and 4 are examples of this scenario, and both exemplify less positive assessments. Similarly, Rachael, a junior business consultant who had read the Tripsas summary, but who had little experience in change man agement, struggled to imagine how it could be applied in practice: “I find that you can’t apply a lot of academia because it’s very good when things are black and white and everything is clearly defined, but a lot of times there’s the grey space.”

Overall, in the absence of collaborative efforts to promote deeper engagement or effective boundary-spanning objects to aid such engagement, the pragmatic knowledge boundary at the interface of research and practice will persist, and the novelty of OT and IS research will continue to hamstring practitioner assessments of it, discouraging its uptake and application.

## 5.3.4. Promoting deeper engagement, boundary object effective

When academics and practitioners engage in more tightly coupled collaboration, and when this collaboration is aided by an effective boundary object, practitioners are able to overcome the difficulties identified above. Under these circumstances practitioner assessments of OT and IS research are less riddled with contradiction and are able to identify its relevance. Furthermore, under these circumstances, practitioners conceive of the symbolic and conceptual value that OT and IS research might have. In other words, they are able to see the value in theory irrespective of its ability to be instrumentally applied. This combination of boundary objects and tightly coupled collaboration can also help practitioners develop interactional expertise in OT and IS research.

In the IT consulting company, consultants well-versed in change management, whose domain-specific knowledge was more aligned to the theoretical constructs used in Tripsas’s (2009) theory, were far more receptive to it. Consequently, the summary that was provided to these individuals acted as an effective boundary object. For example:

Vignette 7: Jamie had recently joined the company as a principal consultant within the change management division. He had yet to be placed on a formal assignment (he was “on the bench”, so to speak) and informed me that he was reading lots of Harvard Business Review articles to ensure he was ready to be “dropped in front of a client”. I asked him what he thought of Tripsas’s theory and the concept of identity as a way of understanding resistance to technological change, to which he responded: “What I found particularly interesting about [identity] is that it’s more important than just a factor to consider. It’s so important because it permeates everything else.” He later expanded: “this idea that there is a lens that everyone is seeing everything through, and that there isn’t an objective perspective: that certainly made me think a bit. Because I think it’s easy when you’re doing organisational design to have a blank piece of paper and to say ‘these are all our boxes’. But it’s almost idealised. So I definitely found it useful.”

Jamie’s ability to understand the theoretical constructs used and the value they confer is aided by his experience of practicing organisational change management and the difficulties associated with this. Appreciation of these difficulties meant that the constructs in Tripsas’s theory resonated with him and the boundary object established a common meaning. That the theory “certainly made me think a bit” suggests the boundary object was successful in facilitating learning and general enlightenment (conceptual utilisation), while his recognition of the value of theory relative to other “idealised” approaches suggests it enabled him to alter his domain-specific knowledge.

During a collaborative discussion, Sophie, a senior business consultant in the change management division, was able to go even further towards developing the theory into something of practical value.

Vignette 8: Sophie reflected that Tripsas's (2009) theory might be a really smart way of explaining to their clients the adverse implications that might occur if they fail to manage change. She remarked that this would have to accompany a wider shift in how change consultants operate: they are typically recruited onto a project in its later stages to ensure a smooth transition to a pre-defined state, but the notion of identity could be deployed in the early phases of a project to determine the scope of change and convey to clients the impact that a new technology might have. At the time of our discussion, Sophie was on a client assignment that involved writing a business case for the implementation of a new technology, the purpose of which was to improve end user access to corporate services, such as HR, finance and training. “What would be a really interesting, it would be at this stage that there could be a conversation with the [senior responsible officer] as to: ‘this is what you are looking to do. What impact is this going to have on your organisation’s identity?’”. When I challenged her to reflect on how the new tech nology might impact her client. she reflected: “One of the key cultural pillars of [the client] is human interaction and they are very passionate about having someone to talk to – that human one-to-one interaction. Now, if we start forcing everybody to use a system to solve their problems, there’s going to be some pushback from it, in fact probably a lot, and people will find ways to work around it." Sophie acknowledged the difficulty that organisational leaders have in articulating change in ways that resonate with people in their organisations, and thought that invoking organisational identity might be a clever way of over coming this, while also acting as a reference point against which leaders could test whether a new technology is consistent: “I think that could be definitely something we could build in, for example ‘we are doing this because it’s brave, or because it’s a challenge, or you’re doing this because it shows diversity’, you know. And that mapping then has that justification, and then if there are big things that don’t map back to those, it’s an indicator that actually there might be some challenge and push back from internally and externally.”

Unlike Jamie, Sophie was working on active client work and was able to link the theory more directly to tangible client problems Firstly, she noted that the theory could be used before implementing a new technology to inform leadership decisions: that is. to advise leaders of the likely impact of a new technology (instrumental utilisation). Secondly, the theory could be used during implementation to support communications to employees and to justify the decision to implement a technology in ways that resonate with employees (symbolic utilisation). In addition to providing a common meaning, the boundary object here provided a means for Sophie to work across the research-practice boundary by reflecting on how to bring OT into her workplace.

Jack, the senior manager from vignette 2, went furthest towards application of OT. During our collaboration, he began to further transform the boundary object into something that was ready for client application.

Vignette 9: After Jack advised me that the summary of Tripsas (2009) would land badly if we put it in front of his client (vignette 2), I pressed him to reflect on how we could overcome this, to which he responded: “So the first solution is to take something like the paper that you sent across. I could pull together a few bullet points, for each paragraph of the paper, a few bullet points that outline the sort of language that consultants would understand. The next step from there, and again I started to jot this down in the context of my current client at the moment, is what all of this means for the local organisation at the moment.. .This would be the diagnostic for the customer context. Then I would explain how I would then communicate that to the customer. We should then see clear traceability from Mary's paper through to how that would then implicate change methodology and how we would convey that to the customer. By the end it should be clear what it means to the customer.” Jack suggested we work together to this end.

Here, while Jack clearly understands the theory, he expects his clients will not (vignette 2). But Jack is able to combine his contributory expertise in consulting with his understanding of the theory to further transform the boundary object. Here, the boundary object motivated collaboration between myself and Jack and provided us with shared interests: it was in both our interests to further transform the boundary object to make it ‘client ready’.

Across vignettes 7 to 9, the Tripsas summary possessed many of the hallmarks of an effective boundary object (Carlile, 2002; Nicolini et al., 2012): It facilitated Jamie’s learning and helped him alter his knowledge; it enabled Sophie to span the research-practice knowledge boundary in her reflections; it motivated Jack and I to collaborate to transform our knowledge and direct it towards a common purpose.

Several conclusions can be drawn from this. Firstly, if a boundary object is used to communicate theoretical ideas to practitioners, this boundary object will be more effective when it closely aligns to practitioners’ domain-specific knowledge. For example, the summary of Tripsas (2009) was a strikingly more effective boundary object to change consultants than to other employees.

Secondly, when a boundary object is effective, and when collaboration actively encourages it, practitioners are able to apply OT and IS research to tangible problems they face. For example, Sophie found numerous ways Tripsas (2009) could be applied to a specific client problem.

Thirdly, when a boundary object is not entirely effective, collaboration can encourage practitioners to further transform knowledge until it is ready to be applied. For example, Jack wanted to turn my boundary object into more simple bullet points directly related to his client before presenting it to them, but only after I had encouraged him to read the summary and reflect on where it might be applied.

Finally, there is evidence that when effective boundary objects are used to support more tightly-coupled collaboration (Barrett & Oborn. 2018). practitioners are well-positioned to develop a high-degree of fluency and interactional expertise in certain theoretical areas, particularly those that relate to contributory expertise they have. Jamie, Sophie and Jack all have contributory expertise in the practical management of organisational change and were all able to discuss related theoretical concepts with a high-level of fluency.

My own involvement in these engagements was non-trivial. When my colleagues responded positively to the theory, I encouraged and prompted them to reflect on how it could be applied. Often, this occurred with little input required. But in the spirit of promoting deeper engagement, there were occasions where I brought my own knowledge – which included both knowledge of OT and experience of practicing change management – to bear. This included describing client work of my own where a deeper understanding of OT and IS research proved enlightening. This suggests that there is a role for academics to promote deeper engagement and communicate the relevance of their work to practitioners.

Overall, there is cause for optimism about overcoming the research-practice gap. There are clear practical implications for scholars: a strategy combining tightly-coupled collaboration with boundary objects can be effective in bridging the gap.

## 6. Discussion

The reason many attempts to understand the causes of the research-practice gap have failed to unearth satisfactory answers is that those answers do not reside exclusively in the world of academia, which is where the vast majority of investigations have taken place. Instead, deep-seated organisational phenomena have engendered organisations that are ill-equipped to harness even the most pre scient OT and IS research. Weick (2001) drew attention to the role of practitioners wedded to gurus and fads in perpetuating the research-practice gap. But while managerial fads and fashions are certainly important to the diffusion of ideas (Abrahamson, 1991, 1996). the vignettes presented in this paper illustrate the role of a pragmatic knowledge boundary that divides practitioners and academics. This boundary hampers practitioners’ ability to evaluate theory, resulting in contradictory assessments and ultimately discouraging research utilisation.

The potentially positive role of boundary objects has additionally been analysed. The effectiveness of boundary objects in facili tating cross-disciplinary collaboration is well-established (e.g. Barrett & Oborn, 2010; Nicolini et al., 2012), yet this had not been demonstrated in the context of the academic-practitioner divide. This paper has found that boundary obiects designed to communicate theoretical ideas to practical audiences, such as the two-page summary of Tripsas (2009), can indeed motivate and aid collaboration between theorists and practitioners. This is particularly the case when there is already an active effort to promote deeper engagement. Promisingly, such boundary objects can enable practitioners to develop interactional expertise in OT and IS research, which supports research utilisation. As demonstrated above, those practitioners with relevant domain-specific knowledge have the strongest foun dation on which to build interactional expertise

It is worth noting that interactional expertise has been defined as “the ability to converse expertly about a practical skill or expertise but without being able to practice it” (Collins, 2004, p. 125). In the context of the research-practice gap, we should elaborate on what it means for a practitioner to develop interactional expertise in OT and IS research “without being able to practice it". Clearly, just because a manager develops a deep understanding of an organisational theory, this does not mean they can practice organisation science – i.e. being able to converse expertly about Tripsas (2009) does not mean a practitioner could, without further training, conduct their own research and publish it in a peer-reviewed journal. It is possible, however, for practitioners with interactional expertise in OT and IS research to weave theoretical insights into their own practices, practices in which they do have contributory expertise. In doing this, they can use theoretical insights to inform their work, either in an instrumental, symbolic or conceptual way. For example, Sophie’s un derstanding of Tripsas (2009) had the potential to spark numerous forms of research utilisation (Beyer, 1997; Beyer & Trice, 1982) that were intertwined with practices of change consulting. It is this that holds the most promise for bridging the research-practice gap.

## 6.1. Implications for practice

The purpose of this paper was to problematize the assumption that the research-practice gap exists solely due to deficiencies in theory and to explore why practitioners do not frequently exploit, harness and apply OT and IS research, with a view to making practical recommendations for bridging the gap. Perhaps the most important of these recommendations is that scholars need not exert so much effort inventing brand new forms of theory, produced through radically different practices. Instead, they would do well to spend a little more time taking stock of the very good theory that already exists, reflecting on where it might represent practical value and taking time to explain the implications of research findings to practitioners.

The research-practice knowledge boundary has a significant impact on academic-practitioner relations and will continue to do so irrespective of which new-fangled approaches to knowledge production are practiced. Overcoming this gap means overcoming the ultimate pragmatic knowledge boundary. Like with any other pragmatic boundary, the trick is to transform knowledge. Demonstrated here is that an appropriate boundary object can be effective in galvanising practitioners to engage with theory, to relate it to their work, and to be creative in imagining ways that it could be usefully applied to genuine problems.

None of this is to say that there is not scope for improvement in the production of OT and IS research. Strategies that focus on producing more relevant theory should be welcomed. But there is no reason to expect that the research-practice knowledge boundary will discriminate in its effects between existing knowledge and new knowledge. Hence. the problems of knowledge boundaries will have to be overcome either way.

Turning to the implications for practitioners, it is likely that any practitioner reading this is already open minded about the benefits that could emerge from deeper engagement with OT and IS research. If, when stumbling across an especially pertinent theoretical idea, a practitioner wishes to introduce it to their workplace, their attempts will face the same obstacles identified above unless they can transform their new knowledge into something their colleagues can engage with. In equipping themselves with interactional expertise in OT and IS research, they place themselves in a privileged position to produce and use boundary-spanning objects that transform their new knowledge. Actively promoting deeper engagement with willing scholars would likely augment this process. As argued by Tsoukas and Vladimirou (2001), “for organizational knowledge to be managed…practical mastery needs to be supplemented by a quasi-theoretical understanding of what individuals are doing when they exercise that mastery.” (p. 989–90). This was recognised by Jack, the senior manager we met in vignettes 2 and 9. He remarked that if a consultant were to attempt such an endeavour, he “would be looking for that individual to then validate their work through a discussion with the originator and ask ‘is this what you really meant?’. Because what you can’t have through that translation is that the underpinning reasoning, logic and meaning get lost, and there’s a risk of that.”

## 7. Conclusion

Several key conclusions can be drawn from this analysis. Firstly, a pragmatic knowledge boundary divides academics and prac titioners. Secondly, the novelty of OT and IS research at this knowledge boundary hinders practitioners’ ability to constructively assess it, which results in contradictions and discourages its application in practice. Finally, when academics and practitioners collaborate to promote deeper engagement, and when effective boundary-spanning objects are used, there are signs that practitioners can both transform knowledge and begin to develop valuable interactional expertise. I will now conclude by discussing some directions for future research and addressing some limitations of this study.

## 7.1. Directions for future research

It has been suggested that the uptake and application of research and theory might function in a way not dissimilar to the adoption and diffusion of innovations (Van de Ven & Johnson, 2006). In the spirit of developing a more objective analysis of the researchpractice gap, a constructive area of future research would be to apply the insights from innovation theory.

Perspectives that view innovation as a process (Garud, Tuertscher, & Van de Ven, 2013) involving “the development and implementation of new ideas by people who engage in transactions with others over time within an institutional context” (Van de Ven, 1986, p. 3), would be well suited to this endeavour and would enable researchers to track theoretical ideas from their inception in academic institutions through to their development and implementation in practice. Rather than viewing innovation as an outcome (Crossan & Apaydin, 2010) and focusing on determinant factors (Ahuja, Lampert, & Tandon, 2008), a process perspective drills deeper into the various sub-processes to explore factors that enable and inhibit success at each stage. The aim is not to uncover statistical causal links, but to infer generative causality (Garud et al., 2013).

Applying this perspective to the research-practice gap would involve reframing theories as novel ideas in the innovation process, which need to be developed before they are implemented in practice. Implementation in this context takes on a meaning akin to research utilisation (Beyer, 1997). That innovation processes can unfold within firms, across multi-party networks, and within communities (Garud et al., 2013), makes this perspective particularly suited to academic-practitioner relations.

From this perspective, it is likely that knowledge boundaries, the focus of this paper, are just one many factors within the inno vation process that exert generative causality on the uptake and application of OT and IS research. Indeed, knowledge boundaries inhibit the recombination of ideas and artefacts across disparate domains of knowledge and practice that is required during innovation processes to span structural holes and connect knowledge across communities (Garud et al., 2013, p. 777). But other factors, including the legitimacy of innovation (Dougherty & Heller, 1994; Lounsbury & Glynn, 2001) are also important and could be explored in the context of the research-practice gap.

Ultimately, the objective of this research should be to examine multiple cases of successful and unsuccessful attempts at research utilisation, to map the end-to-end process that theoretical ideas follow in order to be applied in practice, and to develop an under standing of the generative forces that support and constrain each stage of the innovation process, thereby assuaging or exacerbating the research-practice gap.

## 7.2. Limitations

A genuine limitation of this paper is that the ideas developed were illustrated through a case-study of a single organisation. But while this paper suffers from the same disadvantages as all studies that adopt a case-study design – i.e. limited generalisability – it also benefits from the depth of understanding afforded by the intensive and detailed analysis of a single case (Bryman & Bell, 2011).

Potentially more problematic is that the organisation in question was a consulting company. Consulting companies differ sub stantially from other types of organisation and have a significantly different relationship to management knowledge. For this reason, the findings in this paper cannot be readily generalised to non-consulting organisations without further investigation.

Yet there are several factors that make a consulting organisation an appropriate site on which to study practitioner relations with OT and IS research. In many ways, consultants naturally occupy the role of boundary-spanning organisations (Bansal et al., 2012), “intermediaries that search among the knowledge generated by experts for ideas and facts suitable for dissemination and use" (Starkey & Madan, 2001, S. 9). To paraphrase Michael Porter, if scholars are determining next best practice, consultants need to be bringing the laggards up to standard (Wind and Nueno, 1998, as cited in Starkey & Madan, 2001, S. 7). Furthermore, collaboration between ac ademics and consultants has proved successful in the past. Indeed, McKinsey’s 7-S model that was introduced to consultants in vignette 1 actually arose out of a collaboration with Stanford Business School (Starkey & Madan, 2001).

Jack, the senior manager from vignettes 2 and 9, agreed. For him, consultancy is the “lynch-pin” between academia and practice. What is needed, he argued, was someone to translate research findings. When I asked him where the responsibility should lie for doing this, he responded: “So I think a consultant should have the sorts of skills required to take something very complex and intellectual and translate that into something that fits into an existing proposition or fits with an existing discipline that we use. So taking an academic paper and translating to what it means for business architecture or business change or business analysis. I would see that as being absolutely a consultants role and I started doing it for your paper.”

In summary, consulting companies have a role to play in academic-practitioner relations, and are an appropriate site on which to study them. Nonetheless, to understand how knowledge boundaries mediate academic-practitioners relations in non-consulting companies, further investigations would be required.

Despite these limitations, the arguments developed here represent a step forward in our understanding of the factors underpinning the research-practice gap. By taking a practitioner perspective on this issue, we now have a better understanding of the deep-seated organisational forces acting to inhibit the practical application of OT and IS research. Those determined to bridge the gap should be prepared to encounter these forces. Strategies aimed at increasing the relevance of OT and IS research need to be accompanied by a bold willingness to tackle these forces head on, and those devising such strategies must be willing to take great care in transforming their knowledge so that practitioners can engage fruitfully with it.

The summary is a drastically shortened (two sides) version of the original paper. The key criteria for the summary were that it was clear, concise and easy to digest, while being an accurate reflection of the theory. This summary was intended to act as a boundary object (Carlile, 2002; Nicolini et al., 2012; Star & Griesemer, 1989), so its goal was to be establish a common meaning with practitioners.

In order to ensure the initial meaning of the paper was not lost, key segments were copied and pasted into two sides. However, in order to ensure the summary met the criteria above, several other modifications were made:

• Minor word changes were (on rare occasions) made to copied and pasted segments to ensure clarity.

• A small number of additional sentences were included to act as guideposts and to aid readability of the summary.

• Copied and pasted segments were reordered as required and do not necessarily occur in the same order as they do in the origina paper.

• The paper was structured as a series of questions and answers.

• All in-text references were removed from the copied and pasted segments. This was important for several reasons. While in-text citations are vital for published research, they are of little interest to practitioners and their removal ensured a smoother reading experience.

Summary of Technology, Identity, and Inertia Through the Lens of “The Digital Photography Company”,Tripsas (2009).

## 1. What problems does the theory address?

Organisations often experience difficulty when pursuing new technology. The difficulty that firms encounter when adapting to new technology has been well documented across a range of industries.

## 2. Why do these problems exist?

Usually, two reasons are given to explain why organisations experience such difficulty:

1. Firstly, existing behavioural routines and procedures, codified in organisational capabilities, persist in the face of change and result in suboptimal responses to innovation. Established firms have particular difficulty adapting to competence-destroying techno logical change that requires the acquisition of fundamentally new knowledge and routines. For instance, Swiss watch manufac turers were devastated by the transition from mechanical to quartz movements in watches.

2. Secondly, even if a firm engages in exploration, cognitive elements can be problematic. Interpretation of a new technology as a threat can result in high investment of resources in a rigid manner that preserves existing routines. For instance, newspapers that viewed the Internet as a threat offered fewer novel features on their websites, perceiving the Internet as primarily another vehicle for distributing the newspaper.

However, a third factor has been shown to be important: organisational identity.

## 3. What is organisational identity?

Organisational identity represents both how organisational insiders and outside constituents perceive an organisation.

Internal identity represents a shared understanding by organisational members regarding what is central, distinctive, and enduring about an organisation. It helps insiders to answer questions such as “Who are we? What kind of business are we in?” and guides key strategic decisions such as whether to make an acquisition, enter a new market, or divest a division. When faced with uncertainty or a challenging issue, managers view the issue through the lens of the firm’s internal identity, which guides interpretation and action.

External identity, on the other hand, captures how outside audiences such as institutional actors, customers, suppliers, or com plementary producers view the organisation.

Organisational identity serves a coordinating role, providing a focal point for both insiders and outsiders about what constitutes legitimate action on the part of an organisation. As such, identity guides the development of capabilities, the acquisition of knowledge, the evolution of routines, and the framing of issues.

## 4. Why is organisational identity important to technological change?

Sometimes a shift in the external environment or desired internal change cannot be accommodated within the constraints of an existing identity, so a change is needed. Technologies that deviate from the expectations associated with an organisation’s identity are labelled identity-challenging technologies.

Identity-challenging technologies are found to be difficult to capitalize on for two reasons.

• First, identity serves as a filter, such that organisational members notice and interpret external stimuli in a manner consistent with the identity. As a result, identity-challenging technological opportunities may be missed.

• Second, because identity becomes intertwined in the routines, procedures, and beliefs of both organisational and external con stituents, explicit efforts to shift identity in order to accommodate identity-challenging technology are difficult.

Even seemingly minor shifts from a technological standpoint may challenge the existing organisational identity if, by pursuing the new technology, the organisation violates the core features associated with its existing identity. Because of this, a firm may ultimately need to develop an entirely new organisational identity whereby both organisational members and external constituents must alter deeply held assumptions and beliefs about what the firm represents. However, changing identity is risky. The continuity of identity provides stability both within the organisation and in its projections to outsiders, improving long-term survival. When environmenta conditions necessitate a shift in identity, the ensuing change process is difficult to manage.

## 5. What are the practical implications?

Given the disruptive nature of identity shifts, understanding whether technology is identity challenging is a critical consideration for managers pursuing new technology. Radical, competence-destroying technology that is not identity-challenging may be demanding, but when a technology is also identity-challenging management is faced with even greater hurdles. In this situation management must not only acquire the competencies needed to develop and commercialize the new technology. they must also explicitly direct a potentially hazardous identity-change process. More subtle to recognize are identity-challenging technologica changes that are not radical or competence-destroying. Incremental technological advances that are applied to entirely new markets for instance may violate expectations if a firm’s identity is based on a product market.

Not all identity-challenging technologies are worth pursuing. In particular, organisations evaluating growth opportunities based on new technology are in a position different from that of those facing a complete transition from one technological regime to another. For organisations contemplating growth. the dilemma is whether to bypass a potentially attractive identity-challenging technological opportunity in order to maintain a consistent identity, or whether to pursue the technology and embark on the risky endeavour of shifting organisational identity. Although there is obviously no single resolution to this dilemma, this study highlights the importance of recognizing and evaluating the trade-offs associated with it.

For organisations facing a complete transition from one technology to another, the issues are more complex. In these situations, technology is often identity-challenging because it creates an entirely new product category or industry that performs the functions previously performed by a firm’s products. Examples would include word processing software/desktop printers, which made type writers obsolete. Because sales of products based on the original technology will eventually shrink, management is faced with a daunting undertaking. If management decides to pursue the identity-challenging technology it can either adopt a broad identity that encompasses the new product category/industry or simply shift from one product-based identity to another. Alternatively, an orga nisation may decide to define itself in terms of the original technology and pursue new opportunities that leverage that technology. Again, although this study does not suggest which path is best to pursue, it highlights the importance of analysing the identity im plications of technology.
