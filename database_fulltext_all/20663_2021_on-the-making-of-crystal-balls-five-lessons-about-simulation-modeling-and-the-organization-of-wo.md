# On the making of crystal balls: Five lessons about simulation modeling and the organization of work

## A B S T R A C T

Digital models that simulate the dynamics of a system are increasingly used to make predictions about the future. Although modeling has been central to decision-making under conditions of uncertainty across many industries for many years, the COVID-19 pandemic has made the role that models play in prediction and policymaking real for millions of people around the world. Despite the fact that modeling is a process through which experts use data and statistics to make sophisticated guesses, most consumers expect a model’s predictions to be like crystal balls and provide perfect information about what the future will bring. Over the last decade, we have conducted a series of in-depth, longitudinal studies of digital modeling across several industries. From these studies, we share five lessons we have learned about modeling that demonstrate (1) why models are indeed not crystal balls and (2) why, despite their indeterminacy, people tend to treat them as crystal balls anyway. We discuss what each of these lessons can teach us about how to respond to the predictions made by COVID-19 models as well models of other stochastic processes and events about whose futures we wish to know today.

In early March 2020, researchers from Imperial College London presented a computer model to members of the government of the United Kingdom at 10 Downing Street that simulated the rapid spread of the SARS-CoV-2 coronavirus. The model predicted that the United Kingdom's health service would soon be overwhelmed with severe cases of the SARS-CoV-2 coronavirus and might see more than 500.000 deaths if the government did not act. Almost immediately following the presentation of the model. Prime Minister Boris Johnson announced extensive restrictions on people’s activities around the country. The same model, which predicted that without action more than 2.2 million deaths could occur in the United States, was shared with the White House and new guidance on social distancing quickly followed.

One of the many dramatic influences of the Coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic has been an increased visibility of the role that simulation models play in decision-making and policy development. Since March 2020, citizens around the world have learned that models make predictions based on probabilities and assumptions and that those predictions tell us how they might “flatten the curve” through their own behaviors. More astute observers have learned that building models requires estimating many parameters and that most simulations are stochastic: they inject randomness – like flipping a coin to see if a virtual person in one city infects a simulated person in another – to help estimate the range of likely outcomes. Today, many people know model predictions are central to why they are required to shelter in place, wear face coverings, eat outside, and teach their children from home. As Dr. Michael Levy, a Professor of Epidemiology at the University of Pennsylvania suggested, many citizens around the world were beginning to treat COVID-19 simulations as “crystal balls” – prognostications of what outcomes would arise following specific government interventions.<sup>1</sup> As we have seen time and time again in our own research on the use of simulation for prediction, one of the major factors explaining whether those predictions are disregarded or made performative is whether consumers of models believe that the models are real. Put another way, whether simulation models have the power to shape the future depends on how much consumers of their predictions believe that models are crystal balls or not.

Simulations are certainly not crystal balls. What the COVID-19 pandemic is teaching us about the mass deployment of models in the public sphere, however, is that there is wide variance in how people understand, orient to, and use them for every day decision-making. The relative simplicity of surface representations of COVID-19 models that we may have become accustomed to seeing in the news tend to disguise the massive complexity of data collection, mathematics, and simulation that lay beneath. Epidemiological models are particularly difficult to produce because they must approximate viral factors, (e.g., the virus’ transmission and fatality rates), popu lation factors (e.g., size of infected population, prevalence of immunity), and behavioral factors (e.g., rate of interpersonal contact). The predictive abilities of these models rely on how accurately the modeler can estimate these factors, which is particularly difficult in the current context given that the virus is novel and unstudied, and a pandemic of this scale has never been experienced before.

Although the turmoil wrought by the COVID-19 pandemic has brought discussions of simulation models into the public con sciousness, modeling efforts have been ongoing in various communities of practice for a long time. In 2010, we began a coordinated research project to understand how the organization of work affects and is affected by simulation modeling. We chose to study three occupations whose use of simulation modeling has produced major impacts on daily aspects of people's lives: Automotive engineers who use finite element and computational fluid dynamics models to design the safety, comfort, and performance features of the ve hicles we drive (Leonardi. 2012. 2013: Leonardi & Bailey, 2017: Leonardi, Bailey, & Pierce. 2019): Weather scientists who built custom numerical weather prediction models to facilitate operations planning in multiple industries (Barley, 2015); and Urban Planners who use agent-based models that employ Bavesian melding techniques to make decisions about metropolitan land use, transportation, and environmental planning for the regions in which we live (Woo, 2019a, 2019b; Woo & Leonardi, 2018). Over the past nine years we have conducted more than 350 interviews with modelers and their constituents and spent more than 1500 h observing modelers at work. This decade-long program of research has begun to teach us some important lessons about how simulation models are produced and how they come to affect (and not affect) people’s work and lives.

Given that the COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the human dimension of modeling by revealing immediate implications of representing life and death in models, it is tempting to treat COVID-19 modeling as a unique practice. However, all models’ predictions impact the human life and moral decisions, with some leading to more short- or long-term consequences on our everyday lives than others. For example, in auto engineering, models informing vehicle design choices are directly related to traffic fatalities. Vehicles found to have safety defects must be recalled and improved to save lives. In weather science, modeling results inform decisions made in domains such as agriculture, tourism, and insurances against catastrophes, where human safety is a key concern. In urban planning, models guide policy decisions regarding land use and transportation, which bring long-term impacts on the human conditions based on people’s commute patterns, protection or destruction of natural habitats, and changes in air quality.

In what follows, we summarize five key lessons from our research program, which have direct implications for modeling in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic. In fact, as we shall see, society’s attempts to deal with the effects of the pandemic have brought into relief how much more researchers of technology and organization still have to learn in each of these areas. The high health and economic stakes associated with the world’s response to the COVID-19 pandemic makes learning these lessons quickly all the more important. The good news is that if we dedicate ourselves to this effort, we will make serious progress toward expanding our knowledge of simulation models used in other contexts characterized by inherently stochastic processes, such as fire engineering (Dodgson, Gann, & Salter, 2007), architecture (Boland Jr, Lyytinen, & Yoo, 2007), structural engineering (Lobo & Whyte, 2017), mining (Jonsson, Mathiassen, & Holmstrom, ¨ 2018), vehicle design (Carlile, 2004), climate change (Edwards, 2010), drug discovery (Dougherty & Dunne, 2012), and finance (Beunza, 2019).

## Lesson #1:

## Models Don’t Just Represent Reality, They Make It (But it’s Hard to Prove That to People)

“You can forecast a bad storm in a particular place, and people will take out an umbrella and put on a coat. Well, the impact is less for those people but has no effect on the storm. With infectious diseases, people’s precautions — like social distancing — do change the trajectory of the disease.”

– Steven Riley, Professor of Infection Disease Dynamics, Imperial College London<sup>2</sup>

“The virus just isn’t nearly as deadly as we thought it was… Everybody thought it was, but it turned out not to be.”

– “ ” <sup>3</sup>

Models such as those constructed to estimate the diffusion and impact of the SARS-CoV-2 coronavirus generate predictions about what the future is likely to look like if certain natural or planned action transpires. For this reason, most people think that models merely represent possible future states. But a rich line of research on the performativity of modeling has suggested that models can make the very futures they predict by leading to behaviors that shape reality in line with the model’s predictions (Beunza & Ferraro, 2019: Callon. 1998: Garud & Gehman. 2019: MacKenzie. 2006). In this sense. models are not merely representational. but they are performative. MacKenzie's (2006) study of the creation and diffusion of the Black-Scholes model of options pricing – a model built to identify inefficiencies that could be exploited through arbitrage trading – showed that although predictions of the model were initially inaccurate when academics began to use it in the early 1970s, its adoption by traders during the 1980s and 1990s changed their behaviors in ways that, over time, brought options pricing into alignment with the model’s predictions. A model’s performativity can also work in the opposite way. When a model predicts an outcome that people do not want, they can change their behavior to construct a reality that defies a model’s predictions. Models that incite behavior that undermines their very predictions are considered coun terperformative (Mackenzie & Bamforth, 2018).

In the case of the COVID-19 pandemic, many of the early models of which the public learned – those about how fast the virus would spread, whether the number of infected individuals would outstrip the capacity of hospitals to treat them, and how many people would die if no actions were taken – were intended to be counterperformative. By all indications, activities such as sheltering in place, socia distancing, and mask-wearing, which were taken to assure that the model’s predicted effects would not materialize, were largely successful in most countries—at least initially. But for a large portion of the population, the fact that a model’s predictions led to behaviors that stopped the model’s predictions from coming true was not strong evidence of successful mitigation. In short, this very objective (i.e., counterperformativity) made it hard for people to assess whether the model was accurate and whether those behaviors “mattered.” Tucker Carlson, quoted above, for example, took news that the SARS-CoV-2 coronavirus’ spread was “not as bad as the model predicted" as evidence that the model was wrong, rather than that behaviors counterperformative to the model's predictions helped to assure that those predictions did not come true.

Our research has shown that when a model engenders behaviors that make it performative, people typically do not believe the model produced their behavior, but rather that the model was “accurate” in representing a future that would have happened absent the model’s prediction. This lack of recognition of the performative effects of the model was not typically problematic across our three occupations when the outcomes that occurred were desirable. We observed that it was much more difficult for people to attribute counterperformativity to a model when the behaviors the model predicted did not arise. For example, in our study of Urban Planning, community stakeholder groups who were presented with model predictions of increased traffic and pollution based on potentia changes to zoning ordinances often resisted the changes vehemently to prevent the models’ simulated future from becoming reality. But those same stakeholder groups would also often question whether such models were necessary in decision-making because their predictions (of increased traffic and pollution) rarely came true. Stakeholder groups failed to see that their lobbying behaviors were a direct response to the models purposively designed to prevent the models’ outcomes.

It is easy to see why it is difficult to think counterperformatively. Counterperformativity cannot be counterfactually reconciled. If the model predicted an increase in traffic or pollution that did not occur (because the model predicted it), individuals who ask themselves, “Would pollution not have increased even if the model predicted it and we did not fight for zoning changes?” would have no way of knowing. Similarly, people who might ask “would the SARS-CoV-2 Coronavirus have propagated any faster if people like me had worn masks?” could ask the counterfactual question but will never have data to explore the alternative. And, consequently, those people (who may well listen to good data) will feel justified in their decisions. Our research has shown that it is difficult to convince people that models are performative, and even more difficult to convince them that they might be counterperformative, but that doing so may be key to shaping the futures we want.

Clearly, much more research is needed on how models perform or counterperform and how people interact with them over time. To explore these issues, researchers might ask questions such as: Under what conditions do models become performative and under what conditions do they fail to have performative effects? How can those who make decisions based on a model’s predictions convince people that the counterperformative effects that occurred were enabled because of behaviors taken to actively counter a model’s predictions? How do people make attributions about both the performative and counterperformative effects of models (in other words, why do they “credit” or “blame” certain models, people, or institutions for the outcomes that eventually unfold)?

Lesson #2

A “Model” is Rarely One Model, But A Statistical Estimation of Many Models

“The IHME model is an odd duck in the pool of mathematical models. I fear the White House is looking for data that tells them a story they want to hear. and so they look to the model with the lowest proiection of death."

– Gregg Gonsalves, Professor of Epidemiology, Yale School of Medicine<sup>4</sup>.

Models are based on assumptions about the dynamics that govern complex systems. Those assumptions can be derived from theory, past observational data, or a combination of both. Two modelers can use widely divergent assumptions (in how to model something, in what the model’s parameters are, and in how best to estimate the model’s effects) to examine the same process. One modeler may also build several models, with competing assumptions, or perhaps based on different data sources, to explore the boundaries of a certain issue. Although the existence of multiple and sometimes conflicting models might scare the uninitiated, to modelers this is a normal part of work. Because we can never know in advance which assumptions of any given prediction are correct – even assumptions based on past data, because many past dynamics are not guaranteed to manifest themselves in the future – modelers typically use statistical techniques to create an overarching model characterizing the (sometimes weighted) average of the various models they have built. This aggregation compounds as modelers integrate the results of multiple overarching models of construct ensembles that present averages of averages.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control (CDC) has made it standard practice to aggregate the pre dictions of models submitted by multiple research groups, which are themselves statistical aggregations of many models and model runs. For example, Fig. 1 illustrates a cumulative death forecast for U.S. for the week of July 23, 2020, based on 26 individual nationa forecasts (left panel) and an average of these forecasts called an ensemble model (right panel). The “IHME model” to which Professor Gonsalves referred in the epigraph to this section is a model built by the University of Washington’s Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation. It is one of the 26 individual models that feed into the CDC’s ensemble forecast.

Despite the statistical variability inherent in predictions made by aggregated models, modelers and model consumers often pick models that produce the results they like and ignore others. For example, in our research on automotive design, we found that automotive engineers were divided into a number of occupationally-specific specialties that focused on different aspects of a vehicle’s performance – such as crashworthiness, ride and handling, fuel economy, noise and vibration, etc. These engineers would build models to predict how certain design parameters would affect performance in their area of interest. They would do so by building as many as 200–300 models, each with slightly different assumptions to test the bounds of their design ideas. Although standard modeling practice would suggest these engineers should find a statistical average of their runs, they often chose to present specific single models because they had hunches that they were more accurate, that the chosen model told the story they wanted, or because the model was the easiest to interpret. Engineers from all the different specialties would then combine their chosen models into one ensemble model that represented a compromise between each occupation’s favorite models. As one engineer lamented, “We build a suboptimal vehicle because we optimize the vehicle’s subsystems.”

In the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, this lesson shows us that even the individual models produced by the 26 research groups submitting to the CDC may not have been chosen because they are the “best” model, but for a variety of reasons that are possibly seen as organizationally appropriate or politically motivated. And, which of those 26 models policymakers choose to adhere to and make performative (or counterperformative) can have major consequences. Recognizing that all models are aggregations of models should focus researchers’ attention on how and why models are combined in certain ways, how they are chosen, and how models come to be put into play with one another in the first place. Lessons #3 and #4 below derive from this insight and focus our attention on the organization of work that feeds data and assumptions into models, and the inherent stochasticity of individual and aggregated models, respectively.

To explore these issues in more depth. researchers might ask questions such as: What are the socio-political processes through which some data are given priority over others when deciding what to include in a model? How do certain data streams or models come to be defined as “best” for the task at hand? Where should decision authority rest for determining how data aggregation should occur, or whether one sole data source should be used instead?

Lesson #3:

Models Reflect the Way That Work is Organized to Produce Them

“It’s as if somebody looked over my shoulder when I was half way through putting the work together and took a picture and put the results out there.”

– Justin Lessler, Professor of Public Health, Johns Hopkins University<sup>5</sup>

As alluded to above, simulation modeling is rarely the work of a single expert or organization. Treating modeling results as such can mislead decision-makers and stakeholders, as painfully experienced by Lessler. In early May 2020, the New York Times reported that Lessler’s COVID-19 forecast drafts had appeared in an internal White House document, and that his models predicted the United States would reach a rate of 3000 deaths per day by June first. Lessler’s results caused a backlash due to their shockingly high predictions, and he had to clarify that the published report only displayed one-third of the scenarios he was planning to analyze and present to his colleagues at FEMA (Federal Emergency Management Agency) as part of their usual practice of sharing “work in progress” before producing final projections.

Effective use and application of modeling requires organizational mechanisms to coordinate a multitude of models using differing data, methods, and assumptions about reality. The CDC emphasizes this process by stating “it is important to bring these forecasts together [over 25 teams’ projections of COVID-19 deaths and hospitalizations] to help understand how they compare with each other” (Forecasts of Total Death, n.d.). Similarly, the World Health Organizations (WHO) hosts regular calls among COVID-19 modelers to help reduce discrepancies between models (Enserink & Kupferschmidt, 2020). Failing to understand how work is organized around modeling misses the very thing that makes models a useful tool for organizations: the process in which various internal and external actors engage in heterogenous construction of their uncertain future.

The COVID-19 pandemic has made the importance of organizing work around models more visible than ever, but it is neither new nor unique to this context. In Urban Planning, for example, planners have used simulation models for decades to project urban growth patterns and guide policy decisions. Once planners developed their macro model by integrating various constituent models (e.g., transportation, land use, and environmental), their work was organized as a series of stakeholder deliberations intended to compare their modeling results with their partner agencies’ projections as well as with their constituencies’ vision for the region’s future. After each round of deliberation, planners' initial models were recalibrated and results reimagined, and stakeholder feedback informed key decisions, such as which of their forecast scenarios should be more or less emphasized in policy discussions. The final projection used for their long-term regional plan was a result of the coordinating efforts to integrate different types of knowledge and information among urban planners, public information officers, stakeholders, and policymakers. As this example showed, the first model is never the last model. And, if you were to take any of these proto-models and treat it as the final model, it would not only be inaccurate, but it would fail to convey information essential for the work of planners.

Thus, studying simulation modeling should necessarily involve examining the integral roles that the organization of work and information plays in shaping models’ projections. Researchers may ask: what organizational and knowledge infrastructures exist to support the development of an ensemble forecast, and how? How does the way work gets organized around modeling reflect and serve different organizational values, interests, and goals—and what are their consequences? Exploring such questions will uncover the ongoing and/or emergent organizational practices through which information in various forms (e.g., abstract, quantified, or visual ized) “travels” to be compared, converted, and given authority as a meaningful projection of the future (Day, Lury, & Wakeford, 2014; Mazmanian & Beckman, 2018).

## Lesson #4:

## Models are Stochastic, But People are Determinists

“Don’t obsess about the numbers because they will change... All models lie, but some are more useful than others.”

– Ross E.G. Upshur, Professor of Public Health, University of Toronto<sup>6</sup>

This advice, from the head of clinical public health at University of Toronto, appeared in an article designed to help the public learn how to interpret COVID-19 prediction models. That this quote might appear nonsensical at first glance characterizes a mentality we have seen among many of the participants in our field studies of modeling practice. Interpreting forecast models involves accepting that models are stochastic – inherently characterized by uncertainty, dependency upon initial conditions, and the potential for performative impacts on a systems behavior (Fine, 2007). Modelers often encounter challenges as they share their forecasts with audiences who largely operate under deterministic ontologies - expecting predictions to characterize the future with precision and certainty (Silver, 2012; Tetlock, 2005; Tetlock & Gardner, 2015). We agree with MacKenzie and Bamforth (2018) that people are not “model dopes" who think that everything that happens in models will happen outside of them. But our own research has shown time and time again that most consumers of the predictions made by simulation models orient to models as though the models are making deterministic predictions. And, it turns out, most people feel more comfortable operating in a deterministic ontology because probabilistic ontologies are simply much harder to understand. Kahneman and Tversky won a Nobel prize for showing that humans exhibit extreme difficulties acting based on probabilistic information (e.g. Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). These ontological divides create situations where audiences are unable to mobilize forecast results in decision-making or, potentially more detrimentally, can lead audiences to erroneously assume that model outputs are determinate when they are not.

We have seen these struggles in our own research as we observed modelers try to help their audiences understand and interpret their forecasts. For example, in the case of weather forecasters, one team who produced a model to predict the potential of lightning strikes on the wide-open areas of a military test range spoke at length about how to communicate their forecasts in a way that could support effective decision-making by test planners. The dire nature of the forecasted outcomes (i.e., a lightning striking a team in the field) made it difficult for modelers to convince planners to use the models in their work. Planners operated in a world that required certainty, and their realization that the forecast models could, at best, provide probabilities of lightning strikes paralyzed them from using forecasts in the planning process.

Organizational scholars have studied the challenges of communicating across epistemic boundaries, recognizing that differing bases of meanings can hinder individuals’ capacity to share knowledge (Barley, Leonardi, & Bailey, 2012; Bechky, 2003; Carlile, 2004; Levina & Vaast, 2008). But, the boundaries inherent in communicating model results are particularly wide – often involving varying ontologies for assessing what counts as “objective" or “truth" (Collins & Evans, 2002: Daston & Galison, 2007: Knorr-Cetina, 1999). Thus, there is a need for organizational scholars to explore how organizations manage the challenges modelers face as they seek to communicate their results, and the practices by which organizations come to effectively integrate probabilistic models into deter ministic decision-making practices.

Recognizing these epistemic divides also suggests implications for the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. Organizational scholars are positioned to provide theory-driven explanations for why model outputs are so difficult for decision-makers to interpret, and to reveal the detrimental outcomes associated with misinterpreting forecast models through a deterministic frame. A key area for research will involve examining how to communicate about models in a way that encourages audiences to adopt stochastic frames, as well as when/how these ontological divides negatively impact policy-making debates and decisions. In doing so, scholars can seek to understand how communication strategies for sharing modeling results may differ based on the levels of uncertainty and risks of a given phenomenon being modeled. When both uncertainty and perceived risks are high, stochastic frames might not be appealing to or easily accepted by the audience, whereas less uncertain or less risky situations may allow room for educational opportunities for deterministic audiences to adopt stochastic approaches. For instance, urban planning can be characterized with high uncertainty (i.e., numerous unknown factors that could change the future urban growth) but low risk (i.e., policy decisions’ long-term consequences such as climate change). In this context, planners had extensive stakeholder engagement processes in which their stochastic frames could be explained through public workshops and integrated with their stakeholders’ deterministic vision through rounds of de liberations. Comparatively, the COVID-19 pandemic represents a context with high uncertainty and high immediate risk, where interpreting results ought to happen much more expeditiously. Thus, communicative efforts need to focus on preparatory work for setting proper expectations among deterministic audiences, while avoiding sharing modeling results prematurely.

Research questions derived from this lesson can include. but are not limited to: What are the processes by which simulation models transition between the technical experts producing them and the audiences that seek to apply them? What are the conditions under which audiences come to faithfully interpret the stochastic implications of forecast models? And, how does the nature of a decisionmaking context influence the capacity for actors to be able to rely upon simulation models to inform their actions?

Lesson #5:

How People Trust a Model Depends on How Distant They Are From It

“I have a feeling that a lot of the numbers that are being said in some areas are just bigger than they’re going to be [...] I don’t believe you need 40,000 or 30,000 ventilators. You know, you go into major hospitals, sometimes they’ll have two ventilators, and now all of a sudden they’re saying, ‘Can we order 30,000 ventilators?’”<sup>7</sup>

– Former U.S. President Donald Trump

Modeling is constrained by current knowledge and information availability, which is inevitably incomplete and constantly evolving. Even with the most comprehensive and rigorous data sets, the future will look different from models’ projections largely because of the decisions made and actions taken to shape or alter the anticipated future (as discussed in Lesson #1). As modeling became a topic of public and political interest during the COVID-19 pandemic, modelers have faced criticisms for their work seeming to be a hoax, threat, or conspiracy by audiences who take the modeling results at face value as either right or wrong and question modeling itself as a legitimate way to understand reality and make predictions of the future.

As epidemiologists say, modeling a pandemic is not really about a pandemic or predicting its consequences; it is about helping people think generally and differently about how infections might spread over time (Poisot, 2020). Similarly, modeling the future of organizations and their decisions is not about the projected future, but it is about managing the ways in which organizational leaders and stakeholders currently think about their actions and their potential implications in new ways. Therefore, it is key to understand how modelers work to establish that their models are legitimate and trustworthy such that the models can be brought into important decision-making discussions

When looking across our three occupations, we found that modelers had to engage in at least three key practices to establish the trustworthiness of their models: (1) establishing confidence in the model, (2) demonstrating the model’s usefulness, and (3) engaging the model’s critics. Yet, to be successful at establishing trust in their models, modelers in the three occupations had to enact these three practices differently based on the level of sophistication of their stakeholder’s understanding of the modeling process.

Stakeholders in the urban planning case (local politicians, community groups) had very little knowledge about modeling. Thus, to establish confidence in their model, they had to first demonstrate to stakeholders that using simulation modeling techniques was the best way to answer the planning questions that were so important to stakeholders. Weather scientists, by contrast, worked with stakeholders who already understood that modeling was a useful process. Therefore, their work to establish credibility was to demonstrate just how accurate their models were. In automotive design, engineers worked with stakeholder extremely skilled in modeling themselves. Their main approach to establishing confidence in their models was to demonstrate to others that the model they picked as the “best” model was indeed the best model among all the runs they did.

A second step to establishing trust in models was to demonstrate a model’s usefulness. Here again, members of the occupations we studied varied in the distance their stakeholders had to the modeling process. Urban planners simplified their models and only pro vided summaries of complex modeling outputs in order to prevent stakeholders from feeling overwhelmed by a model’s sophistication. Weather scientists translated their models by explaining how the model and its results would resonate with the existing understandings and expectations of their stakeholders. And automotive engineers transformed stakeholders’ understandings of their models by reframing the objectives of the modeling process to make their models seem relevant and their conclusions seem appropriate given a

stakeholder group’s own goals.

A final step to establishing trust was to engage a model’s critics early and continuously so as to assuage their concerns. Urban planners did this by attempting to neutralize some stakeholders’ antipathy toward their models by revealing the model’s complexity to drown out alternative opinions. Weather scientists engaged critics by attempting to obscure the model’s complexity so as to focus on key takeaways of mutual concern. Doing so helped them to establish partnership with stakeholder and align their interests with those of the modelers. Finally, automotive engineers attempted to disarm their stakeholder critics by displaying a model’s complexity in ways to show that their particular model could not be ignored.

To explore how people come to trust models in various ways, it seems important to ask some of the following research questions: How do people who have varying levels of distance from/familiarity with the modeling enterprise come to a working understanding about whether the model is robust and useful? What levels of data and visualization abstraction are necessary to convince different kinds of consumers that a model is trustworthy? Is trust in a model itself as important as trust in the organizations or people who produced the model (and if so, how is such trust developed or shattered)?

## 1. Conclusion

“We’re building simplified representations of reality. Models are not crystal balls”.

– Neil Ferguson, Professor of Epidemiology, Imperial College London<sup>8</sup>

Models, when done right, combine the best data available with state-of-the-art mathematical techniques to simulate the dynamics of a system based on parameters established by the most appropriate assumptions. In short, models are guesses. It is precisely because of their uncertainty that models cannot exist without an organization of work behind them that establishes a sense of assuredness that, despite the fact that we are guessing, there is likely something to those guesses. Models are certainly not crystal balls. But the lessons we have learned studying the production, use, and effects of simulation models over the past decade have taught us that organizations do a great deal of work to make sure that they are able to provide relatively reliable projections of the future. Sometimes that work is aimed at making a model’s predictions more certain than they are, sometimes it is aimed at making them seem less certain. Other times, that work helps make a model’s predictions to come true, other times it simply helps a model to work.

Herein lies a real opportunity for theorists of information and organizing. Organizational scholars are positioned to build theory to help us better understand the sociomaterial processes by which models are assembled, produced, communicated, and acted upon. Past research, including our own, has revealed the sheer complexity of these practices but, as we hope the research questions provided in each section above suggest, there is much more work to be done to understand when and how models become a meaningful part of our work—especially in novel circumstances with high uncertainty and fast-changing implications on human and organizational be haviors, which make visibility and accountability of modeling work very high. Given the increasing role of modeling in policy decisionmaking, such theories stand to offer an intricate understanding of the conditions under which models become performative, coun terperformative, or simply ignored. It would be crucial to explore our suggested research questions not only from the perspectives of organizations and modeling experts (with varying assumptions) but also from stakeholders who bring varying degrees of knowledge about modeling into the context of work.

Attending to the complexity of modeling practice also holds implications for practitioners and policy makers. Our research on modeling work demonstrates some the dangers of underestimating the social processes surrounding the production, interpretation, and communication of models. Simulation models are designed to simplify complex systems into a format that can inform action. But, the benefits of this simplicity arrive with new challenges. As authorities increasingly integrate models into their work, it will be ever more important to remain vigilant to the dangers of interpreting model outputs without a critical attention to their underlying complexity and the ways in which the complexity gets shared with different audiences. Managers who oversee modelers' work as well as policy makers who draw on modeling results will benefit from considering the following insights based on our five lessons: they can question the conditions under which models were produced: build infrastructures to promote collaborative work with other experts and stakeholders (e.g., comparing and integrating models); avoid deterministic language when communicating about model impli cations and help their audience to build capacity to adopt stochastic frames; and, devote attention to continued and open interaction with publics who may or may not accurately connect model implications with their realities.

In conclusion, we urge researchers of technology and organization to begin to study in earnest how models become part of the social fabric of our lives because, as the COVID-19 pandemic has begun to make clear, the models we make, make our world in the ways we respond to them.
