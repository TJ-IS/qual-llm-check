## A B S T R A C T

Advances in digitalization have changed our apprehension of technology from discrete devices and application software as bounded artifacts, to dynamically evolving social-material entan glements in Digitalized Work Arrangements (DWA). This development makes studying DWAs increasingly difficult and challenges us to advance our methods that define how we can study, observe, and conceptualize DWAs. In this essay, we draw on the mathematical-logical formalism of the Laws of Form (LoF) (Spencer-Brown, 1969) to analyze how six illustrative IS studies conceptualize the social and the material to arrive at distinct perspectives on DWAs. Our analysis reveals three archetypes that capture these studies’ conceptualizations and that inform a dis cussion of how IS research can extend qualitative methods bevond those six works. We offer two contributions. First, we provide novel insights and explanations to key conceptualizations in the study of DWAs. Second, we present the LoF as a pre-ontological and pre-theoretical formalism that enables commensurability of methods and development of novel qualitative empirical methods. Specifically, we demonstrate how the formalism helps articulating the distinctions we draw to refine our object of study and to critically examine and reconstruct other researchers reasoning.

## 1. Introduction

A principal task of qualitative empirical research in the field of information systems is to qualify our relationship with technology and social organization. Such foundational insights support the field’s mission to ideate, discover or rationalize ideas, phenomena or mechanisms, and to capture them in novel concepts that help us refine, extend, or alter our views of the world. It is thus a precondition, and raison d’ˆetre, of qualitative investigation to bring something new into the world—not to quantify the known, but to qualify the unknown.

Recent advances in technology, the persistent pace of digitalization, and the increasing interconnectedness between the social and digital have, however, outpaced the task of refining qualitative methods (Baskerville, Myers, & Yoo, 2020; Cecez-Kecmanovic, Galliers, Henfridsson, Newell, & Vidgen, 2014; Yoo, 2010). While ubiquitous, distributed, and embedded technologies have been acknowledged and researched for decades (Weiser. 1991. 1993). it is only now that they exhibit the capacity to constantly reshape the technological augmentation of our surroundings. Fast-paced innovations ranging from cyber-physical systems found in vehicles to smart home robots (Lee, 2008; Weber, 2017; Wolf, 2007; Wunderlich, Veit, & Sarker, 2019), and, on a larger scale, to smart cities, suggest that conditions for living and working are increasingly becoming interwoven and fragmented (Daskalaki & Kokkinidis, 2017; Khatoun & Zeadally, 2016; Zheng, Capra, Wolfson, & Yang, 2014). As computing becomes more pervasive, individuals experience it as often invisible and otherwise imperceptible. We are already encountering adaptive digital environments, such as the ones we experience with smart home speakers, from which functions, applications or information objects arise or emerge while we use them. This also holds true for our Digitalized Work Arrangements (DWAs) that now consist of the traditional social, offline, analogous, “experiential dimension” vis-\`a-vis a “digital dimension” including apps, video calls, biometric logins and many more (Yoo, 2010). As DWAs evolve, we need to refine our research methods as reproducible and comprehensive means that help observe and describe how we are connected to this digita dimension.

Digitalization has fundamentally changed the nature of the interplay between us and technology. For instance, it has induced altered meanings and patterns of social interactions. including the emergence of new routines and forms of attachment, unknown emotional responses, new forms of appropriation and use, and knowledge exchanges (Yoo, 2010, p. 216). Yet, the adaptability of digital technologies—understood as the possibility to actively adapt our technology, and the technology’s capability to constantly adapt independently from us—insistently calls into question what we still can perceive and observe from the digital dimension. Put differently, it puts into question how we can define the boundaries of what we have traditionally called the IT artifact. The IT artifact—a bounded, delimitable device, application software and the like, that possesses a material notion—is now enmeshed in DWAs as the digital is becoming entangled in our work in countless ways. Many authors have thus extended the customary conception and speak of digital artifacts that “are intentionally incomplete and perpetually in the making”, described as “both an opportunity and a problem” (Kallinikos, Aaltonen, & Marton, 2013; citing Garud, Jain, & Tuertscher, 2008; Zittrain, 2008).

The challenge is how to conceptualize and communicate what we observe as well as how we observe in such a way that we do justice to new, dynamic and enmeshed contexts while providing insights that can be understood and reconstructed. Traditionally, IT artifacts were relatively easy to describe, using a common terminology of specifications and functions (Orlikowski & Jacono. 2001). Given the complex and adaptable nature of modern, adaptive digital environments whose constituents change in-use as unending social network threads that combine technical aspects as well as human rationalizations, it has become infeasible to capture and conceptualize them through traditional means (Kallinikos et al., 2013). We cannot be sure that the ‘object’ we have taken for granted in a previous observation is still of the same kind when observing it a second time. In fact, when researching DWAs. we know that we cannot perceive such constituents in their actual form, because the way we observe our object of study and the distinctions we draw to configure our measurements create a filter (or apparatus) between us and our object of study that only lets us see incomplete re productions of what is actually happening (Østerlund, Crowston, & Jackson, 2020).

Arguably, what we need is a formal assistance to systematically advance how we conceptualize and capture dynamic, embedded, and entangled constituents of DWAs, while allowing for zooming-in and zooming-out (Nicolini, 2009, 2013), and for tracking the constituents’ change over time. Specifically, we need methods that are not only able to describe object-like constituents, but also account for the distinctions we draw a priori to our observation and conceptualization processes. The objective is to avoid arbitrariness of interpretations from insufficiently and potentially ill-defined observations and to allow for balancing subjectivity and (rigorous) formalism at the same time

Put differently: How can we account for the dynamism of our object of study during our research of DWAs? How can we articulate our method and resulting findings so they are open for analysis, critique, reproduction and use by other researchers? How can we safeguard and extend our qualitative methods to keep up with the pace of technological development, the exploding variety of our relations with technology, and the growing diversity of possible perspectives? Crystalized into one overarching research question, we ask: How can researchers articulate distinct possibilities for conceptualizations that drive their inquiry into digitalized work arrangements?

We offer a formalism to capture the distinctions researchers draw when they conceptualize the constituents of entanglements in DWAs. We illustrate this by focusing on one prominent distinction in the IS literature, namely that of social and material aspects entangled in DWAs. The formalism we propose is based on Spencer-Brown, 1969 Laws of Form (LoF). It provides a linguistic device for IS researchers to describe their observations while taking into account the complex and adaptive nature of DWAs. As a pre-ontological and pre-theoretical formalism for research methods, it is agnostic of the ontological and theoretical perspectives adopted at later stages of research. As such. it allows us to capture the assumptions, distinctions. and conceptions we make a priori to our investigations, and to critically review them for the ensuing method design.

Our essay makes two key contributions. First, we provide novel insights and explanations to key conceptualizations in the study of DWAs. Second, in a broader context, we present the LoF as a pre-ontological and pre-theoretical formalism that enables commen surability of methods and the development of novel qualitative empirical methods

The essay is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review past literature on the basic assumptions of empirical research on IT artifacts and digitalized work. In Section 3, we present the notation of the LoF which—while used in various other disciplines—we introduce for the first time to the IS field. In Section 4, we analyze six DWA studies using LoF to illustrate how current IS research assumes different points of view and focal points on DWAs and derive three archetypical forms in the discourse of sociomateriality, We conclude in Section 5 with a discussion of the contributions for the study of DWAs and for the use of LoF in general.

## 2. Digitalized work arrangement research

Theorizing and conceptualizing the IT artifact is arguably at the center of the IS field.<sup>1</sup> However, as technologies evolve into something dynamic and adaptive, our philosophical, epistemological, and ontological stances need to change accordingly (March & Smith, 1995; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001; Weber, 2003; Akhlaghpour, Wu, Lapointe, & Pinsonneault, 2013; Carlile, Nicolini, Langley, & Tsoukas, 2013; Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014; Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2020; Riemer & Johnston, 2017; Sarker, Chatterjee, Xiao, & Elbanna, 2019). Today, IS scholars not only apply the term ‘IT artifact,’ whose early definitions refer to IT devices and software systems, but increasingly use expressions such as “ecosystems of digital content” or “sociomaterial assemblages.” This change in terminology is reflective of a shift towards the distributed nature of information systems and infrastructures that includes, among other things, distributed databases, wikis, social networking platforms, sensor networks or smart consumer applications. As a term, DWAs thus combine the social dimension of today’s complex workplace settings with the digital dimension, featuring digital devices and digitally manifested information objects. In DWAs, as prior research has argued, the digital and the experiential, the material and the social are entangled in such ways that we require novel perspectives to conceptualize their constituents and the related empirica phenomena (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014). With the rapid digitalization, concerns exist in IS research about the potential loss of the IT artifact as a symbol for technology as it “disappears”—subsumed into a social and material entanglement (Baskerville, 2012; Sarker et al., 2019).<sup>2</sup> Before the term sociomateriality was coined, the notion of entanglement was formulated as follows:

“We must theorize about the meanings, capabilities, and uses of IT artifacts, their multiple, emergent, and dynamic properties, as well as the recursive transformations occurring in the various social worlds in which they are embedded. We believe that the lack of theories about IT artifacts, the ways in which they emerge and evolve over time, and how they become interdependent with socio-economic contexts and practices, are key unresolved issues for our field and ones that will become even more problematic in these dynamic and innovative times” (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001, p. 133).

Subsequently, IS scholars point towards two principal constituents for observing artifacts: their material and their social nature (Sarker et al., 2019). This relationship between the material and the social underpins our field’s understanding and the evolution of our conceptions from singular IT artifacts to understanding the digital as entangled in our work arrangements. Indeed. the social-material relationship is so fundamentally profound that “it makes little sense to talk about one without talking about the other” (Leonardi, 2013, p. 60). Thus, we develop conceptions that objectify<sup>3</sup> constituents of the entanglement in order to make them observable or measurable. Such constituents have been framed, for instance, as digitally manifested information objects that are “increasingly editable, interactive, reprogrammable, and distributable” and as a part of “constantly shifting ecosystems” (Kallinikos et al., 2013, p. 357). Search engines, for example, demonstrate each of the aforementioned characteristics, as evident in results that “are constantly in the making… [they] are embedded into webs of technical and organizational relations that further reinforce their instability and ambivalent ontology” (ibid., p. 366). In an elegant argument vouching for the importance of focusing on objectified conceptions in the face of technological advances, Kal linikos et al. (2013) state:

“The dynamic character of digital objects, the fluid nature of digital content, and the shifting architecture of relations into which they are embedded challenge these paramount principles [of stability and recurrent outcomes]; they put a premium on drift rather than stability and control, change and flexibility management rather than accountability, while valorizing expression rather than restraint. (…) [The] conceptualization of digital artifacts and operations as objects singles out the intersection between technology and human practices as the appropriate level of analysis” (ibid., p. 367; emphasis added).

This quote underscores the problem of observing, conceptualizing, and measuring social-material entanglements in DWAs today. More fundamentally, it can be argued that our “infra-language” (Latour, 2005) is not yet sufficiently developed to examine the sociomaterial, necessitating a separation of the social and the material (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014). Authors thus often approach these entanglements through distinctive constituents as an imagined condensation point (Simon, 1969) between the material and the social. Consequently, it is not surprising that IS research has witnessed a proliferation of suggested stances, constructs, research methods, and types of contributions, and ways in which the entanglement of the social and material can be researched, observed, and described. In fact, distinct ontological positions have emerged in IS research, such as agential and critical realism (Barad, 2003; Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014; Leonardi, 2013; Mutch, 2013; Scott & Orlikowski, 2013), stemming from individually constructed re lationships with our object of research based on observations and conceptualizations colored by our experiences and purposes. What is missing is a common language that allows us to describe how we conceptualize the phenomenon prior to our assumed ontologies and theories.

The two current challenges—to advance our knowledge about digitalization as object of study and to advance our methods for observing digitalization—are thus inextricably related. Arriving at a formalism might allow us to systematically differentiate between ontologically disparate notions of DWAs, open up novel possibilities for comparison, and inform the design of novel methods. We next introduce a formalism to address these challenges.

## 3. Laws of Form

The LoF, drawn from the works of George Spencer-Brown, 1969, provide a combinatorial mathematical system of notations and rules: the calculus of indications. The LoF have found application in diverse fields, including mathematics, engineering, sociology, psychology, and others. It follows the tradition of symbolic logic as represented by scholars, such as Boole, Carroll, Cantor, Peirce, Russell, Wittgenstein, and more (Kauffman, 2017). Its notation and basic assumptions are close to the mathematics of Peirce (Kauffman, 2001).

## 3.1. Step 1: Draw a distinction

Central to the thesis of the LoF is the concept of distinction. It proposes that we, as researchers, start our thought processes by drawing distinctions that are of significance to us and with a certain purpose in mind.<sup>4</sup> Drawing a distinction simply means that we recognize a difference, or change, which creates a borderline between two things, sides, or time frames. Distinctions differentiate “something” from “something else,” which requires a conscious cognitive effort. In the LoF, drawing a distinction is the starting point for formalizing observations and the basis for all further conceptualizations to be developed (referred to as calculus). This focus on distinctions is different from other systems in mathematics or logics that usually define a set of axioms (e.g., an ‘object X,’ a welldefined ‘set A’ or ‘all natural numbers’) before any calculations can take place. Drawing a distinction is the starting point in LoF; and action researchers have to willingly submit to as it requires an ongoing effort on their part. This seemingly small difference that the LoF makes, entails a fundamental unlearning and reconsideration in our observations, in that we as observers do not look for objects, matter, substance or content, but for distinctions (Kauffman, 2017; Lau, 2005; Luhmann, 1998).

The notation for indicating distinctions is a simple one—a single sign, called the mark. The mark formalizes a distinction made by: (1) marking the inside, i.e., what is included, (2) marking the outside, i.e., the context in which our distinction exists, and (3) establishing a boundary between a marked and an unmarked side, indicating where the boundary needs to be crossed in order to reach the other side. “Placing-the-mark” is one of the most fundamental operations a researcher can perform, indicating that a researcher has reason to believe that the distinction is observable. Indicating a distinction creates a form, as is illustrated in Fig. 1.

In a hypothetical scenario, a researcher (Peter) can indicate his preference for observing social aspects of technology; he is aware that the social’ is expressed through behaviors, practices and the like: and he knows how to measure or describe what he sees (Fig. 2). What is outside the mark for him is an ‘unmarked state’; it is left blank and not named. By drawing this distinction, he deliberately has chosen to observe and conceptualize the ‘social’—without clearly defining what the ‘social’ really is.

At the same time, indicating a distinction using the LoF hides him as the observer and hides his purpose for drawing the distinction (Baecker, 2006, p. 124). Since the form defines and contains the space, or context, in which the distinction is being drawn, it makes the definition of the form self-referential in the sense that no additional boundary conditions are required for its definition

In our scenario. Peter discusses his work with his colleague Mary. He tells her about how he thinks that social aspects of IS are different from their ‘material’ side in the context of DWAs and explicates his thinking using the LoF in Fig. 3. Mary understands that there are two marked states; the rest remaining unmarked. She asks him further questions, such as how he studies each of these, and what their relationship is.

The form—which indicates a distinction drawn by an observer (Peter)—is observed by another observer (Mary), who needs to focus on the form as well as on the act of observing by reflecting on how variances in observing allow her to arrive at the form presented by Peter. As a matter of fact, what Mary thinks is ‘social’ or ‘material’ might be different from what Peter thinks. As a next step, Mary asks further questions.

In our scenario, another colleague, Paul, joins the conversation. Thus, the form allows us to express our indications in context, which is not necessarily fixed, but might be reflexive, as argued by Paul. In fact, it might invoke further contexts that, as unmarked states, hold indeterminacies and thus necessitate additional re-entries—which, in turn, will help to refine the phenomenon expressed by the form (Fig. 7).

## 3.3. Step 3: Considering oscillations and identity.

The possibility for re-entry has implications for the logics of observing. Re-entry is a time-consuming act for a researcher, in which an indication is reconsidered, or re-enacted, by re-entering the distinction into the distinction. As Fig. 8 shows, an indicated a on the right-hand side is identifiable and able to produce the left-hand side of the equation only if it is the result of numerous re-entries, or reiterations of its distinctions. In fact, for a to assume an identity, it has to oscillate between repeated, and varying, observations

For example, in our scenario, Paul’s observation that agencies reconfigure social and material aspects of work arrangements has been termed as ‘imbrication.’ An imbrication is identifiable, i.e., it assumes an identity, whenever we see agencies reconfiguring (imbricating) the social and the material. Thus, imbrication is a re-entry process, as noted in Fig. 9.

In Spencer-Brown’s logic, an a is an a (identity) only when it is distinct from something else (indication) which it is not—but the existence of the unmarked state (context) is an existential prerequisite for a’s existence.<sup>6</sup>

We need to emphasize that the distinction does not just represent that a has been existing (in the sense of ‘before’), but that the distinction causes a’s existence in the first place, i.e., by drawing the distinction. For our scenario, this means that not only can Paul now observe imbrications happening repeatedly, but that his form provides others with a recipe for observing and conceptualizing imbrication, maybe in other contexts. As a recipe, the form in Fig. 9 tells us: “If you want to learn more about agency, you have to observe changes in the social (e.g., adaptation of work routines) or the material (e.g., reconfigurations of software designs). Your repeated, or continued, observation(s) of this change process (imbrication) will let you recognize and qualify sequences of imbrica tions, which you might be able to use to improve your work arrangements.”<sup>7</sup>

## 3.4. Step 4: Understanding self-reference and context.

At this point, we can clarify how ‘objects’ come into being, or how our observations of DWAs can be conceptualized. In the form calculus, what we conceptualize as an ‘object’ is a self-referential concept, residing in circular, recursive operations of indications represented by the form—mark, cross and re-entry—constituting the quality of self-reference (Baecker, 2015; Glanville, 1990). In this circular “process of becoming,” the object distinguishes itself, is self-distinguishing, and self-distinguished (Glanville, 1990). For qualitative research, this implies that we create meaning not by identifying an object and its characteristics for some purpose, but by assigning and reproducing meaning in the distinctions that we draw. “The purpose of a distinction is to (draw) itself” (Glanville, 1990, p. 6). Put differently, and as we will argue later, only by stirring up this process of becoming—by communicating and discussing it with others—are we able to generate meaning and identity.

This introduction to the LoF shows that the forms allow us to indicate how distinctions lead us to conceptualize our objects of study. From a content point of view, the form is basically empty—yet it invites others to reconsider and act on the indication(s). Forms thus provide a recipe of instructions to others who can then reconsider our distinctions, use, or critically examine them for their own con ceptualizations. As we will see, the form also allows creating complex systems of nested concepts, while creating opportunities for rationalizing and discussing their interrelationships in different contexts. Ultimately, the LoF represents a linguistic device to create formal notations (forms) of our conceptualizations that represent reference points to consider and discuss one's own rationalization and conceptualization process and the related terminology with others in a discursive way. This opportunity becomes particularly interesting when we scrutinize our distinctions by discursively defending our reasoning behind each single distinction with others. This renders our conceptualization or theorizing process a game of giving and asking for reasons for distinctions that is instrumental to arrive at shared meanings.<sup>8</sup>

## 4. Analysis of digitalized work arrangement research

To study DWAs, researchers have adopted various approaches to observing the social and the material. Their approaches differ in the implicit assumptions made, perspectives taken, measures implemented, data collected, and more. Qualitative methods are prin cipally equipped to accommodate such variances to the extent that they can be comprehensively expressed in language.

For qualitative IS researchers, the terminological shift from the IT artifact (Markus & Robey, 1988) to sociomaterial assemblages (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008), to digital artifacts (Kallinikos et al., 2013), to refractions and other conceptualizations (Østerlund et al., 2020) signifies how observations can assume a variety of perspectives in the study of DWAs. After all, it is the researcher’s choice of what object of study to consider and what phenomenon to zoom in on; it is their choice what emerges as the concept under scruti ny—an IT or digital artifact, method, process, or design principle; it is also their choice what theoretical perspective to take for observing it.

We vary our perspectives on our object of study, for instance, because we know that we cannot observe it per se, or directly. As a result, we must decide what aspect of it we can approach by defining our focal point of observation. To decipher, untangle or ‘disenchant’ an object of study as complex as DWAs, various focal points are required, e.g., design principles, or alternatively, insti tutional practices, which delimit the phenomenon to observe. Qualitative studies habitually focus on one or a few of those conceivable focal points. The choice of focal point is regularly taken in close consideration of the purpose and potential contribution of our research, and sometimes even prompted by emergent opportunities

We must then elaborate how we can study the (now isolated) phenomenon under scrutiny in-situ in order to determine a justified point of view. We achieve this by indicating assumptions, regularities, causal relationships, measurement techniques and the like, as conceptualizations—or precedent distinctions—encircling what we assume as appearances of the phenomenon. Some distinctions operate as lenses, some as contexts, which both help to refine the researcher’s stance. Lenses allow us to justify why we can delimit what is to be observed and measured, for instance, when limiting the observation of the interplay between the social and the materia to what is assumed to be an appearance of agencies only. Contexts help refining the given or assumed conditions under which the observation takes place, e.g., when a study choses a particular work setting or asserts the influence of a given environmental condition on the observation process (e.g., digital content being continuously produced on social platforms as something taken for granted for the time of study).

## 4.2.1. Archetype 1. The social and the material, separated

As apparent in Fig. 10, the first three studies perceive the social and the material as disjoint concepts. This perspective provides an archetype for IS researchers that allows to use either concept as a focal point or point of view. Berente et al. (2019) look at how institutional practices relate to the social side; Gregor et al. (2020) discuss how design principles help alter the material aspects of IS. Leonardi (2011) in contrast, uses agencies as a lens to argue how either material or social aspects lead to reconfigurations (imbri cations) that can be observed empirically.

For this archetype, the social and the material (or aspects thereof) can be directly observed and measured. However, their rela tionship is not qualified.<sup>10</sup> Still, A1 helps to weigh them argumentatively against each other and allows for a comparative discussion of studies.

## 4.2.2. Archetype 2. The social and the material, together in context

Orlikowski and Scott (2008) take a different perspective, arguing for the inseparability of the social and the material. They contend that both are present in a sociomaterial assemblage, and only emerge in work performances when constituted from entangled agencies in the flow of situated action (Fig. 11). Thus, the social and material are subsumed, i.e., they are not distinct, in the sociomateria entanglement (assemblage), which now provides the context for looking at performative enactments through the lens of situated practices (see also Table 2). This is notable, given that the social and material have moved out of the focal point to become a contextual concept. This might seem surprising given that this lens was introduced with the term ‘sociomateriality’ at its core. Hence, we recognize this term does not denote so much a novel obiect of study but a distinction in the contextual conditions of observations Although pronouncedly different in perspective and terminology, Kallinikos et al. (2013) follow a similar structure for conceptualizing their inquiry (Fig. 12). They argue that collective rationality rearranges material agencies through social practices (rationalization). acknowledging how dynamic in nature digital content becomes as part of its ecosystem.

The underlving archetype A2 (Fig. 13) acknowledges that the social. the material. and their relationship are hardly observable. Understood as a recipe, this archetype allows us to refine how exactly empirical observations help to operationalize con ceptualizations—and invites other researchers to follow suit. Orlikowski and Scott (2008) do so by addressing the ‘flow of situated practice,’ thus focusing more on the social-performative side of things; Kallinikos et al. (2013) approach the issue from the dynamics of content, and how we rationalize (indirectly by modifying the material through social practice), thus emphasizing more the materia side of things. The archetype thus represents a recipe-like instruction for how we can deal with contextual assemblages. Refining our conceptualizations helps us to recognize DWA constituents from the assemblage of undefined, indeterminate, fuzzy social and materia inferences. It should be noted that the archetype form features a re-entry, which means that the identity of the studied constituent can only be recursively determined. There is no obiect of study’ per se that is to be identified—it emerges from applving the recipe pre scribed by the archetype. In A1. in contrast. it could be observed directly and measured.

## 4.2.3. Archetype 3. Traces of the social and material, and the nature of observing.

Østerlund et al. (2020) take yet another, more distant, perspective on studying DWAs. For them, the social and material are not just unobservable, they are created only by our observing. The authors draw on foundational arguments presented by Barad (1996. 1998. 2003, 2007) to discuss how differently we make sense of what we can observe or measure when we select different approaches, such as a positivist, interpretive, or sociomaterial approach. They particularly emphasize that whenever we apply one of those approaches to observing a phenomenon, we always draw certain distinctions to delimit or enable specific measurements. Such distinctions influence what we can conceptualize as representative of the phenomenon. They show how researchers always implement their respective approach as an “apparatus” that combines prior distinctions. The apparatus then causes (agential) cuts in what we can measure from the dynamic context that we look at. These cuts allow only certain fragments, or traces, to appear from the phenomenon we consider. Hence, what we can observe is just the trace data as fragments, cut out by an apparatus. In other words, the data capturing perfor mances of the researcher create components whose interactions produce the traces that lead to the conceptualization of the object of study as is shown in Fig. 14.

## 5. Discussion

The starting point of our enquiry was the challenge faced to conceptualize and study dynamically evolving DWAs. We suggest that the theories and methods used thus far to study traditional IT artifacts fall short. In this paper, we provide novel insights and ex planations to key conceptualizations in the study of DWAs. We also offer LoF as a pre-ontological and pre-theoretical formalism that enables commensurability of methods and development of novel qualitative empirical methods.

## 5.1. The study of digitalized work arrangements

Our analysis of IS studies and the identified archetypes conceptually advance how the IS field can actively deal with in determinacies in the study of DWAs. The named distinctions serve as conceptual propositions. but ones that carry an inherent indeterminacy in that a second observer must reflect and challenge them. Thus, the LoF formalism functions as an envelope that affords new degrees of freedom for further deliberations, as each form not only defines, but also points towards elements of indeterminacy and uncertainty to be tackled with further scrutiny.<sup>11</sup> This also means that any distinction can be subsequently challenged, for instance, if novel measurement methods become available. Each distinction simply opens an opportunity space to observe and indicate (or “call,” a term used by Spencer-Brown in this context) novel conceptual propositions, which then can be replaced with others, as exemplified in the transition from A2 to A2.1 or A2.2. At the same time, the difference between any two versions of a study can be formally determined by a juxtaposition of the two respective forms.

But if we synthesize the derived three archetypes across all studies, we arrive at a comprehensive characterization of how the various sets of distinctions produce the DWA phenomenon we observe and conceptualize. We report this finding in Fig. 16 as archetype A4.

This opening-up creates ample space for variance in isolating and operatively conceptualizing phenomena related to DWAs in situ— may it be as enacted (A2.1), as rationalized (A2.2), as social-material (A1), or as otherwise conceptualized (summarized as a in A4).

It thus represents the formal demonstration for the claim we made earlier in the introductory section:“the two current challenges, to advance our knowledge about digitalization as object of study and to advance our methods for observing digitalization, are… inextricably related.”<sup>13</sup>

A4 also prescribes that fulfilling the purpose of our research on DWAs at large, namely to develop new knowledge and novel in sights about DWAs, depends on and causes new methods (d versus a, b, c in A4, vis-\`a-vis the considered phenomenon f). It is important to note that this circumstance precedes the discussion of which ontological or theoretical stances may advance our observations. The point is that new methods for empirical inquiry are bound to induce new objects of study; and the more we refine these, the more elaborated methods we will need to develop. We thus now know that the original quest for the IT artifact was probably mis leading—after all, the question is not what it is, but how it assumes its identity as an objectified conception, dependent upon the apparatus, the entangled dynamic context, and how we verbalize it for others.

We have synthesized the archetype A4 for researching DWAs from a methodological perspective. A4 shows us the link between how we understand and what we can possibly understand—which is one aspect in informing our search for ways to advance knowledge about DWAs. The two centermost indications of A4, i.e., conceptualizations and entangled dynamic contexts, point at key in determinacies of the selected studies—and act as a signpost for the limitless space for further inquiry and discovery. This means alternative, and complementary, focal points to those six studies mentioned here are conceivable. The essential insight of A4 however—and the principle of the LoF philosophy in general—is that all new investigations of such focal points need to become part of a discursive process to make any novel insights commensurable with those in existence. How do the new focal points relate to the distinctions discussed here? In what ways are the methods used in those future studies different from the ones known? Our analysis, based on our interpretation of the selected studies, allowed us to formally capture the researchers’ presumed stances towards observing DWAs. This permitted us to explicate how the researchers’ distinctions of focal points, points of view, lenses, and contexts determined whether, to what extent, and how, the social and material become part of the conceptualization. The archetypes should not be considered ontological or epistemological positions. Rather, they are pre-ontological, as they provide the usually implicit boundary conditions from which more elaborate positions and logical arguments can be developed. They thus constitute recipes for how to draw distinctions in order to arrive at such positions. The consequently assumed ontological stance then matters for the reasoning, i.e., logic of arguments and causal explanations.

In our analysis, the researchers’ distinctions carry pre-ontological positions that inform subsequent theoretical concepts. These theoretical concepts suggest ordering mechanisms (in the sense of providing a systematic structure) for observing and ensuing con ceptualizations as representatives (identities) of the phenomenon that we consider. In our analysis, agency is an example for such a theoretical concept that, when applied, shapes the relationship between the social and the material (Fig. 9). The archetypes identified in our analysis represent examples for how such theoretical concepts encircle a phenomenon of interest.

It is noteworthy that the archetypes can lead to antithetical positions, e.g., whether claiming or neglecting the possibility that the observation process interferes with the observed, as seen in A1 versus A3. It is also noteworthy that the archetypes can facilitate refinements and complements of approaches as exemplified in A2.1 and A2.2 that implement A2 in innovative ways. This way, the forms provide an explicit notation for the pre-theoretical observation process, which points at those aspects that require scrutiny in subsequent theorizing processes. The notation thus helps managing the inherent indeterminacy of indications pre-theoretically, and controlling the meaningfulness, or explanatory power, of theoretical concepts. This influence is most pronounced in A3 and A3.1, which posits that the apparatus ultimately determines what can be observed and how this is to be interpreted.

More can be said about the implications of the LoF formalism towards our pre-ontological rationalization and ensuing theorizing processes. An issue we have not discussed for instance, is how the pre-ontological nature of the formalism links with later ontologica commitments. Regarding the theorizing process, the LoF formalism provides a reasoning that Spencer-Brown called “injunctive” (Spencer-Brown, 1969), and that epistemologists have discussed as a predecessor of the more accustomed processes of deduction, induction, and abduction. By understanding injunctive reasoning better, we might be able to improve ensuing forms of reasoning. This might impact how novel methods can be designed and justified, which might lead to changes in our repertoire of epistemological and ontological positions—an opportunity that as we posit should be object of future studies in the field

## 5.2. Laws of Form analysis

The LoF formalism, its notation, and most importantly the underlying philosophy, can guide researchers in various ways. First, forms allow reflection of one’s own distinctions, and whether they are congruent with the intended research approach. Second, forms constitute a recipe for fellow researchers to reproduce the research approach. Third, forms can be analyzed for where in the form notation, and in which ways, refined distinctions could lead to novel insights, thus informing new method development. And fourth, forms together with the LoF formalism provide an instrument to make different approaches commensurable. as illustrated in the derivation of archetypical forms. The latter point should be particularly interesting to the pluralist IS field, whose discourses span languages and theories from various schools and disciplines as diverse as computer science. social science, and philosophy. As one reviewer to this manuscript noted, these “languages (jargons) and associated methods and measurements may indeed be comparable (or not) regarding the phenomena they are reasoning about.”

Our discussion of differing perspectives about the social and the material was just one example in order to illustrate how different theoretical concepts can be compared with one another. We believe that our analysis can provide a blueprint for the synopsis or comparison of various other theoretical concepts beyond agency and address questions of affordances, appropriation, technologica frames, transactive memory, and many more. Table 3 provides just a glimpse of distinctions suggested by this short list of theoretical concepts that might be further scrutinized through a LoF analysis. In fact, an analysis of each might reveal where in the form notation methodical changes could generate possibilities for novel observations, and how this would alter the opportunity space for concep tualizations and the nature of knowledge to be created. We posit that this might be one way to practically and systematically guide our advancement of methods in the IS field.

Although we have not looked at the body of literature relating to the theoretical concepts mentioned in Table 3, we can easily assume that we will find quite a diversity of authors' lenses on distinct aspects. The theoretical concepts at large, as well as the authors’ particular lenses for them, exist in parallel; they relate to each other, and at times overlap, but cannot be reduced into, or mapped onto, one another. The LoF analysis can help. It allows us to represent these theoretical concepts with respect to their specific set of dis tinctions expressed as a form, and their subsequent ontological stances. The LoF analysis thus offers a notation to make them commensurable. While the equivalent in positivist research would be to draw a model with the help of boxes and arrows and to provide correlations, we would benefit from a comparable instrument in qualitative research. Employing the LoF might enable asking and discussing questions, such as “How do technological frames and affordances resonate with each other?” or “How does transactive memory influence adoption?”

A pressing challenge in the design of novel methods is how to integrate machine learning and automated observations, and eventually conceptualizations, into our qualitative methods. In the LoF, all observations, distinctions, abstract concepts, or mecha nisms we assume to be at work are treated similarly. It is therefore not unthinkable to have algorithms suggest distinctions as a result of their data analysis. Even though such distinctions, representing “concepts,” do not originate from the human mind and are potentially inexplicable or their interpretation not immediately approachable, the LoF formalism allows for re-iteration and refinement so that those ‘algorithmic’ observations, conceptualizations, and interpretations can merge with our traditional qualitative, interpretive process. This could possibly open the door for new techniques of designing qualitative research methods and subsequent conceptual ideations.

We also posit that the LoF formalism, in its principle, is also applicable to other research approaches, including quantitative and design science studies, for instance as a preliminary to deductive or abductive reasoning in the theorizing processes. As one reviewer to our manuscript emphasized:

“We are seeing a methodological convergence or at least confluence in the sense that IS and organizational scholars increasingly use both qualitative and quantitative data and leverage computational methods to analyze qualitative data, such as the digital trace data you mention. They do so for both theory testing and theory building. My sense is that (the Laws of Form calculus) has broader applicabilit than qualitative research.”

Another notion we have left untouched when discussing the LoF is the connection between our linguistic abilities and the form. It is a proposition of the theory of linguistic relativity in the philosophy of language that our language primes us as individuals towards what we can see and experience (Hussein. 2012: Rollins. 1972). Whorf (1940). for example, argues that our linguistic system defines how our cognitive mind determines what and how we observe, and what we are able to describe and put into words (Rollins, 1972). In addition, social norms define what we can rightfully say and how we can say it so we are understood (Brandom. 2000). Only what we can describe, we can make distinctions about with the help of forms. We have a unique challenge in front of us, trying to decouple ourselves from our customary jargon in order to open up the possibility for creating novel kinds of observations (Suchman, 2007).

## 6. Conclusion

For our qualitative view on the world, digitalization poses new challenges. When we interpret our qualitative observations, we dynamically interact with our data and re-iterate and refine our conceptualizations. Taking narrow snapshots of field data seems insufficient, given the dynamics of our technology-augmented environments that is bound to increase, rather than decrease. Hence, our qualitative methods have to adapt, favoring plurality over outdated conceptions, multiplicity over competing schools of thought and disconnected ontologies, and “adaptable observing” over an a priori defined and unchanging measurement. At the same time, we need to be cautious to avoid arbitrariness and incommensurability of methods. Accordingly, we make two key contributions. First, we provide novel insights and explanations to key conceptualizations in the study of DWAs. Second, we argue for a formal treatment of conceptualizations as a predecessor and precondition to novel qualitative empirical methods in the IS field, and offer the LoF as a preontological and pre-theoretical formalism for commensurability between multiple conceptualizations and methods. We believe such a treatment can help us open novel possibilities for observation so that we can advance our options for generating insights about our relationships with technology.

Kallinikos et al. (2013) note that observation of digital artifacts is difficult, if not impossible, as the digital artifact and the ecosystem that substantializes it, change in use. A change in artifact causes the social context to adapt accordingly. As a result, their focal point of observation is the dynamism that emanates from the material. Social and material are not exogenous to one another (p. 367). The authors view forms of agency as caused by self-referential and reflexive dynamics, thus bringing “digital artifacts qua objects” into being (p. 366 and 367). From their point of view, relationships between the social and the material are rendered irrelevant because their interactions become non-traceable and non-reproducible due to the occurring dynamism. Relevant is, however, the role of the material as it causes new social practices to occur. As for method, the authors suggest bringing together “collective rationalities… accommodated by the key attributes of digital objects… recurring at the intersection with social practices” (p. 367). The statement that collective rationalities bring digital artifacts into being through social practices is captured in eq. (5).

Østerlund et al. (2020) “propose a set of methodological principles and strategies for the use of trace data, i.e., data capturing performances carried out on or via information systems, often at a fine level of detail” (p. 1). Trace data, the study’s focal point, has some inherent challenges associated with the social and the material. After all, it is the social that is reflected in trace data itself, and it is the material that allows for trace data to be created. The authors propose that there is always an apparatus between the human observer and the digital artifact that either refracts, reflects, or diffracts; in other words, it creates the context for observations. Particularly in the case of the latter, the apparatus plays a vital role in producing the object of study that is set to be studied. In other words, the apparatus is inherently intertwined with what can be observed. and any observation outcome cannot be decoupled from the apparatus itself. The focal point is therefore always dependent upon the apparatus that “marks particular distinctions” by allowing for ‘agential cuts’ to be performed.<sup>14</sup> Eq. (6) formulates this awareness of the apparatus as context of who observes, what is observed, and how. The selected data capturing performances execute agential cuts that determine which interacting components become effective and leave observable traces.
