## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Digital technology Institutional change Institutional infrastructure Affordances Industry exchange fields Issue fields Discourse analysis

## A B S T R A C T

Emergent digital technologies need to be legitimated for them to enable new marketplaces to diffuse and scale. The extant literature has emphasized the role of discourse in framing legiti mation efforts. Despite recognizing the broader role of technology in the legitimation process, these studies have not examined the specific affordances of digital technologies used by field members and also how this relates to the institutional infrastructure of the field to influence the legitimation process. In our study of an industry exchange field, we drew on archival data be tween 1997 and 2001 to examine how members of the then emergent e-commerce industry ex change field achieved legitimation of digital signatures in electronic transactions through legislation. Our research contributes to extant research by showing how the legitimation process involves invoking and scaling affordances through sensegiving, translating, and decoupling mechanisms. We also show how affordances are conditioned by the specific institutional infra structure that supports and enables them, and how issue fields can arise within exchange fields as spaces to (re)negotiate and shape institutional changes. We conclude with implications for further research into the diffusion and scale of digital marketplaces that is of increasing importance in light of recent regulatory debates.

## 1. Introduction

Emergent digital technologies need to be legitimated for them to enable new marketplaces to diffuse and scale. Legitimation is defined as a process that makes the technology “desirable, proper, or appropriate within some socially constructed system of norms, beliefs, and definitions” (Suchman, 1995, p. 574). The legitimating process is nevertheless challenging given that emergent digital technologies bring forward new technology structures and market obiectives that threaten the existing institutional infrastructure (Hinings, Gegenhuber, & Greenwood, 2018). Hence, the emergence of digital technologies triggers institutional change at a field leve (Zietsma, Groenewegen, Logue, & Hinings, 2017).

The extant literature on legitimating digital technologies has predominately emphasized the role of discourse in framing legiti mation efforts (Barrett, Heracleous, & Walsham, 2013; Constantinides & Barrett, 2015; Davidson, Østerlund, & Flaherty, 2015; Kaganer, Pawlowski, & Wiley-Patton, 2010). This literature, although acknowledging the broader role of technology in the legiti mation process, has not examined the specific affordances of digital technologies and how those impact the legitimation process (Faik

Barrett, & Oborn, 2020). It has also overlooked how field members may mobilize or be constrained by a field's institutional infra structure when trying to introduce, embed and legitimate a new technology—particularly one that threatens existing relations, po sitions and associated infrastructure.

Thus, in this paper, we ask how digital technologies become legitimated in industry exchange fields? We focus on the legitimation of digital signature technology in the late 1990s and early 2000s in the US in the e-commerce industry exchange field (Zietsma et al., 2017). We focus on how the field's members invoked the affordances of digital signature technology in their discourse and how these affordances were successfully scaled. We show that the legitimation of digital signature technology enabled the early diffusion of digital marketplaces. Through these data, we examine how field members such as financial institutions, card associations, and technology vendors achieved legitimation through legislation for the introduction of digital signatures in e-commerce transactions.

Our paper offers two main theoretical contributions. First, while previous research has argued that digital technology becomes legitimated through common technical standards (Davidson et al., 2015; Hsu, Lin, & Wang, 2015; Kaganer et al., 2010), we show that such standards need to be enforced through legislation, a central and formal piece of institutional infrastructure. The process by which this occurs can be usefully understood by considering how a disruptive digital technology may give rise to an issue field within an existing industry exchange field. By deploying this conceptual lens, we reveal how an emergent issue field is formed to negotiate the new issue i.e. how digital technology challenges and (re)shapes existing institutional infrastructure. In our case, once legislation is implemented the issue field dissolves. That is, digital technology becomes a standard and is no longer debated as an issue to be resolved. This legitimation process involves aligning the affordances of digital technology with values shared by field members to achieve coherency in the existing institutional infrastructure (Hinings, Logue, & Zietsma, 2017).

Second, and related to the above, we provide an explicit theorization of the affordances of digital technologies and how they impact the legitimation process within institutional fields. We show that although the affordances of digital technology provide great benefits to different field members, these affordances are conditioned on having an institutional infrastructure that enables them. In addition, legitimating digital technology involves a successful scaling of the affordances to strengthen the impact and importance of the technology. We show how, through sensegiving, translating, and decoupling, field members were able to scale the unique affordances of digital signatures, namely, authentication, integrity, and non-repudiation. By incorporating these scaling mechanisms in their discourse, field members legitimated digital signature technology as durable and enduring, continuing to be used even today.

More broadly, our theorization of how emergent digital technology becomes legitimated in an industry exchange field through legislation has important implications for understanding how digital marketplaces diffuse as field members exert pressure and actively demand institutional change. This is of relevance to numerous other empirical settings. For example, the importance of this is seen in the slow adoption of novel technologies such as blockchain where recent research shows that legal uncertainty is among the top barriers to blockchain adoption (Lustenberger, Maleˇsevi´c, & Spychiger, 2021). Our analysis of the early emergence and diffusion of ecommerce marketplaces through legislation of the digital signature technology has important implications for recent regulatory de bates around the diffusion of digital platforms such as the Apple App Store, Meta, and Amazon marketplaces. Our findings suggest that recent efforts to regulate digital marketplaces such as. the European Digital Markets Act (DMA). need to be attentive to the legitimation process of underlying digital technologies and ensure legal certainty. Legal certainty provides a supportive institutional infrastructure that protects field members' interests and investment in technology.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we discuss two types of institutional fields; industry exchange and issue fields, and describe how the emergence of novel digital technology triggers institutional change. We then explain affordances and how they relate to electronic commerce and the legitimation of digital technologies. This is followed by a discussion of our meth odology, and the ways we have collected and analyzed the data through discourse analysis. We then move on to the presentation of our research findings. We conclude with a discussion and implications of our findings.

## 2. Institutional fields and institutional change

An institutional field refers to “a recognized area of institutional life” (Dimaggio & Powell, 1983, p. 148) which involves in teractions among different field members such as suppliers, competitors, partners, and regulatory bodies. These interactions are usually subiect to an institutional infrastructure defined as a set of political. legal. and cultural elements that form the backdrop ot economic activity and governance, enabling or constraining the operation of an institutional field (Hinings et al., 2017). Institutiona infrastructures provide the social norms, rules, and regulations that field members depend on when coordinating relationships and evaluating whether an action is legitimate or not, all necessary for governing transactions in a marketplace (Bakos, 1998).

According to Zietsma et al. (2017), institutional fields can be classified into exchange and issue fields, with each comprised of subtypes. Exchange fields are subtyped by the nature of the focal population such as a whole industry (e.g, e-commerce marketplaces). professional fields (e.g. healthcare services), or social movements (e.g. the slow food movement). Issue fields are subtyped by the nature of the issue being negotiated, governed, and/or competed over such as competitive issues within an exchange field (e.g. new technology, prices, and services), interstitial issues that span fields (e.g. income inequality) and bridging issues that require crossboundary coordination (e.g. transnational governance).

In this study, we focus on an industry exchange field that is characterized by multilateral interactions between exchange partners in specific industries (Zietsma et al., 2017), such as e-commerce marketplaces for books, music, and electronics. An industry exchange field<sup>1</sup> is comprised of a collection of heterogeneous field members who compete against, and collaborate with, one another over common objectives (e.g. selling similar products to the same customer segment). Industry exchange fields' boundaries are permeable to new entrants who can bring new technology that disrupts the stability of the field (Zietsma et al., 2017). Emergent technology is often contested by incumbents based on the technology's performance in meeting current customer needs; investing in emergent digita technologies seems irrational at first because of the inertia of the current institutional infrastructure. In consequence, many in cumbents do not embrace emergent technology until it is too late (Christensen, 1997). Indeed, emergent digital technologies introduce new technology structures and market objectives that threaten the existing institutional infrastructure (Hinings et al., 2018). This disruptive impact hinders the acceptance of the technology among the fields' members. New digital technology hence needs to be legitimated for it to diffuse and scale. For instance, Flynn and Du (2012) show how a new general-purpose university smartcard system revolutionized common university operations, but required legitimation before it was adopted at scale.

New digital technologies can therefore be conceptualized as emergent issue fields that raise competitive frictions within an existing industry exchange field. New issue fields form as industry field members challenge the legitimation of the new digital technologies concerning the established institutional infrastructure. For instance, Markard, Wirth, and Truffer (2016) show how biogas technology expanded rapidly, however, this rapid growth resulted in irreconcilable conflicts between different field members (e.g. energy sup pliers and agriculturalists) leading to a loss of legitimation. In this case, regaining legitimation involved active negotiations over the meaning of biogas technology between diverse field members in an attempt to restore alignment with the energy and agriculture institutional infrastructures. Similarly, Constantinides and Slavova (2020) show how, although initially the monopoly incumbent in South Africa's energy field, Eskom, embraced renewable energy technologies as complementary to coal and crude oil energy pro duction, once these were diffused at scale, Eskom and its network of suppliers began contesting the performance of those technologies. The industry exchange field was faced with an emergent issue field, namely, renewable energy technologies, that threatened the monopoly position of Eskom, as well as the institutional infrastructure that supported it. After a series of new legislation, including the deregulation of the industry exchange field away from a monopoly market, renewable energy technologies became legitimated as an alternative source of energy while constituting new entrepreneurial possibilities (Constantinides & Slavova, 2020). Accordingly, emergent technologies constitute new issue fields that may question the established institutional infrastructure. The disruptive impact of these technologies makes it challenging for industry exchange field members to regulate and govern their interactions using existing institutional infrastructure since those are often not aligned with the changes introduced by the emergent technology. Field members in issue fields, therefore, need to negotiate their positions to build a new institutional infrastructure for their industry exchange field that can accommodate the emergent technology.

In the context of our study, e-commerce marketplaces represent an industry exchange field that consists of merchants (sellers), customers (buyers), financial institutions (e.g. banks), credit card associations (e.g. Visa), regulatory agencies (e.g. legislators), and technology vendors. Commerce marketplaces have existed since the agorae (i.e. Greek for ‘marketplaces’) in ancient Greek cities, where goods, information, and services were exchanged for a fee or bartered for other such items. The first e-commerce marketplaces were introduced in finance and were initially focused on securities and commodities exchanges through electronic data interchanges (See Barrett & Walsham. 1999: Kaniadakis & Constantinides. 2014). These early e-commerce marketplaces were highly institutionalized in financial regulations, which authenticated the identity of transacting parties and protected their assets. However, once ecommerce marketplaces for other business-to-business (B2B) and business-to-consumer (B2C) exchanges began to emerge. such as eBay in 1995 and Amazon in 1994, the interactions among the field members constituting the industry exchange field needed to be coordinated in a secure and trustworthy way to address such issues as information asymmetries between buyers and sellers, dispute resolution and intellectual property rights protection (Bakos, 1998). An issue field, thus, emerged involving the development of new digital technology, namely, digital signatures, which was used to authenticate digital transactions in e-commerce marketplaces. Accordingly, legitimating digital signatures was necessary for the very functioning of e-commerce marketplaces.

Prior research on the legitimation of digital technologies paid little attention to the unique affordances of digital technologies and how they can influence the legitimation process (Faik et al., 2020). Affordances refer to the possibilities for goal-oriented actions of a particular technology (Markus & Silver, 2008). Field members seek to gain legitimation of certain technology by focusing their attention on the opportunities made possible by that technology, that is, invoking the technology's affordances in the legitimation discourse. Next, we discuss affordances and how they relate to e-commerce transactions and the legitimation of digital technologies.

## 3. The affordances of digital signatures and technology legitimation

Digital technologies enable actions because of their inherent features that become salient when combined with the goals of the field members performing the action (Markus & Silver, 2008). That is, affordances are relational and context-dependent. For instance, field members interested in disseminating information are more likely to pay attention to the sharing affordance of technology, while others that care more about communication will focus on the chatting affordance. Therefore, digital technology can have multiple affor dances. As field members perceive the technology in relation to their goals and interests, only certain affordances will capture the members' attention and be enacted (Faik et al., 2020; Vaast & Kaganer, 2013; Volkoff & Strong, 2013). At the same time, the same affordance can be perceived differently across several field members. For example, the visibility affordance of social media, that is, how visible users' behavior, posts, and comments become to others, may be perceived favorably by employees, but may also be a source of risk for organizations that want to limit information disclosure (Vaast & Kaganer, 2013).

In work settings characterized by a high level of interdependencies, such as industry exchange fields, field members need to enact shared affordances to enable work coordination and task achievement (Leonardi, 2013). Without a common approach for appropri ating a technology's affordances, field members will use the technology differently making it challenging to work together toward achieving common objectives. In addition, the presence of an institutional infrastructure that enables the actualization of affordances (Bygstad, Munkvold, & Volkoff, 2016) is required. For example, Du, Pan, Leidner, and Ying (2019) show how a culture of intra preneurship was necessary for the actualization of the financing affordance in blockchain technology.

In the context of an e-commerce industry exchange field, a new digital technology that was introduced to coordinate digital transactions between interacting parties was digital signatures, as mentioned earlier. Digital signatures have three unique affordances. First, to honor transactions, digital signatures authenticate each transacting party's identity (El-Gamal, 1985; Paillier, 1999). Moreover as information messages are being transmitted within e-commerce marketplaces and exchanged across diverse organizational settings, digital signatures afford the integrity of the messages (Gupta, Tung, & Marsden, 2004). Finally, digital signatures afford non-repudiation or tying the messages to the sender to confirm and validate authorship, which is critical for honoring transactions (Zhu, Guo, Gan, & Tsai, 2016). These three affordances allow different opportunities for action that were not viable in early e-commerce transactions. For example, through these affordances e-commerce transactions become accessible to a wide and global audience by establishing stronger security. For businesses, this represents a valuable opportunity for expanding their market reach and customer base by validating the identity of buyers. For customers, they gain easy and quick access to a variety of products and services while trusting the identity of sellers.

In this paper, we focus on how affordances are invoked in field members' discourse. Any discourse that seeks to achieve legitimation provides reasons that make the focal change “desirable, proper, or appropriate within some socially constructed system of norms, beliefs, and definitions” (Suchman, 1995, p. 574). This is done by accentuating the value opportunities of the change such as a new digital technology while accommodating the key objectives of field members. During this process, multiple affordances are invoked where each affordance is associated with the objectives of particular field members. Accordingly, legitimating a technology does not necessarily mean that field members to be mobilized are against the technology, rather, there is a need to make the technology ‘desirable’ by matching its affordances with field members' objectives.

Affordances need to gain scale and stability to enable change (Faik et al., 2020). Faik et al. (2020) proposed three scaling mechanisms for IT affordances; sensegiving, translating and decoupling. In sensegiving, field members attempt to influence how others ascribe meaning to and interpret a specific phenomenon (e.g. a digital technology) (Gioia & Chittipeddi, 1991). Sensegiving discourse can be more persuasive when it draws on established social facts that are usually taken for granted and hence less challenged (Ocasio, Loewenstein, & Nigam, 2015). Through sensegiving, field members offer generic guidelines and rationalization that frame the digita technology in a way that matches their objectives. Field members thus engage in sensegiving to affect institutional change and shape its outcome.

Translating allows field members to relate the affordances of digital technologies to other applications and obiectives within and across fields (Faik et al., 2020). It is critical in the legitimating discourse because it places the digital technology in a broader context by showing how it transcends a specific field (e-commerce in our case) to impact other fields as well (Ocasio et al., 2015). Digital technology is not bound to specific uses, rather it scales up to involve different uses by different field members. During this process, new objectives associated with digital technology might arise to widen its impact (Nielsen, Mathiassen, & Newell, 2014; Ocasio et al., 2015).

Decoupling refers to separating the technology from its enabling infrastructure (Faik et al., 2020). The technology becomes abstracted so that it goes beyond any specific situation (Ocasio et al., 2015). Decoupling allows affordances to scale because of a separation between the digital technology itself (e.g. digital signatures) and the underlving infrastructure (Yoo, Henfridsson, & Lyytinen, 2010). For example, digital signatures are a software-based technology that can be integrated with multiple different financial transaction infrastructures, from credit cards, digital wallets, and USSD (Unstructured Supplementary Service Data), among others. In other words, digital signatures are infrastructure-agnostic. Because digital technologies keep evolving, decoupling becomes critical for wide diffusion and scaling.

In summary, we have argued that industry exchange fields are prone to change upon the introduction of a new technology that threatens an existing institutional infrastructure giving rise to a new issue field. For this technology to be adopted, it needs to be legitimated. We further argued that the legitimation discourse involves invoking the technology's affordances to show how the technology enables field members' objectives, as well as scaling these affordances to strengthen the discourse and enable change. In the next section, we discuss the legitimation of digital signature technology across the US to examine how affordances were used in the discourse, and how these affordances were successfully scaled to result in legitimating digital signatures.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. The case of digital signature technology

Digital signature technology presents an important turning point in the emergence of e-commerce in the 1990s. This is an appropriate case for studying legitimation for several reasons. First, digital signature technology was an important issue field for innovations in e-commerce and digital transactions. Digital signature technology was not just an issue, but rather an issue field because it drew multiple field members (both incumbents and startups) into the industry exchange field and provided opportunities for new entrepreneurial action that could potentially disrupt and reshape existing boundaries. Conducting financial transactions over the internet represented a new digital innovation that challenged the extant institutional infrastructure at that time. Thus, examining how field members legitimated the use of digital signature technology can inform the legitimation process for recent digital innovations in financial transactions (e.g. blockchain). Second, this case is an excellent manifestation of the interdependencies among field members and thus critical for understanding legitimation in industry exchange fields.

Digital signatures authenticate the identity of transacting parties in online transactions and verify the integrity of the transmitted messages. For that, digital signatures can use different types of cryptography (see Fig. 1). In asymmetric (or public key) cryptography, two keys are used; one for encrypting the plain text, and the other for decrypting the ciphered text. In symmetric cryptography, a single key is used for both encryption and decryption, and thus this key must be secret. Finally, digital signatures can be based on an al gorithm (hash function) that provides the digital fingerprint of content transmitted.

Digital signatures enabled secure digital transactions across the different US States when it was originally introduced. However, when the different US States started to regulate the use of digital signatures and set different standards on when digitally signed documents were considered legal, this technology became problematic. States differed on the type of cryptography they would sup port. For instance, some states (e.g. Hawaii, Maryland, Michigan) required the application of public key cryptography for the digita signature to have the same legal effect as manual signatures. Other states (e.g. California, Florida, Georgia) left the choice of the technology open. Digital transactions are distributed and this fragmentation and the lack of consensus over when a digitally signed document should be honored and verified across states resulted in a legitimation problem. Facing this situation, different field members such as financial institutions, card associations, and technology vendors lobbied for federal intervention to legitimate the use of digital signatures and have a unified standard. These field members participated in several congressional hearings to mobilize legislators and legitimate digital signatures.

Achieving legitimation through legislation in our case requires enacting a new law, which lies in the power of the US Congress. The process starts by introducing a bill (from the US Senate or House of Representatives), which represents an initial draft of the proposed law. The bill is assigned to a committee of jurisdiction, which can send it to a specialized sub-committee for study. Congressional hearings take place to give opportunities for witnesses to provide their testimony. Hearings are also held to collect information about a certain phenomenon (which in our case was e-commerce and digital authentication) before the introduction of a bill. Amendments can be made to the bill before taking a vote. The bill needs to pass both the House and the Senate before sending it to the President for fina approval and signature, otherwise, the bill is abandoned and no law is enacted.

## 4.2. Data collection

Our primary data consist of transcripts of the congressional public hearings that took place to legitimate digital signatures along with transcripts of testimony statements provided by witnesses. We chose public hearings because they are considered the main source of data when regulators seek to enact and legitimate a new law (Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005). Public hearings are non-intentional social documents (Rowlinson, Hassard, & Decker, 2014) that are not exposed to subjective distortion. They represent direct reporting of discussions without any external interference (Alvesson & Skoldberg, 2009). Moreover, public hearings entail an abundance of data that reveal different field members' attempts to understand a complex situation (Brown, 2004; Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005) enabling by this, robust interpretations.

## 4.3. Data analysis

We carried out a discourse analysis of the arguments used by different field members, as found in the transcripts of the congres sional public hearings (Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005; Vaara & Monin, 2010; Van Leeuwen & Wodak, 1999). Fig. 2 provides a sche matic overview of our analytic process.

In the first phase of analysis, we first read all of the “texts” – i.e. all the public hearings and statements on the digital signature case as listed in Appendix 1 – to identify central themes around the historical developments leading to the E-Sign Act as explicitly stated or assumed by field members. We then carried out an intertextual analysis to discover other sub-themes that were present in other texts including academic studies, grey literature, newspaper articles, periodicals, and trade publications all reporting on the E-Sign Act and the role of digital signatures in e-commerce transactions. We analyzed the connections among the central and sub-themes, by moving from the part (single texts) to the whole (discourses constituted of the totality of evidence) and vice versa, to examine the com monalities and differences between different patterns of argumentation (Heracleous & Barrett, 2001).

In the second phase of analysis, we drew on previous studies on legitimation to identify the specific discursive strategies used by field members (Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005; Vaara & Monin, 2010). This literature sensitized us that, different discursive strategies are used to accommodate the objectives of different field members. For instance, seeking to gain the support of legislators to legitimate a certain technology requires a different discursive strategy from that employed to mobilize technology vendors because of their varied objectives (e.g. providing legal certainty vs stimulating market relations in e-commerce). In identifying the discursive strategies of each field member, we paid attention to the discursive “features” (Vaara & Monin, 2010) in each strategy including factualization and evidence (e.g. “Japan, Denmark, Germany, Italy, and the European Union are working to provide legal recognition for electronic authenti cation agreements" - The General Counsel for Technology and Intellectual Property at Citibank). references to national identity (e.g. “The importance of electronic commerce to the U.S. economy and to American consumers cannot be understated” - The President of the World Information Technology and Services Alliance), and references to the common good (e.g. “Citibank can afford to go State-by-State, seek approvals … it would be very difficult for the smaller banks in this country to participate in that marketplace” – The President of Digita Signature Trust Company), among others. These discursive features underlined field members' interpretations of the technology and accentuated their communicative actions toward specific objectives (cf. Constantinides, 2013).

In the third phase, we reflected on our discourse analysis while revisiting the literature on affordances (Faik et al., 2020; Vaast & Kaganer, 2013; Volkoff & Strong, 2013). We questioned whether relying solely on discourse analysis helped us in gaining a deep understanding of how digital technologies are legitimated in industry exchange fields. We wanted to better theorize the role of technology affordances in legitimation to be able to answer our key research question and develop a strong contribution to the literature. To this end, and with the support of the review team,<sup>2</sup> we backgrounded our more traditional discourse analysis and foregrounded instead our analysis of technology affordances and scaling mechanisms. We. thus, used digital signature technology's three affordances (authentication, integrity, and non-repudiation) as the basis for our final coding.

During this phase, we analyzed relevant texts to understand how field members sought to justify the legitimation of digita signature technology by associating its affordances with different objectives. Accordingly, we followed three steps in our analysis, namely, (1) identifying the affordances invoked in the discourse, (2) identifying the objectives used in discourse, and (3) under standing how the three affordances enabled change. In instances of disagreements between authors in the coding process (e.g. one author associating an argument with integrity affordance while the other author coding it under non-repudiation), the authors engaged in a discussion to define the prominent affordance(s)/objective(s) and reach an agreement. It is worth mentioning that while coding for affordances, we noticed that some field members' arguments focused on necessary conditions for the actualization of affordances. That is, there was no specific reference to one affordance, rather field members referred to digital signature technology and its affordances as a whole while paying attention to the conditions that made those affordances possible. We captured this code in our analysis as “affordances of digital signature technology conditioned on supportive institutional infrastructure". The outcome of the second step of coding was associating the affordances with the text and identifying three categories of objectives, namely, market, societal, and state federal. Market objectives help field members in achieving their business goals such as expanding their market reach and reducing operational risks. Societal obiectives relate to social values that are cherished by legislators who seek to promote and ensure the attainment of these values such as the value of fairness and equality. State/federal objectives relate to the status of the US and its role as a leader in e-commerce. In the third step of coding, we emphasized the way affordances scaled to enable change and legitimate the use of digital signatures. Therefore, we coded the text according to Faik et al.'s (2020) three scaling mechanisms; sensegiving, translating and decoupling. While we focused on arguments directed toward the legislators in our analysis, we further identified how focal field members drew on other field members' arguments in their discourse. ## 5. Findings

The need for legitimating digital signatures was associated with the commercialization of the Internet and the need to build trust in digital transactions. As e-commerce is no longer bound by the constraints associated with physical commerce (e.g. costs and physica space), the digital environment of e-commerce allowed more merchants to offer products and services online which attracted more customers to carry out their purchases through a one-stop-shop. A report by the US Department of Commerce emphasized the broadening accessibility of e-commerce and its effect on increasing customers' choices:

The sheer number of stores that can be “visited” online far exceeds even the most densely populated retail areas in the country. No longer do customers find their shopping limited to the stores within a reasonable driving or walking distance …Online, customers can shop at stores in other states, in other countries. (Department of Commerce, 1998, p. 41).

However, facilitating wide reach and accessibility was conditioned on the availability of a mechanism that would authenticate digital transactions. In their discourse, field members stressed the differences between physical and digital transactions that neces sitated special treatment for the latter to reap the benefits of e-commerce. Digital transactions required mechanisms for identity authentication, message integrity, and authorship validation (non-repudiation) which were all lacking, presenting serious threats. Digital signature technology through its authentication, integrity, and non-repudiation affordances addressed the challenges mentioned above. The General Counsel for Technology and Intellectual Property at Citibank explained how digital authentication would allow identity authentication, message integrity, and authorship validation for e-commerce transactions:

All of this is accomplished by electronic authentication. These are simple and necessary functions. (US Congress, 1997j, p. 14). This was also emphasized by other field members. Visa's President and Counsel of Visa International clarified:

We would like to be able to use electronic authentication to increase the security of our cardholders and our merchants. (US Congress, 1997h, p. 11).

This discourse further clarified how the affordances of digital signatures, collectively, can stimulate market relations both na tionally and internationally. With digital signatures, e-commerce was seen as an opportunity for creating new products and services and enabling scalability. Field members, specifically bankers, tied digital signatures with their digital business strategy in scaling up their services by offering banking products and services online locally and internationally broadening their market reach, The General Counsel for Technology and Intellectual Property at Citibank stated that:

electronic authentication is fundamental to the conduct of Internet banking. (US Congress, 1997j, p. 14).

Although the above arguments pointed to the importance of digital signatures that would justify legitimating its use, the actual ization of digital signatures' affordances was problematic because the US States enacted inconsistent laws that regulated the use of digital signature technology and set constraints on when a digitally signed document was considered legal. Uncertainty about authentication technologies resulted in under-investment in these technologies which hampered the development of e-commerce and the fulfillment of market. societal. and state/federal obiectives. Field members consequently argued that inconsistent state laws impeded the actualization of authentication, integrity, and non-repudiation affordances by introducing uncertainty, business risk, and cost. This was mentioned by the representative of the Electronic Commerce Forum:

One inhibitor to this, though, I believe, is the multiplicity of State laws on digital signatures; which clearly undermines certainty with the amounts of variety that is present. (US Congress, 1997i, p. 6).

He further continued and clarified that states differed in the scope of application of digital signatures, where some states limited the use of the technology to transactions occurring between governmental agencies only:

For example, at the present time, the statutes of Arizona, California, Delaware, Iowa, Minnesota, New Mexico, Virginia, and Wyoming recognize digital signatures only in connection with communications with or between governmental entities. (US Congress, 1997e, p. 50).

The state laws further outlined varying specifications regarding technical requirements, liability issues, financial and operational requirements for any field member aiming to offer digital certificates. These all added to the cost of market entry. The General Counsel for Technology and Intellectual Property at Citibank explained how inconsistent laws were affecting businesses and detailed the sources of costs arising from the multiplicity of standards:

Some of the States actually have State agencies going in and saying what is the standard for software, hardware, and personnel. States differ on disclosures to consumers and subscribers. States differ on minimal financial resources for certificate authorities providing electronic authentication services. They differ in obligations and liabilities of certificate authorities and the rules applicable to the suspension and revocation of certificates; procedures for maintaining and keeping revocation lists; record keeping; property rights; and keys that have been issued; and State agency rights to revoke, terminate or suspend certificates that are being used in electronic commerce. (US Congress, 1997j, p. 15).

He also mentioned that costs were not limited to the cost of doing business but also involved administrative costs in the legal system:

You have clerks that have to deal with—I guess are going to have to deal with—50 or 60 lawyers, 50 or 60 information security officers, and that is probably on a good day, deal with the certificates and revocation of certificates, and these are under various State laws. (US Congress, 1997g, p. 33).

These costs made field members reluctant to enter the market and invest in e-commerce products. For many field members, including merchants and card associations, scalability, i.e. the ability to offer products across states, was essential to making their investments worthwhile. Inconsistent state laws and varied standards made it difficult to achieve the desired scalability. Field members were thus unwilling to dedicate resources that would not yield a strong return on investments. The Senior Director for Legislative Affairs for The Bankers Roundtable clarified this issue in his testimony:

Institutions are impeded in deploying products and services and from undertaking business plans by the diversity of State laws on authentication. (US Congress, 1998b, p. 12).

Inconsistency in state laws further hindered the attainment of market, societal, and state/federal objectives. First, financial in stitutions argued that large field members could afford the high business and administrative costs associated with complying with different state laws and so could scale up their products to be offered across states. However, small field members could not dedicate such an investment and hence inconsistent state laws hindered their market accessibility. This situation challenged the legislators interests in ensuring equal market entry opportunities through the values of fairness and equality. The President of Digital Signature Trust Company clarified this issue:

Citibank can afford to go State-by-State, seek approvals … it would be very difficult for the smaller banks in this country to participate in that marketplace … absent Federal legislation if we are forced to deal with disparate State laws, a bank such as Zions will probably be precluded from being in the marketplace. (US Congress, 1997c, p. 16).

Second, since digital transactions transcended geographical boundaries, international efforts were undertaken to govern the development of authentication technologies. The inadequate institutional infrastructure in the US resulting from the fragmentation of state laws made it difficult for the US to participate in international efforts in developing a uniform standard for governing e-com merce. This was clear in the testimony of the representative of the Electronic Commerce Forum:

However, before the United States can play a significant role internationally, it is necessary to examine the wisdom of the current multiplicity of state laws. The lack of uniform nationwide rules may inhibit our country's ability to influence de velopments beyond its borders. (US Congress, 1997d, p. 52).

Moreover, field members' argued that their participation in international markets was hampered due to inconsistent state laws which limited their competitiveness and provided foreign competitors, who follow a consistent approach, with a competitive advantage. The General Counsel for Technology and Intellectual Property at Citibank explained:

Foreign countries are allowing electronic authentication without a variety of intra-country rules and regulations. They thus facilitate commerce and the competitiveness of their financial institutions and companies. (US Congress, 1997j, p. 16).

This discourse further pointed to customers and how they, because of the inconsistent state laws, were uncertain regarding the legality of their online purchases and thus hesitant to become involved in e-commerce. The President of RSA Data Security, one of the leading providers of commercial encryption products, mentioned that “… it confuses people and they wonder whether or not digital sig natures are legal.” (US Congress, 1997a, p. 63). This was also highlighted by the General Counsel for Technology and Intellectual Property at Citibank:

50 regimes will confuse customers when it comes to what we have to say to the customer when it comes to what certificates do and what their obligations are and what their rights are. (US Congress, 1997e, p. 13).

Accordingly. field members scaled the impact of inconsistent state laws to different settings. from local and international markets to international collaboration between countries to strengthen their legitimating discourse and persuade legislators of the importance of the issue

Actualizing authentication, integrity, and non-repudiation affordances required decoupling the institutional infrastructure from the technology that authenticates digital transactions. This means that legislators needed to provide the appropriate infrastructure that would legitimate the use of digital signatures while leaving the choice of the technology open to be malleable to changes in authentication technologies. The Deputy General Counsel for the Information Technology Division in the Commonwealth stated:

The appropriate legislative role at this time is one that is very minimal and, in fact, technology-neutral, specifically to avoid the possible consequence of legislating a particular technological implementation which ends up not being the best way to get at security concerns and is not tested by the market as being the most efficient model. (US Congress, 1997g, p. 35).

The Representative of the Electronic Commerce Forum further argued that any field member that has the technical and financia capabilities must not be discouraged from entering the market and an overarching federal legislation would ensure that:

any entity that possesses the technical capability and has the requisite financial resources should not be barred from offering or providing electronic authentication services. (US Congress, 1997f, p. 23).

Increasingly, it appears that the regulatory framework must be modified from one focusing on particular corporate charters and industry segments to one that facilitates entry to the market for new products and services. (US Congress, 1997f, p. 24).

A national standard was thought to create the right institutional infrastructure that allowed the US to participate in internationa efforts and take a leading role. This was clear in the testimony of the Senior Director for Legislative Affairs for The Bankers Roundtable:

Federal action would resolve domestic issues and would create a strong foundation for the United States' actions in the in ternational community. (US Congress, 1997b, p. 31).

In addition, national uniformity would stimulate and coordinate market relations because it would help the e-commerce industry exchange field members in managing their business operations and making investments that would scale up their operations. Visa's Senior Vice President for Corporate Risk Management mentioned:

What Visa and its member financial institutions are looking for most directly is legal certainty … as to the environment in which we are going to make investments and operate. (US Congress, 1998c, p. 7).

The representative of the Electronic Commerce Forum also explained:

global electronic commerce is the final goal and will, in fact, be the full marketplace development of electronic commerce. This means that at some point there will need to be a means of recognizing digital signatures between nations. (US Congress, 1997g, p. 23).

Field members' discursive attempts were successful in mobilizing legislators and resulted in passing the 2000 Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce (E-Sign) Act that nationally legitimated the use of electronic authentication and digital signatures. Following the enactment of the law, the growth of e-commerce sales had increased by 33.5% in the first quarter of 2001 in comparison to the first quarter of 2000 (US Census Bureau, 2001). Different business services took advantage of the new legislation to offer services online. For instance, the manager of the US Postal Service's NetPost Certified program commented that investments in authenticating and conducting postal service transactions online would pay off and be justified with the new law (Chen, 2001). Similarly, mortgage and lending services started to revolutionize their internal processes to become paperless as well as scaling their services to countries outside the US (Crowley. 2002: Hicks. 2001). In a 2000 GAO Report prepared after the passing of the E-Sign Act. Federal Reserve Chairman Alan Greenspan and Comptroller of the Currency John Hawke stated (Greenspan and Hawke 2000: p. 1 - Appendix A, article 29):

The use of electronic signatures … in the financial services industry will likely increase in the future as a result of the imple mentation of the … E-SIGN Act. Since the act promotes the legal validity of electronic signatures, a financial institution may begin to use electronic signatures as evidence of online transactions, such as the acceptance of the terms of a credit agreement by the customer. Further, consumers may begin to use electronic signatures to complete online transactions, such as opening bank accounts, obtaining credit, or establishing an insurance policy or brokerage account.

Similar views about the impact of the E-Sign Act on e-commerce can be found in academic papers, grey literature, newspaper articles, periodicals, and trade publications published before, during, and after the passing of the Act (see for example articles 31, 46, 48 and 69 in Appendix A). ## 6. Discussion & contributions

This paper examined the process by which digital technology is legitimated in an industry exchange field. We provided an analysis of the (then) emergent e-commerce industry exchange field, by using archival data between 1997 and 2001. We examined how members of the field including, financial institutions, card associations, and technology vendors lobbied for federal intervention to legitimate the use of digital signatures and have a unified standard. By explicating the role of digital signature technology affordances in the legitimation process, our paper contributes to an understanding of the legitimation of digital technology in an industry exchange field in several ways.

First, previous research has recognized that digital technology can only be legitimated if participating organizations adhere to common technical standards (Davidson et al., 2015; Hsu et al., 2015; Kaganer et al., 2010). Our research extends these findings, by showing that legitimation in an industry exchange field requires legislation. The disruptive nature of digital technology gives rise to an issue field within an existing industry exchange field. The emergent issue field is formed to negotiate the new issue i.e. how digita technology challenges and (re)shapes existing institutional infrastructure. Once legislation is implemented the issue field dissolves That is, digital technology becomes a standard and is no longer debated as an issue to be resolved. Thus, standards are established not solely through adherence to technical matters, but also by becoming embedded in the extant institutional infrastructure. This is because field members in an industry exchange field (such as the e-commerce field) have very different objectives creating conflicts between them. This heterogeneity (Zietsma et al., 2017) gives rise to the need to create stability across the industry exchange field by legitimating the use of the technology through legislation. Our findings provide an analysis of how heterogeneous field members used the affordances of digital signature technology in their discourse to convince legislators to establish a common standard across all US States.

Specifically, we show how field members use both the challenges and opportunities of the extant institutional infrastructure as conditions (cf. Greenwood, Raynard, Kodeih, Micelotta, & Lounsbury, 2011) for how digital technology can transform the industry exchange field. The industry exchange field of e-commerce in the late 1990s can be considered a relatively “underorganized domain” with little coordinated action between members (Maguire, Hardy, & Lawrence, 2004). For example, field members raised both the challenges with the legal uncertainty across the US States regarding e-commerce transactions, as well as what legal certainty could mean if digital signatures were to be standardized. Accordingly, legitimating emergent disruptive technologies requires institutiona change because the former often challenges the existing institutional infrastructure. At the same time, the required institutional change does not necessarily mean eradicating earlier technology structures and market objectives but rather augmenting and molding them to become aligned with the new forms of operations and interactions arising from emergent technologies. In our case, there was a consensus that the values of fairness and equality and the integrity of the payment system among field members were important. However, there was ambiguity around how to ensure such values in e-commerce. In such fields where the institutional infrastructure shows a limited degree of elaboration (i.e. there are no clear conceptions about whether an action is legitimate or not) but high levels of coherency (i.e. shared understanding of common values and what the future should be like) (cf. Hinings et al., 2017), legitimating digital technologies becomes a matter of aligning the affordances of digital technology with these shared values. Such alignment helps to transform the existing institutional infrastructure.

Our analysis also shows that, although certain field members such as Visa and The Bankers Roundtable held central roles in the legitimation process, because of the affordances of digital signature technology, there was high-risk interdependence between members in the field such that the input of peripheral field members also became important. The diffusion and scale of digital signature technology depended on all field members, not just on the central members. Unless all members joined the e-commerce marketplace, transactions between consumers and producers of e-commerce products and services would be compromised (cf. Adner, 2006). More important for the federal legislators was the point that legal uncertainty would not only compromise local transactions within the US States but also the US's position in international e-commerce marketplaces.

Second, we contribute to recent research examining the role of digital technologies' affordances in the legitimation process (Faik et al., 2020). We show that field members invoked three affordances in their legitimating discourse, namely, authentication, integrity, and non-repudiation. These affordances enabled them to explain the value of digital signature technology in meeting market, societal. and state/federal objectives such as, expanding market reach; serving the public good; providing confidence in digital transactions; and maintaining the integrity of the financial system. Our case shows that affordances can be conditioned by the institutiona infrastructure that supports and enables them. We showed that a supportive institutional infrastructure can enable the scaling ot affordances through sensegiving, translating, and decoupling mechanisms. These affordances scaled up as field members invoked them in their discourse to ascribe meaning to e-commerce and digital signatures. For example, they described the risks of conducting ecommerce without the technology and then explained how digital signatures can address these risks through sensegiving. They also discussed how the technology could be reproduced in other settings, such as conducting business in foreign markets and participating in international efforts that shape e-commerce, thus, translating the affordances of the technology to other contexts. Field members further scaled these affordances by separating the technology specifications from its enabling institutional infrastructure to establish more flexibility across US states, thus, decoupling affordances from local conditions. As the Deputy General Counsel for the Infor mation Technology Division in the Commonwealth stated, “The appropriate legislative role at this time is one that is very minimal and, in fact, technology-neutral” (US Congress, 1997g, p. 35). Through these mechanisms, the affordances were scaled in ways that rendered digital signatures imperative for the e-commerce industry exchange field.

Our findings have important implications for understanding how technology diffusion within digital marketplaces does not follow a top-down approach. Rather field members exert pressure and actively demand institutional change that fosters technology diffusion through legal certainty. The importance of this is seen in the slow adoption of novel technologies such as blockchain where recent research shows that legal uncertainty is among the top barriers to blockchain adoption (Lustenberger et al., 2021). Preparing an institutional infrastructure through legislation that legitimates the use of blockchain is necessary for accelerating the adoption of the technology in a digital marketplace. Enacting such regulation requires utilizing and scaling blockchain affordances through sensegiving, that is, by explaining the potential of the technology to diverse field members, such as providing a decentralized infrastructure that eliminates the need for reconciliations between trading partners, providing data provenance, and enabling a security model that is fault-tolerant and resilient (Lacity, 2018). Translating these affordances could involve emphasizing the wide range of blockchain applications across settings (e.g. healthcare, anti-money laundering, logistics). Finally, decoupling can be enacted by highlighting the existence of multiple types of blockchain technology and hence the need for a regulation that is flexible and technology-neutral. This decoupling is of special importance given that “Innovation in a blockchain component may cause the emergence of new designs in other components of the technology, thereby creating new classes of blockchain technologies” (Spychiger, Tasca, & Tessone, 2021, p 2). At the same time, the scaling of the affordances of blockchain technologies has to be supported by an institutional infrastructure that incorporates unique social norms, technical rules, and other tacit and explicit coordination mechanisms (Lumineau, Wang, & Schilke, 2021).

Beyond blockchain technologies, our analysis of the early emergence and diffusion of e-commerce marketplaces through legislation of the digital signature technology also has important implications for recent regulatory debates around the diffusion of digita platforms such as the Apple App Store, Meta, and Amazon marketplaces. These regulatory debates focus on the alignment of specific digital technology affordances with diverse, often competing objectives. For example, the recent European Digital Markets Act (DMA)<sup>3</sup> seeks to, on the one hand, protect key societal objectives such as the privacy and security of personal data, while ensuring market objectives of fair competition among small and medium-sized businesses, and federal objectives of open innovation. As expert reports on the DMA have noted, however, serving such multiple objectives simultaneously is very challenging requiring additional policies on “forbidden behaviors to which only extreme considerations would justify an exception” and “practices which are in principle considered anti-competitive but for which a pro-competitive justification is possible” (Cabral et al., 2021, p. 3). In other words, just like our analysis of the digital signatures case shows, digital technology affordances need to be associated with the diverse objectives of field members, while highlighting the risks of legal uncertainty in the absence of an enabling institutional infrastructure. As many commentators have already noted, the EU's DMA's efforts to define digital markets' “gatekeepers” by means of size thresholds are ill conceived (Cabral et al., 2021; Jacobides & Lianos, 2021) because they miss the heterogeneity of field members' objectives, as those are afforded by different digital technologies. As argued earlier, peripheral and central field members are closely interdependent and agency is distributed, exactly because of the affordances of digital technology. Such affordances may disrupt established business models, value chains, and organizational processes, warranting new digitally-enabled institutional infrastructures to emerge and diffuse (Hinings et al., 2018).

## 7. Conclusion

In this paper, we examined how digital technology is legitimated in an industry exchange field. We provided an analysis of sec ondary data from congressional public hearings and testimony statements presented at the US Senate and the House of Representatives on the introduction of digital signatures in the e-commerce industry exchange field. We emphasized three affordances of digital signature technology, namely, authentication, integrity, and non-repudiation, and how they were invoked and scaled by field members in their legitimation discourse. We explained that the disruptive nature of digital technology gives rise to an issue field within an existing industry exchange field. The emergent issue field is formed to negotiate the new issue and craft a response to it, after which it dissolves. We further showed that although the affordances of digital technology provide great benefits to different field members, these affordances are conditioned by the institutional infrastructure that enables them.

Our paper has important implications for policy and the regulation of emerging digital marketplaces. First, we highlight the importance of legal certainty in fostering technology diffusion. Legal certainty provides a supportive institutional infrastructure and a safe harbor that protect field members' interests and investment in technology. Legislators need to implement regulation that addresses multiple objectives while leveraging the affordances of emergent digital technologies. Second, field members in institutional fields need to take an active role in building the required institutional infrastructure for their emergent technologies. This role requires an understanding of the affordances of the technology and how they can advance market, societal, and state/federal objectives while acknowledging the importance of scaling these affordances in driving change. Moreover, as building a supportive institutiona infrastructure through legislation takes time, field members need to intervene early to accelerate the diffusion process.

While we acknowledge that the legitimation of digital technologies may not always require legislation, our study shows that legislation may help an industry exchange field resolve disruptive issue fields. For instance, associating affordances with market. societal and state/federal objectives was critical in our case because the legitimating discourse was directed toward legislators. Whether these objectives remain significant in another context is one direction for future studies. In addition, as affordances can be perceived differently by field members, how conflicting views on affordances influence the legitimation discourse is another inter esting area for future research.
