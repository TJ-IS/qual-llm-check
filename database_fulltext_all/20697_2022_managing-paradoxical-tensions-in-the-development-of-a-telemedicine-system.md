# Managing paradoxical tensions in the development of a telemedicine system

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Paradox theory Paradoxical tensions Telemedicine system

## A B S T R A C T

The global pandemic has escalated the demand for telemedicine systems across the world. particularly for vulnerable populations such as the elderly in nursing homes. However, challenges in implementation and high failure rates continue to affect the sustainability and capability of telemedicine systems. This study therefore addresses the question of how to sustain and develop telemedicine systems, and offers a conceptual model developed from longitudinal study data and paradox theory. We found that in the inter-organizational context of telemedicine systems, par adoxical tensions arise from conflict between demands and interests of the telemedicine system versus those of its members. We also identified when the specific tensions of belonging, learning, organizing, and performing are likely to occur. These tensions are addressed through responses, initiated by the hub, that address both system and member level demands and interests through creating collaborative governance, uplifting member capabilities, and targeted resourcing. We further demonstrate the temporal dynamics of how the hub's responses create inter-organizational norms and structures that in turn influence responses to tensions in subsequent phases. We examined variations in members' reactions to the responses, and found that they were influenced by member-specific resource factors, suggesting that while the hub does sustain the development of the telemedicine system through addressing common member demands, there are limits with regard to aspects that are more member-specific. Finally, we show how technology can be both enabler-trigger and enabler-response due to its inherent attributes of malleability and reconfigurability.

## 1. Introduction

The global pandemic that began in 2020 changed the perception of many healthcare providers and governments concerning the use of telemedicine in patient care, especially for the highly vulnerable groups within the community (Bashshur, Doarn, Frenk, Kvedar, & Woolliscroft, 2020). In various countries like the USA and Italy, nursing homes were the ground zero for the COVID-19 pandemic (Barnett & Grabowski, 2020). Many nursing homes were faced with severe staffing and supply shortages while they attempted to protect both patients and healthcare workers from a highly contagious and harmful disease. These conditions in nursing homes created a newfound relevance for telemedicine. Many nursing homes and healthcare organizations now see telemedicine as a critical tool in their ability to continue to provide care for their residents (Cormi, Chrusciel, Laplanche, Dramé, & Sanchez, 2020)

The surge in demand for telemedicine services requires a viable telemedicine system to be in place. Often, there is none as tele medicine projects have a high failure rate (Alaboudi et al., 2016; Cho, Mathiassen, & Gallivan, 2009; Peters, Blohm, & Leimeister, 2015), with some estimating that up to 75% of telemedicine projects are abandoned (Alaboudi et al., 2016). Information systems (IS) research on telemedicine systems have found that implementing technological platforms may not be the main challenge (Cho et al., 2009; Paul, 2010). This is especially so now with the prevalence of sophisticated teleconferencing kits, high speed internet, and webcam-enabled smart devices. Rather, many of these challenges revolve around organizational issues involving the interaction of people, processes, and technology, which result in competing demands.

Telemedicine and IS studies have suggested that these challenges may be conceptualized as paradoxical tensions (Singh, Mathiassen, & Mishra, 2009; Standing, Standing, McDermott, Gururajan, & Kiani Mavi, 2018). Paradoxical tensions have been defined as “contradictory yet interrelated elements that exist simultaneously and persist over time” (Smith & Lewis, 2011). Many of the telemedicine systems are deployed as hub-spoke models, where the hub is a hospital and the spokes are members such as nursing homes (Hansen, Gogan, Baxter, & Garfield, 2019; Singh, Baird, & Mathiassen, 2018). Paradoxical tensions can arise from differing stakeholder goals around the use of technology, leading to a breakdown in collaboration and the failure of the telemedicine systems, especially when the system starts to scale (Hansen et al., 2019; Singh et al., 2018). Paradox theory notes that the deep-seated and persistent nature of paradoxical tensions can, if not adequately managed, undermine organizational sustainability. We argue that paradoxical tensions in telemedicine systems, if not adequately managed, will similarly undermine its sustainability and ability to deliver care. Recognition of tensions and the adoption of appropriate actions can enable telemedicine systems to continue to develop and become sustainable over time. Paradox theory provides explanations for how and why paradoxical tensions arise and suggests broad responses for managing paradoxical tensions.

We therefore draw on paradox theory to answer the research question of how paradoxical tensions in a telemedicine system might be addressed so as to sustain the continuing development of the system. We study this question in the context of a telemedicine system, comprising an acute hospital and seven nursing homes, that has developed and been sustained for almost a decade. We followed closely the development of this telemedicine system from 2011 to 2016; the data collected forms the basis for this study. We kept in touch with the key members of the telemedicine system after our formal study ended. They informed us that even as COVID-19 spread throughout the country and affected other nursing homes' ability to provide care during the pandemic (e.g., physical visits to meet specialists were restricted), the seven nursing homes in this telemedicine system were able to provide continuity of care to their elderly effectively. This paper demonstrates how identifying and addressing the paradoxical tensions that arise in hub-spoke telemedicine systems can enable the development of a viable and scalable telemedicine system that is available to meet the healthcare needs of vulnerable populations even in dire situations.

At the same time, we also contribute to paradox theory with regard to the temporal dynamics in the inter-organizational context. We identified when the specific tensions of belonging, learning, organizing, and performing were likely to occur, due to the conflict between demands and interests of the telemedicine system versus those of its members. We show how the both-and responses rec ommended by paradox theory address both system and member level demands and interests, and further demonstrate the tempora dynamics of how hub's responses create inter-organizational norms and structures that in turn influence responses to tensions in subsequent phases. We also examined variations in members' reactions to the responses and found that they were influenced by member-specific factors. This suggests that when the hub's both-and hub responses do sustain the development of the telemedicine system, they do so through addressing common member demands. There are limits with regard to aspects of demands that are more member-specific. Finally, we show how technology can be both enabler-trigger and enabler-response in different phases due to its inherent attributes of malleability and reconfigurability.

## 2. Theory

Below, we present the prior research in telemedicine and key concepts from paradox theory, which we use as a lens to examine the telemedicine system's development in this study.

## 2.1. Challenges of sustaining a telemedicine system

The term, telemedicine system, refers to the complex sociotechnical system where a diverse set of care providers use medical information exchanged from one site to another via electronic communications to improve a patient's clinical health status (American Telemedicine Association, 2012). While there have been many forms of telemedicine systems (Cho et al., 2009; Singh et al., 2009; Tulu, Chatterjee, & Maheshwari, 2007), our study focuses on chronic care through a hub-spoke telemedicine system, where the hub—usually a hospital—collaborates with the spokes – i.e., other healthcare providers – to deliver care at remote sites. In our case, the hub refers to the specialist department in a hospital and the spokes were nursing homes organizations that worked to jointly deliver healthcare services to their resident elderly patients (Meyers, Gibbs, Thacker, & LaFile, 2012; Shoirah, Wechsler, Jovin, & Jadhav, 2019; Singh et al., 2018).

Early telemedicine system studies have looked at factors for telemedicine system adoption and acceptance such as compatibility of telemedicine system with user practices (Chau & Hu. 2002: Constantinides & Barrett. 2006) and cultural issues (Hanseth & Aanestad. 2003). More recent studies have examined the detailed practices that affect how telemedicine system works e.g., how clinical ex amination and consultations can be carried out through telemedicine for chronic and acute care (Hansen et al., 2019; Nicolini, 2011; Paul, 2010). Some studies have focused on the challenges of implementing and sustaining hub-and-spoke style telemedicine systems, for instance, for tele-stroke telemedicine systems (Cho et al., 2009; Singh et al., 2018) and tele-ophthalmology systems (Srivastava &

Shainesh, 2015). We synthesized their key insights and findings on challenges, and their approaches to implementing and sustaining telemedicine systems.

First, these studies show that one key challenge of hub-spoke telemedicine system is the resource constraints and limitations that most members face. The hub—an acute hospital—has better resources, capabilities, processes, and more professionally trained staff while the spokes or members—e.g., rural hospitals (Cho et al., 2009; Singh et al., 2009; Singh et al., 2018) or medical outposts (Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015)—have significantly limited resources in technology, manpower, and other organizational capabilities. These studies show that successful implementations of hub-spoke telemedicine systems require the hub to “empathize” with the members' current resources and capabilities (Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015), and to ensure that the telemedicine system can accom modate members' constraints and limitations (Cho et al.. 2009). The hub may need to either design telemedicine solutions that are suitable for the members or provide physical and financial resources for the members (Cho et al., 2009; Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015).

Second, the studies also highlight misalignment between the hub and members' processes, given organizational and institutional differences (Cho et al., 2009; Singh et al., 2018). In Cho et al.'s (2009) case, the hub was not able to fully align their process with the members' because their telemedicine process was developed solely by the hub without any inputs from the members. In another case, processes were misaligned because the telemedicine hub and members' existing insurance payment plans were incompatible, thus impeding the telemedicine implementation (Singh et al., 2018). To resolve some of these issues, the studies suggested that hub and members work on aligning their processes by dedicating a coordinating team for the hub-spoke (Cho et al., 2009: Singh et al.. 2018)

Finally, Singh et al.'s (2018) study show that hub and members may even have different perceptions of the challenges to the telemedicine system's sustainability. They found that due to these different perceptions, there were misalignment in the goals and approaches to resolving challenges. For example, some members perceived that the telemedicine system benefited the hub more than them, which resulted in conflicts between the hub and members. Also, the hub and members may experience different challenges at different phases of the telemedicine system development. Moreover, there were significant variations in how each member reacted to the telemedicine challenges. depending on their existing resources and conditions. As such. Singh et al. (2018) suggested that tele medicine system sustainability requires a partner governance structure that will conduct “continuous assessment and management of cooperation as well as coordination tasks to develop sustainable partnerships” (p. 186).

Together, these studies surfaced a variety of challenges that many telemedicine systems face and recommended various targeted solutions in response to each challenge. Nonetheless, despite the various solutions to deal with sustainability challenges such as re sources and manpower constraints, coordination and collaboration risks, or misalignment in processes and goals between hub and members, telemedicine systems may still fail if not continually and actively managed (Singh et al., 2018; Standing et al., 2018). This suggests that deeper, more systemic causes may persist even if these identified challenges have been addressed through recommended practices and processes. For example, there may be inherent conflicts between the hub's and members' identities, given their different norms, values, and expectations (Singh et al., 2018; Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015). Another example is the ongoing need to balance new and existing systems and processes at the members' end (Cho et al., 2009; Singh et al., 2009). Further, there may also be emergent competing goals and interests between hub and members (Singh et al., 2018); in such cases, it is unclear when the recommended approaches to manage challenges are appropriate. For instance, in Singh et al.'s (2018) study, the hub's and members' risk management mitigations were analyzed separately and it was proposed that hub and members can unilaterally act to sustain the telemedicine system in different phases. However, they also found one hub-spoke case where, in spite of mitigations taken by the hub, the collaboration ceased after the initial phase. Hence, it is unclear when mitigations should be enacted by the hub or by the members together with the hub, given that hub-spoke telemedicine system development is mainly driven by the hub (Cho et al., 2009; Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015).

## 2.2. Paradox theory and telemedicine systems challenges

Telemedicine studies have suggested that telemedicine system challenges may be conceptualized as paradoxical tensions (Singh et al., 2009; Standing et al., 2018). Paradox theory provides us with a comprehensive way to characterize the tensions that arise in telemedicine systems. A seminal review of a large number of paradox studies have resulted in a robust categorization of four types of deep seated tensions—belonging, learning, organizing, and performing (Smith & Lewis. 2011). Whilst these have been identified largely from organization, team, and individual level studies, we find that they resonate with the challenges identified in the telemedicine literature and with inter-organizational systems.

The belonging tension is defined as the need to simultaneously maintain individual distinction and also be homogeneous as a col lective (Smith & Lewis, 2011). The telemedicine literature has noted the risk posed by the conflicting demands of members' identity, values, and norms versus that of the collective (Singh et al., 2018).

The learning tension reflects the contradictions between past and future knowledge, as well as radical versus incremental im provements (Smith & Lewis, 2011). We anticipate that staff at both the hub and member organizations, in order to work well together over the telecommunications link to deliver care, would need to acquire significant new competencies that may be in conflict with existing expectations of professional competencies.

The organizing tension reflects competing processes and structures deployed to achieve goals versus existing processes and structures (Smith & Lewis, 2011). The telemedicine literature has noted the challenges of using new telemedicine processes (Cho et al., 2009) that often require increased levels of coordination among care providers across organizational boundaries compared to the existing processes and structures focused on intraorganizational coordination processes

The performing tension reflects the competing strategies and goals of stakeholders (Smith & Lewis, 2011). In telemedicine, it is likely that the hub and members would have differing goals, with the hub being more focused on achieving system-level goals, while members would be more concerned with achieving their own organization's goals.

Why do such paradoxical tensions arise? Paradox theory notes that tensions arise due to contradictions in embedded structures, cultures, artifacts, and practices (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2017). They are often latent and become salient to the actors when certain triggers occur, leading actors to recognize that related organizational elements (e.g., practices) are contradictory (Knight & Paroutis, 2017). The triggers are change (usually organizational shifts due to new opportunities or threats in the environment), plurality (sit uations where there are divergent views, goals or competing strategies of stakeholders) and/or scarcity (resource limitations) (Schad, Lewis, Raisch, & Smith, 2016; Smith & Lewis, 2011).

## 2.3. Managing paradoxical tensions

In addition to providing a systematic way to identify tensions and their triggers. the paradox literature also provides a theoretical basis for thinking about appropriate and inappropriate responses to tensions. Although these responses have been developed largel from organization, team, and individual level studies, we have adapted and applied them to the inter-organizational context. Collectively, how the system responds to the emerging tensions can significantly influence the performance and sustainability of the telemedicine system.

The paradox literature conceptualizes two broad types of responses—defensive or “either-or” responses, which can lead to vicious cycles of increasing tension and poor performance, and active or “both-and” responses that manage tensions and enable sustainability (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2017; Schad et al., 2016; Smith & Lewis, 2011). Either-or responses include splitting (separating contra dictory elements either temporally or spatially), suppressing (prioritizing one pole and allowing it to dominate or overrule the other pole of a paradox), or opposing (parties supporting contradictory poles of a paradox engaging in active confrontation and conflict) (Jarzabkowski, Lˆe, & Van de Ven, 2013). Both-and responses require attending to both ends of the paradox rather than choosing one over the other. Several approaches have been described—synthesis, which accommodates both poles by seeking synergies between them (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2017; Smith & Lewis, 2011), and purposeful iteration of temporal or spatial separation (Poole and van de Van, 1989). While the either-or response of splitting seeks primarily to keep the opposing forces apart, the intention of both-and separation is to integrate and balance the tensions in the longer term. Hence temporal separation is explicitly seen as temporary (Raisch, Hargrave, & Van De Ven, 2018), and attention must be paid to transition points (Poole & Van de Ven, 1989), with opposing forces being balanced through purposeful, rhythmic shifts between periods (Klarner & Raisch, 2013). Similarly for spatial separation, where opposite poles of the paradoxical tension are aligned with different organizational units or members, integration mechanisms are thoughtfully enacted, such as integrative leaders and a common governing body across the units (Smith & Tushman, 2005).

Studies at the organizational level find that top management plays a critical role in how the organization responds as paradoxical tensions become salient. In telemedicine systems, this role is usually played by the hub. If the hub takes no constructive actions to address members' concerns, paradox theory suggests that the tendency is for the hub to respond with either-or responses. This looks beneficial in the short term as it reduces cognitive inconsistencies, anxieties, confusion, and eases decision-making; however, this undermines long-term success (Lewis, 2000; Smith, Binns, & Tushman, 2010). Either-or responses attend to only one end of the paradoxical tension and allow pressure to build on the opposite end. which over time negatively impact the organization's (or in our context, the telemedicine system's) ability to perform to its full potential (Smith et al., 2010). Both-and responses, on the other hand, require the telemedicine system to accept the paradox. The process of acceptance begins when the hub and members recognize that the tensions are persistent and the poles of the paradox are interdependent (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2017). Acceptance paves the way for both-and responses to the paradoxical tension as it generates creativity (pursuit of conflicting goals, broadening of search, questioning of assumptions) and synergy (coordinating elements in a way that is mutually advantageous through joint action, structures, and complex leadership actions) (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2017).

## 2.4. Hub and member dynamics in the development of telemedicine systems

Bevond understanding the possible responses to the paradoxical tensions that arise in telemedicine systems, there is also the equally important question of how the tensions and responses interact over time and affect outcomes. The paradox literature. while noting the relative paucity of research in the dynamics of paradoxes (Schad et al., 2016), nonetheless provides some helpful insights. Smith and Lewis (2011) proposed a Dynamic Equilibrium model that links the above concepts of tensions and responses to outcomes. As discussed earlier, paradoxical tensions are latent until change, plurality, and/or scarcity make these tensions salient to actors. The salient tensions give rise to managerial responses that may be either-or or both-and, as previously described. Thus, managing paradox is a recurring process of identification and response (Ismail & Johnson, 2019; Jay, 2013; Mason & Doherty, 2016).

Either-or responses are defensive in nature, and attempt to maintain consistency, usually with the past (Smith & Lewis. 2011). This reinforces a drive to established routines, increasing the emphasis on consistency (Lewis, 2018). This results in vicious cycles of increasing tension, problems, and the growing inability to move forward (Jay, 2013; Smith & Lewis, 2011), thus leading to organi zational decline over time (Sundaramurthy & Lewis, 2003). Both-and responses, on the other hand, do not attempt to seek an artificia stability but rather accept dynamism (Lewis, 2018). Virtuous cycles result when there are practices that embed routines and habits that encourage learning, creativity, flexibility and productivity, and contribute to long-term sustainability (Jay, 2013; Smith & Lewis 2011). However, the threat of vicious cycles is ever present and managers must therefore be vigilant and responsive to re-emerging salient tensions (Smith & Lewis, 2011).

Much less attention has been given to paradox at the inter-organizational level. Of the two most thorough and influential reviews of the literature on paradox theory, Smith and Lewis (2011) only discussed organization, team and individual settings, while Schad et al.'s (2016) found only four studies (out of 133) at the inter-organizational level. Some were studies of strategic alliances that applied paradox theory to argue that tensions lead to instabilities and, when the opposing forces are not in balance, to eventual dissolution of the alliance results (Chung & Beamish, 2010; Das & Teng, 2000). In the context of the alliances (joint ventures, equity investments, R&D agreements, buyer-supplier relationships), opposing forces of cooperation versus competition, rigidity versus flexibility, and short-term versus long-term orientation have been identified (Das & Teng, 2000). A recent study applied the paradox theory lens to reevaluate over a hundred inter-organizational value co-creation studies (Niesten & Stefan, 2019), and affirmed that as at the organi zational level, inter-organizational tensions were made salient by plurality, scarcity, and change. They also identified governance mechanisms, organizational capabilities, and value appropriation strategies as factors that potentially contribute to virtuous cycles (Niesten & Stefan, 2019).

Studies of ambidexterity in interorganizational settings offers some insights into how paradoxical tensions might be managed in telemedicine systems. One set of opposing forces studied at the inter-organizational level is exploitation versus exploration. Organi zations may seek to address this paradoxical tension by applying ambidexterity through alliances with other organizations in order to pursue both exploration and exploitation. The ability to manage partners (Partanen, Kohtamaki, ¨ Patel, & Parida, 2020), absorb external knowledge, have some slack for experimentation, and senior management's cognitive and behavioral inclination are factors that influence ambidexterity both within and across organizations (Lavie, Stettner, & Tushman, 2010). Such ambidexterous processes and practices are consistent with both-and responses to paradoxical tensions more generally.

Of particular interest to our study is the observation that small-medium enterprises (SMEs), similar to many telemedicine member organizations, are more resource constrained and often having more limited competencies (Rosenbusch, Brinckmann, & Bausch, 2011). They find it very challenging to manage ambidexterity in inter-organizational supply chain relationships (Partanen et al., 2020), and thus do not experience benefits to performance (Kauppila, 2015). The literature suggests that SMEs attempting to achieve ambidexterity through inter-organizational relationships will need to rely on relational, collaborative, long-term relationships with a limited number of partners (Adams, Khoja, & Kauffman, 2012; Beekman & Robinson, 2004) . Another way that small firms may pursue both exploration and exploitation is by being part of a collaborative ecosystem. A study of the Parisian entrepreneurial ecosystem highlighted the critical role played by the hub (Renault in this case) in initiating and developing collaborative governance with 22 start-ups (Lo ˆ & Theodoraki, 2020).

In the context of telemedicine systems, building on these insights about paradox in inter-organizational settings can help to address the dynamics of how the hub's responses to tensions affect not only the telemedicine system as a whole, but also member organizations over time. As member heterogeneity is a given in many telemedicine systems, there is also a need to understand how the responses affect different members, and how members' reactions influence telemedicine system outcomes. In doing so, we seek also to deepen our knowledge of the dynamics of paradox at the inter-organizational level.

## 3. Research design and methods

We chose a single longitudinal case study for our research as we were investigating a “contemporary phenomenon within its reallife context” (Yin, 2003). The goal of this case study is to develop an in-depth explanation for how a telemedicine system was sustained over the course of its development and implementation through responses to paradoxical tensions. This approach is appropriate as it is designed for middle range theory development (Eisenhardt, 1991; Walsham, 1995), where we can draw on our deep contextual un derstanding to unpack the how and why of the focal phenomenon (Yin, 2003).

Given our research question was how paradoxical tensions in a telemedicine system might be addressed to sustain the system's development, we selected a relatively successful telemedicine proiect for our single case study (Berge. 2018: Singh et al., 2018: Sri: vastava & Shainesh, 2015). First, the case study involved a telemedicine system—“SeniorCare”—that was organized in a hub-spoke model. Specifically, the hub, an acute hospital (Acute North<sup>1</sup>), had enrolled seven nursing homes that varied in terms of their affili ation, size, location, and resources (see Table 1). A technology platform was established for joint delivery of services, and cospecialized resources (e.g., knowledge) and shared processes (teleconsultation) were developed.

Second, we had rich access to staff from Acute North (the hub) and the nursing homes involved in SeniorCare, We were involved in studying the project over an extended period of time (over six years)—from its early inception to the growth of the telemedicine system. Third, the telemedicine system development process experienced several instances of paradoxical tensions. Finally, through our involvement with the project, we gained a detailed and in-depth understanding of how the hub's responses to tensions interacted with members' reactions and contributed to sustaining the telemedicine system.

## 3.1. Research context

A little over a decade ago, the government of a developed Asian country initiated a restructuring of its public healthcare system to promote healthcare under a collaborative model, which would align and coordinate the efforts of public sector hospitals and other providers. Acute North was the anchor public hospital for the northern region, which served 700,000 people. With almost 600 beds, Acute North was equipped with state-of-the-art technology and equipment and offered a range of services such as acute and emergency care, geriatrics, cardiology, nursing, and outpatient clinics through their in-house specialists, who are employees of the hospital. One group of providers that Acute North was expected to work with was nursing homes catering to the elderly and chronically ill patients. Each nursing home, on average, managed 200 residents. Many of these nursing homes were run by charitable organizations. Most had manpower constraints and limited access to specialist care due to limited funding. Each nursing home had between 15 and 17 nurses and about 40 nursing aides. These nurses were not trained to provide diagnostics and treatments for the aged. While these homes usually had a general practitioner (GP) visiting them two to three times a week, many patients suffered from dementia or other chronic illnesses that required specialist treatment. Nursing homes were dependent on acute care hospitals to provide specialist services, which were quite costly and required the nursing homes residents to be transported to the hospital for the specialist visits.

## 3.2. Data collection

Data was collected from various sources using multiple methods to ensure reliability (Eisenhardt. 1989). From 2011 to 2013, one of the authors was invited to observe SeniorCare's workgroup meetings as well as conduct exploratory interviews with Acute North and the nursing home members to gain insights into the issues and interactions among the nursing homes and Acute North (see Table 2). Since the telemedicine system was initiated in 2010, we also collected project archival data from December 2010 to December 2016. The archival data consisted of weekly and quarterly meeting minutes, quarterly progress reports, technology updates, annual reports of nursing homes and Acute North, senior management presentations, news and research articles published by Acute North and training documents. There was a total of 233 documents comprising 1449 pages in various formats such as Microsoft Word documents, PDF files, Microsoft Excel sheets, graphical images and slide presentation decks.

In early 2014, the authors proposed a systematic study to explore the development of the telemedicine system as it had developed significantly and participants were more ready to discuss their experiences. Once this formalized study was approved. we used the archival data of attendance lists from meeting minutes to prepare an initial interview list of key people involved in the telemedicine system. This list was shared with Acute North for verification and to ensure the relevant people were included. We conducted a total of 49 semi-structured interviews with representatives from both Acute North and the nursing homes from November 2014 to November 2016 (see Table 2). The interview questions focused on the challenges and benefits of the telemedicine system, relationships with partners, key decisions and how they were made, and how these have changed over time. Each interview lasted between one to two hours. At least two of the authors were present for all the interviews. Each interview was audio recorded and we also took detailed notes. In addition, we continued to participate in SeniorCare workgroup meetings and to have informal meetings and email in teractions with Acute North's clinicians and operations team to discuss the data and our findings.

## 3.3. Data analysis

Our data analysis was conducted in three stages and was aimed at understanding how the hub managed the challenges in initiating, developing, and sustaining the telemedicine system. In the first stage of analysis, we drew on the interviews to understand the actors (hub and members), their actions, challenges, sequence of telemedicine system outcomes, and the key conditions of the project. We triangulated this chronological account with the project's archival data and wrote a case report that was verified by various individuals in the hub. Their feedback and clarifications increased our confidence in the accuracy of the report (Golden-Biddle & Locke, 1993). This first stage helped clarify the telemedicine project phases—phase 1 (initiating the telemedicine system), phase 2 (developing telemedicine system capabilities), and phase 3 (leveraging the telemedicine system)—and the actions taken by the hub in each phase. We used temporal bracketing to help us understand how the hub actions interacted with project conditions and different types of challenges within and across the phases of the telemedicine project (Langley, 1999).

As we iterated between the data and extant literature on telemedicine systems, we realized that the hub's responses to these challenges were critical to how the telemedicine system developed. We noted the observation by two telemedicine studies (Singh et al., 2009; Standing et al., 2018) that paradox theory could be a useful lens. We adopted it as our theoretical lens as we found that it resonated with our empirical data since the challenges manifested as competing demands or paradoxical tensions. For example, the first challenge we identified was the difference in the professional identities of Acute North and the nursing homes. However, on further analysis, we found that this challenge manifested as a belonging tension as Acute North and the nursing homes had to manage their individual identities and concurrently develop a new collective identity.

In the second stage of analysis, we drew on paradox literature (Schad et al., 2016; Smith & Lewis, 2011) to code the interview data for the types of paradoxical tensions, the trigger for the tensions, and the hub's responses (Kirsch, 2004). For example, we coded “existing member knowledge, skills, and modes of learning were significantly different from the new knowledge and modes of learning required for telemedicine system to operate” as a form of learning tension, while we coded organizing tension as that which arose due to the different and conflicting demands of the members' existing organizational processes versus the telemedicine system's joint processes. For each tension we also coded the conditions that triggered the tension (e.g., technology change, scarcity) and the re sponses taken by the hub to mitigate the tension. For example, for organizing tensions, the trigger was scarcity of trained telegeriatrics nurses to adjust to new joint processes

While coding for the hub's responses, we were sensitized by theory to the possibility of both-and (addressing both ends of the tension) and either-or responses (focusing in a sustained way on one end of the tension). Interestingly in our analysis of the case interviews, we found primarily both-and responses by the hub and no either-or responses. For example. both-and responses include how the hub dealt with belonging tensions by building a telemedicine system identity and leveraging relationships with trusted representatives so as to respect members' identities. After coding for the tensions, their triggers, and the different hub's responses, we focused on the impact of responses on the tensions and telemedicine system outcomes in each phase. While in general, the outcomes for the telemedicine system were positive, as theorized in extant paradox literature, we observed variations across the different members. Again, we iterated between the data and extant literature on members' roles in a telemedicine system. This led us to the third stage.

In the third stage of analysis, we wanted to get a closer perspective of members' (nursing homes) reactions to the hub's responses and how that related to the telemedicine system outcomes. We coded the nursing home interviews while triangulating back with hub interviews that were relevant to the nursing homes' reactions. As there was not much literature to guide us, the data was open coded to better understand the types of members' reactions (Miles. Huberman. & Saldaña. 2014). From this open coding, we observed the following reactions to hub's responses in each of the phases. For the initiating phase, those who voluntarily “joined” were naturally members compared to those who were invited and either rejected the invitation or quit the project after some interactions. For developing capabilities phase, with respect to learning tensions, we surfaced two reactions: “steady” where there was a steady participation in the hub's training program and “inconsistent” where members' participation fluctuated. For organizing tensions, we noted “appropriate” reactions from members who were able to provide appropriate patients for teleconsultations and “inappropriate” reactions from members who faced selection problems, such as selecting patients who did not require specialist care for tele consultations or not scheduling enough patients for teleconsultations. (See Appendix Table 2). For leveraging phase, we noted that al members reacted in the same way, in that all participated consistently in the hub's program.

After the open coding, we compared across the members' reactions, and iterated between the data and literature to surface con ditions that influenced the variations among members' reactions (Miles et al., 2014). We then compared the hub's responses across the three phases and that led us to observe how responses in later phases (2 and 3) were influenced by the hub's responses from earlier phases. This enabled us to conceptualize the notions of inter-organizational norms (e.g., partnership, mutual respect, and helpfulness) and inter-organizational structures (e.g., collaborative governance forum, informal communication channels) as part of the dynamics of tensions and responses across time.

## 4. Findings

We present findings in a temporal sequence to highlight the paradox dynamics influencing the development of the telemedicine system. In particular, we show the causality and temporality related to the rise of tensions, hub's responses to the tensions, emergent norms and structures, and variations in members' reactions. Figure 1 provides an overview of the three phases of SeniorCare's development.

## 4.1. Phase 1—initiating the telemedicine system

In 2010, Acute North observed that the chronic shortage of beds in their geriatric ward was exacerbated by unnecessary transfers from nursing homes. The head of the geriatrics department proposed a SeniorCare telemedicine service that would provide elderly nursing home residents access to specialist care through video-conferencing technology. A teleconferencing system to connect Acute North to the geographically dispersed nursing homes was proposed and funding was obtained from the Ministry of Health (MOH) (US \$1.5 million for 5 years). The Integrated Care Agency (ICA) was assigned to monitor this service.

Tension: Belonging tensions became salient as Acute North began recruiting nursing homes to participate in the proposed tele medicine system. While the teleconsultation service would not require additional payment from the nursing homes (two monthly teleconsultation sessions were offered free with paid onsite specialist visits from Acute North specialists), nursing homes had distinctive identities and were wary of the SeniorCare technology-related emergent identity as exemplified by the hub, Acute North.

Acute North's identity was that of a large, well-funded government hospital staffed by highly trained medical specialists and administrative professionals and focused on short term acute care. On the other hand, the initial nursing homes approached by SeniorCare were charitable organizations, funded by religious orders, and relatively resource constrained. Their focus was on providing long term care for elderly residents.

“The relationship we have with residents and their families is amazing. We call them by their names. It's a long-term relationship with them. We never leave them unattended. They are part of our family” (Nurse Director, NH3)

Nursing homes could not identify with the emerging telemedicine system and had preconceptions about acute hospitals:

“Nursing homes see acute hospitals as big brothers who have the money. When acute hospital people go to nursing homes, they tend to be proud, aloof and look down on nurses and smelly homes.”

(Operations Team, Acute North)

Without an initial group of nursing homes, the telemedicine system would not get off the ground

Responses: To address this tension, which threatened the viability of the telemedicine system, Acute North enacted the following responses.

Leveraging boundary spanners: In the face of nursing homes' reluctance, Acute North's geriatrics department head approached NH1's nurse director directly as he had worked with her when she was a nurse at another acute hospital. NH1's nurse director was open to considering collaboration and the potential benefits, in part because she had spent many years in a similar acute hospital.

“We networked very well with the geriatrics department of Acute North as their geriatricians supported us in specialist care.”

(Nurse Director, NH1)

The department head also assured NH1's nurse director that her nursing home's values and norms would be considered as the SeniorCare system developed. As a result. NH1 became the first to join the telemedicine system. Reflecting on this first success. Acute North realized that they could draw on other individuals who had extensive experience working in both acute hospitals and nursing homes. Going forward, Acute North relied on their senior operations manager, who had worked in a community hospital that had deep ties with nursing homes, to serve as the main contact.

“Being from a voluntary welfare organization, I leveraged this to show them (nursing homes) that I could relate to them. I knew the dark side. Then I also vouched for doctors in Acute North showing them as true clinicians and the fact that they care.” (Operations Manager, Acute North)

Creating formal collaborative governance: To assure members that their values, norms, and needs would be considered as the SeniorCare telemedicine system developed, Acute North created a joint working forum, comprising representatives from each nursing home as well as doctors, clinical educators, and operations staff from Acute North. This goal for the working forum can be seen in the quote below.

“We have a big meeting involving all the nursing homes. We have to involve them in decision making.” (Geriatrician, Acute North) This formal collaborative governance mechanism enabled the new collective identity of SeniorCare—which focused on delivering improved care to elderly nursing home residents through innovative collaboration over videoconference—to gradually emerge.

Initiating informal communication: Although the Acute North doctors and managers were open and empathetic, some nursing homes' representatives were reluctant to share their concerns in the joint working forums. The reticence was due in part to the status gap between the highly qualified specialists and operations manager on one side and the less qualified nursing home representatives on the other. Further, these representatives could not fully reflect the concerns of all the nursing home nurses. Acute North therefore com plemented the formal governance with informal communication channels between the nursing homes and Acute North's staff, and encouraged nursing home staff to talk to the Acute North telemedicine team about their concerns. Acute North arranged team visits to the nursing homes so as to familiarize their team with the nursing homes and to “appreciate and understand what nursing homes do” (Meeting minutes, December 2010). Through both formal and informal interactions, the nursing homes described Acute North staff as displaying an attitude of humility, equal partnership, and helpfulness.

“When we ask for resource, they are very helpful. (Acute North staff have) very good attitude. They tell us, ‘Don't worry, I am not causing you trouble. I am here to help you.’” (Nurse, NH4)

Nursing homes' reactions: The responses mitigated the belonging tension between the hub and several nursing homes, with the result that the first four nursing homes (NH1, NH2, NH3, and NH4) were willing to join the telemedicine system. Once enrolled in SeniorCare, these nursing homes began to participate in both the formal collaborative governance and informal communication channels to jointly create shared identity for the emerging telemedicine system.

“We have quarterly meetings and use this platform to ask nursing homes for feedback. Nursing homes participate a lot. We have to customize to the pace and standards of nursing homes.”

(Operations Team, Acute North)

However, not all NHs responded by joining the SeniorCare system—three other nursing homes declined to join in Phase 1. Two of the homes were already participating in geriatric services offered by another acute hospital.

“There are still NHs that still want to stay at arms' length, do not want interference. They would want to keep their collaboration with acute hospital in their cluster in the future. So we are also mindful of the invisible boundaries between clusters and didn't want to step over.”

(Operations Team, Acute North)

The third nursing home participated during Phase 1 but dropped out when their internal senior manager (champion) left for another job.

Tensions mitigated: As the first four nursing homes engaged in the formal and informal interactions via the collaborative gover. nance mechanism and other communication channels, SeniorCare was able to develop its identity as an interorganizational tele medicine system over time. SeniorCare demonstrated with the first four members that nursing homes did not have to radically change their own organizational identity in order to be part of the SeniorCare telemedicine system. The ongoing structures of collaborative governance and informal communications also helped to integrate the newer nursing homes into the telemedicine systems. Hence, from November 2014 to April 2015, NH5, 6, and 7 came on board, with relatively less recruitment effort. For example, the senior nursing director at NH5 learned about SeniorCare at a geriatric conference and was interested in joining. NH6 and NH7 quickly signed on to SeniorCare when they were approached by a member of the SeniorCare telemedicine team.

## 4.2. Phase 2—developing telemedicine system capabilities

The nursing homes were not equipped with broadband internet connectivity or teleconferencing capabilities. Acute North's in formation technology (IT) team was tasked to gather requirements and explore various low-cost technology solutions, such as a software-based solution installed in webcam-enabled laptops linked via mobile broadband.

“Sound must be crystal clear... We need (high definition) HD, very good video and good sound that can even capture silent speakers and also when you are typing the keyboard sound has to be eliminated.” (IT Team, Acute North)

Acute North then decided to install a dedicated teleconferencing system to meet teleconsultation requirements, piloting this in NH1. As the technology was being put into place, there was also a realization that for the telemedicine system to be effective, the nursing home nurses would need to be the “hands and eyes” of the Acute North specialists on the other end of the telecommunications link. This would require reskilling the nursing home nurses as well as implementing shared processes to coordinate care across the telemedicine system.

Acute North's geriatric specialists therefore created a customized training course to train the nursing home nurses—the first in the country to focus on common medical conditions in nursing homes (e.g., dementia, pressure ulcers).

“The TeleG<sup>2</sup> course was spread over nine months, with each weekly session for four hours.”

(Clinical Educator, Acute North)

The Acute North telemedicine team then worked closely with nursing homes to establish new shared processes of teleconsultation and reporting. Specifically, this included ways to select patients and send their medical information to Acute North's telemedicine team before teleconsultations. On the day of the teleconsultation, the process required nurses to set up the teleconsultation equipment and conduct the teleconsultation sessions with the specialist from Acute North.

“Then they need to prepare the machine and materials 20 minutes prior to the tele consultation. If there is a problem in the system, we give one-hour leeway so that (Acute North) IT can respond.” (Clinical Educator, Acute North)

The process ended with nursing homes sending their notes to Acute North's telemedicine team, who consolidated them with Acute North's specialist notes and saved a digital copy of the patient's case notes. Acute North telemedicine team and the nursing homes also established a monthly reporting process to capture key indicators such as the number of hospitalizations, consultations, and trained nurses, which were reported to ICA.

Tensions: Learning, organizing, and belonging tensions became salient as SeniorCare moved into capabilities development Learning tensions first became salient as SeniorCare rolled out training programs for the nursing home nurses assisting with the tele consultations. Existing modes of learning in the nursing homes (manual, hands-on, and on-the-job) conflicted with SeniorCare's mode of learning, which was more technologically advanced, classroom based, and clinical. The knowledge and skills emphasized in SeniorCare's training was viewed as more theoretical and diagnostic, whereas the nurses were accustomed to learning more practica and basic, on-the-iob nursing skills at their nursing homes. The new training programs required them to quickly learn some technological skills as well as a medical syllabus that was roughly equivalent to that of a third year medical student. Finally, scheduling of the four-hour training sessions often clashed with the nurses' work shift.

“One early feedback of the syllabus was that it was too overwhelming and medically oriented. Also, different nursing homes have different staff schedules, so sessions had to be split to work into the nurses' schedule” (Clinical Educator, Acute North)

Organizing tensions became salient as the nursing homes and Acute North began implementing teleconsultations. The organizing tension was due to the oft conflicting demands of the nursing homes' existing organizational processes and of the telemedicine system's joint processes. Nursing homes' work involved many manual tasks with little communication between physicians and nursing home nurses. However, with teleconsultations, nurses were required to prepare patient history for each scheduled patient, work closely with the specialists (even adapting to each specialists style of working through the telemedicine system), keep a record of patients healthcare incidents and outcomes (e.g., number of falls, pressure sores), and report it to the ICA.

“They should pick up the case 1-2 days before so that they can maximize geriatrician's time (others GP can handle). They should communicate patient history to doctors and conduct simple physical exam like palpate patient's abdomen.” (Clinical Educator, Acute North)

Belonging tensions: There were belonging tensions for the nurses, as the new skills and processes required for telemedicine chal lenged their identity norms and values in terms of now having to interact with physicians more actively in a partnering role, and through a video interface. Whereas their typical nursing home work and norms are more passive and physical – as receivers of instructions from physicians who examine the patients themselves and communicate in person to the nurses, the new teleconsultation sessions required close collaboration between the nursing home staff, Acute North's clinicians, and operation team to ensure the jointl enacted teleconsultation was successful

Resource scarcity exacerbated both learning and organization tensions. The nurse directors were hesitant to send their staff for training during their shifts since that took staff away from their daily work. Some nursing homes were struggling to cope with high turnover among the nursing staff. When the teleconsultations were implemented, the nurses' already heavy workload became heavier. Nurse directors were also burdened with scheduling teleconsultations, following-ups after teleconsultations, and monthly reporting.

Responses: Acute North responded to the tensions through targeted resourcing, tailoring, and articulation of value.

Targeted resourcing: To address the learning tension. doctors who were from the same country as many of the nursing home nurses were hired as clinical educators. According to the nursing home nurses. these clinical educators could relate better to them and empathize with their difficulties. Clinical educators were tasked to provide closer “hand-holding" during actual teleconsultation sessions and to conduct small group coaching on patient assessment through on-the-job training at each nursing home.

“We got two clinical educators who were doctors in their own country (same as many nursing homes' nurses). These clinical educators evolved the courses as they learnt more of nursing challenges.” (Geriatrician, Acute North)

Targeted resourcing was also enacted to address organizing tensions. From their interactions in the forums, Acute North came to understand that nursing homes' heavy workload and manpower scarcity could not be ignored. The team obtained ICA funding to hire additional administrative staff to prepare the monthly reports so the nursing home staff no longer needed to

Tailoring to participants: Learning and organizing tensions were also addressed through evolving the initial design of the training program and the shared processes for teleconsultation. The training program was redeveloped in 2013, with inputs from the nurses and nurse directors about the learning challenges. These discussions were enabled by the working forums and informal communication at lower levels, which had been created in response to the belonging tension in the initial phase. Regarding learning tensions, Acute North

tailored both the training content and mode:

“We changed the course to be more practical and nursing oriented and tested the learning through four practical stations such as reflection on case summaries, diagnosing interventions through sample photographs and symptoms. We also split the session into two twohour sessions to avoid their saturation.”

(Clinical Educator, Acute North)

“I really appreciate the training staff. We also give feedback about the training and the challenges. For second batch they have created more modules. They added some modules which are more relevant.

(Nurse Director, NH3)

Through the working forum, Acute North also worked with the nurse directors to tailor the pre- and post- teleconsultation proc esses—for example, reducing the length and complexity of the forms required to capture pre- and post- teleconsultation patient in formation. This further reduced the nurses' load when organizing SeniorCare teleconsultations.

It was also found that conducting teleconsultations for bedbound patients was difficult as the teleconference equipment was placed in a dedicated conference room. This exacerbated organizing tensions as nurses struggled to move bedbound patients to these rooms. Realizing this, Acute North's IT team made the system mobile, by customizing a trolley and mounting the tele-conference equipment on it. However, the initial design was less than ideal—nurses found it hard to move around as it was heavy and had protruding wires. The system was then refined by making it lighter and adding drawers for holding the accessories and tidying away the wires.

“If you ask all vendors at that point in time, they always say you must have a dedicated teleconsult room, must be sound-proof best lighting everything. No way... this is not possible because we went to see on ground, some patients I don't think you should move them…so we bring the system to you.”

(IT Team, Acute North)

Articulating value to participants and their supervisors: Acute North telemedicine team also made presentations to the training and joint working forums to explain how the training and participation in teleconsultations would raise the nurses' professionalism through improving their skills and abilities. This enabled the nurse directors to help resolve the belonging tensions experienced by their nurses.

“They (NHs) have started to see the relevance and value of teleconsultations and are now sending a lot of students” (Clinical Educator, Acute North)

Nursing homes' reactions: While all participating nursing homes did send their nurses for training and adopted new telemedicine practices, there were variations in the reactions to the efforts at addressing tensions. While some reacted positively to the hub's re sponses (NH1, 3, 5), others were less consistent and positive due to idiosyncratic factors (NH 2, 4, and 7). We review three different sets of members' reactions below (See Appendix Table 2 for a summary).

In the first set of reactions, we noted that NH 1, 3, and 5 were very positive towards the training and adopted practices that were consistent with the telemedicine system. Their positive reaction was because they not only saw the value of the training (as seen from quote below) but they also valued the telemedicine system as a means of providing better care through specialist access and nurse upskilling that came from training.

“… motivation is different. They want to learn. The senior management encourages them. They are being told that this course is beneficial for them. The nurse (director) encouragement makes a lot of difference. They are very disciplined in class. They don't mind staying late.”

(Clinical educator commenting on NH3 nurses)

As such, at NH5, the nurse director proactively chose their best nurses for training and NH5 nurses expressed pride in their telegeriatrics training. The NH5 nurse director also purposefully selected teleconsultation patients in line with guidelines regarding pa tient type (those who require specialist care) and made special effort to adopt the practices (see quote below). Another example of how the NHs adopted the telemedicine practices was in NH3, where their nurse director deliberately matched patients' conditions to Acute North's clinician's specialization (e.g., dementia patients matched to dementia specialist).

“NH5, the nurse director synchronized their nurses' shifts to align to teleconsultations. She also excludes patients that are well-managed by GPs from teleconsultations.” (Operations Team, Acute North)

In a second set of reactions, we observed that there was inconsistent nurse training participation and that these nursing homes selected some inappropriate cases, including patients who did not need specialist care, and scheduled too few or too many tele consultation sessions (NH2, 4, and 7). One factor that is related to their lack of/or inconsistent training participation was severe manpower scarcity. At NH2, there was high nurse turnover. At NH4, resources were tight as they were involved in another healthcare IT project (see first quote below). Also most NH4 nurses were not enthusiastic about going as they felt that the training was additiona work (see second quote below).

“Our manpower is very lean and stretched. We are also on nursing home IT system. Because we are piloting, we are putting all our energies in (nursing home IT system). ... So. we have not invested as much time and effort in (SeniorCare) as we wish to. We have not had the time and resources to dedicate it."

(Nurse Director, NH4)

“… Because of (rostering the nurses), we did not send in many staff nurses. (As) timing was very tight. Because they are working. Course starts at 2-3 pm. It was very tiring for them. It was all about manpower.”

(Nurse Director, NH4)

The manpower shortage also led to the inappropriate selection issue as the high turnover at these nursing homes meant that newer nurses and managers were less trained and experienced in patient selection. Consequently, they were more prone to selecting the most convenient patients on the day of the teleconsultations, rather than planning and picking patients who required specialist care ahead of time.

Another factor related to their lukewarm reaction towards training was the lack of a champion that believed in the training's value, unlike the first group of nursing homes. In NH4, the nurse director who was a champion left as the project started. At NH7, the nurse director was not an active champion. We observed in our interview with her (NH7 nurse director) that she appeared less enthusiastic about the telemedicine initiative, and was relatively guarded in her manner. This likely influenced NH7 nurses' commitment to training. This was corroborated by Acute North's clinical educators who noted that in 2015, out of the 10 nurses NH7 sent to training, seven quickly dropped out.

Finally, visiting GPs was a factor related to the inappropriate use of the telemedicine system, i.e., where relatively few telemedicine sessions were scheduled. NH4 had several full time GPs visiting regularly so the nurses found it more convenient to have these GPs attend to their residents than run the teleconsultation sessions, which required the nurses to take a more active role. At NH7. the visiting GPs had several clashes with Acute North's specialists. The GPs complained that under SeniorCare, the specialists' care plans interfered with their work and they therefore decided not to adhere to the specialists' recommendations.

“Our management is a lot more complex and slower as we like to monitor a lot of things. … So I think they are also worried because if there are any issues they are the ones who will deal (sic) this in a day-to-day basis.”

(Geriatrician, Acute North)

When NH7's GPs complained to the nurse director, she decided to limit teleconsultations to one case per week, favoring their own GPs over SeniorCare's telemedicine service.

NH6's mixed reactions to the hub's responses form the third set of reactions. NH6's reaction towards the training provided by the hub was very positive. This was because NH6's organizational culture valued ongoing nurse training and professionalism. NH6's nurse director even took a train-the-trainer approach by sending their staff nurses for training and having them teach the other nurses when they returned. As training capacity grew at SeniorCare, NH6 also sent their enrolled nurses for training.

However NH6 did not select appropriate cases for teleconsultation as they often scheduled patients who did not need specialist care. According to the nurses, this was because every resident in NH6 (including those who are not obviously ill) “must be seen by a doctor at least once in a month.” As a private, for-profit nursing home, NH6 aimed to provide their residents with a higher level of care than those provided in non-profit nursing homes. As their onsite GPs could not cover all NH6 residents in the specified timeframe, NH6 nurse director saw an opportunity to meet their internal KPIs by scheduling spill over residents (many of whom did not require specialist care) for teleconsultations.

Tensions mitigated: Overall, the hub's responses helped to mitigate learning tensions and nursing homes nurses were trained with the requisite skills and knowledge to implement the telemedicine service. The collaborative governance structures and shared goals helped members work with Acute North to tailor the telemedicine pedagogy and training modes.

Most of the nursing homes' nurse directors and nurses (including some nursing homes who were inconsistent in their training) had positive feedback on how the training helped their nurses become more professional, confident, and capable. They reported that their nurses were better equipped for the care of nursing home residents (e.g., they were able to conduct basic clinical assessments, as shown in the quote below).

“Now I am able to see and assess the patients very systematically and thoroughly. So it is easier to do the clinical examination (as) we know what to do and how to do. …. The training is very useful for us as we have improved our skills and we feel more confident in diagnosing.”

(Nurse, NH5)

Acute North clinicians concurred with the nurse directors' assessment and observed that many of the nurses grew more confident in presenting their patients' conditions because of the training program.

“Before training, they will say, ‘I don't know what to do’ and after training, it is ‘I know what to do and when I don't I know where to turn to.'I think this empowerment is a huge difference and if I were to compare the TeleG³ trained nurses and the non-trained nurses. I can see how satisfied and confident (TeleG trained nurses) are in their job.” (Geriatrician, Acute North)

The responses to the organizing tensions were also effective in stabilizing the joint teleconsultations processes so that tele consultations were taking place regularly at all the nursing homes, albeit with variations in the number of residents scheduled per session and the manner in which residents were selected. The SeniorCare managers were patient with the nursing homes, relying on continued communication and encouragement to select patients who would most benefit from specialist care. The issue of reduced teleconsultations due to conflict with GPs, however, required new actions.

“I am not sure whether they really understand what's their role. GPs really need to see the value of this program. But they don't get that we have to work together… in fact there is a possibility that they (GPs) think we are slowing them down” (Geriatrician, Acute North) As we were leaving the field, we learnt from the Acute North team that they were planning to reach out to the nursing homes' GPs to manage this ongoing issue.

“Yes, our head of geriatrics department is thinking of gathering all the GPs together and give them a talk to understand more about geriatrics problems. He will say what our program is doing and TeleG is just a part of approach to better healthcare. We are here to support and advise them, if needed.” (Operations Team, Acute North)

## 4.3. Phase 3—leveraging the telemedicine system

With the scheduled teleconsultations in place, Acute North expanded SeniorCare's telemedicine services to include ad hoc tele consultations, telemortality audits (to discuss patient pain management or pastoral care for palliative patients), and tele multidisciplinary meetings (to discuss patient's progress, including discharge planning or ongoing social concerns). SeniorCare's training program was also expanded with TeleCNE (Continuing Nurse Education), which was aimed at building nursing homes' col lective learning capability.

Tensions: Concurrently, the MOH mandated a new Enhanced Nursing Home Standards (ENHS). Accordingly, all nursing homes had to review and revise their operating processes to ensure they complied with the ENHS. The standards also required that nursing home nurses be trained in the various processes—i.e., advanced care planning, medication management, care for various geriatric condi tions, and pain management (Guide to ENHS, 2014). Performing tensions arose as nursing homes, which had a perennial shortage of manpower, struggled to meet the new ENHS goals of consistent operating processes as well as SeniorCare goals of access to specialist care and uplift of nursing clinical skills.

Responses: It became clear that the nursing homes urgently needed help in training their nurses to meet the ENHS requirements. SeniorCare was well placed to offer assistance given that they had, by this time, developed a deep understanding of nursing home nurses' learning preferences through several years of conducting telemedicine training.

Creating supplementary working forum focused on finding synergies: Acute North created a training steering committee comprising nursing home nurses, SeniorCare clinical educators, and Acute North doctors as a collaborative governance structure focused on identifying ways that SeniorCare could help the nursing homes meet the ENHS training requirements.

“It is more on giving feedback they ask like ‘was this helpful’ and then they ask” what other training do you want” and then they will present the trainings available for next year … It is more about information giving and feedback channeling and of course you need to verbalize what you want to have it.” (Staff Nurse, NH6)

Revising training programs to meet both sets of goals: SeniorCare also responded by making changes to its training, including expanding them to meet new regulatory requirements. For example, Advanced Care Planning (ACP) was now part of ENHS, so Acute North developed a new ACP course and ACP processes that could be delivered through the teleconsultation system. This enabled nursing home members to continue participating in the telemedicine system while addressing the ENHS's requirement. Some of the fundamental components for the previous telemedicine training were also shifted to a module that would help meet the ENHS requirements.

“The training steering committee started last year. (Ministry of Health) introduced enhanced nursing standards in seven areas- ACP, mental health, falls, pressure ulcers, and continuance management, oral care and each nursing home brainstorm on what topic to focus

Acute North's clinical educators help (us) on each topic (and then we had) one full day training at Acute North.” (Nurse Director, NH3) Upgrading technology to support new services: Finally, Acute North's IT team also continued to upgrade its telecom network infra structure and conferencing system so as to support these increased training needs, such as upgrading the teleconferencing system to support more simultaneous video streaming links.

## 5. Discussion

This study examined the research question of how paradoxical tensions in a telemedicine system might be addressed so as to sustain the continuing development of the system. We discuss below a conceptual model based on our findings that accounts for the dynamics, specifically, on a) when and why tensions arise in the initiation and development of a telemedicine system, b) the nature of hub's responses that mitigate these tensions and the resultant inter-organizational norms and structures that influence future responses, and c) the variations in members' reactions to the hub's responses. (See Figure 2).

## 5.1. When and why paradoxical tensions occur

Understanding when and why tensions occur is important, as it enables those managing the telemedicine system to anticipate the tensions and understand their underlying causes. Essentially, tensions occur due to the conflict between demands and interests of the telemedicine system versus those of its members. In the initiating phase, belonging tensions are likely to become salient, as there arises the tug between the two poles of the telemedicine system's emerging identity versus the members' identities. While paradox theory identifies plurality (arising from multiple stakeholders) as a contributing factor, the technological demands of a telemedicine system exacerbate belonging tensions. Compared to the physical visits from acute hospital specialists, the telemedicine system required greater identification by the nursing homes with the new SeniorCare entity. It linked the nursing homes more closely to Acute North and to each other. There were physical telecommunications links and video-conferencing units in the nursing homes, shared processes around the technology, and acquisition of new clinical and technology skills.

In the developing capabilities phase, learning, organizing, and belonging tensions are likely to become salient as new telemedicine system skills, learning modes, and shared inter-organizational processes are perceived as being in opposition to member-centric skills learning modes, identities, and processes. The technology enabled remote delivery of care necessitated that nurses shift to a diagnostic, partnering approach, which required a much higher level of medical knowledge. Remote teleconsultations also triggered organizing tensions because, compared to their less clinically involved role before, nurses now had to take a more active role in keeping records and preparing for each patient scheduled for teleconsultations. The changes in the processes, roles, and skills also created new demands for the nurses and shifts in their identity and role and these led to belonging tension for the nursing home nurses.

Although in this study the performing tension became salient after the telemedicine system was up and running for several years during the leveraging phase, we do not conclude that this would necessarily be the case in other telemedicine systems. The cause of the performing tension in this study was the introduction of new regulatory requirements mandated by the MOH. Performing tensions can arise as early as initiation if members perceive that the goals of the telemedicine system are in conflict with their goals. This was not the situation here as Acute North was careful to align goals at the beginning by adopting a posture of helpfulness and collaboration

These tensions occurred in the context of new technology enabled practices between Acute North and the nursing homes. Our study shows how technology serves as a trigger for paradoxical tensions and adds to paradox theory in that it has generally conceptualized triggers as contextual factors such as plurality, scarcity, and change. First, IS research shows that technology's capabilities and affordances directly enable novel and innovative organizational endeavors by providing the foundation for creating new processes and routines (Nambisan, 2013; von Briel et al., 2021). The teleconferencing system enabled the new specialist consultation process, which in turn led to new learning and organizing modes. Thus, we observe how technology-enabled change can directly trigger organizing and learning tensions. Second, technology can be indirectly linked to plurality in that the development of new technology-enabled systems and processes often brings together different stakeholders with their own goals and identities (Tilson, Lyytinen, & Sørensen, 2010). In this way, the technological development is intertwined with plurality, which triggers the belonging and per forming tensions in the telemedicine system. Wimelius, Mathiassen, Holmstrom, ¨ and Keil's (2021) study on technology renewal as a key trigger for paradoxical tensions resonates with our finding.

## 5.2. Responses and reactions to tensions in inter-organizational settings

The responses enacted by Acute North across the telemedicine system were “both-and,” thus helping to mitigate each of the tensions by addressing both poles of system-level demands and nursing home demands (See Appendix Table 1). This enabled the telemedicine system to continue develop. While the hub's responses were able to sufficiently mitigate the tensions, closer examination at the member level showed variations in members' reactions to tensions and the hub's responses. In the initiating phase, the responses to belonging tensions resulted in four nursing homes committing to the telemedicine system, but another three dropped out. In the developing capabilities phase, about half the nursing homes participated in training and used the system consistently and appropri ately, while the other half did not (See Appendix Table 2). We first discuss below the hub's responses to the tensions and then the members' reactions.

Responses to belonging tension (boundary spanners, formal collaborative governance, and informal communication) gave mem bers voice, so that conflicting member and hub identity norms and values could be surfaced and discussed as the emergent tele medicine system identity was being shaped. Responses to learning, belonging, and organizing tensions (targeted resourcing, tailoring/ simplifying, and articulation of value to members) thoughtfully reduced members' cognitive dissonance that arose from reconciling their existing capabilities and processes with the new capabilities and shared processes. Responses to the performing tension (sup plementary working forum, expanded training programs, and upgrading technology) helped members meet both the additional nursing home performance goals imposed by the new nursing home standards. while continuing to meet the goals of participating in SeniorCare's teleconsultations.

Telemedicine studies have noted the need for the hub to empathize with members' resource and capabilities constraints (Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015). Research on inter-organizational ambidexterity also highlighted the issue where SME's limited competencies and resourcing resulted in significant challenges as they attempted to manage paradoxical tension in alliances (Partanen et al., 2020; Rosenbusch et al., 2011). Lô and Theodoraki (2021). in the context of a start-up ecosystem with many small firms, noted that the hub had to play a proactive role by initiating collaborative governance. In our study, we see the hub, Acute North, responding proactively to tensions in a both-and manner. In addition to the response of initiating collaborative governance, Acute North also enacted re sponses that mitigated limited member capabilities through tailoring of training, motivating learning through articulation of value, and assisting in the implementation of technology infrastructure. The hub also mitigated limited member resources through simpli fying processes and targeted resourcing.

Members' reactions to hub's responses were varied. As discussed above, inter-organizational research on ambidexterity found that individual member's organizational capabilities and resources (e.g., leadership, slack resources for experimentation) affected their ability to manage such tensions (Lavie et al., 2010: Partanen et al., 2020). Similarly, in our study, we found that the extent of resource scarcity at the nursing homes influenced their reactions to the hub's responses. Where there was significant turnover of nurses or where the nursing staff were stretched because the nursing home was involved in other concurrent projects, the participation in training was inconsistent and the use of teleconsultations was compromised. The presence or absence of a steadfast senior management champion also influenced members' reaction. In two cases, the champions left the nursing homes: One champion left at the initial phase, resulting in the nursing home dropping out of the telemedicine system, while the other left during the capabilities development phase, resulting in inconsistent training participation, the scheduling of fewer patients for teleconsultations, and the selection of patients based more on convenience than on need.

We identified an additional factor that influenced nursing home's reaction to the tensions and responses—their access to alternative source of care (i.e., GPs). Interestingly, while this access resulted in lower or inappropriate use of teleconsultations in three nursing homes, the manner it influenced use was different. In one nursing home, GP access interacted with manpower scarcity, resulting in nurses scheduling fewer cases for teleconsultation. They used the GPs instead as the latter required less effort from the nurses. In another nursing home, the GPs objected to the patient's care plans arising from the teleconsultation. The nursing home scheduled fewer teleconsultation sessions to reduce conflict with the GPs. In yet another nursing home, the nurses used the teleconsultations to meet the nursing home's own KPI of having every patient seen by a doctor every quarter. This resulted in patients being scheduled for the specialist teleconsultations even when they did not need specialist care.

The identification of these member-level factors leads to an important point: While the hub plays a key role in sustaining the telemedicine system through both-and responses, there are limits to what the hub can do. We found that the hub's both-and responses were targeted at the system-wide level. They mitigated tensions by addressing common challenges across nursing homes (e.g., war iness of telemedicine system's emerging identity, overly challenging training content/schedule, additional reporting requirements, etc.), and had done so sufficiently to sustain the telemedicine system. However, there are member-level factors (resourcing, senior management champion, access to alternative care) that the hub may not be in a position to address. This highlights the organizationa boundaries in the inter-organizational context of telemedicine systems, which limit the extent to which the hub can influence each individual members.

Nonetheless, the boundaries or limits to the hub's influence on members may also change over time and factors that initially appear to be idiosyncratic and member-specific may be linked to common underlying issues. For example, because the GP issue manifested in different ways, it took some time for Acute North to realize the important role GPs played in nursing homes. Acute North then made plans to systematically reach out to each nursing home's GPs to share SeniorCare goals and explore processes to better coordinate care with GPs.

## 5.3. Temporal dynamics of tensions and responses

Our conceptual model provides some insights regarding the dynamics of tensions and responses—how they relate across phases. Specifically, we found the responses in each phase created norms and structures that not only mitigated the tension in the existing phase but continued to significantly shaped the responses in subsequent phases. In the initiating phase, the responses to the belonging tension led to collective telemedicine system norms of partnership, mutual respect, and helpfulness as well as structures such as the collaborative governance forum and informal communication channels. These resultant norms and structures provided the means by which members could provide inputs on the challenges they faced in the next phase of developing capabilities as training and shared processes were rolled out. They also enabled two-way discussions that shaped the responses to the learning and organizing tensions through adaptations to training and shared processes as well as the articulation of professional value to the nurses themselves.

The responses to the learning, organizing, and belonging tensions, in turn, created additional norms and structures for the tele medicine system. For example, norms were developed that emphasized practice-oriented, hands-on training in smaller chunks of time to fit around work schedules. These encompass both clinical and technology skills, with on-the-job reinforcement by the trainers during teleconsultation sessions. The responses also resulted in structures such as more proactive diagnostic roles for the telemedicine nurses in the nursing homes as well as collaborative processes between these nurses and the specialists. Again, these resultant norms and structures. together with those from the initiating phase, influenced the response to the performing tension in the next phase. The communication channels enabled a quick sensing of tensions, as members struggled with the demands of meeting the additiona mandatory nursing home standards while continuing to meet teleconsultation goals with limited manpower. The responses to this tension leveraged on the norms and structures from previous responses—extending the formal governance forum with a training forum to address the new set of external performance requirements, and building on the existing training programs by incorporating training to meet the new regulatory requirements. The technology system that was in place was also upgraded to support the increased use, as some of the additional training was now done via teleconferencing. This third set of responses further extended and institutionalized the governance and technology structures and embedded the training norms.

Building on these findings, we believe that collective norms of partnership, mutual respect, and helpfulness and the formal and informal collaborative structures play a critical role in the telemedicine system's mid to long-term sustainability. Not only are these norms and structures based on a both-and principle, which is appropriate for mitigating paradoxical tensions within each phase, they can prevent any latent tensions from becoming salient by providing ongoing awareness of potential triggers and by drawing attention to new tensions that arise over time in other phases. This thus adds support to Singh et al.'s (2018) paper about the importance of ongoing management in telemedicine systems.

We also highlight technology's role as part of the response. For example, part of the hub's response of tailoring to participants included selecting, configuring, and modifying parts of the telemedicine system to accommodate the different nursing homes' context (e.g., building a user-friendly, mobile cart for teleconsultations). SeniorCare also reconstituted the teleconferencing system into a remote learning platform for all the nursing homes.<sup>4</sup> The teleconferencing system is part of the hub's response and becomes one of the inter-organizational structures that complemented and reinforced the new norms of practice-oriented training and hub-members collaboration. That technology can be both enabler-trigger and enabler-response is due to digital technologies' inherent attributes of malleability and reconfigurability (Kallinikos, Aaltonen, & Marton, 2013; Yoo, 2012).

## 6. Contributions and conclusions

Our study contributes to the research on telemedicine by addressing the question of how to address the competing demands that often undermine and even derail telemedicine systems. In addressing this question, we also contribute to the broader paradox liter ature, particularly to the less studied area of paradox dynamics in the inter-organizational context. First, we find that the opposing poles of each of the tensions in inter-organizational telemedicine systems arise from opposing demands and interests at the system leve versus those at the member level. We contribute to an understanding of the dynamics of paradox by noting when belonging, learning, organizing, and performing tensions are likely to arise in the inter-organizational telemedicine system context. We also provide a consistent link to the paradox literature by using the more generalized categorization of tensions (largely established in the intraorganizational context) and applying it to the inter-organizational context.

The continued development of the telemedicine system in our case study was facilitated by the hub's enactment of “both-and” responses that address both telemedicine system and member needs. This provides a theoretically grounded basis for explaining the observations in previous telemedicine studies on the need for empathy (Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015), and for clarifying the principle and spirit that needs to underlie previously suggested mechanisms, such as having a partner governance structure and a coordinating team for processes (Cho et al., 2009; Singh et al., 2018). It also provides a potential explanation for why these mechanisms may not be as effective in some cases—they may have been deployed without the underlying “both-and” principle. For example, partner gover nance may have been enacted in a manner that primarily focused on hub requirements. Finally, it reminds us of the creativity and synergies that are possible with the “both-and” approach, as exemplified by the various response detailed in this study.

The findings demonstrate how the hub's both-and responses developed and sustained the telemedicine system through creating inter-organizational norms and structures that persist over time and in light of changing circumstances; these not only addressed the current tension but were also built on over time to create further both-and responses as other tensions become salient. The interorganizational literature notes the necessity of balancing tensions in order to sustain alliances (Das & Teng, 2000). This study shows that this “balancing” process recurs as a variety of tensions become salient over time, and will require continuing enactment of responses, which are influenced by the structures and norms created by previous responses. Further, the both-and responses together with this finding of persistent norms and structures help to explain why collaborative governance structures and partnering norms have been recommended in telemedicine and inter-organizational studies of paradox (Niesten & Stefan, 2019). They lay the foundation for sensing and accepting tensions as well as crafting further responses. In the context of hub-spoke telemedicine systems where members have limited capabilities and resources, we also argue that both-and responses that develop training norms to increase member and collective system capabilities are critical, as are both-and responses that address resource constraints.

Our study contributes to the paradox theory by elaborating on the inter-organizational hub and member dynamics, where members are small organizations with limited resources. As noted by Lo and Theodoraki (2021), in such a context, the hub plays an important role in setting up collaborative governance. We also show the important role that the hub can play in helping to level up small members' capabilities through training contextualized to member constraints. But there are limits to the hub's role. The hub's both-and responses can address common factors underlying the tensions; however, it might be less able to provide responses to factors that are idiosyncratic to individual members. Extrapolating from prior research on challenges in inter-organizational ambidexterity, such idiosyncratic factors in SMEs may be due to variations in their access to slack resources, to acquire external knowledge, in cognitive and behavioral attitude of leaders, and in partner management capability (Lavie et al., 2010; Partanen et al., 2020).

Finally, our study contributes to paradox literature by clarifying the different roles technology play in the dynamic process of responding to paradoxical tensions in inter-organizational systems. Within this dynamic process, technology was not just an enabler of change but also a structural context in which the hub's other responses were implemented (e.g., teleconferencing system used as a platform for new training programs). Specifically, these roles were due to technology's capabilities, affordances, inherent malleability, and configurability. By incorporating technology's capabilities and affordance, it became part of the change trigger. At the same time, because of technology's malleability and configurability, the entire technical system could evolve over the different phases and thereb assist to integrate and build a new context where spatial. temporal. and sectoral boundaries were reshaped. The telemedicine system context we studied, which included this new community, enabled the hub to co-develop, with nursing home members, new responses to emergent tensions. These insights are in line with studies that suggest the need to recognize how mitigation of paradoxes involves realigning both the social and technical (Tilson et al., 2010; Tilson, Sørensen, & Lyytinen, 2021). This is especially relevant at the interorganizational level as new inter-organizational contexts are often built on technology that link activities separated by physical organizational, and temporal boundaries (Tilson et al., 2021; von Briel et al., 2021).

This study is based on a single longitudinal case, which generated a useful conceptual model to inform future research in other settings and thus to elaborate and substantiate findings and assess boundary and contextual limitations to the model (Lee & Bas kerville. 2003). Of note, our case study involved a relatively well-funded hub, which worked with less-endowed partners from varving backgrounds (charity and for-profit). It was also focused mainly on one clinical domain – telemedicine for chronic care of geriatric patients – and the hub and members had a synergistic relationship in providing complementary forms of care for their patients. Future research could consider how our model applies in other large-scale telemedicine systems where the hub and members are equally wel (or less) endowed, where they may have coopetitive (competitive as well as collaborative) relationsips, or where multiple clinical domains are managed through the telemedicine system. Also, our study focused mainly on the inter-organizational tensions, though the belonging tension was salient primarily to the nursing home nurses, Salient paradoxical tensions may arise at the individual group and organizational levels (Lewis & Smith, 2014) which may then influence the system-level. Thus, future research could explore how paradoxical tensions occur at multiple levels in a telemedicine system and how these tensions and their interactions may influence the types of responses required by the hub and members.

The importance and societal value of recognizing, responding to, and mitigating paradoxical tensions that can undermine effective operation of telemedicine systems is evident in the growing role of telemedicine in healthcare systems challenged by the world-wide COVID-19 pandemic. We close on the encouraging note of how this telemedicine system, which was nurtured and developed in the last decade, was leveraged in 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic's lockdown. After the pandemic started, the hub leveraged the existing formal and informal structures to assist the nursing homes in SeniorCare in implementing business continuity plans and infection control measures through meetings over the teleconferencing system. At the same time, the telemedicine system ramped up their scheduling capacity and specialists' availability as teleconsultations had tripled since it became the primary mode of care during the lockdown period. While other nursing homes in the country had trouble gaining access to external specialists, nursing home residents in SeniorCare continued to have access to specialist care.
