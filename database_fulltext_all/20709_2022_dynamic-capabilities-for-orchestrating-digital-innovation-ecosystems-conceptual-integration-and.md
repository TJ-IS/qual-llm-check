# Dynamic capabilities for orchestrating digital innovation ecosystems: Conceptual integration and research opportunities

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Digital innovation Ecosystem orchestration System integration Complementor management Digital technology

## A B S T R A C T

While most previous research on orchestration of digital innovation ecosystems has examined governance structures, our knowledge of relevant dynamic capabilities remains abstract and lacks conceptual integration. This imbalance limits current knowledge to the extent that digital inno vation ecosystem orchestration is mainly considered a structural issue. Based on a synthesis of related literature, we present a novel dynamic capabilities framework that builds on sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring capabilities for digital innovation ecosystem orchestration. We differentiate two major challenges addressed in orchestrating such ecosystems: component challenges and complement challenges. We propose six concrete routines that collectively help address these challenges. These include co-creating architectural knowledge, cultivating bound ary objects, renegotiating system integration, screening complementors, co-specializing in terfaces, and restructuring complementarities. We illustrate each of these routines with examples from the automotive sector. In addition, we sketch trajectories for future research both at the level of the individual routines and at the level of the overall framework.

## 1. Introduction

Value propositions increasingly bundle physical and digital components that collectively constitute lavered. modular product architectures (Yoo, Henfridsson, and Lyytinen, 2010). As the pervasiveness of digital technologies in such architectures grows, the nature of innovation is fundamentally transforming (Yoo, Boland, Lyytinen, and Majchrzak, 2012). Given the rising complexity of value propositions, most digital innovations are understood to emerge from larger ecosystems of diverse actors, with each actor contributing parts that are combined into a final customer solution (Nambisan, Wright, and Feldman, 2019). A digital innovation ecosystem is defined as “a loosely coupled set of autonomous actors (people and organizations who interact without hierarchical fiat) involved in the development and implementation of innovations enabled by digital technologies" (Wang. 2021, p. 397).¹ Mechanisms to orchestrate and govern such ecosystems are hence essential for digital innovation success (Kindermann et al., 2021). Executing such mechanisms often lies in the hands of a central actor, the orchestrator. The orchestrator is the ecosystem hub firm that, due to “individual attributes such as size, technological leadership, or unique resources and capabilities” (Giudici, Reinmoeller, and Ravasi, 2018, p. 1372), possesses the prominence and power (Dhanaraj and Parkhe, 2006) to lead the process of aligning the various interests and activities of ecosystem members towards a common value proposition (Adner, 2017).

Extant information systems (IS) research reveals that the distinct characteristics of digital technologies cause unique tensions among the actors involved in ecosystems (e.g., Svahn, Mathiassen, and Lindgren, 2017). Numerous studies explore how orchestrators can manage such tensions by implementing specific governance structures, including rules and regulations on ecosystem access or actor interaction (e.g., Tiwana, Konsynski, and Bush, 2010; Wareham, Fox, and Cano Giner, 2014). The insights generated by this research stream, which often centers on architecture (Nambisan, 2018), have enriched our understanding of how governance struc tures established by ecosystem orchestrators need to be set to create and capture value in the presence of digital innovation. However, recent studies also underline the importance of orchestrators’ ability to adjust structural features of the ecosystem dynamically (e.g., Schreieck, Wiesche, and Krcmar, 2021; Tan, Pan, Lu, and Huang, 2015). These findings contribute to a second, and smaller, stream of research that emphasizes the essential role of dynamic capabilities enabled by deliberate learning routines (Helfat and Raubitschek, 2018; Iyengar, Sweeney, and Montealegre, 2015). Related works suggest that dynamic capabilities help explain the evolution of ecosystem structures and of the governance elements supporting such structures (cf. Teece, 2007; Venkatraman, El Sawy, Pavlou, and Bharadwaj, 2014). Nevertheless, our knowledge of dynamic capabilities that accommodate the unique characteristics of digita innovation in ecosystems remains abstract and lacks conceptual integration. The lack of a coherent framework on such capabilities is problematic for two reasons.

First, given the imbalance of research towards structure-based inquiries, digital innovation ecosystem orchestration might be perceived mainly as a structural problem for which largely static remedies (e.g., rules, regulations, etc.) can be identified. Conse quently, the role of higher-order capabilities—and of the knowledge integration and learning processes underlying their gen eration—remains conceptually underdeveloped (Autio and Thomas, 2020; Nambisan, 2018). Second, literature still has to provide sufficiently granular and actionable suggestions as to how practitioners can build and implement orchestration capabilities that accommodate the unprecedented dynamism accompanying the value multiplicity of digital innovation (Henfridsson, Nandhakumar, Scarbrough, and Panourgias, 2018). This is a particularly pressing issue in view of the high failure rates of digital innovation eco systems (Wang, 2021).

To advance theorizing on digital innovation ecosystem orchestration as a dynamic capability, a revised understanding of the set of underlying routines is required. We hence ask: What are distinct routines underlying the effective orchestration of digital innovation eco systems? We employ Teece’s (2007) dynamic capabilities concept of sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring capabilities to inform the generation of a new, integrative framework. Drawing on Adner and Kapoor’s (2010) ecosystem perspective, we understand digita innovation ecosystem orchestration to be directed towards both upstream component challenges and downstream complement challenges. It is our conceptual goal to integrate previous insights into the topic (MacInnis. 2011). Synthesizing the fragmented body ot existing research, we identify six distinct routines that help overcome related challenges: co-creating architectural knowledge, cultivating boundary objects, renegotiating system integration, screening complementors, co-specializing interfaces, and restructuring complementarities. The proposed framework expands existing research on digital innovation ecosystem orchestration. First, we shift scholarly attention to the role of learning and routine development in digital innovation ecosystem orchestration. thereby complementing earlier perspectives focused on governance structures. Second, our framework provides a novel set of orchestration rou tines that specifically accommodate the implications of digital innovation within ecosystems. Third, for each of the six routines proposed, we delineate knowledge gaps that might inspire future research. In addition, we discuss implications for practice and policy.

## 2. Conceptual background

We proceed in two steps. First, we review previous insights into the orchestration of digital innovation ecosystems. Second, we explain the foundations and underlying logic of our newly proposed orchestration model.

## 2.1. Research on orchestrating digital innovation ecosystems

Digital innovation can be defined as “a product, process or business model that is perceived as new, requires significant changes on the part of adopters, and is embodied in or enabled by information technology” (Fichman, Dos Santos, and Zheng, 2014, p. 333). The digital technology included in this type of innovation can afford the sensing, tracing, and memorizing of information (Wang, 2021). It is (re-)programmable (Yoo et al., 2012) and hence not necessarily bound to one single product or functionality (Henfridsson et al., 2018; Lusch and Nambisan, 2015). Such flexibility spawns new degrees of modularity, so that digital innovation is often organized in a “layered modular architecture” (Yoo et al., 2010, p. 725).

While digital innovation does not exclusively occur within ecosystems, its specific characteristics often give rise to the emergence of such structures (Nambisan. Lyytinen. Maichrzak, and Song. 2017). Adner and Kapoor (2010) suggest that orchestrators of ecosystems need to manage interdependencies with two types of external actors—upstream component providers and downstream com plementors. Component providers include the suppliers of products and services that are bundled by the focal firm. In that, this conceptualization echoes the notion of the value chain (Porter, 1980). Previous research has shown that modularizing product and service offerings can lead to a specialization of component providers (Adner and Kapoor, 2010: Brusoni and Prencipe, 2001) which puts the orchestrator into the role of a system integrator (Prencipe, Davies, and Hobday, 2003). What differentiates an ecosystem from a traditional value chain however is that orchestrators take into consideration both component providers and not contractually governed complementors (Autio and Thomas, 2020). Complementors are those actors whose products and services are bundled, together with the focal firm’s offering, by the customer. While they do not participate directly in the supply chain of the focal firm (Adner, 2017), complementors play an essential role in its value creation and capture (Milgrom and Roberts, 1990; Teece, 1986). When complementarities are nongeneric, orchestrators need to find ways to orchestrate their provision (Jacobides, Cennamo, and Gawer, 2018). Many ecosystems operate a platform to provide complementors with a point of access (Gawer, 2020).

Orchestrating component providers and complementors essentially means to align their activities in a way that promotes the ecosystem’s value proposition (cf. Adner, 2017; Autio and Thomas, 2020). IS research addressing digital innovation ecosystems has strongly focused on exploring the rules and regulations—the governance—orchestrators can draw on to achieve such alignment (e.g., Boudreau, 2012; Parker, Van Alstyne, and Jiang, 2017; Wareham et al., 2014). In the context of digital innovation ecosystems, governance might include organizing access to and participation in the ecosystem, determining interaction structures among actors, or deciding on incentives to promote specific actor behavior, for example, through pricing (e.g., Helfat and Raubitschek, 2018; Huber, Kude, and Dibbern. 2017: Tiwana et al., 2010: Wareham et al., 2014). According to this view, orchestrators may face a structural problem as the solution to their alignment challenge resides in finding a suitable governance structure. El Sawy, Malhotra, Park, and Pavlou (2010) introduced a complementary perspective, which Helfat and Raubitschek (2018) developed further. They propose the dynamic capabilities concept (Eisenhardt and Martin, 2000) as a lens to understand how actors can sustain competitive advantage in digital innovation ecosystems. Dynamic capabilities enable firms to generate and reconfigure operational capabilities (cf. Schulze and Brusoni. 2022). They further facilitate supernormal returns as they enable firms to achieve an evolutionary fit between ecosystems’ operational capabilities, structures, and external environments (Teece, 2007). To realize such a fit, firms might also need to adjust their ecosystem governance structures (Helfat and Raubitschek, 2018; Zobel and Hagedoorn, 2020). The dynamic capabilities perspective suggests that digital innovation ecosystem orchestration primarily addresses the development of adaptation capabilities emerging from knowledge integration and learning processes (Teece, 2007). Hence, orchestrators may face a processual problem. Research following this perspective has identified several capabilities relevant to ecosystem orchestration. These include scanning and inte grative capabilities (Helfat and Raubitschek, 2018) and managing third-party interaction, for instance, with boundary resources (Schreieck et al., 2021). Despite these findings and other important advances (e.g., Tan et al., 2015; Venkatraman et al., 2014), our knowledge of dynamic capabilities within digital innovation ecosystems remains abstract and lacks conceptual integration. While scholars have paid considerable attention to the structural view, they have garnered limited insights into the complementary proc essual view. We conclude that a new, integrative framework of dynamic capabilities essential for the orchestration of digital innovation ecosystems is needed. The conceptual underpinnings of such a model are discussed next.

## 2.2. Foundations of a new model

Despite the existence of a dominant orchestrator, innovation trajectories within ecosystems are not exclusively determined by the orchestrator but also shaped by other ecosystem members' efforts to exert control² (Eaton. Elaluf-Calderwood. Sørensen. and Yoo. 2015). Conflicts between orchestrators' and members' interests might provoke breakdowns of innovation trajectories (cf, Van de Ven and Sun, 2011). If unaddressed, such breakdowns might lead to ecosystem bottlenecks, defined as those elements “whose performance, cost, or scarcity constrains the focal offer’s value proposition, thereby limiting its demand or growth” (Kapoor, 2018, p. 6). However, bottlenecks might also emerge through triggers external to the ecosystem. for instance. as the result of a changing environment. Since innovation ecosystems are often marked by high degrees of interdependence (Jacobides et al., 2018). local breakdowns in one part ot the ecosystem might have severe effects on the delivery of the entire value proposition. Consequently, structures and processes that previously supported the delivery of the ecosystem value proposition might become unfit. Orchestrators managing the continuous breakdowns emerging from the tensions inherent to digital innovation ecosystems or external change can spur learning processes such as experience accumulation or knowledge articulation and codification (Zollo and Winter, 2002). Previous research shows that it helps facilitate change within the ecosystem if orchestrators consider positive and negative feedback during the introduction and further development of digital technology (Sandberg, Holmstrom, ¨ and Lyytinen, 2020). Through this, orchestrators can devise organizationa routines that allow them to apply gained knowledge to future orchestration challenges. Such routines pertain to “highly patterned, repetitious, or quasi-repetitious” behaviors (Winter, 2003, p. 991) and are bundled to form dynamic capabilities (Pentland, Feldman, Becker, and Liu, 2012). Dynamic capabilities enable orchestrators to reconfigure operational capabilities and adjust organizationa structures accordingly, which makes them particularly valuable for accommodating the high levels of change in digital innovation contexts (Teece, 2007; Venkatraman et al., 2014). Orchestrators applying their dynamic capabilities by exploring and pursuing nove value creation opportunities within the ecosystem continue to gain experience and knowledge, thereby informing the gradual evo lution of these capabilities. Accordingly, the ability to learn—or learning-to-learn—has been defined as an even higher-order dynamic capability that drives the regeneration of regular, i.e., renewing, dynamic capabilities (Ambrosini, Bowman, and Collier, 2009; Collis, 1994; Schilke, 2014).<sup>3</sup>

In our research, we draw on Teece’s (2007) notion of dynamic capabilities as organizational capabilities needed “(1) to sense and shape opportunities and threats, (2) to seize opportunities, and (3) to maintain competitiveness through enhancing, combining, protecting, and, when necessary, reconfiguring” (p. 1319) the ecosystem’s resource and capability base. Breakdowns can occur at both the component and the complement level, causing critical challenges (Adner and Kapoor, 2010). Component challenges relate to factors that “limit value creation by constraining the focal firm’s ability to produce its product” or service (Adner and Kapoor, 2010, p. 310). Such challenges might result from breakdowns either at the level of supplier innovation (provision of components) or at the leve of system integration (interaction of components) (Kapoor, 2018). Complement challenges relate to factors that delay the “availability of suitable complements” to the focal offer (Adner and Kapoor, 2010, p. 313). Such challenges might restrain customers from fully realizing the value proposition and hence diminish the overall product or service value (Ethiraj, 2007). Consequently, customers adoption rates decelerate, thereby eroding an ecosystem’s competitive advantage in the long run (Adner and Kapoor, 2010).

As digitization increases, firms gradually transition their organizing logic from focusing on component providers to including complementors (Sandberg et al., 2020). Accordingly, we assume that during an ecosystem’s evolution, routines related to component challenges are generated earlier than routines related to complement challenges. Having clarified the nature of the routines that constitute our new framework, we subsequently develop their specific content.

## 3. Routines

In the following, we present our new orchestration framework comprising capabilities of sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring to address component as well as complement challenges. For each of the six emerging fields, we define a concrete challenge and offer insights into potential solution mechanisms, supported by examples from practice. We conclude by advancing a proposition for each of the routines. The model is illustrated in Fig. 1 and summarized in Table 1

## 3.1. Solving component challenges

## 3.1.1. Co-creating architectural knowledge (component/sensing)

3.1.1.1. Routine definition. Co-creating architectural knowledge relates to routines of deploying digital technology to jointly create and continuously maintain architectural knowledge, in specific regarding technology and its use. We define architectural knowledge as the knowledge about how components/technologies can be integrated “to form a coherent new […] architecture” (Jaspers, Prencipe, and Ende, 2012, p. 420).

3.1.1.2. Concrete challenge. Pagani (2013) suggests that digital innovation ecosystems are subject to dynamically shifting bottleneck positions—a process which can be associated with the value multiplicity of digital technologies (Henfridsson et al., 2018). More specifically, as environmental conditions change and the ecosystem evolves, the locations of control points for value creation and capture within the ecosystem are likely to shift, too. Given that the knowledge domains necessary for digital ecosystem innovation are often highly heterogeneous (Lyytinen, Yoo, and Boland, 2016), sensing opportunities and threats by detecting bottlenecks becomes a difficult task for orchestrators alone (Yoo et al, 2012). As orchestrators individually explore alternative product or service architectures, their search patterns might also be subiect to path dependencies (Yavavaram. Srivastava, and Sarkar. 2018) that preclude the exploration of specific domains. The resultant frictions and delays in building architectural knowledge affect orchestrators’ ability to generate architectural innovation in the long run (Baldwin, 2018; Jaspers et al., 2012).

3.1.1.3. Solution mechanism. This challenge points to orchestrators’ task to coordinate the joint creation of relevant architectural knowledge across the ecosystem. Brusoni, Prencipe, and Pavitt (2001) and Lee and Berente (2012) show that generating knowledge relevant to the ecosystem’s architecture is not bound to a single ecosystem actor. They suggest that firms’ knowledge domains partly overlap and are not necessarily limited to the requirements of the firm building them. Sensing in digital innovation ecosystems is hence dispersed among various actors (cf. Giudici et al., 2018). These processes not only support the identification of problems within product and service architectures (Lee and Berente, 2012), but also the identification of promising opportunities.

Digital technology adopts an important role in enabling the sensing, tracing, and memorizing of data relevant to the co-creation of architectural knowledge (cf. Wang, 2021). Such technology can help compile granular data that allow orchestrators and other actors to (jointly) detect opportunities for and threats to value creation and capture even in more complex architectures. For instance, the collection and storing of data through digital technology has been an essential driver for adopting an internet-of-things (IoT) logic in many industry branches, especially in the automotive sector. The development story of German electric vehicle start-up StreetScooter. acquired by Deutsche Post DHL Group in 2014, represents an example of how digital technology can affect collaboration across the ecosystem. StreetScooter facilitated the generation of architectural knowledge within its ecosystem by emploving a product lifecycle management (PLM) system that integrated the functionalities of various subsystems (e.g., CAD, ERP, CRM) across the ecosystem to monitor joint product development processes in real time (Kampker, Gerdes, and Schuh, 2017). By fostering the integration of pre viously separate systems, StreetScooter created a “single source of truth” (Kampker et al., 2017, p. 112) for data across all development processes. Ecosystem members could thus work with the same data set, drawing on digital shadows of the vehicle models (Kampker et al., 2017). StreetScooter hence reduced the risk of potential bottlenecks emerging from discrepancies in data and their interpre tation. With the PLM system facilitating the sensing, tracing, and memorizing of relevant information, StreetScooter managed rapidl to detect irregularities and delays in the vehicle production process and to respond accordingly.

Proposition 1. A digital innovation ecosystem will be the more innovative and effective at delivering its value proposition, the more or chestrators learn a) how to develop digitally-enabled systems that allow them to build architectural knowledge jointly with ecosystem members and b) how to use these systems to detect potential bottlenecks within the architecture.

## 3.1.2. Cultivating boundary objects (component/seizing)

3.1.2.1. Routine definition. To act upon the opportunities and threats discovered during co-creating architectural knowledge, or chestrators might cultivate boundary obiects. that is. develop routines to build and modify digital boundary obiects for component providers. We define boundary objects as “touchstones that help reveal differences, build common ground, and facilitate knowledge integration” (Zuzul, 2019, p. 740).

3.1.2.2. Concrete challenge. Yoo et al. (2012) argue that knowledge heterogeneity across actors pushes innovation activities towards the periphery of ecosystems. Such shifts impede the centralized orchestration of innovation and might cause risks of uneven rates of development and unpredictable product interdependencies (Ethiraj, 2007). However, sharing parts of each other’s knowledge bases may not be sufficient to ensure successful innovation. To make the gained knowledge operational, component providers need to be put in a position where they can develop a shared language that allows them to present their understanding of problems and corresponding solutions (Carlile, 2002). A lack of shared language can lead to misunderstandings and confusion that may not be resolved by verba exchange among the actors involved (Bechky, 2003). This might jeopardize realizing the opportunities detected as part of orches trators’ architectural knowledge, and, as a result, desired innovation outcomes might not materialize.

3.1.2.3. Solution mechanism. Boundary objects help facilitate the development of shared language in ecosystems with increasingly heterogeneous knowledge bases (Ganco, Kapoor, and Lee, 2020; Hong and Snell, 2013). Star and Griesemer (1989) note that such objects need to be “both plastic enough to adapt to local needs and the constraints of the several parties employing them, yet robust enough to maintain a common identity across sites” (p. 393).<sup>4</sup> In the digital realm, boundary objects might, for example, relate to digital representations (3D drawings; cf. Boland, Lyytinen, and Yoo, 2007) or software applications. They not only help translate heterogeneous knowledge bases but also facilitate creative and unpredictable development processes (Lyytinen et al., 2016). Trans ferring the early stages of product development to the digital domain can drastically reduce the costs of experimentation. The benefits of digital technology are unique and closely linked to its (re-)programmability and its ability to trace and store the data generated during experiments (Wang, 2021; Yoo et al., 2012). However, previous research shows that boundary objects need to fulfill specific criteria to be useful (e.g., Bergman, Lyytinen, and Mark, 2007; Carlile, 2002) and that the design and choice of such objects is not trivial (Zuzul, 2019).

As for the emergence of types of IoT logic within the automotive sector, the experimental and simulation affordances directly enabled by digital technologies are essential for innovation. For instance, BMW has recently joined forces with NVIDIA to create the simulation platform Omniverse, a digital tool for virtual factory planning (BMW PressClub, 2021). With this tool, the automaker cannot only shift many factory-planning activities to the digital world, but also engage in new forms of supplier collaboration (BMW PressClub, 2021). The case of StreetScooter more specifically illustrates the benefits of digital boundary objects: the firm drew on such objects by simulating digital vehicle models (Kampker et al., 2017). These simulations were then translated into interim hardware prototypes that presented a common ground for discussion among the component providers involved (Kampker et al., 2017). The interim hardware prototypes also proved highly valuable in reducing the silofication of the various disciplines and departments engaged in the development process. More specifically, under its prototyping system, StreetScooter developed each interim hardware prototype to test different hypotheses about proposed solutions (Paluch et al., 2020). This allowed StreetScooter to focus on specific task requirements in each interim hardware prototype (Paluch et al., 2020). The results of the ensuing discussions informed the subsequent development phase.

Proposition 2. A digital innovation ecosystem will be the more innovative and effective at delivering its value proposition, the more orchestrators learn a) how to provide digital boundary objects tailored to the needs of their component providers and b) how to flexibly adjust these objects to specific task needs.

## 3.1.3. Renegotiating system integration (component/reconfiguring)

3.1.3.1. Routine definition. Renegotiating system integration refers to orchestrators' efforts to facilitate the reconfiguration of inno vation trajectories for the product and service architecture. While the routines introduced earlier concerned the identification of opportunities and the initiation of innovation processes to seize them, renegotiating the system integration aims at rearranging the architecture’s modules and how they are linked across the various layers. Specific features of digital technologies enable and support such renegotiation.

3.1.3.2. Concrete challenge. Given the complexity and dynamism of digitally-enabled value propositions, emerging bottlenecks may not be sufficiently addressed by mere component innovation. In this case, innovation at the system level—that is, architectura innovation—may be required (Henderson and Clark, 1990; Kusunoki and Aoshima, 2010). The need for architectural innovation can also arise from the accumulation of component innovations. Boland et al. (2007) describe the potential cascading effect of singular component or subsystem innovations, explicating that initial innovations are “subsequently shaped and reshaped by multiple, reverberating innovations” (p. 642). The resulting “wakes of innovations” (Boland et al., 2007, p. 631) might considerably alter the way in which components interact, thereby requiring new product and service architectures. As component innovations may emerge autonomously and, from the perspective of the orchestrator, unpredictably (Yoo et al., 2012), such architectural reshaping does not necessarily need to follow a grand scheme (Boland et al., 2007). Despite these circumstances, orchestrators might want to find ways to prevent incompatibilities of components within the larger product or service architecture.

3.1.3.3. Solution mechanism. In contexts of technological uncertainty, communicating the overall vision of the value proposition, and of changes therein, might help guide ecosystem innovation (Boland et al., 2007). Such an “organizing vision” (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997, p. 458) can help actors “interpret, legitimize, and mobilize” the innovation necessary to fulfill the ecosystem’s value proposition (Wang, 2021, p. 414). For orchestrators, it can serve as a tool to offer guidance to component providers, even if uncertainty is high and orchestrators are not in full control (cf. Datt´ee, Alexy, and Autio, 2018). As such, the vision the orchestrator defines specifies the space in which component providers may adjust architectural features. How exactly ecosystem actors use this space might be subject to their joint negotiation (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997). The discourse required for such negotiations can be enabled and supported by the memorizing and communication function of digital technology (Wang, 2021). Here, digital technology plays an essential role in defining a trading zone for ecosystem negotiation (Galison, 1997).

As automakers implement novel digital technologies to transition towards IoT principles, they also span trading zones in which they can coordinate architectural innovations with ecosystem members. StreetScooter’s ecosystem approach neatly illustrates the idea of a renegotiating system integration that is enabled by digital technology. While StreetScooter communicated a clear vision of the ve hicle’s production principle to component providers, the firm did not prescribe component specifications in form of detailed in structions; specifications were rather negotiated among StreetScooter and their ecosystem members (Kampker et al., 2017). Component providers themselves decided on the specifications of the modules they would supply. As adjustments made within one component might affect the integration of other components in the product system, component providers often had to renegotiate specifications among each other, with StreetScooter adopting a moderating role (Kampker et al., 2017). For that. the previously introduced PLM system represented an indispensable tool for interaction and communication. Based on the digital shadows built for the vehicle models, the consequences of changes in architectural features could be easily predicted and afterwards debated. Ecosystem members were thus able to influence component interfaces actively—along with the rules by which components form the overall production architecture.

Proposition 3. A digital innovation ecosystem will be the more innovative and effective at delivering its value proposition, the more or chestrators learn a) how to use digital technology effectively to craft and communicate the values and vision underlying the value proposition and b) how to create digitally-enabled spaces in which system changes can be bilaterally or multilaterally debated.

## 3.2. Solving complement challenges

## 3.2.1. Screening complementors (complement/sensing)

3.2.1.1. Routine definition. As orchestrators engage in routines to screen complementors, they proactively search for and recruit complementor firms to secure high complement quality.

3.2.1.2. Concrete challenge. Assuming that complementor orchestration is essentially based on a two-sided market problem (Kapoor, 2018), ecosystem orchestrators compete against other ecosystems through the number and quality of complementary offers (Bou dreau, 2012). The degree to which such offers are tailored towards the interface specifications of the ecosystem determines the overall product or service performance (Anderson Jr., Parker, and Tan, 2014). Complementors, however, might seek to offer their products on various platforms to increase their market reach—a practice called multihoming (Cennamo, Ozalp, and Kretschmer, 2018). As digita innovation ecosystems can be highly distinctive (Cennamo and Santalo, 2013), complementors face trade-offs regarding the extent to which they specialize in or even join individual ecosystems. Hence, when competing against other ecosystems, orchestrating firms need to be able to attract potential complementors to join the focal ecosystem. A lack of such competence represents a critical liability. However, unrestricted access to a largely anonymous third-party community of complementors might result in quality issues and disruptions, which would impair the overall value proposition (Cennamo, 2018; G. Parker et al., 2017; Van Alstyne, Parker, and Choudary, 2016). In other words, to navigate the value multiplicity of digital innovation effectively (Henfridsson et al., 2018), or chestrators need deliberate mechanisms for complementor selection (Chen, Tong, Tang, and Han, 2022; McIntyre and Srinivasan, 2017; Rietveld and Schilling, 2021). Tiwana (2015a) proposes that due to the difficulty of process control, orchestrators need to implement rigorous screenings of ecosystem extensions, or input control.

3.2.1.3. Solution mechanism. Orchestrators can attract complementors by installing suitable governance structures or simply by increasing the size of the other market side, i.e., the customers. Fang, Wu, and Clough (2021) also point out how important it is for orchestrators to hold temporary gatherings with prospective complementors. Such gatherings can be particularly valuable for or chestrators in the early stages of the ecosystem when complementors are still highly uncertain about whether to join the ecosystem or not (Datt´ee et al., 2018; Fang et al., 2021). Providing space to gain ecosystem knowledge, such temporary gatherings are similar to what Nonaka and Konno (1998) describe as the concept of “ba" and can take place in the form of, for example, developer conferences (cf. Ozalp. Cennamo, and Gawer. 2018). Foerderer (2020) finds that interfirm exchange at Apple's 2016 Worldwide Developers Conference spurred innovations by complementors. However, in addition to reaching out to complementors, Apple has also installed procedures of screening complementary products (Song, Xue, Rai, and Zhang, 2018).

Examples for complementor recruiting mechanisms can also be found in various automakers’ efforts to boost their car connectivity services by establishing own app developer platforms. For instance, at BMW's CarData and Volkswagen's OKAPI platforms, users can access telematics data, allowing them to develop new services or apps based on these data (BMW Open Data Platform, 2022; Volkswagen OKAPI, 2022). The example of Mercedes-Benz’ application programming interface (API) platform Mercedes-Benz /de velopers is particularly instructive in this context. Mercedes-Benz’ approach to selecting potential contributors for its ecosystem builds on temporary gatherings and includes the regular attendance at fairs, congresses, and hackathons. Such events give platform repre sentatives the opportunity to present their offers and engage in discussions with interested developers. The organization of tourna ments, such as the “Mercedes-Benz Digital Challenge” in which participants were granted access to a number of novel APIs and software development kits (SDKs) to develop mobility applications (Mercedes-Benz Digital Challenge, 2017), also reflects strategies to screen the market for talented developers. Award-winning developers are invited to “continue discussions with the Mercedes-Benz team to potentially continue development beyond the Digital Challenge” (Mercedes-Benz Digital Challenge, 2017). As such, the automaker tightly controls further development of generated applications.

Proposition 4. A digital innovation ecosystem will be the more innovative and effective at delivering its value proposition, the more orchestrators learn a) how to detect potentially valuable complements but also b) how to screen the quality of complementors contributions.

## 3.2.2. Co-specializing interfaces (complement/seizing)

3.2.2.1. Routine definition. Once orchestrators have recruited complementors, they need to ensure that complementors’ contributions are compatible with their ecosystem platform. We propose that ecosystem orchestrators engage in routines of joint learning to promote co-specialization with complementors. Such routines could include the joint development and adjustment of interfaces that allow orchestrators to ensure interoperability of the later product or service elements

3.2.2.2. Concrete challenge. As orchestrators seek to extend the scope of the ecosystem, they provide complementors with interfaces that reveal information about the product and service architectures underlying a value proposition (Constantinides, Henfridsson, and Parker, 2018). With this, they signal the design features complementary products and services need to exhibit to operate compatibly with their focal offering. As they provide a common ground for diverse ecosystem actors, such interfaces can be understood as a type of boundary object. However, different from our earlier conceptualization, we now particularly emphasize the controlling functions boundary objects can exert (cf. Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). Complementor interfaces are an essential nexus by which or chestrators can control the value space of their digital innovation (Henfridsson et al., 2018; Schreieck et al., 2021); hence, the design and development of such interfaces are particularly critical in digital innovation ecosystems.

Previous IS research has mostly focused on APIs and SDKs as digital representatives of complementor interfaces. Despite such interfaces’ importance for innovation within ecosystems, their development and provision remain a challenge to orchestrators. On APIs, Wulf and Blohm (2020) note that “many API initiatives fail because of insufficient knowledge about the targeted segment of third-party developers” (p. 252). As orchestrators develop the ecosystem and fail to fully consider the perspective of complementors, compatibility between the platform and its complements is at risk. Misspecification of interfaces, however, might also make orches trators vulnerable to competitors. Karhu, Gustafsson, and Lyytinen (2018), for instance, demonstrate that when interfaces are too open, hostile firms might copy (parts of) the platforms’ core and take some of the complementors.

3.2.2.3. Solution mechanism. As the ecosystem’s value proposition evolves, orchestrators need to adjust complementor interfaces to facilitate, but also control, continued participation (Gawer, 2021). Orchestrators thus provide interfaces that ensure constant align ment between their own and complementors’ objectives (Wulf and Blohm, 2020). To obtain the necessary knowledge about com plementors’ goals (Wulf and Blohm, 2020) and responses to changing interface specifications (Chen et al., 2022), orchestrators might engage in a mutual exchange of information with complementors (cf. Gawer, 2021).

Complementors’ rejection of an interface configuration initiated by orchestrators can also give rise to dynamics that lead to a joint shaping of such interfaces. For the iOS service system, Eaton et al. (2015) describe how Apple’s efforts to control innovation within the ecosystem by providing specific boundary resources were contested by coalitions of other ecosystem actors. As a result, some of Apple’s boundary resources were reshaped, and this tuning was distributed across the ecosystem (Eaton et al., 2015).

Firms in the automotive sector that further develop third-party communities for their car connectivity services face similar chal lenges. The example of Mercedes-Benz illustrates the joint shaping of interfaces. In addition to collecting developers' feedback at fairs. congresses, and hackathons, the team around the carmaker's API platform fostered learning processes by launching early versions ot APIs. The Connected Vehicle API was first offered as an experimental product, explicitly positioned as still being under development (Mercedes-Benz /developers, 2018). Based on the feedback and suggestions of developers, a new generation of telematics data APIs was released. A similar approach was chosen for developing the mobile SDK, which was first tested by a smaller number of app de velopers. A few months later, the SDK was officially launched. However. based on user feedback, it was discontinued soon after and Mercedes announced its future focus on APIs (Mercedes-Benz /developers, 2021). This shows how orchestrator and complementors jointly shaped the choice of interfaces.

Proposition 5. A digital innovation ecosystem will be the more innovative and effective at delivering its value proposition, the more orchestrators learn a) how to anticipate complementors’ reaction to interface specifications and b) how to engage in regular feedback loops with complementors to build knowledge about specific development needs

## 3.2.3. Restructuring complementarities (complement/reconfiguring)

3.2.3.1. Routine definition. When mere alignment with complementors does not suffice to generate necessary innovation, ecosystem orchestrators might want to generate routines for structuring processes of complementarity internalization and externalization. While the former might allow solving persistent bottlenecks at the complementor level, the latter can create value by shifting innovation efforts towards the wider ecosystem.

3.2.3.2. Concrete challenge. A proactive approach to selecting—and learning with—complementors might yield a substantial advantage over competing ecosystems. However, some bottleneck situations require more fundamental changes in an ecosystem’s complementarity structures. For instance, the performance of complementary offerings might constantly be below orchestrators’ expectations (e.g., Gawer and Henderson, 2007). As new value paths evolve within digital innovation (Henfridsson et al., 2018), ecosystem bottlenecks might also shift. Given the complexity of layered, modular product and service architectures, the effects of emerging or shifting bottlenecks on the overall value proposition can be particularly severe. Under such conditions, uncertainty reduction becomes a major concern of orchestrators. In the case of third-party development of specific complements, recalibrating interface specifications—as proposed in the previous chapter—might not achieve sufficient uncertainty reductions (cf. Gawer, 2021). However, when orchestrators develop complements, they might not be able to leverage the generativity digital innovation might offer (Hagiu and Spulber, 2013; Zittrain, 2007).

3.2.3.3. Solution mechanism. To reduce uncertainty in and retain control of the innovation process (Helfat and Raubitschek, 2018), orchestrators might decide to source critical complements in-house, that is, produce complements themselves (Cennamo, 2018). Orchestrators' market entry can have both positive and negative effects within the community of complementors (Zhu. 2019) Exploring Google's entry into the market of photography apps, Foerderer, Kude, Mithas, and Heinzl (2018) show that such a move can spur additional innovation as general consumer interest increases. However. Wen and Zhu's (2019) findings suggest that the mere threat of Google entering an app segment of its Android platform might also lead to lower innovation efforts of complementors. Nevertheless, internalizing complementarities might be an effective means to increase control and achieve predictability of ecosystem innovation. Prior to adopting entry strategies, orchestrators, however, should carefully consider the configuration of participation in complementary markets to minimize negative consequences (Gawer and Henderson, 2007). Externalizing the development of com plementary offerings—that is, shifting from in-house to third-party development—constitutes the flipside of such restructuring efforts. Hagiu and Spulber (2013) propose firms should consider such a move when transitioning from earlier to later lifecycle stages of digital innovation ecosystems. Retreating from the market might drive innovation in third-party offerings (Hagiu and Spulber, 2013). Cen namo et al. (2018) and Parmigiani and Mitchell (2009) add to these findings by underlining the importance of dynamically adjusting the balance between in-house and third-party development. These studies indicate that such balancing can substantially influence overall value creation (see also Masucci, Brusoni, and Cennamo, 2020).

The need for securing potential bottleneck positions within the ecosystem also drives automakers’ decisions on the development of car connectivity services. The example of Mercedes-Benz reveals processes of complementarity internalization: the Mercedes-Benz’ API platform covers only a limited set of APIs and hence not all types of telematics data that third-party products could potentially harness. By selectively opening, or externalizing, parts of its digital resources to complementors, the carmaker can control the scope of external innovation efforts while deliberately retaining some domains in-house. In 2015, Mercedes-Benz joined a consortium with Audi and BMW to acquire the mapping service provider HERE from Nokia (Newcomb, 2016). Mapping services are a critical ingredient for the functioning of connected car technologies in general and for autonomous driving technologies in particular (Newcomb, 2016). With this acquisition, Mercedes-Benz addressed a potential future bottleneck in the further development of its cars. As a result, the automaker internalized the previously external complementary market for mapping services for car mobility.

Proposition 6. A digital innovation ecosystem will be the more innovative and effective at delivering its value proposition, the more orchestrators learn a) how to decide where to source complementary offerings and b) how to anticipate the possible consequences of sourcing changes.

## 4. Discussion

Having developed our model for digital innovation ecosystem orchestration, we now discuss its contents. First. we elaborate on how the proposed routines dynamically shape each other. Second, against the backdrop of the current state of knowledge, we identify research opportunities for each of the six routines. Third, we lay out the overall theoretical implications of our novel framework. Fourth, we provide insights into how practitioners and policy makers might benefit from our findings.

## 4.1. Interrelations of orchestration routines

Dynamic capabilities research suggests that sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring capabilities are interrelated. Clearly, the extent to which firms can reconfigure existing resource and capabilities bases in response to changing environments depends on the investments that were made as part of seizing promising opportunities (Teece, 2007). In turn, firms’ investment decisions are informed by the set of opportunities and threats previously sensed (Teece, 2007). These interrelations also occur in the context of the routines outlined in our framework. For instance, whether orchestrators enact their routine to renegotiate system integration hinges on how effectively they cultivated boundary objects that enable interaction among ecosystem members. If orchestrators successfully leverage boundary ob jects, they might pursue new value creation opportunities—which, in turn, might increase the need to reorganize system integration. Whether orchestrators detect such opportunities in the first place, however, also depends on their routines to co-create architectura

knowledge.

Interrelations also exist between the two domains of component providers and complementors. As orchestrators’ ability to deliver the value proposition hinges on whether they can overcome challenges in both domains (Adner and Kapoor, 2010), deficiencies within the routines of one domain can negatively affect the outcomes of the other domain (cf. Park and Mithas, 2020). For instance, if an orchestrator is unable to manage system integration properly, potential complementors might find ecosystem membership less attractive because the delivery of the value proposition is at risk (Adner, 2006).

## 4.2. Research opportunities

In the following, we discuss knowledge gaps and research opportunities for each of the six proposed routines. Table 2 summarizes the research opportunities and delineates implications for practitioners and policy makers.

## 4.2.1. Research opportunity 1: Co-creating architectural knowledge

Previous research has provided ample descriptions and theoretical rationales related to the joint building of architectural knowledge. Most studies focused on the phenomenon of overlapping knowledge domains (e.g., Baldwin, 2018; Brusoni et al., 2001) or examined adaptations of these overlaps due to technological changes (e.g., Lee and Berente, 2012). However, as digital technologies offer extensive affordances to sense and trace actors’ activities (Wang, 2021), such knowledge overlaps can grow considerably. The tensions that might arise from ecosystem actors having extensive knowledge about other actors’ activities remain largely unaddressed and thus create an opportunity for further research. For instance, as component providers gain access to information relevant to building architectural knowledge, they might leverage the bargaining power they hold over the orchestrator (Lee and Berente, 2012). In turn, if orchestrators move beyond the mere gathering of architectural knowledge and explore component-related domains, they raise their bargaining power over component providers (Lee and Berente, 2012). These mutual knowledge explorations might heighten tensions between component providers and orchestrators as both might achieve a position where they can integrate the other side. Hence, future research could strive to uncover conditions that help mitigate tensions related to this routine.

## 4.2.2. Research opportunity 2: Cultivating boundary objects

Previous research compiled a comprehensive understanding of boundary objects (Bechky, 2003; Star and Griesemer, 1989) and of the specific features that make them so useful for shared language development (Bergman et al., 2007; Carlile, 2002). If such boundary objects include digital technology, they are, at least to some extent, reprogrammable (Yoo et al., 2012)—and this renders them partly agnostic to a specific functionality (Henfridsson et al., 2018). As a result, these types of boundary objects can be highly malleable which might cause disagreements among actors about the most suitable form (programming) of such objects. While Bergman et al. (2007) indicate that it may take long to find the appropriate boundary object representation, we lack detailed knowledge of how orchestrators can achieve the most useful representation of the boundary object. The evolution of boundary objects might even entail conflicts among the parties involved. Zuzul (2019), for instance, explores how boundary objects caused matter battles, or “high-stakes conflicts” (p. 739), among actors in the context of two smart city projects. Examining strategies and mechanisms to mitigate and overcome such conflicts can be highly informative for advancing our knowledge of this routine. Against this background, future research could also investigate the combination of several boundary object forms. Carlile’s (2002) categorization of different types of boundary objects can be a starting point to explore their varying configurations.

## 4.2.3. Research opportunity 3: Renegotiating system integration

Despite important insights into how ecosystem visions form and evolve (e.g., Datt´ee et al., 2018; Swanson and Ramiller, 1997), research on the topic in the digital innovation ecosystem sphere is still limited. While studies have well documented that boundar objects can serve as tools that allow productive negotiations among ecosystem members, it is ultimately the overarching vision with its related digital technology that spans the trading zones where exchange emerges (Boland et al., 2007). StreetScooter (Kampker et al., 2017), for example, initially communicated their vision without extensive codification. However, to use digital technology to store and communicate vision-related information (Wang. 2021). previous codification of the vision is indispensable (cf. homogenization ot data, Yoo et al., 2010). Previous research remains inconclusive regarding the potential implications that such extensive codification might have on the effectiveness of the vision. Given the high level of flexibility required for firms to thrive in contexts of digital innovation (Nambisan et al., 2017), codifying large portions of the vision might also entail detrimental effects (Vaast and Levina, 2006). Hence, examining the effects of codification and the interplay between codified and uncodified portions of such visions presents a promising avenue for future research.

## 4.2.4. Research opportunity 4: Screening complementors

The layered modular architectures of digital innovations (Yoo et al., 2010) offer unprecedented openness towards a community of third parties (Nambisan et al., 2019) that might remain largely anonymous to the orchestrator (G. Parker et al., 2017). Thus, defining screening mechanisms to accommodate the potentially detrimental effects of such openness remains a priority to orchestrators. However, wrongly specified screening processes might also emerge as a liability to ecosystem innovation (Van Alstyne et al.. 2016: Zittrain, 2007), for instance, if the likelihood of joining an ecosystem is reliant on participation in a temporary gathering. The so cialization processes at temporary gatherings might also have the (unintended) effect of exerting too much control over third-party communities (cf, Ouchi, 1979). Future research could hence develop a better understanding of how orchestrators learn to combine relevant selection tools to become most effective at attracting the desired type of complementors without excessively limiting the set of possible complements at a too-early stage. Fang et al. (2021) provide some insights into the diffusion channels and social mechanisms that work at temporary gatherings and eventually help orchestrators convince attendees to join the ecosystem. Such insights might present a promising starting point for related analyses.

## 4.2.5. Research opportunity 5: Co-specializing interfaces

Given the (partial) lack of direct interaction between orchestrators and the third-party community in digital innovation ecosystems (G. Parker et al.. 2017). interfaces become a critical nexus with considerable influence on the effectiveness of orchestration efforts. While Eaton et al. (2015) show that Apple’s interfaces were shaped as a result of conflict between the orchestrator and the community of complementors, co-specializing might also be the outcome of proactive collaboration efforts initiated by the orchestrator (cf. Mercedes-Benz example). Previous research does indicate how important it is for orchestrators to identify the type of complementors they intend to target with their interface (Wulf and Blohm, 2020). However, our understanding of the dynamics unfolding when orchestrators proactively strive to build such knowledge is limited. Specifically, it remains unclear how orchestrators can most effectively engage in collaboration with a largely anonymous third-party community and how they can identify valuable feedback. To address these limitations, we see potential benefits of introducing knowledge from user innovation (e.g., Franke. Von Hippel. and Schreier, 2006) into future research projects. This literature might offer valuable insights into distinct mechanisms to integrate interface users into development processes and engage in active learning. Orchestrators might also facilitate such learning by adopting the perspective of its complementors when evaluating interfaces specifications. While Parker and Axtell (2001) show the advantages of adopting suppliers’ perspectives, there are currently no similar analyses on the complementor dimension. It also remains unclear how orchestrators can actively shape complementors’ perspectives—that is, engage in perspective making (Boland and Tenkasi, 1995)—to facilitate collaboration

## 4.2.6. Research opportunity 6: Restructuring complementarities

Given the value multiplicity entailed in digital innovations (Henfridsson et al., 2018), orchestrators’ efforts to change competitive relations to complementors appear necessary to retain control over value creation and capture processes within the ecosystem. However, our understanding of restructuring complementarities is still skewed towards the internalization side. Most existing studies limit their focus on complementarity internalization, emphasizing firms’ decision to enter a complementary market or not. A decision not to enter a complementary market, however, does not equal the exit of such a market. Hagiu and Spulber (2013) indicate that orchestrators gradually externalize complementary development as they mature and conditions become more favorable. Yet it remains unclear in how far they might employ externalization at a later stage and in a similar strategic manner as internalization. A com plementary market exit might also be related to an economic downturn. It would be interesting, however, to explore strategic mo tivations for leaving complementary development entirely to third parties. In this context, research could examine concrete outcomes of externalization efforts. Specifically, exploring both positive and negative outcomes of externalization would help build a more comprehensive understanding of how orchestrators can scope their engagement within the ecosystem.

## 4.3. Theoretical implications

Based on El Sawy et al.’s (2010) suggestion to examine digital innovation from a dynamic capabilities perspective, we identified six distinct routines as constitutive of digital innovation ecosystem orchestration. In that, we complement earlier research that has traditionally emphasized governance structures of digital innovation ecosystems (cf. Nambisan, 2018). We see three major areas of theoretical contributions. First, our framework shifts scholarly attention to a largely neglected domain of research on digital inno vation ecosystem orchestration, that is, the role of learning mechanisms and routines at the level of the ecosystem orchestrator (cf. Steininger, Mikalef, Pateli, and de Guinea, 2022). Wang’s (2021) recent information ecology theory proposes that managing the part–whole integration in such ecosystems can be supported by digital technology providing relevant information. Our framework is consistent with this view and extends it by suggesting that this information might fuel learning processes and eventually enable the emergence of organizational routines. We conceptualize the development of orchestration routines as emerging from the constant alignment efforts that focal firms make to attenuate breakdowns in innovation processes. Second, we build on and expand previous conceptualizations of orchestration in ecosystem contexts (Helfat and Raubitschek, 2018; Schreieck et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2015; Venkatraman et al., 2014) by specifying a novel set of concrete routines based on a synthesis of relevant research. In line with our definition of digital innovation (Fichman et al., 2014), the identified routines are either enabled by digital technology or accommodate the implications of innovations embodied in digital technology. In that, the proposed routines are specific to the context of digita technology use, thereby complementing knowledge developed in more general contexts (e.g., Dhanaraj and Parkhe, 2006; Nambisan and Sawhney. 2011). Further. we characterize the orchestration of digital innovation ecosystems to be aimed towards the solution of two challenges: component challenges and complement challenges. Our model of ecosystem orchestration integrates literature from two major research streams that have considerably shaped IS research: system integration and platform research. In that, we emphasize that research on effective ecosystem orchestration needs to draw on insights from both supplier management and com plementor management literature. In addition, based on the development of the six orchestration routines, we provide a set of testable propositions. Third, we identify previously under-researched domains related to ecosystem orchestration. These insights might guide future research on ecosystem orchestration and be of value to scholars seeking to extend our understanding of how orchestrators learn to steer ecosystem actors (cf. Webster and Watson, 2002).

## 4.4. Implications for practitioners and policy makers

Our model has implications for both practitioners and policy makers. First, as the development of ecosystem orchestration ca: pabilities requires learning, orchestrators might want to establish internal structures which assist them in systematically capturing and analyzing previous orchestration experiences. As Zollo and Winter (2002) suggest, such learning includes mechanisms of experience accumulation, knowledge articulation, and knowledge codification. These processes need to be actively managed. Orchestrators might thus need to assign clear responsibilities as to which department within the firm oversees collecting and codifving relevant knowledge about ecosystem orchestration and decides which types of knowledge to retain. In turn, a lack of clear responsibilities might risk fragmenting the knowledge and learning relevant to ecosystem orchestration. The propositions provided in this article might guide orchestrators towards possible key priorities of such learning. Second, by differentiating between component and complement pro viders within the ecosystem (Adner and Kapoor, 2010), our model cautions against adopting a one-size-fits-all solution to ecosystem orchestration. Orchestrators might want to develop awareness of the role and location of an ecosystem actor to decide which set of routines might be suitable to (re)align the actor’s innovation behavior. To achieve this, it might be helpful to build competencies that allow the orchestrator to (visually) map the ecosystem surrounding the firm and changes therein (cf. Iansiti and Lakhani, 2020). Third, as orchestrating digital innovation ecosystems is based on learning processes. firms need sufficient exposure to orchestration situations to accumulate relevant knowledge. However, when power in ecosystems is highly concentrated and members suffer from winner-takeall-or-most dynamics (Cusumano, Gawer, and Yoffie, 2019), orchestration capabilities might be unevenly distributed across an in dustry. Given the growing relevance of digital innovation ecosystems (Nambisan et al., 2017), policy makers hence might want to find ways to provide firms with access to the knowledge and best practices required to orchestrate such ecosystems. Governmental funding schemes for the establishment of local ecosystems comprising a set of smaller actors present a promising approach in this direction. When formulating corresponding funding policies, policy makers need to define suitable instruments that help collect and codify actors' orchestration insights throughout the ecosystem development (e.g., Acciai. 2021). Such learning collections might be effectively compiled by universities conducting accompanying research on the unfolding ecosystem dynamics (cf. Fleming and Sor enson, 2004). These universities can then oversee the dissemination of gained knowledge through ecosystem-internal tools (work shops, courses, etc.) or, more broadly, through scientific and non-scientific publications. However, given the wide scope of routines outlined in our framework, the effectiveness of such efforts hinges on a thorough understanding of ecosystem members’ specific needs in orchestration capabilities. Hence, it can be of great benefit to include ecosystem members early on in the formulation phase of related policies, for instance, through special dialog formats (cf. Haddad and Benner, 2021).

## 5. Limitations and conclusion

Our consolidated framework offers manifold contributions, yet is also subject to some limitations that open paths for future research. First, while employing Adner and Kapoor’s (2010) ecosystem model has yielded interesting insights, it might also constrain our perspective on digital innovation ecosystem orchestration. It suggests that ecosystem orchestration routines address two broad types of challenges, emerging either at the level of component providers or at the level of complementors. However, these two groups of ecosystem members are not further differentiated. Especially the group of complementors can be assumed to include a large di versity of actors. Ye and Kankanhalli (2018), for instance, emphasize the important complementary role that users can play in digita innovation processes. Future research can address this issue by investigating specific routines for the different types of complementors in an ecosystem. Second, it would be insightful to complement our orchestrator-focused framework with the perspectives of component providers and complementors (cf. Pierce, 2009). Given the innovation democratization accompanying digital technologies (Yoo et al., 2010), power differences among actors might become less salient. As a result, the boundaries between ecosystem roles slowly become blurred. leading to settings where there is not one single orchestrator (Lyytinen et al., 2016). Hence, understanding the orchestration routines of peripheral actors can extend our knowledge about digital innovation ecosystem orchestration. Third, as previously indicated, extant research differentiates various types of dynamic capabilities, including incremental, renewing, regen erative and improvisational dynamic capabilities (Ambrosini et al., 2009; Pavlou and El Sawy, 2010). The effectiveness of each of these types depends on the degree of environmental dynamism. While the routines proposed in this article largely focus on renewing dy namic capabilities, that is, capabilities that accommodate moderate to high degrees of environmental dynamism, it would be highly interesting to study orchestration routines for digital innovation ecosystems associated with incremental, regenerative, or even improvisational dynamic capabilities.
