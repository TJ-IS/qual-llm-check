# Aligning adoption messages with audiences’ priorities: A mixed-methods study of the diffusion of enterprise architecture among the US state governments

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Rhetoric Persuasion Identification Frame ambiguity Enterprise architecture US state governments Mixed methods

## A B S T R A C T

Prior studies on the diffusion of complex Information Systems (IS) innovations have leaned on the rhetoric of persuasion perspective to formulate rhetorical strategies that can persuade adopters to engage in adoption behaviors. Yet, most of them ignore the shifting priorities and changing identity of the audience. To address this gap, we extend the perspective by examining how in novators need to evolve the adoption message of an innovation by aligning it with the audience’s diverse and shifting priorities (and related identities). We trace rhetorical changes that the Na tional Association of State CIOs (NASCIO) introduced to promote the diffusion of enterprise ar chitecture (EA) between 2000 and 2012 among the 50 US states. We conduct a mixed methods analysis: first we qualitatively discern changes in the content of the rhetoric (message change); then we use a latent semantic analysis to measure the frame resonance between NASCIO’s shifting rhetoric and the audience’s changing priorities (alignment with audience priorities). Our findings highlight the importance of: 1) frame ambiguity that renders a complex IS innovation appealing to varied audiences over its diffusion trajectory; and 2) listening to the community members’ pri orities and aligning the adoption message with their dominant beliefs. Our analysis posits the rhetoric of identification as a complementary lens to account for the co-evolution of shared priorities and identity alignments between innovators and their audiences.

## 1. Introduction

Most information system (IS) scholars are familiar with the diffusion story of Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems: until the mid-1990s, ERP vendors promoted the systems by touting their economic benefits: increased efficiency, better integration, and sys tematic application architecture. Although the message appealed to some rationally inclined CEOs, it failed to attract the minds and hearts of others. In the late 1990s, ERP vendors and consultants changed their message and positioned ERP systems as a solution to the dawning Y2K problem. The fear worked and the adoption rates surged (Markus and Tanis, 2000; Wang, 2009). Similar stories are common in diffusing many complex IS innovations: total quality management (TQM) experienced a rapid growth when it was framed as a solution to nagging quality issues (Scarbrough, Robertson, and Swan, 2015; Zbaracki, 1998); Customer Relationship Manager (CRM) systems gained momentum when they were portrayed as a solution to declining customer loyalty (Wang and Swanson, 2008). In all these cases, adoption rates took off when the promoter’s adoption message suddenly clicked with the audience’s present priorities (Wang, 2009).

Given the prevalence of this phenomenon, innovation scholars have turned to discourse theories to examine the role and impact of symbolic devices conveyed by innovators<sup>1</sup> as rhetorical means to promote diffusion. Specifically, the scholars have probed whether the innovators can, and under what conditions, formulate effective adoption messages and to what extent such messages succeed in building adoption momentum in the focal community (Barrett. Heracleous, and Walsham. 2012: Currie. 2004: Green Jr., 2004: Markus, Dutta, Steinfield, and Wigand, 2008; Swanson and Ramiller, 1997). The focus in these studies has been on discursive in teractions between the innovators and the focal community, or within the community. In particular, studies have examined how these interactions erect a shared socio-cognitive model which community members draw upon to make sense of and act upon the innovation (Strang and Meyer, 1993; Swanson and Ramiller, 1997). Scholars have also advanced concepts such as computerization as a socia movement (Elliott and Kraemer, 2008; Iacono and Kling, 2001; Kling and Iacono, 1988; Markus et al., 2008) or organizing visions within institutional fields to understand the structuring and role of such models (Miranda, Kim, and Summers, 2015; Swanson and Ramiller, 1997; Wang, 2009).

While past discursive studies have been useful in revealing the evolution of the innovation rhetoric and its impacts on diffusion outcomes, several areas remain poorly understood. Prior studies have not carefully considered how the diverse and shifting priorities within the adoption audiences impact the evolution of rhetoric. Studies report analyses of aggregate responses from adopter groups (Davidson, Osterlund, and Flaherty, 2015; Heracleous and Barrett, 2001) and often simplify or gloss over the presence of multiple priorities and even contradictory interests among adopters (Cheney, 1991; Jarzabkowski, Sillince, and Shaw, 2010; Sillince, 2005). Yet, we know that audiences and their interests and priorities change over time and across the population. Prior studies also offer limited insights into innovators’ strategies to identify and deal with such diverse priorities. In consequence, most studies focus on temporal strategies to evolve the adoption message across diffusion stages (Green 2004; Green et al. 2008), or on strategies to attract the audience en masse (Miranda et al., 2015; Wang, 2009). What these studies miss is that in the context of complex IS innovations innovators need to also make sense of the diversity of the audience’s prevailing contingencies and interests, and how they can dynamically align their adoption message with the audience’s shifting priorities.

The void of heeding to the audience’s diverse and dynamic priorities can be partly traced to the dominant theoretical underpinnings of innovation rhetoric studies. Most studies draw on Aristotle’s classic theory of rhetoric and its analyses of the roles of symbolic devices (Aristotle trans., 1991). This is a view we call rhetoric of persuasion. Central to this perspective is to probe the content, structure, and delivery strategies of adoption messages and how they as a whole will create a persuasive message (Burke, 1951). However, overshadowed in such analyses is the question: what is the audience and what matters to it? These issues, however, become increasingly pressing in diffusing complex IS innovations. Due to their complexity. they offer multiple interpretations and myriad ways to build an adoption message around it. At the same time would-be adopters are prone to listen to “the wisdom of the crowd” and gestate over time a shared socio-cognitive model guiding sense-making of and acting upon the innovation (Currie. 2004: Kaganer. Pawlowski, and Wiley-Patton, 2010; Nielsen, Mathiassen, and Newell, 2013; Swanson and Ramiller, 1997). Yet, when the focal community remains fickle with possibly fluid and shifting priorities this leads to incoherent sense-making and diffuse responses (Davidson et al., 2015; Huang and Zmud, 2010). What should an innovator do in such situations? This calls for finding a ‘fit’ between the created adoption message and the audience’s dominant current priorities.

Heeding this conundrum, this paper examines how innovators of a complex IS innovation who face audiences with diverse and shifting priorities can evolve their adoption message in ways that recognize and draw upon the complexity of the innovation and align it with the audience’s shifting priorities. Specifically, we ask two questions: (1) how do innovators evolve the adoption message of a complex IS innovation given the diverse and shifting priorities of the audience? and (2) how do the innovators’ understanding of the audience’s diverse priorities impact the evolution of the adoption message and its effects?

Per Machiavelli’s seminal characterization of three types of intelligence, our focus is on interactions between an innovator’s evolving understanding and framing of the complex IS innovation—the first intelligence—and how it relates to the innovator’s evolving understanding of her audiences and how these interactions impact the shaping of the adoption message—the second intel ligence. To the best of our knowledge, issues related to the second intelligence and how it relates to the first intelligence during the innovation diffusion have not been fully understood and analyzed as a dynamic and evolving phenomenon. Nor has there been an alyses of how the two ‘intelligences’ are adjusted and realigned to some sort of ‘fit’. Due to a void of detailed studies and theory on the topic we conduct an exploratory mixed methods study (Venkatesh. Brown. and Bala. 2013: Venkatesh. Brown. and Sullivan. 2016) which examines the evolution and effects of National Association of State CIOs' (NASCIO) innovation rhetoric from 2000 to 2012 in promoting enterprise architecture (EA) adoption among the 50 US state governments. We first conduct a qualitative rhetorical analysis to address the first question (1); next, we conduct a quantitative latent semantic analysis (LSA) to address the second question (2). Findings from the two analyses are synthesized through meta-inferences to demonstrate how NASCIO’s listening to and understanding of the audience’s priorities shaped its rhetoric in ways that it ultimately became increasingly successful in promoting the EA diffusion.

Our analysis makes two contributions to the study of diffusing complex IS innovations. We show (1) how frame ambiguity (Jar zabkowski et al., 2010) serves as a useful mechanism to build diffusion momentum such that a complex innovation can be made to appeal to a wider gamut of audiences’ priorities; and (2) how listening to the community members’ shifting priorities informs the evolution of adoption messages and helps align the adoption message. In line with these findings we direct attention to the benefit of anchoring analyses of innovation diffusion in complex settings to rhetoric of identification (Burke, 1969) which is offered here as a complementary lens to account for the audience’s shifting priorities (Cheney, 1983).

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We first review the literature on innovation rhetoric to build a conceptua framework that informs our subsequent analysis. Next, we formulate the mixed method study design. The third section reports the findings around the evolution and impacts of NASCIO’s innovation rhetoric. In conclusion, we discuss how the findings contribute to innovation rhetoric studies on complex IS innovations.

## 2. Theory review

## 2.1. Literature on innovation rhetoric

Researchers have increasingly paid attention to the critical role of symbols and language and their impact on the diffusion of complex IS innovations (Barrett et al., 2012; Green Jr., 2004; Wang and Ramiller, 2009). These studies have focused specifically on the role of innovation rhetoric—“political and interest-laden discourse” (Suddaby and Greenwood, 2005, p. 39–40)—that shapes how the meaning and purpose of the focal innovation is understood in the focal community, and the extent to which rhetoric persuades the audience and nudges them toward adoption (Barrett et al., 2012; Currie, 2004; Green Jr., 2004; Heracleous and Barrett, 2001; Wang, 2009; Wang and Ramiller, 2009).

The literature on innovation rhetoric can be roughly divided into three streams (Table 1). The first stream focuses on the evolution of innovation frames. It draws mainly upon concepts originating from sociology of cognition and knowledge, and analyzes the creation of innovation meaning in the context of social movements and organizing visions. Studies share a macro-level focus in that they seek to unveil changes in community-level innovation frames defined as ‘deep’ structure shared meaning of an innovation (Barrett et al., 2012; Snow and Benford, 1988). For instance, Markus et al. (2008) identified three frames informing the deployment of automated un derwriting software in the mortgage industry: it started as a productivity tool for government-sponsored enterprises in the 1980s, but evolved later to a productivity tool deployed by large lenders in the early 1990s, and finally for all lenders in the late 1990s. Similarly, Davidson et al. (2015) specified four visions that guided the deployment of personal health records; and Constantinides and Barrett (2015) unveiled multiple frames informing a regional IT infrastructure development.<sup>2</sup> Most studies use process analyses and historica accounts to offer rich narratives of how the innovation's meaning evolved either as a result of technological advances or changes in the socio-economic environment. These studies also reveal underlying rationales behind frame changes, and how the changes possibl mobilized adoption behaviors.

The second research stream has focused on the evolution of rhetorical strategies. Here, scholars build on Aristotle’s works on rhetoric and identify multiple rhetorical strategies that help mobilize adoption behaviors (Barrett et al., 2012; Bernardi, Con stantinides, and Nandhakumar, 2017: Erkama and Vaara, 2010: Green Jr., Babb, and Alpaslan, 2008: Suddaby and Greenwood, 2005). We classify the studies in this stream as a meso-level perspective, because they unveil distinct delivery strategies for adoption messages that will shift the meaning of focal innovation at the macro level while recognizing the role of components of adoption messages at the micro level. For instance, Bernardi et al. (2017) identified rhetorical strategies such as ‘bracketing of differences’ or being ‘open about differences’ to communicate new policies and to construct a vision (frame) of a health sector reform in Kenya; Barrett et al. (2012) showed how software movements anchored around alternative frames by utilizing elements of logos (knowledge), pathos (emotion), and ethos (community values). These studies conduct content analyses about innovation messages deploved within the community as to understand how rhetorical strategies change—for instance, expressed in shifts in logos, pathos, and ethos<sup>3</sup> components of the message—and how such changes impact the diffusion outcomes.

The third stream seeks to get deeper into the underlving structure of the evolving rhetoric to understand how such evolution impacts the audience’s understanding of the focal innovation. Accordingly, we call this stream a micro-level perspective. These studies commonly apply well-established logical or inferential frameworks such as Toulmin’s structure of argument or Aristotle’s enthymeme to scrutinize in detail the structure of an adoption message (Berente, Hansen, Pike, and Bateman, 2011; Creed, Scully, and Austin, 2002; Green, Li, and Nohria, 2009; Harmon, Green, and Goodnight, 2015; Heracleous and Barrett, 2001). For example, Berente et al. (2011) analyzed the claims, grounds, and warrants in messages used in promoting the uses of and engagement with virtual worlds.

## 2.2. Research challenges

Jointly the three research streams illustrate at different levels of analysis that the evolution of innovation rhetoric matters for the innovation diffusion. Diffusion outcomes can be broadly understood by studying the evolution of frame content (the first stream), associated community-level rhetorical strategies (the second stream), or deploved micro-level rhetorical structures at the message level (the third stream). Because of their focus on different elements and levels of rhetoric and related sense-making, the three streams complement each other. Some scholars have also started to search connections across the streams to understand how the impact of innovation rhetoric travels across the levels, and vice versa. These have covered interaction between macro and meso-level (Barrett et al., 2012), and meso and micro-level (Heracleous and Barrett, 2001)).

When reviewing the findings and explanations associated with the three research streams we can also note areas in need of greater clarification. While the audience’s experience and actions have been central in the analysis of the evolution of innovation rhetoric (Hsu. Huang, and Galliers, 2014: Sillince. 2005: Suddaby and Greenwood. 2005), past research has not fully explored how the diversity in the audience’s priorities impacts rhetorical change and related innovation outcomes. Specifically, most analyses center around the temporal and/or contextual evolution of an adoption message. Most analyses assume that such evolution over time consolidates into a single dominant priority within the audience that matches the adoption message. For instance, Markus et al. (2008) connect the success of the diffusion of automated underwriting software in the mortgage industry to the issue of increased productivity that appeals to involved stakeholders; similarly, Heracleous and Barrett (2001) posit that the primary reason for brokers and underwriters to resist the adoption of electronic trading systems was their technical limitations and related effects of work content and productivity. While such succinct and simplified accounts have the benefit of parsimony, they tend to overshadow the cognitive burden faced by adopters when ambiguity and diverse opinions characterize the innovation content (Wang and Ramiller, 2009), or when the adopter’s priorities in reality conflict with the adoption message (Hsu et al.. 2014)

The issues of how the diversity of opinions and multiple priorities drive the evolution of innovation rhetoric has been observed in only a few studies: Green et al. (2009) identified multiple contrasting beliefs such as the quality, waste, and cost that guided managers’ responses to adopting Total Quality Management systems; Jarzabkowski et al. (2010) identified a gamut of interests that shaped the adoption of strategic practices in a UK business school; and Klein, Sørensen, Freitas, Pedron, and Elaluf-Calderwood (2020) revealed how the conflicts interests of different actors led to controversies surrounding the adoption of Google Glass. While the issue has be raised by critics of diffusion studies (Lyytinen and Damsgaard, 2001; McMaster and Wastell, 2005), most innovation rhetoric studies have not fully recognized or accounted for diversity in the audience’s priorities and how innovators can and need to adjust the adoption message accordingly.

Relatedly, prior studies offer limited insights regarding how innovators learn to deal with diversity in the audience’s priorities. This issue is largely ignored at the macro-level research stream, which focuses on evolving narrative accounts of existing or emerging innovation frames. The meso- and micro-level research streams offer primarily strategies to change rhetoric temporally across diffusion stages, but do not offer clear strategies for fitting'. For example, several studies posit that early adopters respond better to ‘pathos’ appeals (emotion-based arguments) while late adopters are attracted to logos and ethos appeals (Green Jr. et al., 2008; Green Jr., 2004); or that innovation frame clarity can improve adoption rates in early stages, but not in later stages (Currie, 2004; Miranda et al., 2015). Others have proposed to frame the innovation as a solution to a popular business problem to attract the audience en masse (Wang, 2009). But this strategy is likely to be ineffective when multiple urgent and potentially competing business problems cal simultaneously for management attention (Cheney, 1991; Jarzabkowski et al., 2010). Overall, the research tends to ignore the call for rhetoric changes as to address multiple, shifting priorities within the same group of audiences during the same diffusion stage

Addressing these research gaps is important for practical and theoretical reasons. From the practical standpoint, the complexity of contemporary IS innovations entails that would-be-adopters will encounter higher knowledge barriers to identify, choose, and implement the innovation (Attewell, 1992; Newell, Swan, and Galliers, 2000). Adopters are likely to turn to the focal community to establish a first-level understanding of the innovation (first intelligence): what the innovation is, what it does and why, and how to implement it (Nielsen et al., 2013; Swan and Newell, 1995; Wang and Ramiller, 2009). However, the focal community is diverse, and due to social media and the current speed and spread of communications, the audience has also become larger and wider with increasingly heterogeneous interests creating a larger problem of listening (second intelligence). Yet, how innovators find a delicate balance between numerous evolving priorities and choose adoption messages that befit the current diffusion context remains poorly understood.

From the theoretical standpoint, the impact of diverse and potentially competing priorities in the evolution of innovation rhetoric has been weakly theorized and empirically scrutinized. The three research streams share a common interest in the changing behaviors of innovators viewed as rhetors–those who craft a persuasive innovation rhetoric by adjusting their adoption messages, changing delivery strategies, or restructuring their argument. The shared theoretical foundation across these studies is what we term the rhetoric of persuasion (Aristotle trans., 1991). This perspective deliberates on the problem of how to choose the content and structure of a proper message (Burke, 1951). Under this view, the diffusion process is characterized by the rhetor’s struggle to come up with a ‘correct’ adoption message that will appeal to a broad audience and the clever adaptation of the message structure or its delivery strategy to change the audience’s mind (Currie, 2004; Davidson et al., 2015; Markus et al., 2008). Benford (1997) notes eloquently the possible limitations of the view:

…much of the [social movement] literature is written as though participant mobilization were simply a matter of movement activists pushing the appropriate rhetorical button. While it is true that several writers acknowledge ‘framing hazards’ and contingencies, these are presented primarily as problems movement elites can overcome provided they devise a ‘potent’ or ‘resonant’ framing strategy (p. 421).

This limitation can only be addressed by recognizing the critical impact of the diverse audience’s priorities and asking how in novators can reckon with it while they work with their innovation rhetoric. This will expand the view beyond the content and structure of the innovation message by heeding the interactions between an innovator’s adoption messages and the audience’s diverse priorities. In line with this we will next explore how innovators can listen to the audience and identify their priorities (second intelligence), and how they can align their adoption message with the diverse and shifting priorities of the audience (i.e. how the first and second in telligence interact during innovation diffusion).

## 2.3. Sensitizing model of rhetorical dynamics

How can innovators evolve the content and structure of their adoption message when they face audiences with diverse, possibly competing and shifting priorities? To account for such dynamics, we need to build a model of 1) how to frame the adoption message given the audience, and then 2) how to conceptualize the ongoing interactions between the rhetor and her audience. We will first conceptualize the framing process: the content and structure of adoption messages by drawing upon models of the evolution ot cognitive frames (Elliott and Kraemer, 2008; Iacono and Kling, 2001; Markus et al., 2008). These are viewed as interpretive schemata (acono and Kling. 2001) providing a higher level understanding of how the innovation works. how it can be applied, and what effects it will have. An innovator’s adoption messages are linguistic manifestations of such frames in that they supply adopters with cognitive ‘handles’ to make sense of the innovation (Bernardi et al., 2017; Davidson et al., 2015). This is the innovator’s account of the first intelligence of the focal innovation as framed for a given audience.

The process of frame construction is called framing (Benford and Snow. 20o0). It builds a shared understanding of the meaning of the innovation within the community. Three core elements of framing are: (1) diagnostic framing, which articulates a set of (business) problems or opportunities in relation to the focal community that can be addressed or realized by adopting the innovation—this element supplies the why-rhetoric which contextualizes the innovation into a business setting and supplies would-be adopters a schema to draw conclusions regarding the innovation’s salience to their context; (2) prognostic framing, which provides a structural account of the innovation content and characteristics—this framing guides adopters to understand the working of the innovation as to overcome barriers associated with the innovation’s know-what rhetoric; and (3) motivational framing, which justifies the engagement with the innovation by conveying it as a solution to specified problems and opportunities expressed in the why-rhetoric. Generally, motiva tional framing overcomes barriers associated with the innovation know-how (i.e., how the focal innovation will address specified problems).

The three framing processes provide the foundation to formulate adoption messages as a trifecta: 1) a problem (or opportunity) at stake (diagnosis framing), 2) a solution supplied by the innovation (prognostic framing), and 3) a justification for the solution (motivational framing) (Fig. 1). To improve the persuasiveness of the adoption message the three elements need to be diligently crafted and uniquely positioned in relation to a given audience. For instance, Miranda et al. (2015) posit that the clarity and diversity of business problems (diagnostic framing) influence diffusion success; likewise Wang (2009) states that popular business problems (diagnostic framing) raise the audience’s interest and legitimize the innovation; and Currie (2004) shows that evolving the solution and aligning it with the audience’s problems (motivational framing) increase diffusion success. Similarly, several others have sug gested that a strong justification (motivational framing) is crucial in improving the diffusion rates (Greenwood, Suddaby, and Hinings, 2002; Wang and Swanson, 2007).

Next, to account for the diversity in the audience’s priorities during the diffusion process we build on studies that have analyzed audiences’ diverse dynamics. Barrett et al. (2012) conceptualize the innovation diffusion as a dynamic of interactions between the rhetor’s ideology, her frames, and the audience (Fig. 2). At the deep structure level, a rhetor’s ideology informs the message con struction and produces a specific variation in frames which are then reflected in shifts in the adoption messages. The adoption message will be communicated to different audiences using alternative framings. Likewise Hsu et al. (2014) conceptualize innovation diffusion as a degree of frame resonance between innovation rhetoric and the audience's contingencies. When innovators build their rhetoric congruent to the audience’s contingencies, the innovation is more likely to be adopted. In both studies, rhetors recognize differences in the audiences and modulate their adoption messages, respectively. The audience is not a mere passive target of rhetoric in that their (non)responses invite rhetorical changes, when the innovators care to listen. By accounting for the audience’s response dynamics, these studies convey a richer understanding of diffusion as a dynamic rhetorical process where rhetors and the audience jointly coproduce the innovation framing and shape the innovation outcomes. This can be read as a constant confluence of the first and second intelligence.

In line with this we formulate a dynamic rhetoric model to analyze the diffusion of complex IS innovations as a rhetorical dynamic as summarized in Fig. 2. Throughout the innovation diffusion, the rhetor’s ideology forms a foundational belief system that informs the ongoing construction of adoption messages. Each message reflects the innovator’s core beliefs and conveys related cognitive schemata of the trifecta: the problem/opportunity, the solution provided by the innovation, and the justification for the solution (a.k.a. rhetor’s first intelligence about the focal innovation). These messages are communicated through multifaceted framing processes while the rhetor learns more about her audience. The effectiveness of adoption messages in mobilizing a pro-adoption bias depends on how well the message is structured (the positioning of the three components), how well the message is communicated (when, by whom), and how well the message aligns with the audience’s current priorities (i.e., the degree of frame resonance). As the rhetor listens to the audience’s (non)responses (rhetor’s second intelligence about the innovation), the rhetor will adjust the ideology (the first intelli gence) and/or adoption message to align the innovation better with the current priorities of the audience.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. Research setting

Our research setting is the diffusion of enterprise architecture (EA) in the 50 US state governments between 2000 and 2012. The US state governments provide us a chance to investigate a medium-sized innovation community that has a sufficient degree of hetero geneity in terms of economic, cultural and social factors. We chose EA because a significant amount of publicly available data is available on the evolution of EA concepts and applications in public government. The diffusion of EA in general has been troubled due to the proliferation of EA concepts with no clear sign of convergence on related applications and practices (Bui, 2017). In this paper we define EA loosely as complex innovation concept denoting a range of technical and managerial practices to organize firm-wide IT infrastructures and related business process capabilities for specific organizational goals (Bradley, Pratt, Byrd, and Simmons, 2011; Ross, Weill, and Robertson, 2006). The definition allows us to treat EA in a generic way and determine the temporally shifting boundaries of the phenomenon without cornering us in a particular approach of EA du jour.

Within this community we examined the rhetorical strategies of the National Association of State CIOs (NASCIO) to promote EA. The members of NASCIO comprise representatives of the all US state agencies (e.g., state CIOs, IT directors) as well as industry practitioners who have an interest in state-wide IT deployments (e.g., consulting firms). As part of its mission, NASCIO organizes collaborations among its members to conduct studies and publish white papers and policy guidelines that help NASCIO members to deal with new critical IT issues. For IT leaders within the US state governments, NASCIO has acted as a broker for innovative IT practices including EA, cybersecurity, data management, or IT governance.

On the research topic—EA diffusion—NASCIO acted as an active innovator among the US state governments. NASCIO’s audience included the state CIOs and senior IT leaders across all 50 states who had to decide whether the government would adopt EA. Though NASCIO’s membership is the same as its target audience, NASCIO during EA diffusion served as a semi-independent innovator for several reasons. First, while some state CIOs served on NASCIO’s EA committees, they typically represented a small number of “first movers”. As early adopters, they sought to convince remaining states to adopt EA. They faced the classic “crossing the chasm” problem where early innovators had to adjust their rhetoric to attract the rest of the population (Moore, 1999). Second, the degree of the overlap in the membership between NASCIO and its target audience is low due to the community dynamics. A typical state CIO has a short tenure–22 months on average (NASCIO, 2005b). As a result, NASCIO’s rhetoric and the audience’s priorities are constantly changing, fluctuating the overlap. Finally, the likelihood for a state CIO to operate both as an innovation promoter and as an adopter is low. People who volunteered at NASCIO to promote EA were not the same people who decided on EA adoption; and state CIOs who participated in NASCIO’s work still had to argue for the value of EA to key decision makers at their state (e.g., functional managers, CFOs, and politicians serving on management boards). NASCIO’s innovation rhetoric in this regard played an important role in providing “ammunition” for the state CIOs to argue in favor of EA.

## 3.2. Research design

We followed an exploratory mixed methods approach (Venkatesh et al., 2013; Venkatesh et al., 2016). A two-strands convergent mixed methods design was chosen because it allowed us to apply separate analyses on separate text corpus to answer two different research questions and to combine findings to “obtain more complete and corroborated results” (Creswell and Clark, 2018). Fig. 3 provides an overview of our research design. First, to grasp the evolution in the content and structure of the adoption message (RQ1), we conducted a content-based rhetorical analysis following the tenets of interpretive structuralism (Phillips and Hardy, 2002). This method has been employed in past IS studies for similar purposes (Berente et al., 2011; Heracleous and Barrett, 2001). Second, for RQ2 we probed the degree of frame resonance between adoption messages and their audiences’ priorities by applying a quantitative latent semantic analysis (LSA). LSA is a well-established computational method for structural text comparison (Evangelopoulos, Zhang, and Prybutok, 2012; Larsen and Monarchi, 2004) and it offers means to systematically measure the overlap between the innovator’s rhetoric and the audience’s priorities. This approach contrasts well with past IS innovation studies which have examined the evolution of frame resonance primarily by matching qualitatively texts expressing an innovator’s and the audience’s priorities (e.g., Hsu et al. (2014)). The findings from qualitative and quantitative analyses will be integrated in the last step to make meta-inferences that canvass a richer understanding of the evolution of innovation rhetoric. We follow guidelines suggested by Venkatesh et al. (2016) to establish the validity of our inferences.

## 3.3. Qualitative strand: Rhetorical analysis

We first conducted a content analysis to reveal the evolution in the content and structure of NASCIO’s adoption messages. Our text corpus included all EA materials published by NASCIO’s Enterprise Architecture and Governance Committee between 2000 and 2012. The EA Committee was established by NASCIO in 1999 to help US states understand and adopt EA concepts. The period between 2000 and 2012 is ideal for our research question because it covers the inception of the EA concept in state governments (around 2000) to its widespread use around 2010. Due to its longevity and comprehensiveness the data corpus can be viewed as one of the best recorded discourses concerning EA and its adoption among the 50 US states. All together, we collected 34 publications (over 2000 pages).

We followed theory-driven thematic coding (Bovatzis. 1998) to identify and organize changes in NASCIO's rhetoric. We analyzed the text corpus using a coding framework that drew on our theoretical model (Fig. 2). Specifically, each publication was coded for the following elements: the publication’s general purpose; identified problem for EA adoption (diagnostic framing); specified EA char acteristics such as definitions, components, and processes (prognostic framing); specified justification such as how EA can solve specified problems; and examples of successful EA applications (motivational framing). The coding was entered in NVivo software and organized into a 34 × 5 matrix which summarized the evolution of NASCIO’s rhetoric. Next, we probed the matrix to detect changes in the structure of the adoption message to identify distinct periods in NASCIO’s rhetoric. Specifically, we interpreted each change in EA definitions (prognostic framing) as a signal of a potential discovery of a new rhetoric. Overall, two distinct periods with alternative EA definitions and adoption rationales were identified. We also identified two publications that provided transitional rhetoric justifying the shift from one rhetoric framing to another: these narrated how and why NASCIO had adiusted its EA rhetoric to better fit the EA concept to the audience’s contingencies.

Finally, we analyzed the content of discourse for each period to articulate the content and structure of the adoption message therein. Specifically, we examined the three frame elements and synthesized NASCIO’s overall argument for each period. We combined those arguments with our evidential reading of NASCIO’s websites and publication to identify the driving ideology and frame con struction (adoption messages). To check the reliability of the coding framework (Appendix A), we asked another researcher to apply our coding guidelines to a select sample of the materials (Boyatzis, 1998). The coder was provided with segments of three publications (13 pages in total) and asked to follow the coding guidelines and code for the problem and justification frames. We excluded coding for solution, because 1) it was a simple process to search for EA definitions, and 2) it requires technical understanding of EA that the coder did not have. Our own codes and the coder’s had over a 70% agreement score, an acceptable score to establish coding reliability (Boyatzis, 1998).

## 3.4. Quantitative strand: Latent semantic analysis

Next, we conducted an LSA analysis to compare the evolution of NASCIO’s innovation rhetoric (from qualitative strand) with the evolution of state CIOs’ priorities to detect changes in the degree of frame resonance, and how these dynamics related to diffusion outcomes. First, to understand the dynamic shifts in state CIOs’ priorities, we collected another text corpus from NASCIO’s semiannual conferences. Each vear. NASCIO organizes two conferences where state CIOs and other interested parties gather to report and discuss salient IT issues and “best practices.” In each conference, NASCIO conducts a survey among all participating IT leaders, and the results are aggregated in a ranking of IT priorities for state CIOs in the coming vear. These reports made up the second text corpus and provided evidence of dominant priorities among state CIOs over time. For our study, we collected nine reports covering years 2002–2011. For earlier years (1999–2001), we relied on topics published in NASCIO’s conference agendas, other raw survey data collected during the conference, and the content of conference presentations. Second, to understand the underlying belief systems of state CIOs' priorities, we examined statutes that formally enact the office of CIOs in four representative states—Kansas, Massachusetts. Michigan, and Hawaii—and the office of the federal CIO (i.e., the US CIO). This supplement text corpus includes 150 pages in total.

Using the two text corpuses, we compiled sets of CIOs’ priorities that were present in the distinct rhetorical periods identified in the qualitative analysis. Because the ranking of state CIOs’ priorities changes over time, we assigned a value expressing the priority using its reversed order of the ranking for each year. For example, in a given year, if a priority was ranked number one out of ten CIO priorities, it would receive ten points. The aggregate points would reflect how CIOs ranked a priority in each period. After this step, we populated a list of priorities and their ranking for a given period. We also validate the rankings by cross-checking the expressed priorities with the significant socio-economic events and IT legislations during the study.

In the next step, we analyzed the evolution of frame resonance which is expressed as the degree of overlap between NASCIO’s rhetoric and state CIOs’ priorities. We determined the overlap by calculating the cosine similarity between the list of NASCIO’s EA identified problems (diagnostic framing in qualitative strand) and the list of the state CIOs’ priorities in a given period. The cosine similarity has been used in latent semantic analysis to calculate the content overlap between text documents (Evangelopoulos et al., 2012; Larsen and Monarchi, 2004). Appendix B provides more details on our calculations. And because each priority has a different ranking by state CIOs, we also calculated a weighted cosine similarity measure to assess how much a key problem in NASCIO’s rhetoric matched the state CIOs’ ranked priorities in a given period. We identified key problems in a given period by counting the number of times a problem was featured in the title or summary of NASCIO’s publications. If a problem was prominently featured in at least two publications, it was categorized as a key problem. For example, if information sharing appeared in the title or summary section of four different publications, and the overall reading of those publications confirmed our assessment, then information sharing would be counted as a key problem.

## 4. Findings

We identified two periods in NASCIO’s EA rhetoric. We first report findings from our qualitative analysis which focuses on the evolution of content and structure of adoption messages. Next, we report findings from our quantitative analysis of how changes in NASCIO’s rhetoric followed the evolution of state CIOs’ priorities. Finally, we report meta-inferences that integrate the analysis into a dynamic model of innovation rhetoric

## 4.1. Evolution of NASCIO’s adoption messages

## 4.1.1. Initiation period: 2000–2004

In 1998, NASCIO received a grant from the Department of Justice (DoJ) to facilitate and encourage information sharing between state governments. NASCIO conducted an initial feasibility project, which concluded that inter-agency information sharing is highl important. To achieve this goal, NASCIO recommended establishing a national architecture for information sharing between gov ernment agencies (NASIRE, 2000). Following the report, NASCIO recognized EA as a way to streamline data sharing, establish data commonalities, and erect essential infrastructure. In 2000, NASCIO launched its EA development task force as a means to facilitate information sharing among state governments. The committee issued a series of documents to help its members understand the EA philosophy: a white paper in 2002 to justify EA use, a toolkit in 2002 to guide EA adoption, and an EA readiness assessment and maturity instrument in 2003 as a means to manage the process of adoption. These documents made up key content of NASCIO’s rhetoric during this period.

It is not surprising that NASCIO’s ideology during this period was that of a collaborator with the DoJ as to promote ‘rational’ EA adoption among the state governments. This is evident in NASCIO’s website (2002) where the EA working group described itself as follows:

NASCIO’s Architecture Working Group is working in partnership with the U.S. Department of Justice, Office of Justice Programs (OJP) to institutionalize enterprise architecture in state and local government. The Architecture Program has produced deliverables designed to aid government entities as they build a technology adaptive enterprise through implementation of an architecture that is responsive to business processes and the supporting technology. (NASCIO’s website, 2002).

The adoption message that NASCIO used for EA adoption was that EA would create an “adaptive enterprise architecture” that enabled information sharing, enhanced service delivery, and improved government functions. This was proclaimed in NASCIO's website:

Adaptive enterprise architecture effectively supports the business of government, enables information sharing across traditional barriers, enhances government’s ability to deliver effective and timely citizen services, and supports agencies in their efforts to improve government functions. Enterprise architecture supports the identification and optimization of the entity’s interrelated business processes and resulting IT systems. The enterprise architecture promotes a constant re-evaluation of enterprise needs and is the best way to build a technology adaptive enterprise. (NASCIO’s website, 2004).

How did NASCIO articulate the adoption message? Regarding the prognostic framing EA was specified as a technical “blueprint” that would establish the infrastructure for information sharing. In the EA Development Tool-Kit version 2.0, EA was defined as: “an overall plan for designing, implementing and maintaining the infrastructure to support the enterprise’s business functions and underlying networks and systems” (NASCIO, 2002a, p. 234). NASCIO adopted a technical view of EA and portrayed it as a planning approach to create IT infrastructures that would support information sharing and increase data integration (NASCIO, 2002a).

In diagnostic framing, NASCIO portrayed the lack of information sharing and poor interoperability as key IT problems that gov ernments faced. To ground this argument, NASCIO cited several regulations on information sharing as key warrants for the proposed EA approach, as well as reported successful cases of good information-sharing practices. When compared to other listed EA problems, information sharing was mentioned at a higher frequency, accounting for 18 out of 32 coded references. It stood out as the key problem with three documents solely devoted to information sharing out of all 11 published documents. Besides information sharing, other specified EA problems were IT procurement, regulations for security, and e-government.

For the motivational framing, EA was proposed as a solution to information sharing. Out of all the specified EA problems, moti vations for EA as a solution to information sharing accounted for the majority of justifications provided (four out of five coded ref erences). In its first report on information sharing, NASCIO justified the business case for information sharing using an economic rationale: shared information is more accurate, more timely, more complete, and less expensive (NASIRE, 2000). The rationale was strongly backed by authoritative sources: a letter from Chairman Tom Davis of the House Oversight Subcommittee on Technology and Procurement Policy encouraged participation in NASCIO’s EA program as well as expressed goals for other agencies to adopt NASCIO’s EA program (NASCIO, 2002b, p. 2).

How did the state government audience respond to NASCIO’s rhetoric? In 2004, NASCIO assessed 10 states’ EA adoption and learned that short state CIO tenures and state budget deficits were blocking the adoption of EA. CIOs faced difficulties in justifying EA investments due to funding shortages and the pressure to show short-term results (NASCIO, 2004). Particularly, EA was at that time an abstract and unfamiliar concept (with low observability and triability), and many CIOs felt it was a high-risk low reward initiative given their short tenure (on average 22 months). To help supporting EA adoption, many respondents requested “technical assistance” from NASCIO and were troubled by NASCIO’s ideology and its adoption message. They viewed NASCIO’s focus on information sharing as too limiting, as substantiated in a 2005 survey. NASCIO recognized that it needed to diversify its funding stream to broaden the scope of its EA program:

Funding for the NASCIO EA program must match the priorities of the states and the federal government. Funding must encourage and enable the continued development and maturity of state enterprise architecture operating discipline, NASCIO should seek additionai funding from other sources besides the Bureau of Justice Assistance. Other potential sources include additional federal agencies, foundations, and corporate partners. (NASCIO, 2005b)(bold text added).

In summary, during the Initiation period, NASCIO’s ideology was of a collaborator with the DoJ to institutionalize EA in state governments for increased data sharing. Its adoption message was framed as a need to build an “adaptive enterprise architecture,” which would address key technical problems such as information sharing and interoperability. EA was defined as a technical blueprint to create a common architecture for data standardization and interoperability. However, NASCIO’s initial message was not wel received; many states found NASCIO’s justification for EA to be unconvincing for local use and limited in scope. Subsequently, these concerns prompted NASCIO to adiust its rhetoric during the next period

## 4.1.2. Growth period: 2005–2012

At the beginning of this period NASCIO conducted two studies to assess its EA program. The first was an assessment tour of 10 states (2004), and the second was a national survey of EA adoption (2005). These assessments helped NASCIO realize that while there had been substantial interest in EA adoption, most EA efforts had focused on technical aspects—a part of the whole EA concept—while other areas in EA deployment such as business, information, or solution architecture had been largely neglected. EA programs had inadequately addressed the why question (i.e.. EA justifications). which raised a concern about the relevance of EA for states. These concerns prompted NASCIO to adjust its rhetoric. Since 2005, NASCIO’s innovation rhetoric has been expressed in series of publi cations that focus on concrete, short-term value propositions that back the use of EA. In addition, NASCIO has downgraded the justification of EA based on information sharing benefits, and rather frames EA benefits around critical emergent IT management practices including cloud computing, data governance, and business analytics. The EA adoption rationale has centered on government transformation, and the goal is to make EA a necessary “passage point” for new government practices.

During this period, NASCIO’s ideology shifted away from being a close collaborator with the DoJ to being an enabler of state governments’ initiatives—a rhetorical shift deemed more appropriate to influence the audience. This becomes evident in surveying NASCIO’s website where its description has changed from “partnership with the Department of Justice” to “enable the mission of state and local government”. This change was made in January 2006, a few months after the 2005 national survey on EA adoption. NASCIO appears to have adjusted its rhetoric based on the states’ feedback:

The NASCIO Enterprise Architecture program was developed to enable the mission of state and local government. Government must continually reinvent itself to remain relevant by effectively and efficiently providing services to the citizens of this country. The path to this continual transformation must embrace leadership, management, coordination, communication and technology throughout government. Enterprise architecture is the discipline to appropriately define and leverage these capabilities within the complexities of government. (NASCIO website, published Jan 12, 2006)

The new ideology also shifted NASCIO's adoption messages and framing processes. In 2005, NASCIO published a short “Business Case Summary” which claimed that “EA is not an end in itself, rather it is a management engineering discipline that can guide the path toward transformation and provide the tools for management of change in government" (NASCIO. 2005a, p. 3). This signified a shift in NASCIO’s rhetoric. In the first period, NASCIO’s agenda was primarily to ‘push’ EA as a means to information sharing; but in the second period, NASCIO portrayed EA as a means to government change. This rhetorical move broadened the scope of EA applications. In addition, to help state CIOs justify EA to state-level constituents. NASCIO emphasized telling success stories of EA initiatives that CIOs would find valuable:

NASCIO has collected success stories from a variety of sources including its various awards programs. The NASCIO Architecture Working Group members have found that success stories provide an invaluable dimension of the underlying analysis when presenting the business case for EA related projects. (NASCIO, 2005a, p. 3).

How did NASCIO articulate the new adoption message to its audience? During this period, the prognostic framing changed. From 2005 EA has been defined as a “management engineering discipline” enabling government transformation. The definition represents a significant shift from the previous meaning of EA as a technical blueprint. The new definition is business-oriented where EA is viewed as a valuable managerial approach to govern during the digital age. NASCIO’s EA approach portrays state CIOs now as critical change agents in their governments and urges them to treat EA as a critical management discipline that gives them credibility and accountability and “not simply a methodology for managing technology” (NASCIO, 2007b).

Enterprise Architecture is a management engineering discipline that presents a holistic, comprehensive view of the enterprise including strategic planning, organization, relationships, business process, information, and operations. (NASCIO, 2007a, p. 1).

Subsequently, the diagnostic framing was changed. NASCIO’s 2005 survey concluded that information sharing should not be the primary focus of EA; it should be “something else” defined by activities that CIOs will engage in as they deploy EA (NASCIO, 2005b). That “something else” was about government transformation that could cope with increased budget deficits and growing citizen demands. The key problem shifted from a technology-driven problem (information sharing) to urgent business problems faced by government leaders. During this period, NASCIO’s EA program started to focus on issues that were placed high on state CIOs’ agenda including electronic records management (4 designated documents out of 20), cloud computing (3/20), data governance (3/20), business analytics (2/20), and IT procurement (2/20). The logic was to position EA as a means for government transformation and demonstrate its power in supporting whatever challenges the CIOs would face:

The benefits of agility through utilizing Enterprise Architecture for overall support of your directions include: better investment man agement, more effective execution, integrated program management, alignment of resources to the needs of government, government responsiveness to change, reduction in unnecessary redundancy, higher return on assets, effective resource deployment. (NASCIO, 2007a, p. 2)(bold text added).

NASCIO's motivational framing was also broadened to cater to a wider range of priorities faced by its audience. Specifically. EA was positioned to solve critical and specific problems that state governments faced while they tried to transform their services (e.g., electronic record management, change management, business analytics, cloud computing). To this end, NASCIO sharpened its rhetoric in a series of publications which elaborated the impacts of EA in addressing state governments’ prominent IT priorities including data governance, business analytics, and cloud computing. The publications were no longer about “how to do EA” as an engineering practice but about “how to do your priority initiatives with the help of EA”. The publications offered both guidelines and evidence of how EA had helped states to address primary challenges. In doing so. NASCIO started to justify EA adoption in strategic terms by positioning EA as an integral part of managerial solutions for state CIOs.

How did the audience respond to NASCIO’s new rhetoric? By 2012, 24 states had a formal EA program. Out of the 24 states, 11 formally used NASCIO's EA framework, or some of its elements. Out of the six states that had adopted EA after 2005, three utilized NASCIO’s EA framework (WA, MI, HI) and one state considered NASCIO’s framework in its development process (CA).<sup>4</sup> While the evidence is not conclusive, it does suggest that NASCIO’s adjusted rhetoric was followed with a growing uptake of EA among states.

In sum, after 2005, NASCIO’s ideology was shifted to become an “enabler” of state governments (Table 2). Its adoption message changed to focus on “government transformation” rather than “information sharing”. Subsequently, NASCIO adjusted its framing processes where EA is defined as a “management…discipline” that supports state CIOs’ priorities. The target of EA deployment was a broad problem set of government transformation and concomitant management issues. This diagnostic framing allowed NASCIO’s EA program to align to a majority of priorities important to state CIOs. NASCIO also strategically positioned EA as a supporting, com plementary initiative that helped key state initiatives related to government transformation. The approach allowed EA investments to be piggybacked with emergent IT initiatives and avoided making EA programs “compete” with urgent priorities. This, if nothing else, made EA widely acceptable to state CIOs as well as other decision makers

In these capacities, they are no different from private sector CIOs (Grover, Jeong, Kettinger, and Lee, 1993). Yet, there is one big difference which is hinted at in the legislation. Many duties of state CIOs are specified through executive orders or legislation whereby the office of a state CIO is a public office that must satisfy duties outlined for public interests. Prior research has also pointed out that public-sector IT managers often have different goals than private-sector IT managers do: to create public or common value rather than to pursue profits, to maintain social justice rather than to pursue economic performance, or to avoid risks and reduce costs rather than to pursue competitive advantage (Rocheleau and Wu, 2002). Accordingly, we posit that one crucial aspect in the state CIO belief systems is to manage the state’s IT resources in the best interest of its public constituents.

Over time, state CIOs’ duties have remained relatively stable, but the specifics will fluctuate depending on the political and eco nomic environment and technological change. This is evinced whenever a state reorganizes its IT governance or restructures its IT department: it will introduce new statutes and legislations to reframe the responsibilities of the state CIOs. For example, the state of Massachusetts enacted in 2017 an Act to reorganize its IT functions when the state faced rising security and privacy concerns, with the goal to improve data security and privacy and to promote better service delivery.<sup>6</sup> Subsequently, the statute that articulates the duties and goals of the state IT department and state CIO was updated to reflect the additional duties: appointing a state chief information security officer, appointing a state chief privacy officer, and reviewing IT projects for compliance with security and privacy best practices and policies, among others. Hence, another aspect of the state CIO’s beliefs is to manage the state’s IT resources to stay complian with regulations (both state-level and federal-level)

The compliance aspect of state CiOs’ duties is reflected in the evolution of their priorities. Table 4 captures the evolution of these priorities between two periods of NASCIO’s rhetoric. When coupled with major socio-economic events and regulations during each period, we can observe a strong correlation of CIOs’ expressed priorities with such events. During the Initiation period, the top three priorities are e-government, IT procurement, and homeland security. This coincides with major socio-political events of this period: the enactment of the E-Government Act of 2002 encouraging the adoption of e-government in state governments; the dot-com bubble burst in 2000 that pushed states into a fiscal crisis and forced them to look for short-term cost-savings through IT procurement: and the 9/11 attack and the enactment of the Homeland Security Act in 2002 with the Federal Information Security Management Act (FISMA) mandating that states adopt multiple new security measures. Other events explain other observed priorities: the 2002 Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPPA) prompted states to focus on privacy issues (#6), the 1996 Clinger-Cohen Act on IT management and the creation of NASCIO in 2001 signaled interest in IT governance (#4), and CIOs’ growing role in the public sector (#7).

The same trend can be observed in the second period. The 2006 e-discovery amendments to the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure (FRCP) prompted states to look into electronic records management and e-discovery (#4); the HITECH Act in 2006 created a wave of interest in Health Information Technology (#10): and the 2008 economic recession and subsequent federal reform plan in 2010 steered state priorities to cost-savings like consolidation (#2), virtualization (#3), shared service (#5), budget and cost control (#8), and cloud computing (#9). The introduction of FISMA in 2002, the increased number of cyber-attacks on governmental agencies, and the FISMA reform in 2014 placed information security as the top priority during this period. Along with it came new security topics such as disaster recovery/business continuity (#12), identity and access management (#13), privacy (#25), and data and information management (#30). The emerging business intelligence trends started in 2010 triggered state CIOs to explore business intelligence initiatives (#20) and data and information management issues (#30).

In sum, over the two periods of diffusion rhetoric, the fundamental belief systems of state CIOs remained stable: they are expected to act as public officials with their aim to manage public IT resources (1) in the best interests of the public constituents, and (2) to stay compliant with regulations (both state-level and federal-level). Given such beliefs, state CIOs’ priorities will fluctuate over time and will be triggered by critical socio-political events. In addition, different states will have different priorities depending on current issues at the state level. This creates a challenge for NASCIO, which needs to adjust its rhetoric to best appeal to the increasingly hetero geneous priorities of its audience. Next, we examine to what extent changes in NASCIO’s rhetoric were effective in addressing these diverse and shifting priorities.

## 4.2.2. Alignment of NASCIO’s rhetoric with CIOs’ priorities

How well did the evolution of NASCIO’s rhetoric align to the evolution of state CIOs’ priorities? To answer this question, we assess the degree of frame resonance by using similarity measures discussed in the method section. This shows the level of alignment between identified problems in NASCIO’s EA rhetoric and state CIOs’ diverse and shifting priorities.

During the Initiation period, NASCIO’s rhetoric had a medium overlap with state CIOs priorities in that the diagnostic framing has a cosine similarity of 0.58 (out of 1) with the state CIOs’ priorities.<sup>7</sup> However, the rhetoric had a low overlap adjusted for weighted priorities (weighted cosine similarity of 0.21 (out of 1)). NASCIO’s early rhetoric targeted only one priority—information sharing—but that issue had the lowest priority ranking at the time. Having a medium overlap of priorities, but a low weighted overlap, suggests NASCIO’s rhetoric had a reasonable spread, but a weak alignment with the state CIOs’ priorities.

The weak alignment between NASCIO’s rhetoric and state CIOs’ priorities can be confirmed by some anecdotal evidence. At the 1999 NASCIO conference, a survey of 155 IT professionals in attendance suggested that information sharing was indeed perceived to be an important issue for the state IT managers: 92% of respondents believed information sharing is highly important. Yet, although information sharing was clearly an interest for state CIOs, it was not a highly ranked priority. Out of the 73 presentations made be tween 2000 and 2004 at NASCIO conferences, only two focused on information sharing. The reason was that during this period, states faced fiscal crises and had to prioritize short-term and efficiency-focused IT initiatives like outsourcing, or initiatives demanded by new regulatory interventions such as e-government and security (NASCIO, 2005b). Thus, framing EA as a solution to information sharing—a low-ranking priority—failed to resonate with state CIOs in this period

In the second period, NASCIO's rhetorical shift positioned the EA adoption message better in relation to CIOs' priorities. While NASCIO’s rhetoric retained a medium level of overlap with state CIOs’ unweighted priorities (cosine similarity measure of 0.55), the shifting rhetoric achieved a higher level of overlap with weighted priorities (weighted cosine similarity of 0.39). The rhetoric had maintained a good spread, but achieved a higher alignment in relation to state CIOs’ priorities. Compared to one key problem covered in the previous period, the rhetoric now covered a larger number of key priorities (seven key priorities). Moreover, these problems in NASCIO’s rhetoric had high priority ranking including the fourth priority (e-discovery) and the ninth priority (cloud computing), and also covered “hot” areas of interest such as business analytics. ## 4.3. Meta-inferences across findings

We will use insights from the qualitative rhetorical analysis and quantitative LSA analysis to formulate meta-inferences that gain richer insights. We use both inductive and deductive reasoning while formulating meta-inferences (Venkatesh et al., 2016). We rely primarily on a bridging method to reach consensus across our findings (Venkatesh et al., 2013). Our meta-inferences are summarized in Table 5. By itself, the qualitative strand unveils how the content and structure of NASCIO’s innovation rhetoric changed over time. NASCIO’s ideology during this period switched from a “collaborator” of DoJ to an “enabler” to state governments; the adoption message shifted from information sharing to government transformation; and the framing processes evolved from framing EA as a technical blueprint to EA as a management discipline. from using economic rationales for EA adoption to offering practical business cases of EA applications. At the same time. the quantitative strand reveals how state CIOs' priorities align with changes in NASCIO's rhetoric as well as the effects of this change. When compared with the first period, the rhetoric in the second period reaches a higher degree of frame resonance and a positive correlation with the “optimal alignment line.” The improvement likely contributed to a better uptake of NASCIO’s EA framework among the states.

Jointly the two strands reveal additional insights on why NASCIO’s rhetoric worked (or did not work). The issue touches the structural and action parts of rhetoric. In the first period, NASCIO’s ideology did not fit well with state CIOs’ belief systems. The funding from the DoJ created tunnel vision on the importance of information sharing and subsequently limited EA’s scope of appli cability. In the second period, NASCIO’s ideology reaches a better fit with state CIOs’ belief systems. The notion of government transformation conveys a better adoption message which aligns with state CIOs’ priorities. More importantly, the changed ideology allows NASCIO to appeal to a broader range of CIO priorities which broadens EA’s scope of applicability. What made NASCIO’s rhetoric change effective was not only how adoption messages were framed and communicated (first intelligence) but also how NASCIO listened to its audience and evolved adoption messages to fit the audience’s diverse and shifting priorities (second intelligence).

While the meta-inferences illustrate how NASCIO's innovation rhetoric evolved in response to the diverse and shifting priorities of its audiences, we need to situate our findings in the broader context to see whether there is a possibility that NASCIO’s rhetorica changes were purely driven by developments in the EA field, and not by its audience’s priorities. Fig. 5 shows the evolution of EA concepts within the public and private sector (Appendix C provides details and sources). Two observations can be made. First, it is evident that there are cross-sector influences regarding EA development. Early EA developments from the Department of Defense (DoD) triggered EA developments at the federal level (1999) and subsequently state-level (NASCIO in 2003), but also led to EA de velopments in the private sector (Open Group’s TOGAF in 1995). Some private influences were found in the public sector, such as IBM representative sitting in NASCIO’s EA team during the development of its framework (NASCIO, 2002a). A closer look at the EA documents published by different state governments further confirms that multiple cross-influences shaped the adopted EA frame work. For instance, Arizone’s EA used elements from NASCIO’s framework, federal framework, and TOGAF framework<sup>8</sup>; and Michigan used elements from NASCIO’s framework, federal framework, and Gartner framework.<sup>9</sup>

Additionally, a second observation can be made about the nature of change in NASCIO’s EA framework. While NASCIO’s shift from a “technical blueprint” to “management engineering discipline” (Table 2) is similar to evolution in other frameworks (e.g., TOGAF v8.1 in 2003 to TOGAF v.9 in 2009, Gartner 1997 to Gartner 2002, or META 1999 to Gartner 2005). the shift at NASCIO seems rhetorically unique. Particularly, the term “management engineering discipline” is not used by other frameworks. A quick Internet search shows that the term was only adopted by some state governments (e.g., California, Washington). Coupled with the two studies done by NASCIO in 2004 and 2005 to assess its EA development, the evidence suggests that shifts in NASCIO’s rhetoric is unique and responds to its challenges (i.e.. better alignment with audiences' priorities). albeit the shifts are indeed in line and reflect the changes done in other frameworks (i.e., shifting from technical-focus to management-focus). This analysis represents an opportunity for future research to scrutinize the effects of NASCIO’s rhetoric on state governments while also taking into account the broader changes in EA concept.

## 5. Discussion

This paper has explored how innovators within a focal community need to evolve their adoption messages around a complex IS innovation and align it with the audience’s diverse and shifting priorities. Our study makes two contributions to the literature: (1) for studies on innovation rhetoric, our findings demonstrate the importance of keeping the rhetoric flexible to appeal to broadening heterogeneous priorities of the target audience: (2) for studies on complex innovation diffusion within a focal community, we illustrate how shifting priorities of community members can substantially inform innovators how to evolve the adoption message.

## 5.1. Frame ambiguity in complex IS innovations

The later success of NASCIO's rhetoric suggests that for any reasonably complex IS innovation facing potentially diverse audience priorities, it is instrumental to keep the adoption message flexible. This calls for frame ambiguity enabled by complexity of the IS in novations. Through the change in its rhetoric NASCIO could identify with a wider range of priorities and its message was better received by its audience. While prior studies suggest that nascent, salient, and well-defined business problems are pivotal to invite the audience’s engagement (Wang, 2009), NASCIO’s experience suggests that the rhetor needs to diagnose a wider range of concrete business problems. Additionally, maintaining flexibility in rhetoric by variations in diagnostic framing and justifications allows in novators to react to changes in the audience's whims and interests (Zbaracki. 1998). Given the complexity of the innovation, this will grant the innovation functional versatility and offers a wide range of applications and value to users (DiMaggio and Cohen, 2005) which can appeal to a broader set of audiences, if they are properly identified (Baier, March, and Saetren, 1986; Eisenberg, 1984; Jarzab kowski et al., 2010).

The notion of frame ambiguity in innovation rhetoric draws parallel with other innovation and management concepts. For instance,

Jarzabkowski et al. (2010) revealed that strategic ambiguity enabled constituents with different interests to attribute different meanings to the same strategic goal, resulting in improved collective action. Just like frame flexibility, strategic ambiguity enables organizations to promote unified diversity, diffuse conflicts, and preserve strategic options (Eisenberg, 1984). In another example, studies in boundary objects posit that to be effective, boundary objects need to be flexible enough to contain various meanings attributed by multiple users while remaining robust enough as a common reference point enabling multi-way interactions (Star and Griesemer, 1989). In fact, researchers have called for more studies on the role of strategic planning tools (e.g., Porter’s five forces, EA) as boundar objects in strategizing practices (Spee and Jarzabkowski, 2009). In light of these studies, we encourage future studies to explore the role of frame ambiguity in helping strategic tools (e.g., EA) as boundary objects to promote adoption and use among prospective adopters.

## 5.2. Rhetoric of persuasion and identification during innovation diffusion

Our findings show that for complex IS innovations, interactions between innovation promoters and their audience are mutual and constitutive. On the one hand, innovation promoters need to actively listen to their audience to identify and understand their priorities and adjust the innovation rhetoric accordingly (Currie, 2004; Heracleous and Barrett, 2001). On the other hand, the audience plays an active role in shaping the framing which has not been reckoned with in the past. Instead of being passive recipients of the innovation rhetoric, adopters will engage in discussions of innovation meaning (Constantinides and Barrett, 2015; de Vaujany, Carton, Domi nguez-P´ery, and Vaast, 2013) by participating in community-level sense-making (Gorgeon and Swanson, 2011). Some will step up to become innovation promoters and build new adoption messages (Barrett, Joshi, and Walsham, 2007; Markus et al., 2008; Sillince, 2005). In our study, many state CIOs gave presentations on EA applications, participated in NASCIO's surveys and in the EA committee to develop guidelines, or shared their views regarding CIO priorities that align with the EA discipline. Their actions and non-actions informed NASCIO of how to change its rhetoric and its focus to make it resonate better with the audience. Moreover, most state CIOs personally did not need to be convinced of EA’s benefits. Rather, their short-tenure imposes a burden to justify long-term strategic initiatives (e.g., EA adoption) over short-term gains (e.g., outsourcing); and thus, they needed NASCIO’s rhetoric as forceful “ammunition” to convince state-level decision makers of EA’s benefits. Such behaviors were rooted in their belief systems and identity as public CIOs which demands managing IT resources in the best interest of the public constituents and in compliance with regulations The story of EA diffusion in the US state governments is mainly about how NASCIO evolved its ideology to align with the dominant belief systems of the state CIOs, which helped align EA with the shifting priorities of the state CIOs.

Building on these insights we can now extend the theoretical framework of innovation rhetoric as shown in Fig. 6. The figure suggests that the diffusion is shaped not only by the content and structure of the adoption message and its underlying framing stra tegies. but also by the audience's changing priorities and shifts in their belief systems. These are largely shaped by the adopter's identity, relationships and position in the (institutional) field. At the structural level, the audience’s identity informs and influences its priorities; at the action level, the audience’s diverse and shifting priorities mobilize adoption behaviors, which need to be ‘nudged’ by the innovator’s appropriate rhetorical responses. The audience is never given and fixed; and its influence on the forcefulness of the rhetoric needs to be accounted for by the rhetor’s listening. Through listening, the adoption and non-adoption behaviors of the audience can inform changes in adoption message, and even in the ideology of the rhetor. In the case of NASCIO, the listening was done through a survey and an assessment study, but it is also feasible to assume that NASCIO used other “listening” mechanisms such as conference meetings and round tables, interactions between state and industry representatives in the EA committee, or through in dustry reports from consulting firms. Identifying those “listening” mechanisms will be a fruitful direction for future studies.

While prior studies have focused primarily on the two components on the left by leaning heavily on the rhetoric of persuasion, the components on the right invite studies to embrace another perspective–the rhetoric of identification (Burke, 1969). This is an audiencecentered conceptualization of rhetoric (Cheney. 1983) which asks: to what extent can an audience be drawn under the spell’ of a unique rhetoric by associating the adoption message with the audience’s values and character? When an innovator, through her rhetoric, signals a significant overlap between her priorities expressed in the innovation rhetoric and those of potential adopters. the innovator can identify herself to be close to the potential adopters (Cheney, 1983; Crable, 1977) and connect the audience’s deeper values (i.e., social identity) to those of innovators (Cheney, 1991; Mackenzie, 1978). When it happens, a “social bond” is created between the innovator with her audience (Mackenzie. 1978). giving the adoption message a higher chance to be positively received (Cheney, 1983; Cheney, 1991). This is an exercise in the second intelligence to understand and identify with the audience.

This process was evinced in NASCIO’s case. When NASCIO noted a lukewarm response to its initial EA adoption message, and learned about the root causes of the problem (e.g., state CIOs’ short tenure that limits long-term initiatives, limited scope of information sharing), it changed its ideology (i.e. and related social identity) from a “collaborator with DOJ” to an “enabler” of state governments. Its frame evolved from EA as a “technical blueprint for information sharing” to EA as a “management discipline for government transformation.” When this happened, NASCIO’s ideology was better aligned with the state CIOs’ identity, creating a “social bond” more likely to induce pro-adoption behaviors.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

This research comes with limitations and opportunities for future research. First, we examined the evolution of a single successfu complex IS innovation to extract the persuasive elements around its evolving rhetoric with no comparative case of a failed one. Simultaneous rhetoric around EA in other pockets of the public sector (federal agencies) and within private industry (corporations) naturally played an important role in changing NASCIO’s rhetoric and cannot be discounted. Future research should examine how parallel streams of rhetoric cross-pollinate and influence the diffusion. Second, innovators often face legal or other constraints for rhetorical changes. Occasionally, innovators choose to seek new audiences that will better match with the innovation’s current framing rather than attempting to revamp their rhetoric (opportunistic identification of new audience). In this regard, we need to study contexts where innovation is re-purposed and re-contextualized by creating a new rhetoric. Third, we did not systematically explore and account for the effects of rhetoric on actual adoption rates and decision-making process of prospective adopters. Future studies need to incorporate related issues around causality such as time lags, the role of economic fluctuations, and the presence of peer pressure to distil the true effect of innovation rhetoric. Fourth, we did not have detailed accounts of how NASCIO balanced out the pros and cons of rhetorical moves, nor the actual reactions and assessments of potential adopters regarding NASCIO’s rhetorical changes (e. g., how the rhetorical changes help justify long-term initiatives). In-depth case studies can be conducted in future to unveil the interna dynamics within NASCIO and the states regarding EA adoption. Finally, our study examined a specific and limited population in a unique institutional field in a narrowed timeframe. Therefore, we can generalize to other settings only in limited ways. Future studies are invited to validate our findings in other settings that involve more heterogeneous populations.

## 6. Conclusion

Overall, our study shows the salience of innovation rhetoric and a deeper need to understand its evolution during the diffusion of a complex IS innovation as a set of interactions between changes in adoption messages and the audience’s shifting priorities. The former is focused on Machiavelli’s “first intelligence” and reflects the innovator’s evolving understanding of the focal innovation; the latter is focused on the “second intelligence” and reflects the innovators’ understanding of the audience’s understanding of the innovation in a local context. Together, they provide a more encompassing foundation to unveil how the diffusion of a complex innovation unfolds. In the spirit of second intelligence we hope that our study has also reached its intended audience and shows that we have deployed successfully our first and second intelligence to encourage future research on this fascinating topic.
