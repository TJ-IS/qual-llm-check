# Exploring health-analytics adoption in indian private healthcare organizations: An institutional-theoretic perspective

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Health-analytics Case study Adoption Journey Institutional theory IT adoption

## A B S T R A C T

In India, private hospitals are at the cusp of adopting health-analytics (HA) technology to manage their organizational performance through data-driven decision-making. Past studies have analyzed the applications and benefits of HA. Our study builds on this descriptive base to investigate the patterns of HA adoption and the institutional factors which impact adoption. We conducted a cross-sectional field study that involved ten Indian private healthcare organizations and four health-ecosystem partners and analyzed the case study data using an institutional theory lens. Our cases reveal that the breadth and depth of HA adoption varies and falls into three distinct patterns: far-sighted, conservative, and niche. We assess how these patterns are influ enced by the two key dimensions of institutional environments (material-resource environment and institutional environment). We highlight distinctive factors (management support for building organizational trust on data-driven decisions and IT-Medical practitioner collaboration) that exert important contextual influences on HA adoption. Our study identifies areas of commonalty for HA adoption across national healthcare settings as well as contextual aspects representative of the burgeoning Indian healthcare field.

## 1. Introduction

Healthcare organizations worldwide have been investing in emerging information technologies to address their business issues related to cost reduction, patient care, and performance management. Health-analytics (HA) is an emerging technology that provides several opportunities for hospitals to improve their healthcare processes and outcomes (Ferranti. Langman. Tanaka. McCall. & Ahmad. 2010; Raghupathi & Raghupathi, 2013, 2014). HA applies analytical models on top of the aggregated data produced by the foun dational information technologies (ITs) and medical systems, including Hospital Information Systems,<sup>1</sup> Electronic Medical Records (EMR), picture archiving and communication systems, and lab management systems. HA can be leveraged to drive fact-based decisions on planning, management, and measurement (Cortada, Gordon, & Lenihan, 2010; Davenport, Harris, & Morison, 2010). It applies to clinical data (Hu et al., 2016), operational data (Eun, Lee, & Yih, 2017), administrative data (Chalmers, Siska, Le, & Knoer, 2018), and strategic data (Oztekin, 2018) assets. HA solutions apply statistical, contextual, quantitative, predictive, and cognitive methodologies on aggregated data from large warehouses and repositories (Raghupathi & Raghupathi, 2013). They provide business insights that eventually help transform the business processes in many hospital departments (Ward, Marsolo, & Froehle, 2014). Such business process transformation can result in increased efficiency of patient care, reduced healthcare costs, and improved clinical outcomes (Cortada et al., 2010).

While, in general, the use of analytics technology sounds promising, past information systems literature points to several factors that impact its adoption, including technical factors such as data aggregation (Ghosh & Scott, 2011), organizational issues such as management support (Ahmadi, Nilashi, & Ibrahim, 2015), and environmental conditions, such as healthcare regulations (Venkatra man, Sundarraj, & Seethamraju, 2015). Several researchers, however, opine that knowledge from general IT literature may not be directly applied in a healthcare industry context, owing to the differences in how IT value is captured across different industries (Brooks, El-Gayar, & Sarnikar, 2015; Chiasson & Davidson, 2004, 2005; Sherer, 2010, 2014; Venkatraman et al., 2015). Therefore these researchers call for healthcare-specific studies on IT adoption.

To-date, much IS field research on healthcare IT adoption has focused on the adoption of foundational IT and medical systems (such as hospital management systems, EMR, picture archiving and communication systems) within organizations, while a few studies address HIS adoption of country-level health services (cf. Currie, 2012; Currie & Guah, 2007) or health technologies such as health information systems and electronic medical records (cf. Guillemette, Mignerat, & Par´e, 2017; Sherer, 2010; Sherer, Meyerhoefer, & Peng. 2016) regionally. Many researchers have utilized an institutional-theoretic perspective. Specifically, researchers have highlighted institutional logics (Currie & Guah, 2007; Sahay, Sæbø, Mekonnen, & Gizaw, 2010), including actors’ responses (Burton-Jones et al., 2020; Guillemette, Mignerat, & Par´e, 2017), and isomorphic forces for adoption (Currie, 2012; Gopalakrishna-Remani, Jones, & Wooldridge, 2016; Sherer, 2010; Sherer et al., 2016), which represent aspects of the institutional environment. Consideration of the material-resource environment. which is a critical aspect of institutional theory (Scott. 2013: Scott. Ruef. Mendel. & Caronna. 2000). has received less research attention among IS scholars. With regards to research on health analytics, Galetsi and Katsaliaki (2019) literature review finds that HA-related studies are rare, without a basic categorization of HA applications. The few related studies (Ghosh & Scott, 2011; Venkatraman et al., 2015) that focus on factors influencing HA adoption are limited by their scope. To date, there are no comprehensive, theory-informed studies of HA adoption, particularly in a rapidly transforming and expanding healthcare context like India.

In line with the aforementioned calls for IS-theory based research in healthcare (Sherer, 2014), we draw on institutional theory to carry out a qualitative cross-sectional case study with Indian private healthcare organizations.<sup>2</sup> We address the following research question: “How do institutional, market, and organizational level factors influence the adoption and uptake of HA in private hospitals in India?” We use the term adoption to include both the initial acquisition and sustained use of a new information system. Institutional theory is robust in supporting explanations of behaviors in different domains and can be expected to produce unique insights from both the theoretical and practical perspectives on adoption of health analytics applications in this context. Hospitals do not operate in isolation but as actors within the healthcare environment; decisions to adopt (or not) HA are influenced by this environment. An institutional theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Scott, 2013; Scott et al., 2000; Thornton & Ocasio, 2012) lens thus is appropriate to analyze these cases, as it conceptualizes industry influences on IS activities (Chiasson & Davidson, 2005).

We choose the domain of Indian private hospitals for several reasons. First. India has the diversity of offering world-class medical care at one end and affordable low-cost care at the other extreme (Brady & Saranga, 2013). Such diversity in the market has the potential to provide insights into various patterns of HA adoption and the impact of factors highlighted in past research across diverse cases. Second, there is a significant growth potential for the Indian healthcare sector, with 74% of national healthcare spending happening in private hospitals (IBEF, 2018). Finally, Indian private hospitals are in early stages of adopting HA (Venkatraman et al., 2015) to provide better medical care.

We adopted a case-study approach to examine closely at the institutional market and organizational factors impacting HA adoption across this variety of Indian hospitals. Our cross-sectional field study methodology entailed working with twenty-seven respondents from ten private healthcare organizations and four health ecosystem partners. Our data analysis demonstrates that adoption is a complex process and highlights three different patterns, which do not necessarily follow the incremental maturation suggested by maturity models such as the Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society (HIMSS) model (HIMSS, 2021). Our study also highlights how industry and market contexts contribute to the variations in HA adoption across the healthcare organizations and provides insights into several institutional factors which influence the same.

## 2. Literature review

Health analytics solutions build on a number of underlving technologies. Hence, any variations in the adoption of the latter can impact the level of HA adoption, too. To provide a grounding and background, in this section, we first review HA and the associated systems (Section 2.1). followed by a discussion of maturity models for healthcare (Section 2.2). The healthcare ecosystem is institutionally quite different from other industries, and this also has a bearing on how organizational phenomena such as HA adoption may develop (see, for example, Chiasson & Davidson. 2004, 2005: Johns. 2006). Therefore, in Section 2.3, we outline an institutional perspective of the healthcare industry environment (Scott et al., 2000) and relevant research in this stream of research.

## 2.1. Health-analytics (HA)

Health-analytics (HA) refers to an array of technologies that provide a way to transform data into actions through analysis and insights in health care decision-making and problem-solving (Raghupathi & Raghupathi, 2013). HA overlays strategic, clinical, administrative, and operational applications on data produced by medical equipment (Liu & Huang, 2020; Ward et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2013) such as hospital management system, EMR, medical imaging systems such as X-Ray, MRI, and ECG, and picture archiving and communication systems. However, the advancements in technologies in the last decade, such as patient portals, wearable tech nologies, personalized fitness trackers, apps on smartphones, CRM systems, and more (Mackert, Mabry-Flynn, Champlin, Donovan, & Pounders, 2016), have significantly increased the volume, variety, and velocity of data, often termed Big Data in healthcare (Wang & Alexander, 2015). The architecture of HA has four major building blocks (Raghupathi & Raghupathi, 2014): a) HA data sources, the foundational IS/IT and medical infrastructure which produce data, b) HA data integration, the tools needed to collect the data from the source, process, and transform them into an analyzable form, c) HA applications, the various techniques and tools used in data analysis and the analytics model, and d) HA data management platform, which provides a data fabric and manages the end to end data life cycle (El Aboudi & Benhlima. 2018: Raghupathi & Raghupathi. 2013).

HA are intended to help organizations address their business issues related to cost reduction, patient care, and performance management (Mehta & Pandit, 2018; Venkatraman et al., 2015). While HA’s benefits are promising, past research notes severa technical (e.g., data quality), organizational (e.g., management support), and environmental factors (e.g., health regulations) impact its adoption process (Venkatraman et al., 2015). And depending upon the level and extent of those factors existing in organizations, their influence on the degree of HA adoption varies. An understanding of these underlying contexts can help organizations plan their HA adoption.

## 2.2. Maturity models for HA

Maturity models assess an organization on various factors in linearly scaled maturity levels (usually level 1 to 5) based on defined attributes of the factors for every level. Maturity models for healthcare information systems have been developed by professional organizations as a guide to practitioners. For general health information systems subdomains such as telehealth, EMR, and picture archival and communication systems, there are several works such as those by Carvalho, Rocha, and Vasconcelos (2015); Carvalho, Rocha, and Abreu (2016); Carvalho, Rocha, van de Wetering, and Abreu (2017) and Gomes and Rom˜ao (2018) dealing with maturity models. For HA, a high-level maturity model framework developed by Brooks et al. (2015) views the strategic, technical, and healthcare process-related dimensions as maturity indicators. Likewise, another model, HISMM-DA (Carvalho, Rocha, Vasconcelos, & Abreu, 2019), deals with operational and technological factors (e.g., data, reports, and HA usage). Apart from the above, industry bodies and societies also offer commercially HA maturity assessments and certifications, popular with hospitals. These HA maturity assessment models include AMAM (HIMSS, 2021) and HAAM (Healthcare Analytics Adoption Maturity) (Sanders, Burton, & Protti, 2013). HIMSS’ HA-maturity assessment describes business-process level adoption of analytics, and HAAM focuses primarily on data and reporting capabilities.

While these models can be useful, there are three significant issues for applying them in research and also for practical guidance. First, these growth maturity models are based on the premise that an organization’s technology adoption and related processes evolve through a sequential process of development and growth towards a more advanced maturity. The implicit assumption that “predictable patterns exist and unfold as discrete stages” may not be true in practice (Lasrado, Vatrapu, & Andersen, 2015). They do not consider leapfrogging effects (Schilling, 2003), where organizations adopt the latest technologies without going through incremental phases. The second issue is that the available models (Brooks et al., 2015; Carvalho et al., 2019; HIMSS, 2021; Sanders et al., 2013) are inwardlooking, focusing primarily on the technology and organizational processes such as the foundational systems that produce data, data management processes, and organizational adoption of analytics across various business functions. While these are important factors, focusing on internal factors exclusively downplays industry and market-related factors that can significantly impact IT adoption (Chiasson & Davidson, 2005; Venkatraman et al., 2015). Third, maturity models such as HIMSS and AMAM do not have a particular IS theoretical foundation and are derived primarily by consulting organizations. For our purposes, they do not address specific, relevant factors of the industry context or the institutional factors which affect healthcare organizations’ actions related to HA adoption.

## 2.3. Institutional considerations for HA adoption

The context for HA adoption is different from analytics in other industries and is therefore important to consider (Chiasson & Davidson, 2005). Healthcare data pertains to human life and well-being, where data quality, integrity and privacy are paramount (Pandey et al., 2020; Winter & Davidson, 2018). Health data is private and sensitive, and hence, more than in any other industry, special ethical, legal, and social constraints apply in handling such data (Iyengar, Kundu, & Pallis, 2018). Human intervention cannot be eliminated in HA. as diagnostic suggestions and decisions must be verified by licensed medical professional, and more importantly. accepted and consented to by the patient (Bezemer et al., 2019). Unlike the professions in many fields, medical professionals tend to emphasize their autonomy and independent judgment, irrespective of recommendations from analytics applications (Sherer. 2010). While in other industries, analytics adoption is business-driven, in healthcare, there is the issue of dual administration (Chiasson & Davidson. 2004). entailing medical personnel on the one hand and on the other. business managers who want to benchmark with practices of the industry. Therefore, there generally must be alignment between the beliefs and viewpoints of business, medical, and IT stakeholders to advance HA and other health information systems projects. In other words, value creation from IS/IT in healthcare is not a business or revenue-only discussion. It involves human lives, well-being, and health outcome, which cannot be assessed adequately in purely monetary terms (Chiasson & Davidson, 2004; Sherer, 2014). Given these contextual factors, we turn to insti tutional theory for insights on HA adoption.

## 2.3.1. Theoretical insights on the institutional environment for HA

Healthcare organizations operate in institutional fields that include key participants (competitors, suppliers of products and ser vices, consumers, other ecosystem players such as payers, testing labs, pharmaceuticals, etc.) who transact business with one other in an industry setting governed by laws and regulatory measures (DiMaggio & Powell, 2000). Within this environment, institutional pressures and the context emanating from the network of industry actors influence the participants’ actions (Burton-Jones et al., 2020; DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Fichman & Kemerer, 1993; Fichman & Kemerer, 1999). The concept of isomorphism addresses why similarities develop within fields as a result of memetic, coercive, or normative pressures to conform to legitimated organizationa forms, structures, practices, and strategies (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). For instance, Currie (2012), exploring the institutiona isomorphism phenomenon in UK National Health Service, explains how memetic pressures in EMR implementation conflicted with physicians’ professional dominance. Sherer (2010) and Sherer et al. (2016) describe how coercive, memetic, and normative pressures impact physician adoption of EMRs. A study by Gopalakrishna-Remani et al. (2016) found mimetic and coercive pressures influenced the level of HA adoption in US hospitals, whereas normative pressures did not.

Institutional logics are the beliefs and assumptions that shape actors’ actions and behavior and embody the guiding principles that underpin activities (Currie & Guah, 2007; Thornton & Ocasio, 2012). Thornton, Ocasio, and Lounsbury (2012, p.2) define logics as “frames of reference that condition actors’ choices for sensemaking, the vocabulary they use to motivate action, and their sense of self and identity.” Studies of individual actors working to introduce health information technologies generally focus on their response to institutional pressures. For instance, Burton-Jones et al. (2020) examine how institutional forces can influence the actors’ actions in the digital transformation of healthcare. Guillemette et al. (2017) explore the role of institutional work (practices of individual actors) in transforming IT functions within healthcare settings over time in order to align themselves with organizational visions and motivations.

Long-term macro-level changes in institutional structures can also result in changes in actors’ beliefs and institutional logics (Macfarlane, Barton-Sweeney, Woodard, & Greenhalgh, 2013). Actors can also collectively shape or change institutional logics (Thornton & Ocasio, 2012) through is what is termed institutional entrepreneurship (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983, 2000), where actors initiate changes that contribute to transforming existing or creating new institutional logics. There may also be multiple, competing institutional logics in play, where the dominant logics can determine an organization’s actions (Van Den Broek, Boselie, & Paauwe, 2014). In their study the UK National Health Service, Currie and Guah (2007) detail how institutional logics changed over time and identify conflicting institutional logics arising from the priorities of various institutional actors involved in a national health infor mation systems project. Similarly, Sahay et al. (2010) detail the interplay of conflicting institutional logics shaping the HMIS implementation in a developing country.

Healthcare organizational processes such as management support (Ahmadi et al., 2015; Hikmet, Bhattacherjee, Menachemi, Kavhan, & Brooks. 2007: Ingebrigtsen et al., 2014: Lavalle. Hopkins. Lesser, Shockley, & Kruschwitz, 2010: Sun. Cegielski, Jia, & Hall. 2018) and response to technology-resistance (Abdekhoda, Ahmadi, Gohari, & Noruzi, 2015; Boonstra & Broekhuis, 2010; Esmaeil zadeh, Sambasivan, Kumar, & Nezakati, 2015) are important to understand adoption of health information systems; these processes have been studied extensively within the health information systems literature (Davidson, Baird, & Prince, 2018). However, gover nance arising from the institutional field is also key to understanding how or why HA adoption may proceed within or across orga nizations within a field. External governance within a field includes regulations impacting the adoption of hospital information systems, such as EMR (Sherer et al 2016) e-health (Klöcker, Bernnat, & Veit 2014) and the associated standards and policies deployed by governments. Moreover, at the regional or nation level, overall societal resources, priorities, legal structures, and so on, in addition to healthcare field effects, influence how health informational systems can develop, diffuse, and be adopted. Gebre-Mariam and Bygstad (2019), in their study on the process of digitalization of hospital information systems in developing countries, demonstrate the underlying causal chain involves emergently transformed interactions between societal and technical elements.

## 2.3.2. A conceptual model of the institutional environment for HA adoption

The applications of institutional theory to healthcare IS research noted above highlight how influences outside the healthcare organization – isomorphic pressures to conform to legitimated actions, institutional logics that guide actors and actions, regulatory and societal constraints– all have an impact on how organizations may respond to the opportunities that HA applications present. Growth maturity models to assess HA adoption are appealing but limited, as they assume one sequential path to maturing in adoption and focus primarily on internal factors rather than institutional influences. Currie (2009) posits that “Institutional theory offers a conceptually rich source to observe the non-linear (as opposed to linear) routes of information technology adoption and assimilation across markets and orga nizations.” Our study explores this possibility for HA adoption.

To do so, we build on research into institutional factors (isomorphism, institutional logics) evident in much previous healthcare IS research to incorporate more fully a consideration of the material-resource dimension of institutional environment (Scott et al., 2000). The material-resource environment includes the factors related to supply-demand, technologies, and market structure. Supply-demand consists of the demand and market expectation of the health services and the availability (supply) of human resources such as medica practitioners, doctors, nurses, and paramedics and technology resources such as IT and medical infrastructure needed to fulfill the services (Scott et al., 2000). Market structure relates to the alignment of various players who influence the flow of resources (Scott et al., 2000). The organizational population refers to competitors who offer similar healthcare services, and which may present competitive memetic pressures towards health information systems adoption (Ahmadi et al., 2015). Fig. 1 depicts the healthcare industry environment in our study that is relevant to HA adoption from this institutional theoretic perspective

In the study that follows, we examine Indian healthcare organizations and their budding adoption of HA within the context of this conceptual model for the healthcare institutional environment.

## 3. Research methodology

Given the potential growth of the Indian healthcare industry, the institutional diversity, the unique social and industry charac teristics. and the many organizations working towards HA adoption. we chose a cross-sectional field study using multiple case study organizations (Yin, 2009). Cross-sectional field studies sit between in-depth case studies and broad-based surveys and are conducted at purposively selected sites. These studies involve shorter, less intensive data collection than in-depth case studies and are less structured in data collection than surveys (Yin, 2009). Cross-sectional field studies enable a broad exploration of research questions and theo retical elaboration (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007). Using a theoretical, non-random sampling approach, the cases were chosen based on prior knowledge about the organizations and their potential to offer empirical evidence on the issues the study intends to explore (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007). The factors determining the selection of the organizations include location, accessibility, persona contacts, and participants’ interest in supporting our research process.

## 3.1. Respondents and organizations

Our unit of analysis is at the organizational level. In line with past research (Brady & Saranga, 2013; IBEF, 2021; Srinivasan & Chandwani, 2014), we selected the following category of cases that represent the Indian private healthcare industry: (i) Corporate hospital chains with a significant focus on profit and offering world-class medical service; (ii) Low-cost hospitals chains that provide primary and secondary care to local populations in semi-urban areas; and (iii) Healthcare organizations with a hybrid model that charge those who can pay while providing subsidized care for those who cannot afford treatment.

We studied fourteen healthcare organizations (Table 1: C1 to C14) and interviewed twenty-seven respondents (Table 1: R1 to R27). All the organizations are well established and have been in operation for more than ten years. In each case study organization, the key respondents are actively involved in adopting and using healthcare information technologies. They are senior executives (e.g., CEO, COO, CIO, Senior Surgeons, MD, VP) with experience ranging from twenty to thirty-five years. We chose participants from the business, IT, and medical domains to bring different perspectives and validation of data. The case profiles include not only the hospitals (see Table 1a, C1-C10), but also participants from healthcare ecosystem players (see Table 1b), such as a healthcare IT services and product vendor (C11), an insurance service provider (C12). a process & management consultant (C13). and a member of government health policy committee (C14). The above ecosystem players work closely with many healthcare organizations and hence, can provide market insights into HIS adoption.

## 3.2. Data collection

The selection of such diverse cases helped us get cross-sectional views of the participants. The semi-structured interviews broadly cover understanding the healthcare organization’s current business process in the context of HA adoption use-cases, plans, challenges, data, decision-making processes, and the respondents’ views on the organizational conditions. environment, and challenges to HA adoption. We also included in our discussions the type, size, and other contextual factors of the organization. In addition to interviews, we also attended multiple meetings to see a demonstration of the hospital management systems, EMR, and analytics models developed by the IT team wherever feasible. Across twenty-seven participants, the average time length of the case study interviews was about sixty-three minutes. As suggested by Creswell (2013), we also validated the interview responses about participants' dav-to-day work with other types of information such as physical artifacts (analytics reports and process documentation), presentations made by business executives and CIO, demonstrations of the system by the IT administrators, publicly available data on organization’s per formance and research reports on Indian healthcare. We recorded the interviews with the participant’s permission, transcribed them, and sent them to the respective interviewees for editing and correction where needed. We assigned codes to research sites and in terviewees to maintain confidentiality with our informants. The interview data was voluminous, running to 425 A4 pages; we report data via quotes from the interviews to support our analysis.

However, as we progressed through the analysis, we inductively added new codes and refined the existing codes. ## 4.1. Research context

India is the sixth-largest economy worldwide, with a GDP of US\$ 2.7 trillion. As per Indian Constitution, health is a concurrent subject with the central government and states having common interests (Bhandari & Dutta, 2007). While the central government sets the policies, standards and regulations, states have the responsibility to execute them (Gupta, 2018). Indian healthcare serves a vast population of 1.35 billion residents, which brings both challenges and significant growth opportunities.

## 4.1.1. Challenges

Low healthcare spending, huge supply-demand skew, and a very low doctor-to-resident ratio are key healthcare sector challenges in India. Even though India’s GDP ranks sixth in the world and is expected to grow at 6.8% per annum according to the Internationa Monetary Fund (IMF) (https://www.imf.org/en/Home), its healthcare accounts for just 6% of India’s GDP (IBEF, 2021). This is considered very low by international standards when compared with the developed economies such as the US, which spends over 17.7% of its GDP (CMS, 2019). Further, given India’s large population, there is a huge supply-demand skew of medical practitioners and other healthcare support staff (HealthWorld, 2019; Srinivasan & Chandwani, 2014). The doctor: resident ratio in India is 1:3629, which is more than three times higher than the WHO recommended ratio of 1:1000 (IBEF, 2021). In addition, there are gaps in the foundational infrastructure and public health care, primarily due to the differences in economic and demographic development across the Indian states (Peters & Muraleedharan, 2008; Srinivasan & Chandwani, 2014).

## 4.1.2. Growth opportunities

Despite the abovementioned challenges, healthcare is the fastest-growing sector in India, with expected revenue of US\$ 372 Billion in the year 2022 driven by a high compounded annual growth rate of 17.69% between the years 2016–2022 (IBEF, 2021). The percentage of healthcare spending is also expected to rise to 19.7% of the GDP by 2027 (IBEF, 2021). Economic liberalization pro moting private and foreign capital investments in the past three decades has contributed to a significant rise in income levels and led to the burgeoning of an aspirational middle-class population (Aslany, 2019; Ghani, 2021; Roy, 2018). Therefore, currently, private healthcare is the preferred choice of patients, and hence dominates with market with a 76% share (IBEF, 2018). Further, 40% of the private healthcare spending happens in high-end corporate healthcare organizations (IBEF, 2018). The above growth is also fueled by affordable government healthcare insurance schemes (Angeli, Ishwardat, Jaiswal, & Capaldo, 2018) and the promotion of medica tourism where people from abroad take advantage of the reasonably high-quality treatment of Indian healthcare organizations at relatively low costs (Burns, 2015; Kishore, 2021; Medhekar, Wong, & Hall, 2019; Sengupta, Mukhopadhyaya, Weerasinghe, & Karki, 2017; Shukla, 2021; Suri, 2019).

In summary, the Indian healthcare story is a mix of challenges and opportunities that contributes to the wide variation of health information technology adoption and growth (Radhakrishna, Goud, Kasthuri, Waghmare, & Raj, 2014). Our study is set in the above context, wherein private hospitals in their attempt to capitalize on significant growth opportunities, are looking to modernize their technologies through the adoption of health analytics solutions (Dash, 2020; MarketsandMarkets Inc, 2021).

## 4.2. Patterns of health-analytics adoption

We found that HA adoption varies in scope (breadth of applications) that spans five major domains: clinical (e.g., tests, diagnosis, and treatment), operations (e.g., scheduling, assets, supplies, and facilities), administrative (e.g., HR, staff performance, insurance), enterprise/strategic (e.g., marketing, client relations), and financial (pricing, costing, revenue, budgeting). In terms of model complexity (depth), we found that the healthcare organizations primarily use simple descriptive models (e.g., statistics on admissions, bed occupancy, etc.), while a few of them use predictive models (e.g., estimating admission volume) and prescriptive models (e.g., surgical theatre usage to maximize the revenue). Overall, our study found that the extent and nature of HA adoption vary in terms of depth and breadth, depending on the type of organization, and on the departments within the organization involved with HA adoption and use.

We uncover three distinct patterns of HA adoption which we term far-sighted, conservative, and niche. Table 3 below presents our key findings with the identification of adoption patterns in each healthcare organization as far-sighted, conservative, and niche. with reference to the breadth and depth of HA adoption in that case study organization. Further analytic details of the nature of HA adoption in each of the hospitals are presented in Appendix A.

## 4.2.1. Far-sighted adoption pattern

The far-sighted adoption pattern is exemplified in large corporate hospitals such as C1 and C4 (see Tables 1, 3). Their approach is characterized by long-term goal orientation and differentiation through technology. A case in point is C1, where a respondent reported that, “the group has very smartly sort of expanded its ecosystem, now it’s the complete sort of healthcare ecosystem, unlike no other player” (R2). These organizations make a constant effort to enhance the maturity of HA adoption and develop analytical models for newer use cases across business domains in support of several specialty areas in healthcare. Adopting a long-term approach, these organizations focus on enhancing healthcare excellence through HA technology. As articulated by the respondent (R2) below, these healthcare organizations take a structured sequential path in the adoption of HA technologies. Explaining the sequential nature of adoption, a respondent stated that “I think the last thing for adoption is recognizing that it can’t all happen at the same time, and being intentional, and sequential in terms of how we would structure that journey...” (R2)

Further, we found that in this pattern. hospitals find it to be important to demonstrate quick wins, in order to get buv-in from other organizational members in the adoption process. One respondent observed that “if you can start to show some quick wins or something that we didn’t really know, it sort of drives the interest in pursuing this further on the clinical, non-clinical side.” (R2).

In another hospital, successful implementation of electronic medical record system was expected to motivate other organizational members to accept HA adoption. A senior manager noted that “earlier this year we did EMR implementation in the largest hospital in the group, and that’s doing pretty well, and we do hope with that success we’ll be able to enthuse everybody adopted in the group. In about one to one and half years we should be there” (R9).

These organizations work to differentiate from others through a sound foundational data infrastructure including the ability to collect and use clinical data and thereby provide better service (see Fig. A3, C1 and C4, Appendix A). A chief operating officer explained how data are used to make informed decisions. He states that “I look at that entire sort of journey, and there is data that is collected at every point, and I use that data to make informed choices on how I will change something, my levers, if you will, to provide that patient a better clinical service” (R2). These hospitals have a strong motivation and competitive drive to differentiate with HA adoption when compared with others. A respondent contended that “I feel we have more of a drive than our competitors, primarily because our core idea is to provide affordable health care” (R10). Pointing out the potential impact of HA adoption on cost efficiencies, he argued that “unless we adopt technology fast, it is going to be extremely difficult for us to cut costs in the future” (R10).

Thus, the far-sighted healthcare organizations use their sound data infrastructure, competitive drive, and quick wins to help them increase the breadth and depth of HA adoption and thereby work to achieve improved cost efficiencies and clinical performance outcomes

## 4.2.2. Conservative adoption pattern

This pattern of HA adoption was observed in mid-tier and small healthcare organizations and is exemplified by C2, C5, C6, C7, C8, and C9 (see Tables 1, 3). Their HA adoption approach was characterized by short-term goal orientation and selective adoption of HA solutions based on business-case justification and tangible returns on investments. Organizations that have adopted a conservative adoption pattern do not prioritize HA in the clinical domain since it is comparatively more complex than others, and returns are longterm and sometimes intangible. Acknowledging the importance of using clinical data for analytics, a respondent bemoans that “we are way behind (in HA adoption maturity) because we are not capturing the clinical data” (R22). Organizations in this category primary focus on running an efficient and profitable business. Arguing that efficient management of commercial aspects is more important than clinical aspects for health analytics efforts, one respondent observes that: “everyone will be strong on the (health management system) HMS side because it is all commercial, billing, utilization, and all those things. Doctors, they need to get the money, and so the entire backend will be strong. But the clinical side of it may not necessarily be of the same level.” (R7).

These organizations focus on HA applied to administrative, operational, and financial domains and employ less complex descriptive models. Pointing out the need for more data points for a deeper analysis of data that uses predictive or prescriptive models, another respondent observed: “whatever we have, it is all descriptive. If modeling has to [must] be done, that needs several hundreds or thousands of data points. We are very far away from any kind of predictive or prescriptive analytics” (R12).

Lack of technology infrastructure was found to be one of the reasons for low levels of HA adoption in these healthcare organi zations. The lack of technology infrastructure in these organizations is characterized by dated equipment, outdated legacy systems, poor data quality, inadequate digitization of records, and inadequate levels of data capture. A manager acknowledged that “we stil carry a lot of legacy equipment which are still dated,” (R7), and that it was “not a good thing from scalability, from adopting some of the modern technologies and ideas that we want to do, that’s one of the key limiting factors” (R7).

As pointed out by a senior executive (R20). clinical treatment, consequent to poor data infrastructure, may have to be based on inadequate and poor-quality data. Identifying the problems associated with data capture and potential loss of data even if it is captured, R20 observed that: “we don’t have the software, we are not keeping the soft copy, manually entering the reports; definitely the patient might lose the reports: or even if he (the patient) has. he might not get it and sometimes we might not enter the reports completely in the notes. Whatever is filed in the folder, most of the patients keep but still that there is scope of losing the data.” (R20). Having digital records is not enough if the quality of those records is not good and the data requires cleaning. As noted by a chief information officer, “we have to [must] do a lot of cleaning work before we try to do some kind of analytics.” (R3)

In summary, organizations with conservative adoption patterns typically are short-term oriented, adopting HA primarily in commercial and administrative domains rather than in clinical. These firms generally suffer from poor data infrastructure. are limited to the use of descriptive models, and are more interested in tangible immediate returns on HA investment.

## 4.2.3. Niche adoption pattern

This adoption pattern is distinguished by innovation in niche medical areas and leapfrogging HA maturity stages with the extensive adoption of advanced HA technologies in those focused domains. Exemplified by the niche plavers such as C3 (an ophthalmic hospital) and C10 (a genomic testing lab), organizations with the niche-adoption pattern view health system technologies and analytics as their key business enabler. For instance. C3 has deploved a voice-based medical assistant on every medical consultant's desk that recognizes and transcribes the conversation and populates the EMR automatically, This has significantly reduced the medical practitioners' time dealing with information technology (IT) and increased their face time with patients. Similarly, C3 has designed vans with all the necessary medical and IT technologies to travel to remote villages in India and deliver health care. They use an advanced application that enables photographs of retina/eyes taken from patients’ personal mobiles to be uploaded for remote diagnosis. C10 brought to market several innovative healthcare products which use advanced data and artificial intelligence-driven genomic analysis of human blood and saliva samples, in order to predict the probability that a person is susceptible to various diseases. All the above applications are heavily data-driven. These niche players have then leapfrogged into advanced health analytics by adopting advanced technologies As pointed out by a respondent these firms, “live on analytics, … fairly mature, and I think our business is depending on analytics” (R23)

Since these niche healthcare organizations focus on one medical specialty and therefore do not have the information technologies/ systems (IT/IS) complexities associated with supporting of multi-specialties in a full-service hospital, it was easier for them to manage HA adoption. As noted by a respondent: “everything is focused only on one specialty, it becomes easier. When you are a multi-specialty with consultants, then it becomes difficult” (R7). In addition, this concentration on one clinical specialty enabled these healthcare organi zations to invest in specific foundational technology infrastructure that can deliver economies of scale and successful clinical out comes. Unlike in a multi-specialty healthcare organization where investments in health analytics are spread across various specialties, these niche plavers are able to consolidate their investments in one specialty and leapfrog to predictive and prescriptive analytics models that could deliver sound clinical as well as commercial outcomes. Ascertaining the importance of focusing on one domain, a respondent observes, “I think we have to be focused on what we want, rather than getting a big analytical system which takes a lot of time and energy to convince the management” (R23). Thus, organizations with niche adoption pattern leveraged HA not just for bringing effi ciency into their care processes, but also as their key business enabler.

Building on our initial analysis of three patterns of HA adoption, in the next section we apply an institutional theoretic perspective to explain the impacts an organization’s material-resource environment and institutional environment on its HA adoption pattern and management decisions regarding HA adoption.

## 4.3. Institutional influences on patterns of HA adoption

At an organizational level, technology adoption matures over time (Hwang, 2005; Yamauchi & Swanson, 2010). In that process, organizations, however, take different paths, as illustrated in the context of HA adoption in Section 4.2. Consideration of the orga nizational field, and of the healthcare industry’s material-resource and institutional environments by Scott et al. (2000) provides the contextual grounding for examining factors influencing HA adoption pattern, the choice of domains and the extent (breadth and depth) of adoption in our case study healthcare organizations.

Key actors in the organizational field in India in which our case study organizations operate include healthcare organizations (hospitals or hospital chains) (represented by C1—C10) that interact with and are supported by medical insurance payers (C12), pharmaceutical firms (manufacturers of medicine and drugs), and medical equipment vendors. The industry is also supported by information technology (IT) services vendors (C11) and IT consulting organizations (C13) that provide general information systems and importantly, the foundational health information systems and health analytics applications. The above represent the organizational-level actors. The industry and activities of the above actors are governed by the regulator (C15) - the Indian Gov ernment’s Ministry of Health, which formulates policies and enacts laws and health regulations and state governments that monitor them (see Fig. 1).

## 4.3.1. Influence of material-resource environment

Supply-demand of resources, technologies, and market structure impact hospital operations, and in turn, their HA adoption patterns.

4.3.1.1. Supply-demand. Earlier, we discussed the critical issues in the supply-demand of trained healthcare providers and related resources in India. Our participants highlighted this constraint:

“The biggest challenge today… is lack of quality doctors, lack of nurses. Anything from a supply side of the medicine, there is a constraint” (R7).

“Trained people, experienced people, qualified people and the demand for such like health care….So definitely India is a country, less number of beds, less number of doctors, less number of specialist doctors, so you have a human resource problem” (R1).

Three challenges accentuate the supply-demand skewness: (i) the lack of ‘quality’ doctors and nurses (allied health care pro fessionals), (ii) the limited number of trained specialists, and (iii) the resources crunch in terms of beds and physical facilities. The heightened supply-demand skewness means that hospitals are overcrowded and hence under pressure. The above impacts the amount of time doctors can afford to spend with patients: “some of our doctors see 100 patients a day” (R11), and “they [i.e., doctors] hardly spend a few minutes to talk” (R12). This view corroborates the finding of an international study across sixty-seven countries (Irving et al., 2017).

In terms of HA adoption patterns, the above issues primarily impact organizations that have far-sighted and conservative adoption patterns (those offering medical services in several healthcare domains), as compared to niche-healthcare organizations, which have fewer complex operations and patient engagement is appointment driven. As observed by a respondent, “it’s (niche organization) fairly an appointment driven organization, so it’s very easier because we don’t have the issue around time and so many other complexities that you have in a multi-specialty hospital” (R7). Contrasting with the organizations with far-sighted and conservative adoption patterns where HA adoption is made harder with these complexities, this respondent further opined that “our problems are fairly well understood as opposed to a multi-specialty hospital, which still largely runs on emergencies. People [there] constantly live under extreme pressure, which makes it tough for somebody to implement systems” (R7).

4.3.1.2. Technologies. HA technology overlays on the data produced by the health information systems. Hence, there is a correlation between the maturity of foundational health information systems and the maturity of HA adoption (see also Fig. A3 Appendix A). As explained in section 4 2 and Table 3. we found that organizations that have embraced far-sighted and niche adoption patterns had mature hospital information system technologies. As noted by a respondent, a few organizations (e.g., C1, C4 & C10) built mature IS/IT infrastructure to lead with “world-class technology for India as [a] mission” (R1) and differentiate themselves from others by “bringing the best technology for best healthcare” (R1). In contrast, organizations that present the conservative pattern had inadequate foundational technology infrastructure that limit HA maturity. Outdated legacy equipment and systems, the inability to capture data, and the resulting poor quality of data constrained healthcare organizations’ ability to adopt HA.

4.3.1.3. Market structure. While the aforementioned supply-demand skew appears as a common issue for all, healthcare organizations in India operate in a diverse material-resource environment impacted by the structural aspects of the market. The market structure relates to how different actors in the organizational field engage with one another (Chiasson & Davidson, 2005). We considered two relationships: (i) engagement with their clients (service offered, the scale of operation, markets/clientele addressed), and (ii) engagement with their medical professionals (working relationship). These engagement approaches, in turn, impact the nature of the adoption pattern (far-sighted. conservative. and niche) of the healthcare organization

Organizations that have adopted the far-sighted pattern (C1 and C4) offer a range of healthcare services to their clients on a large scale. This large scale helps bring operating costs down and increases profitability, a part of which the corporations redirect to technology investments. We observed that these organizations also addressed the market segments outside India, catering to medica tourism. A respondent reported the scale of such demand and the reasons: “We see patients from over 120 countries…. They come because of the reputation of the unit, and the clinicians here” (R2). When the scale of operations is significant, and there is a market of patients from countries with higher healthcare standards, the hospitals embark on a more mature and far-sighted adoption pattern to cater to the demands of their clients. Further, these organizations are constantly on the lookout to identify new technologies. A respondent in a far-sighted organization expressed this outlook: “If there is anything new in the medical horizon anywhere, our chairman is the first person to say, is it legitimate from the clinical perspective? And if so, we will get it here. It doesn’t matter what the cost is.” (R2). They also leverage data and HA to enhance the other aspects of engagement, such as client experience and continuation of care

Compared to organizations that has adopted the far-sighted pattern of HA adoption, organizations with a conservative adoption pattern do not have the advantage of large operations and the infrastructure capability to collect and leverage data at scale needed for health analytics. Hence, they prefer a conservative approach to HA adoption constrained by the available data assets through the limited scale of their operations.

The second aspect of market structure relates to how healthcare organizations engage medical professionals, which is a critica supply-side relationship. There are three different engagement models in the case study organizations: as employees, as consultants, and hybrid. In the employee model (as in C3 and C10, the niche players) all the medical practitioners are on the organization’s payrol and are full-time employees. The doctors are not allowed to practice on their own outside the organization. The consultant mode (followed by conservative organizations) mainly employs visiting consultants (physicians) on an as-needed basis with a smaller ratio of physicians-to-patients on the payroll. These visiting consultants work in many hospitals on a fee-for-service basis. This can help cut the individual hospital’s personnel costs (Dash, 2014).

The hybrid model is a mix of the two (as in C1 and C4, the far-sighted organization), wherein medical practitioners are either fulltime employees (on payrolls) or consultants (on a fee basis) but dedicated full-time to the hospital. Doctors are allowed to work in their own clinic after hours, while working for the hospital on a “fee for service" basis. These far-sighted organizations, owing to their scale of operations and market attractiveness (benefits, and working environment), can benefit from dedicated resources (as in the emplovee model) and the flexibility to limit costs (as in the consultant model). A chief information officer (CIO) observed, “even though the doctor is ‘fee for service’, and he is free to have his practice outside, ….[I] feel comfortable saying that more than 95% of our doctors who are in that category… are as good as saying they are employees, which is the power of our organization to retain talent and keep people with us” (R1). Nonetheless it can be a challenge to ensure the specialist doctors follow internal processes and policies when they are not full-time employees. As noted by a respondent from another far-sighted organization, “I think the biggest challenge we are facing is getting them [doctors] to follow our operational processes. So, that itself is a challenge with visiting consultants… but luckily, we have most of the doctors 80% are full-time doctors. So that should not be a big issue” (R11).

How medical practitioners (doctors) are engaged with the hospital has implications for how they respond to health information system and HA adoption initiatives. In organizations displaying the conservative pattern, medical practitioners are visiting consultants who many not comply with the documentation and clinical data entry requirements of health care. A respondent from a conservative organization attested that: “Many of the hospitals, they have visiting consultants. They don’t have full-timers; they are running from one hospital to another hospital. All they care for is their share of whatever fee from the treatment etc. So, they (the organizations) are not able to enforce discipline in terms of documentation” (R26). Data capture is another challenge in these organizations that limits their ability to adopt HA. These medical practitioners, who may see up to 100 patients daily, are reluctant to invest time in data entry, as this respondent observed: “the average EMR [data entry] irrespective of how fast, for a patient, still takes 2 min. So, if the use of technology is going to add the time, I spent with you by 25%, okay, then I want to know what’s in it for me. Okay? The hospital says, ‘I want this data.’ Doctor says, ‘What’s in it for me?’ (R25)

In organizations that are niche players, doctors are employed full-time, as noted by a respondent, “we don’t have any visiting consultants… it’s a very full-fledged approach like a corporate environment where the process compliance is of the highest order” (R7). The healthcare organizations displaving the niche-pattern, operate in specialized medical segments with specialized doctors emploved full. time on their payroll, generally do not experience the same level of resistance from doctors generating the data necessary for HA from clinical information systems they use. Thus, niche organizations with the employee model had more advanced HA adoption than conservative organizations with the visiting consultant model. The far-sighted organization following a hybrid (i.e., “almost” employee) model are positioned to mitigate the supply-demand issues while at the same time able to generate a higher level of compliance for HA usage among physicians. Both these scenarios contrast with the reluctant HA usage observed in the conservative pattern. This lack of adoption can set off a snowballing effect, because the lack of usage implies a lack of data, which in turn leads to poorer outcomes from HA applications, which further disincentivizing its usage

## 4.3.2. Influence of institutional environment

The institutional environment, which has been the focus of much institutional-theoretic work on healthcare information systems, includes the dominant institutional logics in the organizational field, i.e., belief systems, actors, and governance systems (Scott et al., 2000: Thornton & Ocasio. 2012). Given the considerable opportunities and barriers in Indian healthcare (Section 4.1). healthcare organizations can respond differently not only to the material-resource environment but also to the institutional environment. Institutional factors such as the selection of resources and how they are combined and deployed are influenced by institutional logics (beliefs and rule systems) (Greenwood, Suddaby, & Hinings, 2002) that, in turn, influence HA adoption patterns.

4.3.2.1. Institutional logics. We observed that far-sighted pattern organizations (C1 and C4) with a long-term vision implement HA adoption in a systematic, purposeful and strategic manner. These hospital organizations were driven by a professional logic to healthcare, wherein efficient and effective healthcare practices and clinical outcomes take the central stage. Explaining the emphasis these organizations place on the clinical outcomes and their ability to take risks, a respondent notes: “It would be anything that is cuttingedge, <C1> will have it, and I think it’s the commitment to sort of clinical outcomes, and the willingness to invest and take risks where other plavers don't want to do it" (R2). These organizations used HA adoption as a differentiator in delivering a positive customer experience. A respondent observed: “We are obsessive about the customer experience. And we will always invest in technology that I think is the bigges differentiator with us" (R2). Suggesting that clinical outcomes are the priority. he asserted that. “clinical outcome is always first. then second ideally is the whole customer experience perspective” (R2). Standardization of healthcare facilitated by the adoption of HA can reduce errors, improve data quality, positively impact the quality of care, and is a critical determinant in the adoption of far-sighted pattern. As noted by a respondent, “the biggest impact of healthcare analytics will be standardization in the way healthcare is delivered. That standardization will drive this learning core to become very, very short. Doctors will make less mistakes, doctors will be much more accountable once the healthcare analytics takes in. standardization takes in. and that will really translate to much better-quality care" (R9)

In contrast to far-sighted pattern organizations, organizations displaying the conservative adoption pattern were attuned to the segment of low-cost care in the Indian market (see also Section 4.1) and hence were influenced by a dominant market logic of serving a large volume of the relevant population. The primary concern with the conservative pattern organizations on operational and administrative processes rather than clinical processes and outcomes was mainly due to handling significant patient volumes. A respondent from a conservative-pattern organization highlighted this point as he argued that “for any management in any hospital, administrative data is always weighed higher than clinical data… if you’re able to give them fantastic (management information system) MIS reports and dashboards on how the hospital’s business is going to do, they would prefer that to clinical data” (R12). Essentially, their focus was on managing the basic operational and administrative processes. Respondent 12 articulated how this administrative focused affects levels of HA adoption: “You might be aware of the HIMSS or the other option like the seven levels of adoption. We have not reached that level, we’ve got a basic registration system for patients, we can manage beds, we can manage pharmacy, and we can manage the inventory and manage mainly the functions of the hospital - bed transfers, charging, billing the patients, all that is by the HIS [health information system]. The only clinical information that we collect is in the labs” (R12). Without focusing on clinical outcomes and accumulating clinical data needed for health analytics applications, the organization could not progress towards broader or deeper HA adoption.

Their path towards HA adoption was nonlinear and gave evidence of leapfrogging into the latest technologies, in their focused domain or area, which could become a differ entiator of their business, rather than a linear path of advancing the breadth and scope of HA applications broadly. The success of their innovation-focused strategy was attributed to the organization focusing on health data acquisition initiatives and data use in HA applications in their focused area: “The fact is that we have around 24 patents and we were on some of the earliest discoveries of some of the medical issues within the country largely coming out of the data,” (R7).

In summary, the healthcare organizations we studied adapted differently to institutional logics in their environment, and hence in their approaches to HA adoption. However, innovation-focused and clinically-focused organizations also needed to run their business profitably and wanted to expand their market volume. Moreover, in all HA adoption patterns, organizations had to respond to the logic of medical autonomy. Under this logic, physicians emphasize their professional independence, as they hold their core expertise closely to maximize their power in the healthcare system vis a vis administration (Scott et al., 2000; Sherer, 2010; Thornton & Ocasio, 2012) This logic poses a challenge to administrative dominance in how health information systems are adopted by doctors and was evident among healthcare specialists in far-sighted and niche healthcare organizations we studied. Medical autonomy affects doctors openness to attending to the insights from HA applications. A respondent articulated how experienced medical practitioners rely more on their own experience rather than on data: “Clinicians that have been in medicine for 50, 60 years …. they might even disregard what the data is telling them because they know and for them, they will, you know, hold the patient’s hand, and they will tell you exactly what the problem is” (R2). Newer generations of medical professionals, in contrast, tend to trust the data and use data as a basis for their clinical de cisions. As noted by a respondent, “the young ones are supremely confident, trained in the latest cutting-edge technologies, are purely datadriven, and they will say, well, I think I know what it is. I am going to wait for the reports. I won’t intervene till I have data to back it up” (R2) This challenged the organizations we studied to give their medical specialists professional freedom to decide how to work with HA applications while also pushing them towards greater adoption.

4.3.2.2. Actors’ beliefs. Institutional logics are manifest through organizational actors’ beliefs, viewpoints, and actions (Currie & Guah, 2007). We found that despite a similar institutional environment in which the healthcare organizations operated, key actors in the organizations we studied held different beliefs that contributed to different HA adoption patterns.

4.3.2.2.1. Beliefs about opportunities. Even though supply-demand skew is a common challenge for Indian healthcare organiza tions, leaders in the far-sighted pattern organizations viewed the issue as an opportunity to invest in technology, in contrast to the leaders in conservative pattern organizations. Suggesting that this skew is a driver, and that HA is a solution, a respondent opined that: “How do you solve the human resource problem? … we can use technology to manage patients better; thereby, you can augment them” (R1). He pointed out how such technology adoption strategies lead to the adoption of predictive models from basic hospital information system: “that (technology adoption) leads to the overall pyramid of applications right from the Hospital information system, right to predictive analytics, that’s kind of what I see” (R1). In contrast, in an organization representing the conservative adoption pattern, supply-demand skew is a major hurdle: As observed by a respondent, “(we) are not used to going in for long implementations and the challenges, people challenge that comes with it, they are just not able to invest the time and effort in it [HA adoption]” (R26).

4.3.2.2.2. Beliefs about excellence. Leaders in far-sighted organizations, emphasizing the importance of clinical outcomes, believed that HA can bring in clinical excellence, transparency, accountability, and in turn, quality of care, and that adoption of analytics accelerates measurement and evidence-based decision making and thereby excellence. A senior executive opined that if “you employ analytics, you start measuring. So, when I say that, this is (leads to) the clinical excellence report” (R1). Another respondent similarly argued that “the biggest adyantage of healthcare analytics will be to bring transparency and accountability with health care. I think that defines everything else because once that comes everything else in terms of auality, in terms of how we view and treat our patients, everything will fall into place” (R9). Another participant explained the value of HA adoption in terms of the scope it offers for improvement. He opined that “there is so much of richness in what we can and will do with analytics. and how it drives decision making..., and I truly believe this is the only way to go” (R2). A senior executive suggested health analytics as a strategic solution to deal with the specific challenges of large patient volumes and mass transactions in the Indian healthcare sector, observing that with “analytics finally we can say it is something for which the time has already come, and especially for a country like India, which is known for its volumes and mass transaction, analytics is the only way that we can go forward” (R9). Thus, in far-sighted pattern organizations, leaders believed that HA is the right way forward to deal with the specific challenges of health care in India and that it would deliver the accountability, measurement and transparency required for

improving clinical outcomes and quality of care.

4.3.2.2.3. Beliefs about time orientation. In our study, executives from healthcare organizations that displayed the conservative HA adoption pattern were concerned about short-term benefits, and tangible and faster return on investments, more than clinical out comes and quality of care. A respondent stated that “Hospitals (conservative ones) tend to invest “into medical equipment, etc., [which] will give you returns much faster ... rather than the IT technology [needed for HA1“ (R22). Another lamented on the short-term orientation driving these organizations and observed that “most people [unfortunately] believe in short-term revenue, and that drives what they are doing today" (R23). Suggesting that the benefits of capturing and analyzing clinical data are long-term in nature, another noted: “What is the incentive a hospital gets by capturing clinical data? It is just long-term. It is not a short-term” (R22). In contrast, these organization members believed that the results of analytics are tangible and immediate when applied to operations. A respondent explained the reasons behind the conservative adoption pattern, pointing out that, “the results are tangible in operations data, whereas, in clinical data, the outcomes are not tangible or immediate. It could (only) be seen over a period” (R3).

Leaders in healthcare organizations displaying the niche HA adoption pattern considered HA as a foundation for innovation and for solving some pressing clinical issues. The respondents in these organizations also recognized that building a data and analytics platform takes time and were willing to invest time and resources. One respondent stated that it is difficult to attribute monetary value to the clinical benefits derived from HA. He observed that, “when it comes to clinical…. the benefit is only quality so that cannot be given a value, the value is thousand times more than the monetary value” (R7). Moreover, HA adoption was seen as a trigger for innovation as this respondent noted “that (post-HA adoption) is where some of the medical innovations really come into the picture, which we try to use it for gaining new ideas around some of the problems that we have” (R7). Thus, the respondents in the healthcare organizations displaying the niche adoption pattern relied on the invaluable clinical benefits and innovation opportunities that could be derived from HA adoption over an extended time.

4.3.2.2.4. Competing beliefs. While the above discussions bring out differences in actors’ viewpoints from the three adoption patterns, we also found differences in beliefs between stakeholders within the organization that reflected the multiplicity of institu tional logics. As a case in point, in C1, the chief information officer (CIO) was keen to ensure the medical professionals complied with electronic medical record data entry into the systems (professional logic - clinical excellence through data-driven decisions), but the chief operating officer (COO) felt that it might not be the proper use of clinician’s time (market logic – addressing the demands of volume). The chief operating officer pointed out this difference in viewpoints by stating that “it is probably his [CIO’s] viewpoints saying all clinicians should 100% use the system to capture data” (R2). He suggested that “as the operation guy running the unit. I will tell you if I get my clinician to physically key in all the information that he or she is collecting, his volumes or patients that he sees will drop from 5x to 1x” (R2) This tension between logics within a far-sighted organization was evident with the chief operating officer, who was responsible for operations and efficiencies and was concerned about the potential reduction in the volumes of patients a clinician could see, emphasized the market logics, while the chief information officer, who was responsible for driving the HA adoption and wanted clinicians to comply with the data management requirements, embraced professional logics.

4.3.2.3. Governance systems. Governance systems refer to managerial support, processes, and controls which shape the IT artifact (Chiasson & Davidson, 2005). We found that mature internal governance systems supported HA adoption in the large/multi-specialt healthcare organizations displaying the far-sighted HA adoption pattern, in comparison to the midsize/small multi-specialty orga nizations displaying the conservative pattern, which had several management-related challenges in HA adoption. Top management support is a commonly observed factor influencing IT adoption and use in the information systems’ adoption literature. Here we highlight aspects of management support structures and governance that differentiated the far-sighted patter from others, including support for collaboration, patient-centric approach, learning from mistakes, and support for positioning technology. We found that the management belief that HA technology is a differentiator drove managers' support for HA initiatives and changes, in hopes to deliver a competitive advantage. As noted by a respondent, “the support and their [management] belief on technology as a differentiator and not just a differentiator but also strategic element is what positions us to do all of this” (R1). Another respondent indicated that their “executive sponsor is a CEO, the champion on analytics,” (R9) and pondered, “what better support you can actually get?” (R9). Stressing the importance of collaboration between clinicians and business personnel, a chief information officer explains how this could lead to better outcomes: “I (clinician) give the business analyst clinical insight, and you give me the technical (HA specialist) insight; both of us stand together. I am an expert in clinical, and you are an expert in technology; if we both come together, we have something” (R1). Further, key actors in these organizations perceived analytics to give them additional insights regarding patient care. As noted by a chief operating officer, this enabled the organization to adopt a patient-centric approach and ask, “is there anything that the system can do to help you (the patient) perhaps be less stressed about being in a hospital. less frustrated about being in a hospital. less anxious about being in a hospital?" (R2). He asserted “this data helps me do that, and then the analytics part gives me proxies,” (R2) to work with and improve.

Another aspect of support in organizations displaving the far-sighted pattern was their ability to allow mistakes to be viewed as part of the learning process. A chief operating officer explained that their current competitive positioning was based on this important thinking. He reflected on this phenomenon: “…Where we are today […] it started with us willing to say, we will make mistakes through this process, and we won’t hold [those] mistakes against us. So, we said that part of the journey to get to a better place is to make mistakes” (R2). Managers in organizations believed that learning from mistakes is a stepping stone for successful assimilation of technologies, and HA technology is positioned to support the belief that if you “embrace data plus intuitive level decision-making” (R7), it will lead to improved clinical outcomes and quality of care.

In healthcare organizations displaying the conservative pattern, governance challenges related to ability, vision, intent, and ex ecutive champions limited the extent of their HA adoption. Inability and lack of knowledge to use even the basic HA solutions for improvement was a constraining factor in these organizations. As observed by a respondent, “the problem has been that people’s inability to look at what can they do with it (HA & data)” (R25). Even though managers and clinicians in these conservative organizations “know the buzz words like data analytics, they probably don’t know really what they want out of it (HA & data)” (R26). For commercial reasons, these organizations would like to increase patient volumes but did not see the value of data. A member of one organization commented, “everybody is interested in getting more patients, but I don’t think they see data as an important – existing data as an important source of information to do that" (R26). Another reason that limited the ability of organizations with the conservative pattern to advance HA adoption was the lack of executive champions and the belief that HA is merely a technology issue. A senior manager noted that: “unfortunately, the CEO thinks that this is an IT manager’s job. That’s the problem. If the CEO said this is important, I’m going to drive it from my office, then it works” (R25). Thus, healthcare organizations that typified the conservative pattern, had governance systems that included attribution of low value to data, inability to glean insights from data analyzed, lack of knowledge to use analytics solutions, and absence of executive champion(s) at the senior management level.

Governance also arises from external actors with the coercive authority to regulate activities within the institutional field. How ever, in India, coercive forces related to health information regulations have not been effective, leading to poor compliance by healthcare organizations of health data collection and management. Even though, “per the clinical establishment act, there are very clear norms of various categories of hospitals (CAT A, B, C, etc.,) on the facilities, record maintenance, etc.” (R24), this respondent lamented that “this is hardly followed in India" (R24). This act was not explicitly about data digitization, as was the case of the Health Information Technology and Clinical Health (HITECH) Act and the Medicare Access and CHIP Reauthorization Act (MACRA) in the US. Hence, given India’s size and diversity, “the threshold [for digitization] is very low from the side of the government …. [and] there is not much of a stick or a push from the government saying you need to do this thing” (R2). The decisions to purse health information technologies are largely left to the individual healthcare organizations, and as noted by R2, “it is us driven by our need to be obsessive of these things” (R2). Ineffective government regulation thus has had a cascading effect on the adoption of data-related technologies by healthcare orga nizations, in which progressive organizations (far-sighted and niche- organizations in our study), are self-regulated irrespective of mandates and purse adoption of technologies whereas others do not.

## 5.1.1. Patterns of HA adoption

The HA adoption patterns we identified – far-sighted, conservative, and niche– evolved due to deliberate HA adoption choices that organizations make, given the market opportunities and challenges they face. In our study, we analyzed these patterns using the breadth (clinical, operational, administrative, strategic/enterprise and financial domain scope of HA) and depth (complexity and sophistication of the HA models) dimensions (see Section 4.1 and Appendix A). HA adoption patterns reflect and provide insights into the key focus areas of Indian healthcare organizations. Organizations adopting a far-sighted pattern systematically plan step-by-step and progress to mature HA adoption in both breadth and depth dimensions, starting from simpler to more complex use cases. The conservative pattern remains focused on narrowly defined administrative analytics (the non-clinical domains and basic descriptive models). The niche pattern leapfrogs (as needed) to greater depth of advanced and more complex HA applications but primarily in specific domains.

Our study also finds a clear association between the type of organization and the above patterns. The far-sighted HA adoption pattern was associated with corporate hospitals that target high-end clientele (including medical tourism) and advanced secondary healthcare requiring investments in appropriate information technology. In contrast, the conservative pattern was associated with mid-tier and smaller organizations targeting the basic or primary healthcare market, which may not need sophisticated health IT to support the care processes. The niche pattern was associated with players focused on only one medical specialty across population segments.

## 5.1.2. Institutional factors

Material-resource environment includes the supply-demand of resources, market structure, and technologies. Supply-demand skew is the biggest challenge in the Indian healthcare market. Hospitals are challenged to attract medical practitioners to work for them and to then motivate them to comply with the policies and processes pertaining to the usage of analytics technologies (for instance, data management). Given that medical practitioners traditionally have emphasized their autonomy and power, the business model (market structure) of their engagement with the hospital also adds to the above challenges. Compared to organizations that have practitioners dedicated to hospitals, the ones that depend on external consultants for service faced more issues in HA adoption, owing to the lack of control over practitioners’ actions. The technology infrastructure needed and used to address the diverse healthcare needs of a large population is also an essential factor.

Institutional environment includes institutional logics, actors’ beliefs, and governance systems. Among the multiple logics in the institutional field, we found that HA adoption is influenced primarily by professional and market-driven institutional logics. The potential for coercive influence of the state in shaping HA adoption patterns was not evident, owing to weaker compliance with health regulations related to the digitization of health records and a lack of mandates by the government in India. This may change in due course if the government takes a firmer stance in future.

We observed that the far-sighted pattern of HA adoption was driven by a professional logic to bring efficiency and effectiveness into healthcare practices and enhance clinical outcomes and consequently leverage HA. The actors of far-sighted organizations viewed the supply-demand skew as an opportunity to adopt HA. They had a long-term focus, established governance processes and senior management support to promote clinical-IT team collaboration, trust in HA-led decisions, and an atmosphere of learning from mis takes. These actors offered professional freedom to medical practitioners and positioned HA as an extension of their core skills. while exercising controls and compliance on HA usage.

We observed that the niche HA adoption pattern was also driven by a professional logic but the focus of organizations was on clinical innovation in their core business domain, where HA is seen as a lynchpin. Owing to their focus on a single specialty, they did not have the operational complexities and the supply-demand challenges seen in the other organizations and other adoption patterns. These organizations were deploying advanced HA technologies in the areas not attempted by others.

We observed the conservative pattern was driven by the market logic to address the primary healthcare needs of the vast popu lation. In these organizations the actors’ focus was short-term, and their views were guided by the operational, administrative, and financial aspects of their business. Hence, they did not find a significant need for HA-driven clinical care. Their executives lack the knowledge, vision, and progressive intent to promote HA in the clinical domain and focused instead on analytics addressed to financia and administrative issues.

## 5.2. Insights for theory

The HA adoption patterns identified in our study illuminate and provide specificity to the existence of variations in HA adoption identified in past studies (Raghupathi & Raghupathi, 2014). The breadth-depth, two-dimensional model of assessing HA adoption provides additional insights on variation. Our study also highlights the possibilities of a non-sequential adoption pattern that can be thought of as a manifestation of the leapfrogging effect (Chen & Li-Hua, 2011), wherein entities can continually adopt a wait-andwatch mode and leapfrog at the right time (instead of incremental change). Leapfrogging (Schilling, 2003) has been theoretically investigated in the research literature (e.g., Brezis, Krugman, & Tsiddon, 1993; Goldenberg & Oreg, 2007, Schilling, 2003) and is present in different industries (e.g., Fong, 2009; Kimenyi & Moyo, 2011), including the Indian financial sector (Business Line, 2021). Our study suggests that leapfrogging happens in the healthcare industry with regards to health information technology adoption and related health services innovations. While we observed leapfrogging only in niche organizations, as technologies change, leapfrogging at the right time could provide a better return on assets and could therefore be applied by different types of organizations.

From an institutional theory perspective, leapfrogging could influence actor beliefs and actions at an institutional level. That is, leapfrogging could make today’s laggards into innovators of tomorrow, impacting the organizational population and competitive market structure and in turn triggering newer kinds of memetic pressures. Leapfrogging could also impact the material-resource environment by shifting the supply-demand equation. Telehealth is a relevant example of this impact on material-resource environmen (Wosik et al., 2020). Organizations that leapfrogged with the required technical resources and organizational processes for scaling their telehealth services could tip the competitive balance of the market. As such, this is an important area for future research.

The adoption pattern we found brings insights into the literature on maturity models such as HIMSS for HA (HIMSS, 2021) and others [e.g., (Brooks et al., 2015; Carvalho et al., 2019; Gomes & Rom˜ao, 2018; Sanders et al., 2013)]. While they are usefu frameworks for practitioners to consider, the core issues with such models are the implicit assumption that the adoption process is sequential and incremental and primarily under the control of the organization. These frameworks do not consider fully the implications of external, institutional factors. Our study offers insights for a view of HA maturity models as a dynamic path-based maturity assessment with due consideration of institutional factors.

Although institutional theory has been widely used in healthcare information systems research, institutional approaches tend to focus on the institutional environment dimension [e.g.. (Burton-Jones et al., 2020: Currie & Guah. 2007: Guillemette et al., 2017: Sahay et al., 2010)]. Our study brings some balance to the literature by emphasizing and incorporating the material-resource environment as an important and differentiating dimension. The supply-demand (material-resource environment) asymmetry has been identified in the past (HealthWorld, 2019; Srinivasan & Chandwani, 2014), but the healthcare organizational responses we observed (use of technology to manage challenges of human resource shortage: creating an organizational environment to attract personnel and also retain them) suggest nuanced ways in which organizations respond to this market condition, Market structure is often treated as a macro-level institutional factor that influences the flow of resources (Chiasson & Davidson, 2005; Scott et al., 2000). Our research highlights how far-sighted organizations such as C1 (which is large) can leverage their economies of scale and business model related to prac titioners’ engagement to influence market structure and mitigate the challenges of asymmetries between patient volume (demand factor) and doctor availability (resource supply). Attending to the interaction between supply-demand and market structure and organizational responses to these interactions can help to explain variation in health IT adoption dynamics and outcomes.

## 5.3. Practical implications

A practical implication of the observed adoption patterns is that one size of HA adoption does not fit all organizations. Depending on its business goals, an organization can focus on a broad scope or a narrower one, or organizations (as in C3 and C10) can leapfrog into best-of-breed technologies (e.g., on Cloud) and advance their adoption based on their business needs without going through an incremental approach. However, in our study context, the supply-demand skew in the Indian market is also a hard reality, and doctors are overloaded attending to patients. This market constraint may inhibit a healthcare organization’s ability to implement both the data collection infrastructure necessary for HA and HA applications, particularly those pursing the volume logic and strategy.

Executives must also recognize and be prepared for resistance from medical practitioners (Abdekhoda et al., 2015; Boonstra & Broekhuis, 2010; Davidson et al., 2018; Esmaeilzadeh et al., 2015) to adoption of HA applications, especially when external con sultants (in pay-for-service mode) are used. The resistance from medical practitioners could be owing to a lack of trust in HA-led decisions, priority when doctors have only a limited time for patients, and challenges to professional autonomy when hospita management attempts to dictate HA-use practices (Sherer, 2010). Healthcare organizations could drive the adoption of HA by posi tioning the technology not as a replacement for practitioners but as an extension of their capabilities (i.e., intuition plus data-driven decision advantage) to help them do their jobs better through the active collaboration of IT and medical competency aided by creating champions (Miech et al., 2018) from medical practitioners who have successfully used data.

## 5.4. Limitations and future research

A limitation is that our research is focused on private hospitals in India rather than including publicly funded hospitals. We did so because 76% of healthcare spending in India is in private hospitals where modern technologies are adopted. Future studies with public organizations are needed to understand the varying influence of factors such as institutional logics, resources, and political leadership on HA adoption. Additional studies are needed to explore our insights concerning HA adoption in different countries, particularly in settings where government regulation is stronger as well as in countries which rely primarily on public sector healthcare organizations. Finally, additional studies are needed to investigate the institutional effects of segmented markets at the meso-level with different institutional logics (addressed market segment or locations) and leapfrogging effects and the implications for the adoption of health analytics processes and outcomes.

## 6. Conclusions

In this study, we used an institutional-theoretic lens to explore the institutional and contextual impacts on HA adoption through a cross-sectional field study with ten Indian private healthcare organizations and four health ecosystem partners. We considered the limitations of maturity models, the non-sequential and leapfrogging phenomenon, and the breadth and depth of adoption as possible considerations in HA adoption maturity. We presented three types of HA adoption patterns (far-sighted, conservative, and niche) and presented new insights on the impacts of material-resource environment (supply chain and business models) on HA adoption, institu tional logics in driving HA adoption decisions, and the role of actors/logics in shaping HA innovation in the face of an intense resourcecompetitive context.

Market research studies have acknowledged the high growth potential of the Indian healthcare sector in general and private providers in particular. Healthcare analytics, an organizational-level technology, is widely seen as one of the levers not only to industry growth but also to enhance health outcomes by mitigating the pressing issues seen in Indian healthcare. Several hospitals have already started their pathway to HA adoption. We anticipate the healthcare sector in India and elsewhere will increasingly recognize the potential of healthcare analytics and adopt these applications. With the focus on digitization of medical records, new envisaged government mandates, and increased digital interactions between the players in the health ecosystem, data-led decision-making can help transform the growing healthcare sector of the Indian economy. We hope our study contributes to this transformation by providing insights into the processes, pathways, and challenges of adopting this promising technology.
