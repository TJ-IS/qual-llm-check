# The argumentative salience of technology frames of reference: An analysis of argumentative discourse in the development of a health information exchange initiative

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Technology frames of reference Frame structure Argument mapping Frame salience

## A B S T R A C T

This study examines the impact that the argumentative salience of technology frames of refer: ences has on the execution of complex IS implementation projects. It employs Toulmin's argument model to develop argument maps that depict the structure of argumentation that took place during the development and implementation of an interorganizational health information ex change initiative (HIE) that took place in southeast USA. Toulmin's argument model faciliated the portrayal of frame salience in terms of three structural properties (i.e., blindness, indifference, and ownership). The study used these properties to show how the breadth, depth and conspicuity of frame structures changed during the course of the project and demonstrated how these changes impacted both the level of contestation observed in the project as well as project outcomes. In addition to lending insights that are specific to this case, our conceptualization of frame structure lends itself to cross-case comparisons and future theory building as the impact of these argument structures can be evaluated in a multitude of different contexts

## 1. Introduction

The potential of information systems (IS) to improve the flow of clinical. financial, and administrative information between the eclectic interests that make up the U.S. health care system has provided strong incentives for large investments into these systems. In 2009, for example, the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 (ARRA) dedicated an unparalleled \$19 billion to promote the adoption and use of health information technologies. Investment into these systems has also continued to grow. U.S. investment in health information technologies was \$2.8 billion in 2013 and grew to \$7.1 billion in 2017 (Morse, 2018). Despite investor confidence in these systems however, implementing new IS into the extremely fragmented US healthcare system (Cebul, Rebitzer, Taylor, & Votruba. 2008) has proven to be an extremely daunting undertaking. This is illustrated by a long litany of failed electronic health record systems implementation efforts in the U.S. (Baker, 2021; Charette, 2018; McCann, 2014). A major source of difficulty in implementing these systems has been the challenge of managing a multiplicity of stakeholders who have different perspectives and competing priorities. As was vividly demonstrated by global lockdowns during the Covid-19 pandemic, for example, clinical imper atives often run contrary to financial expediency concerns. Similarly, clinician resistance has oft been cited as a major impediment to the successful implementation of health information systems (Janssen. Donnelly. Elder. Pathmanathan. & Shaw. 2021: Krall. 1998:

Odom & Willeumier, 2018).

To address the challenge of implementing new IS into complex multi-stakeholder organizations, Orlikowski and Gash (1994) proposed the use of Technology Frame of Reference (TFR) theory to help shed light on the social processes that shape stakeholder interpretations of technology and its organizational applications. TFR's basic premise is that organizational member acceptance, deployment and actions towards information technologies are negotiated by their shared interpretations of these technologies (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994). Studies based on this premise have contributed to our understanding of technology assimilation in complex organizations by addressing difficult questions such as: how to develop effective governance strategies that can manage incongruent or ambiguous framings of technology (Wang, Megdaglia, & Jensen, 2021); how to manage IT requirements determination for projects that are not only characterized by multiple stakeholder perspectives, but also changing views (in the event of long-term endeavors or changes in business conditions or key stakeholders) (Davidson, 2002); how diverse groups in an organization can negotiate the “best practices” that are embedded in software packages (Yeow & Sia, 2008), and how to facilitate IT-enabled change when confronted with stakeholder views that are not only incongruent, but also inconsistent (Young, Mathiassen, & Davidson, 2016).

Although TFR research has generated important insights into how to address the challenge of implementing new IS into complex multi-stakeholder organizations, our understanding of the underlying dynamics that link technology frames to business processes and organizational outcomes remains nascent. For example, while the TFR literature has extensively investigated the consequences of incongruent stakeholder frames on IS related organizational outcomes (Davidson, 2006; Davidson & Pai, 2004), we still do not fully comprehend the structural properties of these frame differences (Davidson, 2006; Young et al., 2016). We also have a limited un derstanding of how these structural properties vary over time. This paper consequently seeks to advance the potential of the TFR framework by addressing unanswered questions related to the role that frame structure plays in shaping IS related organizationa endeavors.

Specifically, this paper focuses its attention on answering the following research question: How do temporal changes in the structura properties of Technology Frames of Reference impact outcomes of complex IS implementation projects?

To accomplish this, we borrow from Toulmin's (2003) theory of argumentation which allows us to depict the structure of tech nology frames in this study in terms of distinct argument properties and study their evolution over time. Arguments are well-suited for depicting the “structural” properties of socio-cognitive phenomena because there is a clear sense in which arguments are understood to be bounded social constructs. As Toulmin (2003) asserts, all arguments, regardless of the field of argumentation, can be depicted in terms of a universal structure that is based on the same, fundamental building blocks. This universal feature of argumentation has immense potential for theory building in the ideographic domain of TFR research as it could help foster cross-case comparisons and expand the reach of its insights.

We employ Toulmin's conceptualization of argumentation to examine the structural evolution of the Technology frames of reference in a case concerning an initiative to create a health information exchange (HIE) between three healthcare organizations located in Southeast USA. We introduce the concept of argumentative salience to show how stakeholder contestation in the key frame dimensions of this study (i.e., technical, clinical, financial, and political) evolved throughout the project and how this affected key project outcomes.

Our research contributes to the literature by offering insights into the role that argumentative discourse plays in structuring the technology frames that influence organizational outcomes. An argument centered approach to depicting frame structure facilitates evaluation of the following key aspects of frame structure: level of contestation – by using argument properties such as the presence or absence of supporting grounds to describe distinct categories of contestation; and frame evolution over time – by examining how the properties of argument change during the course of an initiative. Our study suggests that IS implementation outcomes are impacted by the level of contestation that occurs at each stage of the project. While, for example, our research showed that shallow frames impeded planning stages of implementation by reducing the extent of contestation, shallow frames were beneficial in operational stages as it facilitated the execution of specific tasks. By consolidating insights across these two categories of frame structure, argument theory can facilitate the development of more generalizable conceptualizations of TFR theory that could help extend its utility by fostering crosscase comparisons. While, for example, our research is situated in the idiosyncratic context of the U.S. healthcare system, the argu mentative configuration of these three aspects of frame structure is likely to have comparably significant impacts in other organiza tional contexts.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: (1) The paper begins by introducing TFR and Toulmin's argument model as the theoretical lenses that it will use to answer its research question. (2) It then offers a description of the case that was the subject of this study as well as the methods used to collect data and to construct and depict the technology frames of reference that shaped this endeavor. (3) The paper then proceeds to provide the results of its argument mapping process. (4) The paper then concludes with a discussion of these results and an evaluation of its implications for practice as well as future research.

## 2. Theoretical foundation

## 2.1. Technology frames of reference (TFR)

The origins of the TFR framework can be traced to the seminal work of Orlikowski and Gash (1994) who drawing from the literature on social cognition defined technology frames as, “that subset of members' organizational frames that concern the assumptions, ex pectations, and knowledge they use to understand technology in organizations”(p. 178). The basic premise underlying technology frames is that organizational member's acceptance, deployment, and actions towards information technologies are negotiated by their shared interpretations of these technologies (ibid). Orlikowski and Gash built on this premise in their seminal paper and demonstrated that different stakeholder groups are likely to have different perspectives of the usefulness, importance, and significance of techno logical artifacts and that these differences in frames have a notable impact on the outcome of technological innovation in organizations.

To help structure their investigation of technology frames, Orlikowski and Gash identified the nature of technology, technology strategy and technology-in-use as the three frame dimensions that influenced the organizational sensemaking around the groupware technology that was the subject of their study. Davidson's (2006) review of the TFR literature found that although there have been variations in precise definitions and names, these generic dimensions are also applicable to a broad array of TFR studies that examine frame content pertaining to other technologies in a variety of organizational settings. Apart from the similarities in frame dimensions identified across the various TFR studies, Davidson's (2006) review found that most of these studies, in keeping with the ideographic assumptions of the TFR lens, provided unique descriptions of frame content. Consequently, as a means of extending the reach of this lens Davidson (2006) advocated for more general and analytic assessments of the structural properties of frames as this would facilitate more cross-case comparisons and the articulation of a broader interpretive theory of IT and organizational change

The field has made significant progress towards this end with a growing number of studies that have generated insights related to the structural properties of frames (See Table 1). Azad and Faraj (2008), for example, performed a longitudinal 10-year study of an e-Government implementation project to describe the processes of frame evolution that helped translate and reconcile competing frames into what they termed a “truce frame”. They also defined these processes in term of their evolutionary structure (i.e., frame differ entiation, frame adaptation and frame stabilization).

Olesen (2014) similarly conducted a 10-year study that evaluated sensemaking in one organization in relation to multiple systems with an emphasis on the power structures of the various frames. They found that an unchanging dominance of the technological frame resulted in inefficient use patterns as this did not resolve the underlying frame incongruence between the various groups. Yeow and Sia (2008) lend insight related to how these frame incongruencies are discursively negotiated and demonstrated the relationship between discursive strategies and rigidity/flexibility of frames. Davidson (2002) also contributes to the examination of the rigidity and flexi bility structures of frames by introducing the notion of shifting salience. Davidson's (2002) paper showed how a shift from a focus on broad IT-enabled business transformation to projects with a narrower scope impeded the overall progress of the project during an especially tumultuous period in the organization.

In addition to studies that have used a process view to elaborate on the rigidity and flexibility aspects of frame structure. there has also been an increase in the number of papers that examine frame structure in terms of coverage, consensus or the extent to which frame content is shared across project stages. Young et al. (2016), for example, examine the role that inconsistencies within frames play in shaping the trajectory and outcomes of IT-enabled change in highly dynamic contexts. Similarly, Mazmanian (2013, p. 1226) adds nuance to the notion of consensus or congruence by introducing the notion of shared assumptions of heterogenous practice (i.e., “frames of reference are congruent, but the content of these frames suggests that everyone will, and should, act differently in relation to a particular technology”). Mazmanian's research suggests that frame rigidity could be conceptualized in terms of tolerance of dif ference or broadmindedness. Bjorn, Scupola and Fitzgerald (2006) also contribute to a coverage-based conceptualization of frame structure when they examine the role that frame expansion plays in aligning individual technological frames.

Table 1 above summarizes the key studies that address the role that frame structure plays in IS implementations. While it is apparent that the field has made considerable progress related to its conceptualization of frame structure, this remains an underex plored area of TFR research. As Table 1 indicates most of our insights related to frame structure are deduced from dynamic studies of frames that infer structure by depicting changes in frame content over time. While this has helped to shed light on the malleable nature of these structures, our understanding of the unique properties of frame structure remains limited. For example, although Davidson's (2002) paper introduced the notion of shifting salience, the structural properties of these changes in salience are still relatively obscure. In Davidson's paper these shifts in frame salience were depicted as changes in the relative prominence of the frames throughout the course of the project (e.g., from IT Delivery Strategy frame to Business Value of IT frame). While this facilitates broad comparisons of how shifts in salience impact organizational outcomes, our understanding of distinct properties of frame salience such as the degree or extent of salience remains limited.

To help synthesize these two aspects of frame structure we draw upon Toulmin's (2003) argumentation theory (described in Section 2.2). Argument theory offers a powerful lens for gaining insight into the levels of contestation as the concept of contestation is essential to the definition of argument. Argument theory provides constructs that can help depict varying levels contestation in terms of differing configurations of argument. Similarly, since the process of argumentation typically involves rebuttals or counter-argumentation, arguments are often expected to change or get refined over time. This property of argumentation lends itself well to its application in describing the temporal dimension of TFR evolution or its evolution over time

Toulmin also helps accommodate ideographic origins of TFR by acknowledging that arguments are subjective expressions of an individual or group's unique conceptualization of reality. Arguments are therefore properly understood and evaluated with respect to a particular context. However, despite the idiosyncratic nature of argumentation, Toulmin's theory also suggests that any argument can be characterized in terms of a universal structure that pervades the diverse contexts of its enactment. Indeed, the structural charac teristics of arguments (e.g., claims and grounds or evidence) make them well-suited for depiction in the form of a “frame” as argu mentative claims are typically bounded or limited by their grounds. To highlight this potential, we introduce Toulmin's structural approach to argumentation below

## 2.2. Toulmin's structure of argument

An argument can be defined as a mode of communication whereby an individual makes an explicit claim and then supports this claim to persuade others to accept it while anticipating criticism (Habermas, 1984). Toulmin's (2003) theory of argumentation pro poses that a valid argument has a proper form, analogous to a legal argument that can be laid out for inspection. This proper form consists of key components that are utilized by people who assert something that they want others to believe. According to Toulmin the inferential building blocks of any argument, regardless of the field of argumentation, are claims, grounds (or ‘evidence’), warrants, backing, qualifiers (or modalities) and rebuttals. Claims are the explicit appeals or central assertions that are offered for others to believe or “the conclusion whose merits we are seeking to establish” (Toulmin, 2003, p. 90). Grounds are evidence produced in support of a claim and are given in answer to the question, “What do you have to go on?” The general format is: “Given these grounds, I assert that this claim is true.” Warrants show the logical connection between claims and grounds. Warrants answer the question: “How did you get from these grounds to that claim?” Claims, grounds and warrants are the fundamental building blocks of argument (Fairclough, 2003). The second triad Backing, Qualifiers and Rebuttals serve in a secondary role as supports to the basic elements. Backing provides additional justification for the warrant; Qualifiers are used to communicate the degree to which we are to accept the claim as true; and Rebuttals manage potential objections by stating conditions under which the claim might hold or not hold.

Toulmin argued that this model would hold true across different contexts because although the specific content of these building blocks (e.g., the nature of a claim or its supporting grounds) may vary depending on the field of argumentation, they would never theless need to conform to these structural constraints to qualify for classification as arguments. This universal feature of Toulmin's model makes it an especially adept tool for depicting the structural properties of technology frames of reference as it does not violate the ideographic assumptions of this lens. The inferential characteristic of Toulmin's argument model also makes it especially wellsuited for depiction in the form of a “frame” as it establishes well-defined boundaries for its argument constructs. Argument claims (in the Toulminian tradition) must be linked to grounds to be valid and these grounds in turn help to “frame” or limit the persuasive reach of the claim. Fig. 1 presents an illustration of Toulmin's argument model.

Toulmin's argument model has typically been derived from a single claim or proposition that is ascribed to an individual claimant or author (Fletcher & Huff, 1990; Toulmin, 2003). Argumentation, however, could also be viewed more broadly in terms of Habermas, Lennox, & Lennox (1974), Habermas (1996) discourse where members of a social group seek to make sense of their experience and to find a collective interpretation, to develop an explanation and to establish new orienting knowledge (Strydom, 2000). Viewed through this lens, argumentation can be seen as an important mechanism in the structuration of technology frames of reference. Indeed, arguments are a central means of resolving frame incongruence as arguments are persuasive appeals that are offered in anticipation of criticism or contestation. Toulmin's model allows us to depict these appeals as structural forms that can be evaluated in terms of structural properties. To illustrate consider the following arguments drawn from the example in Fig. 1:

Argument A (with qualifier):

Claim: There are dogs nearby.

Qualifier: So, chances are… (Rebuttal: There could be wolves or coyotes nearby)

Grounds: You hear barking and howling in the distance.

Argument B (no qualifier):

Claim: There are dogs nearby.

Grounds: You saw a dog in the neighbor's yard.

Argument B that had no qualifiers could be viewed as more assertive or persuasive than argument A based on this structura property of the argument structure. Toulmin's model thus provides us with a structural means of assessing the argumentative salience of an argument. We define argumentative salience as the structural property of an argument that lends credence to the persuasive appeal of an argumentative claim. The presence or absence of qualifiers is one example of a structural property that could impact argument salience. The presence or absence of the following structural components that are used to support a claim could similarly impact argument salience: Grounds, Warrants, Backing and Rebuttals. The presence of grounds, warrants, and backing help strengthen

the salience of a claim while rebuttals weaken this salience.

This paper uses the above model to help tease out the structure of argumentation in the health information exchange (HIE) initiative that is the subject of this study and to explain how the structural evolution of the relevant technology frames of reference impacts organizational outcomes in this endeavor.

## 3. Research methodology

In keeping with the interpretive roots of TFR, this paper adopts a qualitative, in-depth approach (Baskerville & Myers, 2002; Myers, 1997) to examine the structural evolution of technology frames of reference. In particular, following Easterby-Smith, Golden-Biddle, and Locke (2008), we chose a single case study to allow close observation of the organizational actors' everyday practices based on fieldwork and the collection of supplementary data. We also adopted the technique of argument mapping to depict and analyze the temporal changes in structural properties of the frames in this study. We provide details related to the site of the case study as well as this paper's approach to data collection and analysis in the next section.

## 3.1. Site description

This study examines a large-scale IS project aimed at implementing a healthcare information system (HIS) to enable data exchange between a private health system (referred to as Southeast Health System—SHS) and a community-owned cancer center (Eagle Cancer Center—ECC). SHS is a large private health system comprised of four acute hospitals each of which operates within a separate market (in the same state) and has a distinct group of affiliated physicians and clinics associated with their respective facilities. Eagle Cancer Center is a community owned cancer radiation and research foundation that has close ties to SHS' flagship hospital (Lakefront Regiona Center – LRC) and is contiguously located to LRC's facility. ECC and LRC have a collaborative partnership related to the treatment of cancer where ECC, in conjunction with an affiliated group of oncology physicians, would provide outpatient services to cancer patients and refer them to LRC when these patients required inpatient care. Consequently, the American College of Surgeons jointly accredited ECC and LRC for the care of cancer patients. Also, the National Cancer Institute (NCI) through its National Cancer Institute Community Cancer Center Program (NCCCP) selected both organizations in June 2007 to receive a grant that would fund the electronic linkage and transfer of medical information between these two sites as well as the NCI database to facilitate cancer research. This grant was the genesis of this research endeavor.

## 3.2. Data collection

The data used in this paper was collected through a series of forty semi-structured interviews that were conducted between November 9, 2010 and October 12, 2011 (See Appendix A for interview guide). Interview length varied but lasted about one hour on average. We interviewed most subjects only once; however, the project manager was interviewed at intervals to obtain updates on project progress. Informed by past research capturing work practices through interviews (Orlikowski & Scott, 2015; Scott & Orli kowski, 2014), we conducted informal, open-ended and semi-structured interviews in order to collect as much detail as possible through careful listening to the “stories” of project participants of their direct experience, and personal reflection and arguments during the various stages of the implementation project (Walsham, 1993, 2006). This allowed us to gather retrospective (2007–2010) and longitudinal (2010− 2011) data. We audio-recorded all the interviews and had them professionally transcribed. Note-taking during the interviews was minimal to make the interview feel more conversational than interrogative. We wrote our reflections and memos shortly after the interview to consolidate the insights derived from the interview. In addition to interviews, we collected documents on system demonstrations, listened in on conference calls and examined archival data in the form of reports, news clippings and other filed documents. We also exchanged follow-up emails with some of the study participants to clarify certain details. We concluded our interview rounds when details about the implementation process became repetitive, i.e., after theoretical data satu ration (Bowen, 2008). We provide a summary of the interviews sorted by organization and stakeholder group (department) in Table 2 below.

It should be noted that data collection was based on interviews and therefore captured individual reflections on the HIE project and not the actual discourse that took place during the project. The frames developed from this process consequently are retrospective constructs based on Schon's ¨ (1987) retrospective ‘reflections on practice’ and do not capture the real-time ‘reflection in practice’ or the tacit ‘knowing in action’ aspect of technology frames that would have been manifest during the conduct of the project. We posit however that these frames are a.

reasonable proxy for the frames that occurred in the actual conduct of the project as they indicate the aspects of the process that were salient to the respondent. It should also be noted that the arguments captured in the maps do not reflect the sort of back and forth contestation that could have taken place in practice, but rather capture reflective argumentation or the sensemaking that is involved when individuals attempt to anticipate or resolve potential or actual challenges to a particular frame of thought (Hoffmann, 2016a, 2016b). This sort of “reflective” argumentation is well aligned with TFR theory as these retrospective cognitive processes help to structure future planning and action. In other words, they serve as the reference points for technology frames of reference

## 3.3. Modes of analysis

## 3.3.1. Construction of technology frames of reference

The first step in analyzing the data from the recorded interviews was the identification of the cognitive dimensions of the frames that shaped the HIE initiative. To accomplish this we used a grounded approach that extracted these dimensions by first iteratively reading the interview transcripts and then adopting the line by line open coding of these documents as recommended by (Charmaz, 2006) and Strauss and Corbin (1998). This process was conducted using QSR Nvivo and generated approximately 5500 initial codes. To reduce this data into manageable categories the paper then proceeded to an axial coding approach that looked for ways that the interviewees grouped or categorized the thinking around the project. This approach was adopted to minimize the imposition of the researcher's cognitive frames or categories on the transcripts, and to highlight the correspondence between respondent conceptuali zations of the proiect and our depiction of the organizing frame dimensions for this endeavor. We accomplished this by looking inductively for “framing” words such as “perspective,” “view,” “belief,” “standpoint,” “knowledge” and “angle” that were employed by the interviewees. These words were selected because they are associated with cognitive stances that could justifiably be rendered as ‘frames. These framing words were then grouped according to the types of adjectives or adverbs used by the interviewee to describe or identify these words.

This process yielded the following four frame dimensions that emerged from the interviewees: 1. Clinical Perspective; 2. Financial Perspective: 3. Technical Perspective: 4. Political Perspective. These four frame dimensions have face validity as three of the dimensions (Financial, Technical, and Political) are typical concerns for any IT related organizational process. A clinical perspective is also unsurprising in healthcare settings. It is also important to note that although some of these frame dimensions are associated more with certain stakeholder groups than others (e.g., clinical – clinical leadership; and financial – financial leadership) none of these di mensions were exclusive to the stakeholder groups. Clinical leadership, for example, could also be versed in financial aspects of the endeavor, but might not be as conversant in this area as individuals with expertise in finance.

## 3.3.2. Construction of argument maps

This paper adopted the technique of argument mapping to depict the structure of the frame dimensions that were elicited in the analysis described above. Argument mapping is based on Fletcher and Huff's (1990) adaptation of Toulmin's argument model to develop a diagrammatic form that illustrates the interrelationship among components of arguments which can be used to graphically analyze the merits of an argument (Hirschheim, Murungi, & Pena, ˜ 2012; Pawlowski, Watson, Davis, & Kaganer, 2008). This method involves dividing documents into topic blocks, subdividing the blocks into discrete arguments, and then identifying the argument components.

To elicit these argument components from a written document, this study adapted Fletcher and Huff's (1990) process to deconstruct arguments in three separate stages, which involved moving from more general aspects of an argument to the more specific aspects.

These stages are described as follows:

• First Pass: Referencing the axial codes that were developed when we identified the frame dimension employed in this study, we proceeded to identify topics, arguments, and the most obvious key claims in each frame dimension (i.e., clinical, technical, financial, and political). In this stage we identified the following main stages of argumentation in the project:

1. Initiation; 2. Strategic Alignment, and 3. Vendor Selection.

We also identified the core question that anchored the argumentation in each stage.

• Second Pass: Mark all claims and identify grounds for each claim. In this stage we identified all the assertions that were offered in response to the core question and any grounds that were offered in support of these claims.

• Third Pass: Within each argument, identify sub claims, elaborations, and reiterations. In this stage we examined the transcripts for any key pieces of the argument that were not captured in previous steps.

Below we illustrate a simple application of the three passes to the following paragraph drawn from the following transcript of our interview with SHS' IS System Administrator (See Table 3):

They will interface with our system and provide the portal. So, my piece of the puzzle is just setting up the communications between the two systems. ## 4.1. Initiation stage (June 2007 – March 2010)

The key event of the initiation stage was an NCI grant to the LRC-ECC Cancer program in June 2007. This grant was designed to enable the electronic sharing of medical data between the two organizations as well as to facilitate linkage to the NCI database to further cancer research. Although the NCI grant jointly accredited and funded LRC and ECC as a single cancer care program providing services along a continuum of care (ECC provides outpatient care and LRC provides inpatient cancer care), the two entities are autonomous with separate and distinct electronic medical record (EMR) systems.

The central question that served as a fulcrum for the argument map (see Fig. 2) depicting the discourse during this stage were: How is project defined and what is its central motivation? Since the interviews were conducted after the project had been initiated, answers to this question were provided retrospectively after respondents were prompted by the interviewer to look reflectively to the origins of the HIE project. The argument map for this stage reveals four distinct types of answers to this question; each one corresponding to one of the frame dimensions manifested in the study (i.e., clinical, technical, financial, and political).

From the financial dimension the project's initiation was described (by all stakeholder groups that incorporated this dimension in their discussion of the project) as a claim that attributed its origins to a financially conducive environment and that defined the project in terms of a cost/benefit analysis of its implementation. Similarly, the technical dimension yielded a separate claim that credited technical innovation with the project's origins and construed the project in terms of a desire to improve operational processes. The political dimension also produced a unique claim that couched the endeavor in terms of inter-organizational and interpersonal power dynamics. This dimension was also characterized by the articulation of a need to engage with an external stakeholder group or with a perspective that was different from the respondent's point of view.

In keeping with Toulmin's model, each claim in all four frame dimensions was supported by grounds that help to justify the legitimacy of the different claims. There was also no evidence in the transcripts of a contradiction or disputation of these grounds which lends credence to the notion that these dimensions were considered valid conceptualizations of the endeavor by stakeholders in the project. The sole counterclaim found in the argument map of the initiation stage was the presentation of an alternative view that challenged the notion that technology should motivate or define any strategic endeavor. This counterclaim originated from members of the clinical leadership team who asserted the pre-eminence of clinical motives in the field of healthcare. ## 5. Case summary and discussion

Table 9 summarizes the case findings along the three stages of the HIE project. On this basis we discuss the following: (1) how levels of argumentative salience impact frame contestation, and (2) how changes in profiles of argumentative salience over time impact project outcomes.

## 5.1. Impact of argumentative salience on level of contestation

As Table 9 indicates, the structures of frame salience that informed the discourse in this HIE implementation initiative changed significantly between the three stages of the project. In this section we extend the findings of Section 4, which focused on frame dimension salience, to examine how changes in overall frame salience with regard to the three salience characteristics (i.e., blindness, indifference, and ownership) impacted levels of contestation in the HIE initiative.

## 5.1.1. Impact of blindness on level of contestation

Blindness in the HIE initiative was manifested by the absence of argumentation with respect to one or more of the four dimensions of frame structure (i.e., technical, financial, clinical, and political) that we observed in the project. Since blindness is manifested by the absence of argumentation with respect to a frame dimension, blindness can be used to describe the breadth of frame structures. Higher levels of blindness would then signify narrower frames as it indicates the invisibility of one or more frame dimensions to some stakeholder groups. Similarly, lower levels of blindness would signify broader frames as it suggests the increased visibility of frame dimensions to stakeholder groups. Narrow frames therefore are less likely to contribute to contestation than broad frames as they reduce the scope of potential areas of disputation.

As Table 9 shows, there was a steady decrease in the manifestation of blindness as the project progressed from the initiation to vendor selection stages. Although stakeholders showed blindness with respect to the political dimension in the initiation and strategic alignment stages of the project, there was no blindness evident in the vendor selection stage. Similarly, while there was some blindness with respect to the financial (IS technologists) and technical (Business Leadership) dimensions at the project initiation there was no blindness with respect to these dimensions at the strategic alignment stage. Frame breadth consequently can be seen to gradually broaden as project moved from initiation to strategic alignment and vendor select stages. This broadening of frame structure also corresponds with increased levels of contestation in latter stages of the project. This contestation was primarily associated with po litical considerations which were largely invisible to stakeholder group in early stages, but more apparent in latter stages. Blindness reduces the level of contestation by effectively excluding some stakeholder groups from the frame negotiation process.

## 5.1.2. Impact of ownership on level of contestation

Ownership levels of frame salience were manifested by complete argumentation relative to a particular frame dimension or the presence of claims, grounds and warrants to support or criticize the assertions of a particular frame dimension. Ownership levels of frame salience therefore can be used to represent the “depth” of frame structures as this indicates full (or “in-depth”) argumentative engagement with the content of a frame dimension. As Table 9 shows, at the project's initiation ownership was predominantly attributed to the technical dimension. This is indicated by all stakeholder groups (except the business leadership) showing ownership levels of argumentative salience with respect to this frame dimension. The in-depth articulation of technical concepts during the initiation stage, when compared to the relatively shallow engagement with concepts from other frame dimensions during this stage indicate that technical considerations were the priority for stakeholders at this stage. Ownership then shifted to the financial dimension during the strategic planning stage of the project with all stakeholder groups (except IS technologists) showing ownership levels of salience with respect to this dimension. The shift in ownership thus reflects a shift in priorities to match the escalation in financial incentives surrounding the project (i.e., transition from about \$300,000 project to a \$10 million endeavor that was associated with \$30 million in incentive payments from the federal government). The structure of ownership however drastically shifted at the vendor selection stage which manifested ownership in three of the four frame dimensions (i.e., financial, technical, and political). This indicates that. unlike in previous stages of the project where a single dimension was clearly prioritized. in the vendor selection stage there were multiple priorities.

More widespread levels of ownership in a project therefore can signify increased contestation as it indicates that multiple stake holder groups continue to promote their views in regard to a given frame dimension. The continued promotion of views indicates contestation as it suggests that agreement with respect to one or more frame dimensions has not been reached. We observed this in the vendor selection stage of the HIE initiative where there was contestation related to whether or not the vendor selection decision should have been governed by the logic of financial and technical considerations (which pointed to a single vendor solution) or politica considerations (which pointed to a multi-vendor solution). This represents a distinct feature of the contestation that was observed during this stage (i.e., different from broader frame structure or reduced blindness) as it refers to the depth of contestation at this stage. Increased ownership required decision makers to consider multiple dimensions of argumentation at levels of argumentative detail (or depth) that would not ordinarily have been necessary for the vendor selection task. If priorities with respect to frame dimensions had been established prior to the vendor selection stage, this might have been a relatively objective or non-contentious decision that could have been made by ranking potential vendors based on cost and technical offerings.

The absence of established priorities with respect to frame dimensions then is also likely to have functioned as a catalyst for increased levels of contestation as stakeholders navigated competing priorities. When, for example, the technical dimension was established as the primary dimension for decision making (as it was in the initiation stage) it would have been expected to have stakeholder groups with technical expertise lead the decision making. This would have held true even if other stakeholder groups manifested ownership levels of engagement with respect to the technical dimension because groups with ownership levels of engagement with a frame dimension do not necessarily have equal expertise or knowledge of this dimension. High ownership across frame dimensions in the vender selection stage therefore is likely to have increased the level of contestation at this stage by obfuscating which stakeholder group or what type of expertise should have preeminence in decision making. Indeed, a prominent source of contestation during vendor selection stage was the perception that stakeholders were encroaching on aspects of the project for which they had little expertise. The project manager, for example, expressed concern that business leaders were trying to guide a technica decision without fully understanding the technical features of the vendor offering. Similar concerns were raised by the business leadership who questioned the ability of technical staff to understand the intricacies of business processes.

## 5.1.3. Impact of indifference on level of contestation

Indifference levels of frame salience were indicated by the presence of a claim pertaining to a particular frame dimension, but without offering any grounds or other elements of Toulmin's building blocks (e.g., acknowledging that the project had a technica component, but not providing any details of this component or providing rationale for its significance). Indifference levels of frame salience reflect a stakeholder group's articulated awareness of content pertaining to a frame dimension and can therefore be used to assess the “conspicuity” of frame structures. Conspicuity can be considered a distinct feature of argumentative frame salience as it represents a characteristic of frame structure that is visible (i.e., it is distinct from blindness), but nevertheless not intriguing enough to merit much attention or interest (i.e., distinct from ownership). Conspicuity suggests that either a stakeholder group is satisfied with the outcome of frame negotiation, or the dimension is not perceived to be salient by the group. In either case, indifference reduces levels of contestation with a project phase. Frame dimensions that manifested high levels of indifference among stakeholder groups are acknowledged as legitimate factors in the project, but not given much consideration.

A stakeholder group therefore could potentially have full comprehension of a dimension, but manifest indifference because the stakeholder group does not consider the dimension to be germane to addressing a particular project concern. Similarly, indifference could signify that a stakeholder group is satisfied with the outcome of frame negotiations within a frame dimension or considers the claims in this frame to be non-contentious. High levels of conspicuity therefore indicate low levels of contestation even when frames are broad (due to the absence of blindness).

Table 9 shows that the structure of indifference was notably different in each of the three stages of the project. The level of indifference increased from initiation to strategic alignment stages of the project (i.e., frame became more inconspicuous), but this indifference became drastically less pronounced (i.e., frame became more conspicuous) in the vendor selection stage. The low level of conspicuity in the vendor selection stage corresponds with the increased contestation that we observed in this stage. It is also inter esting to note that the decrease in indifference in the vendor selection stage was also marked by increased levels of ownership in the political frame dimension. Political considerations are typically partisan and are therefore less likely to be viewed from a dispassionate or neutral perspective. Low levels of indifference (or high conspicuity) therefore are more likely to be associated with ardent and partisan contestation than high levels of indifference (or low conspicuity), which are more closely related to dispassionate or neutral contestation.

Low conspicuity in the strategic alignment stage of the project suggest that this stage was marked by lower levels of partisan contestation than occurred in the vendor selection stage. While this could be viewed positively in terms of the absence of acrimony, the absence of contestation during this stage also implies the absence deliberative consideration of opposing views. As was discussed earlier in this section, the prevalence of indifference within a frame dimension also suggests that deliberative issues with respect to the dimension have either been resolved or have not risen to a level of prominence that would merit stakeholder engagement or attention. While this could be construed as a strength of the argumentation that took place during this stage, the prevalence of indifference within a frame dimension (as was discussed earlier) also suggests that deliberative issues with respect to the dimension have either been resolved or have not risen to a level of prominence that would merit stakeholder engagement or attention. Low conspicuity during this stage therefore signified that there were stakeholder groups that did not consider the strategic alignment process to be of great deliberative significance or one that merited much attention.

## 5.2. Impact of evolution of argumentative salience on project outcomes

While Section 5.1 evaluated how the argumentative salience of frames impacted the levels of contestation observed in the HIE initiative, in this section we examine the impact of frame salience on project outcomes. We also examine how this salience relates to the operational (or performative) and deliberative characteristics and objectives of each stage. The initiation stage of the project, for example, could be described as an operational stage of project as the main objective in this stage was the implementation of an electronic link between a regional medical center (LRC) and its affiliated cancer center (ECC). All stakeholder groups acknowledged the performative nature of this stage even though they had different perspectives on the purpose or motivation for this task. The strategic alignment stage, on the other hand, was a more deliberative stage of the project as it involved realigning the health system's business strategy to keep step with the new regulatory environment (or the passage of the HITECH Act). The vendor selection stage would then typically be considered a performative stage of the project as it would involve picking a vendor based on its fit with the strategic imperatives that were developed in the previous stage.

As Table 9 demonstrates, the structure of argumentative salience changed significantly over the course of the project. These changes in salience not only impacted the level and type of contestation that occurred at each stage of project (as was discussed in the previous section), but also depending on evolutionary timing of these changes, influenced the outcome of these contestations. While, for example, narrow frame structures (or blindness) in the early stages of the project did not contribute to any observable difficulties during these stages we noted that this exacerbated the contestation that was experienced in the vendor selection stage because some stakeholder groups felt blindsided' by the sudden salience of a hitherto invisible frame dimension. Indeed, we posit that early stages of projects are better suited to broader frames (or lower levels of blindness) than later stages because stakeholders are less likely to be strongly vested in their particular stances in early stages of ideation. Additionally, narrow frames in the strategic alignment stage are particularly problematic because this is a deliberative or planning stage of the project that is best served by a broad pool of ideas. Having high levels of contestation when undertaking strategic alignment is ideal as the deliberative process can be described as the weighing of the merits and demerits of competing approaches to achieving organizational goals. Similarly, deep frame structures (or high levels of ownership) during strategic alignment are likely to strengthen the deliberation in this stage by ensuring that this deliberation is anchored in empirical evidence or logically sound grounds.

On the other hand, high levels of contestation in performative or operational stages of projects can adversely affect the project by contributing to the problem of “analysis paralysis.” This was manifest in the HIE initiative by the project manager expressing concern about the politicization of what he considered to be a straightforward purchasing activity. The presence of deep frame structures in the vendor selection stage therefore could be attributed to inadequate deliberation in the strategic alignment stage of the project. Increased deliberation during the vendor selection stage did not lead to consensus as it was more difficult to agree on the intricate details of this stage without first establishing guiding principles to help manage this complexity.

At the vendor selection stage, consequently, it would have been more beneficial to have had more inconspicuous frame structures as this would have provided stakeholders who did not have performative responsibilities with oversight over the project. This would have encouraged accountability without impeding the execution of the purchasing task. In the HIE endeavor. however. the levels of frame conspicuity were reversed such that the deliberative strategic alignment stage was marked by inconspicuous frame structures while the performative vendor selection stage was marked by more conspicuous frames. While inconspicuous frame structures can facilitate execution of tasks, they nevertheless also impede robust deliberation. The reason the project encountered difficulties in the vendor selection stage therefore may not lie solely in the contestation that was manifest in this stage, but by the lack of robust contestation (shown by inconspicuous frame structures) in the previous stage. Indeed, we propose that although extreme partisanship can impede any enterprise (whether deliberative or performative), the prospect of resolving such partisanship is improved if it can be directed towards the negotiation of broad principles that could then guide practice

## 6. Contributions and implications

With this study, we contribute to TFR theory development by shedding light on the structural properties of technology frames and demonstrating how changes in these structures impact organizational outcomes. We accomplish this by using Toulmin's argument model to describe frame salience in terms of three structural properties (i.e., breadth, depth, and conspicuity) that correspond with varying levels of argumentative interaction with key frame dimensions $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , blindness, indifference, and conspicuity). Extant TFR research has placed great emphasis on understanding how frame differences (or frame incongruence) impact organizational outcomes (Davidson, 2006; Davidson & Pai, 2004), but provided little guidance on operationalizing the structural properties of these frame differences (Davidson, 2006; Young et al., 2016). Our study, on the other hand, focuses its attention on the characteristics and con sequences of different configurations of frame argument structures. By focusing on frame structures instead of context specific dif ferences in frame content, this study offers a conceptualization of frames and the framing process that can be applied to cross case comparisons.

For instance, Davidson (2002) describes the shifts in salience that occur in two prominent frames of her study (i.e., business value of IT and IT delivery strategy frames) in terms of the changing breadth of these frames. Davidson writes, “Broader definitions of the BIS project arose when business value of IT was highly salient, but a narrow definition of BIS reemerged when developers focused on IT delivery strategy” (p. 350). This is similar to what occurred in our study when the project moved from the initiation to strategic alignment stage and was manifested by a decrease in blindness between the two stages (see Table 8). Our study however extends Davidson's work by adding the structural properties of depth and conspicuity to help describe this shift in salience. In addition, our study advances Davidson's (2002) approach to capturing frame breadth by offering a more generalizable depiction of this aspect of frame structure. While Davidson used contextual cues such as figures of speech and metaphors to describe the changing breadth of frame structures, our study employs Toulmin's argument to depict this in more universal terms (i.e., levels of blindness or the absence of claims pertaining to a particular frame dimension)

Similarly, our depiction of technology frames of reference in terms of three levels of frame dimension salience (i.e., blindness, indifference, and ownership) could be used to lend additional insights to Young et al.'s (2016) concept of frame inconsistency (or “a group's contradictory or conflicting assumptions and expectations about IS development or implementation’") by offering a mechanism for assessing the varying levels of inconsistency within these groups. Indeed, our study showed that the argumentative salience of frame dimensions was not always consistent with what would have been expected based on a stakeholder groups organizationa function. We noticed, for example, that during the strategic alignment stage of the project, the clinical frame dimension was char acterized by indifference from clinical leadership. Similarly, business leadership showed indifference to the financial dimension of framing during the project initiation stage of the project. This inconsistency or discrepancy between a stakeholder group's traditional or functional orientation and its level of association with an affiliated frame dimension portends additional inconsistencies within the stakeholder group as the prominent driver of salience in these cases is something distinct from their common professional affiliation.

Our work also contributes to the stream of research that examines how the evolution of frames impact organizational outcomes by showing that the effectiveness of frame structure is dependent on the stage or stage of an IS proiect. We found. for example. that while more ownership or deep frame structures (indicated by higher levels of contestation) during the strategic alignment stage would have benefitted the project by enriching its deliberation, ownership during the more operational vendor selection stage impeded project effectiveness. This corresponds to early work by Kilduff, Angelmar, and Mehra (2000) who showed that although cognitive diversity was helpful in open-ended planning stages of projects, more focused cognition was needed during implementation stages. Our depiction of frame salience in terms of breadth, depth and conspicuity therefore helps to shed light on why frame incongruence, which is typically detrimental to project outcomes (Davidson, 2002; Olesen, 2014; Young et al., 2016), is sometimes inconsequential to project outcomes (Yeow & Sia, 2008) or even beneficial (Hsu, 2009). Similarly, we showed how frame structures in early stages of the project can adversely impact outcomes in latter stages of the project. For instance, our study illustrated how narrow frames (or the prevalence of blindness), which was not problematic in the initiation stage of the HIE project, posed a problem in the latter vendor selection stage even though the frames had significantly broadened (i.e., there was no blindness) in this contentious stage of the project. This finding also points to the enduring nature of the role that institutional logics (Friedland & Alford, 1991; Reay & Hinings, 2009) play in frame contestation. Our research suggests that while the level of argumentative frame salience can impact the degree ot contestation that is manifest at various stages of the project this may not correspond to any changes in underlying or long-standing contestation that exists (or pre-exists) between stakeholder groups

This finding has important implications for practice. It suggests the need to compensate for historical attention deficits by allocating more resources to the overlooked frame. Attempting to resolve frame conflicts without considering the historical context of these conflicts is unlikely to yield optimal results. Indeed, one of the challenges that the project management department faced in this case was the high rate of turnover in this department (three different project managers during this endeavor). The project management department was also a new department. Further. we noted that although the proiect management department maintained a lessons: learned document the project manager indicated that these documents were seldom referred to during this project. Attempting to manage the project and to reduce frame incongruence without developing an appreciation of the historical frame tensions was an uphill task. This highlights the need to develop effective mechanisms for maintaining and transmitting rich narratives of the historica context of these types of projects. We posit that transforming the lessons learned document from technical briefs into rich case stories that include narratives of political tensions could help achieve this goal

Additionally, by examining the impact of three distinct levels of argumentative frame salience during various stages of an implementation effort this paper is able to offer suggestions to practice related to optimal levels of salience at various points of a project. We found that indifference in performative stages of the project was not problematic as it allowed stakeholders to focus on aspects of the project that were most relevant to their areas of expertise. On the other hand, although blindness did not appear to pose problems in the initial stages of the project, blindness caused problems at later stages. Since it is not reasonable to expect all stake holders to have ownership levels of attention for the entirety of a complex, our study suggests that a more effective communication strategy would be to attempt to eliminate blindness in all stages of a project by ensuring that all stakeholders have a basic awareness of the core frame dimensions that impact the project. For example, at the outset of the HIE project, it would have been important to emphasize to all stakeholders that the success of the project depended on effectively executing the requirements of all four frame dimensions (i.e., clinical, financial, technical, and political). Additionally, to improve planning aspects of a project it would be beneficial to ensure aim for ownership levels of engagement during these stages of a project.

## 7. Limitations and future research

While we have highlighted the significance of our study for the development of TFR theory and practice, we would also like to not that the study contributions and implications should be interpreted with some limitations in mind. One such limitation stems from our approach to construct argument frames retrospectively based on interviewee recollections of project events/stages rather than observation of their real time argumentation. The notion of frame salience consequently may have been biased by the fact that recent activities are likely to be more salient than actions that took place in the more distant past. We posit, however, that these differences in stakeholder recollections can be used to illustrate important structural differences in the technology frames of the project stakeholders. Indeed, these retrospective reflections are particularly useful in capturing the stable and enduring structures of frame dimensions. It is these aspects that are most likely to influence future decision making. Further, in line with Giddens and Pierson (1998), we believe that people are knowledgeable and reflexive, and often know more about what they do than researchers might give them credit for (Orlikowski, 2002).

Notwithstanding the above attempts to mitigate against the issue of studying argumentative frame structures from retrospective interviews, we acknowledge that, to capture ongoing and evolving practices, it is important to observe rather than enquire about what the individual did (Bourdieu, 1977). Future studies based on a more deliberate design to capture real-time expressions argument might vield additional insights by facilitating a richer exploration of the argument frame construct that includes the spontaneous articulation of argumentative claims.

Another important limitation of this study is that the empirical findings of the case analysis are limited to this project and cannot be generalized to other contexts without further studies. We suggest, however, that examination of the structural components of the argumentative salience of technology frames of reference portends well for cross-case comparisons of the impact of various configurations of frame salience during the various stages of an IS project. The salience structures uncovered in this study $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ ownership, indifference, and blindness) could be applied to future TFR related studies. To accomplish this, future research should be conducted in different organizational settings to examine how the patterns of blindness, indifference and ownership that were observed in this study relate to studies in different contexts. In this study, for example, we observed heightened levels of ownership at latter stages of the project. This, however, is not necessarily a typical process. It is conceivable that projects could have heightened levels of salience at the onset of the project that tapers off towards the end. Indeed, since the findings of this study were based on interviewee recollections of the project and not real time it is likely that our findings of salience were biased in favor of more recent events. A future study that accesses real time stakeholder conceptualizations of a project could help enrichen our understanding of these three aspects of salience.

Additionally, in this study we adopted a simple approach to depicting the argument structure of TFRs. To establish the three categories of argumentative salience (i.e., ownership, indifference, and blindness), we examined whether the discourse contained claims pertaining to frames (absence of claims indicated blindness) and whether these claims, when present, were supported by grounds (absence of grounds indicated indifference). Future studies could generate richer insights by incorporating other components of Toulmin's (2003) argument model (i.e., backing, warrants, qualifiers, and rebuttals) into evaluations of frame salience. An examination of the qualifiers used to moderate argumentative claims, for example, could shed light on differing levels of indifference or ownership. Future studies could also examine the different types of grounds that are offered to support claims. In Davidson's (2002) paper, for example, frame salience was established by examining the types of figures of speech and metaphors that were used to lend persuasive credence (or salience) to a particular frame. The impact of these types of rhetorical grounds on frame salience and levels of contestation could be compared against the impact of more empirical grounds.

## 8. Conclusion

This study was designed to investigate the impact that the argumentative salience of technology frames of references has on the execution of complex IS implementation projects. We accomplished this by using Toulmin's argument model to map the structure of argumentation that took place during the development and implementation of an interorganizational health information exchange initiative (HIE) that took place in southeast USA. This approach enabled us to portray frame salience in terms of three structura properties (i.e., blindness, indifference, and ownership). The study used these properties to show how the breadth, depth and conspicuity of frame structures changed during the course of the project and demonstrated how these changes impacted both the leve of contestation observed in the project as well as project outcomes. In addition to lending insights that are specific to this case, our conceptualization of frame structure lends itself to cross-case comparisons and future theory building as the impact of these argument structures can be evaluated in a multitude of different contexts. We hope that our study will draw increased research and attention to the role that technology frames of reference play in IS projects, as well as inspire new research that examines how the structuring of these frames impact project outcomes in a variety of settings and configurations of stakeholder relationships.

## Appendix A. Interview guide

Questions targeted at eliciting views of the overall goals of HIE initiative:

1. Please provide me with your understanding of what the HIE initiative is and what are its overall goals and mission.

2. How do the goals of the HIE initiative relate to the overall goals and mission of your organization?

3. How do the goals of the HIE initiative relate to the overall goals and mission of other organizations/stakeholders involved in this endeavor?

4. How do the goals of the HIE initiative relate to your particular function within your organization? How do the goals of other organizations/stakeholders relate to your function?

5. What level of involvement did/do you have in setting the goals and objectives of the HIE initiative?

6. Do you have any general opinions or gut feelings about this initiative? What are your expectations of this project?

Questions designed to elicit thoughts on implementation process:

7. As you understand the initiative, how will its implementation affect your organization's work practices?

8. As you understand the initiative and the work practices of other organizations involved in this initiative, how will its imple mentation affect your particular job function's work practices/processes?

9. As you understand the initiative, how will its implementation affect your particular job function's work practices/processes?

10. What key concerns do you believe need to be addressed in order for the new system to work well for you?

11. What key concerns do you believe need to be addressed in order for the system to work well for other stakeholders involved in this initiative?

12. What level of involvement will you have in the implementation of the HIE?

13. How much confidence do you have for a smooth implementation of this initiative?

14. How would you describe, define and/or measure a successful implementation?

Questions designed to ascertain perspectives of the consequences of the HIE initiative

15. What in your opinion will be the long-term effect or consequence of a successful implementation for your organization, other stakeholders involved in the implementation and your job function? What about a failed initiative?

16. Describe in your own words what the end product will look like in an ideal or best-case scenario for this initiative. From an overall perspective, from your functional areas perspective and how you'd imagine others to perceive the end product.

17. Reality is rarely ideal. Are there any departures from the ideal scenario that you expect to occur? Any that you'd be willing to live with? Any deal breakers or items that would cause you to recommend pulling the plug on the project?
