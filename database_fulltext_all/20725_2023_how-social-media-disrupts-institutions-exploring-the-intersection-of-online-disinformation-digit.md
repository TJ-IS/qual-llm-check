# How social media disrupts institutions: Exploring the intersection of online disinformation, digital materiality and field-level change

## A R T I C L E I N F O

## A B S T R A C T

The diffusion of disinformation via social media has become a pressing societal concern for business leaders and policy makers. In recent years, online disinformation has been implicated as a source of field-level institutional change across a variety of societal contexts. To better un derstand how online disinformation changes institutional issue fields, we explore how digital materiality affords users opportunities to create and propagate disinformation. We introduce and define three social media material features: modular content. content flow. and manifold network structures. From these digital materiality elements, we articulate three disinformation affordan ces: crafting, amplifying, and partitioning. We rely on several vignettes – far-right political conspiracy group. OAnon, anti-vaccination (i.e., anti-Vaxxers). and flat Earth beliefs – to illustrate how social media users exploit digital materiality and enact disinformation affordances. Our theoretical development also contributes to our understanding of how online disinformation disrupts institutional issue fields. In particular, we highlight several potential changes to insti tutional issue fields regarding power centralization, subfield structures, and institutional infra structure. We conclude by offering recommendations for future research and social media policy.

## 1. Introduction

Is the Earth flat? Although the question may seem absurd, many people believe otherwise (Landrum & Olshansky, 2019). Today, “Flat Earthers" reach millions of people through YouTube, disseminating videos that emphatically support the notion that the surface of Earth is a two-dimensional plane. Similarly, numerous users on Facebook espouse dangers of vaccination (Olive, Hotez, Damania, & Nolan, 2018). “Anti-Vaxxers,” rejecting medical evidence, proclaim that vaccinations contribute to negative health outcomes and increase the rate of disease transmission (Smith & Graham, 2019; Swire-Thompson & Lazer, 2020). Additionally, government actors and political activists routinely manipulate images and videos on social media platforms to cause their messages to go viral, enhancing their political fortunes (Bradshaw &. Howard. 2018). These examples illustrate that the spread of online disinformation – intentionally deceptive information meant to mislead others (Bennett & Livingston, 2018; Del Vicario et al., 2016; Jerit & Zhao, 2020; Swire-Thompson & Lazer, 2020) – has become pervasive and “a societal issue of growing concern” (Cook, Ellerton, & Kinkead, 2018: 1). Recognizing the potential implications of disinformation for organizations and organizing activities, scholars of information systems and organizations argue that false information poses a grand challenge that impedes the generation of economic and social value

(Gardberg et al., 2021; Lyytinen & Grover, 2017).

Extant scholarship recognizes numerous harmful effects of online disinformation on societal institutions (Allcott, Gentzkow, & Yu, 2019; Del Vicario et al., 2016; McKay & Tenove, 2021), especially how disinformation might undermine institutional legitimacy (Bennett & Livingston, 2018; Vaidhyanathan, 2018). That digital technology mediates the societal effects of disinformation poses new questions for institutional theory. Such forms of institutional change reveal that many central institutional constructs are outdated in a society where much interaction occurs in a virtual space (Powell, Oberg, Korff, Oelberger, & Kloos, 2017). Emergent relationships between human actors and technological artifacts requires us to revisit prior theorizing (Leonardi, 2010; Leonardi & Vaast, 2017), as technology makes new institutional processes possible (Faik, Barrett, & Oborn, 2020; Powell et al., 2017). From this perspective the materiality of technology matters in regard to understanding contemporary institutional change (Friedland & Arjali\`es, 2021). In fact, a burgeoning body of work has begun to examine how institutions and fields change as a result of technology-mediated interactions (Alaimo, 2022; Dahabiyeh & Constantinides, 2022; Faik et al., 2020; Ratner & Plotnikof, 2022) It is at this juncture between empirica studies of disinformation and a focus on how materiality influences field-level institutional change that motivates our research focus, where we ask: How do actors use social media to create and disseminate disinformation in order to change institutions?

Individuals create and circulate online disinformation to disrupt institutions (Kapantai, Christopoulou, Berberidis, & Peristeras, 2021), for example, to diminish a political rival (Bradshaw & Howard, 2018) or to cast doubt on medical expertise (Dib, Mayaud, Chauvin, & Launay, 2021). From this perspective, disinformation erodes perceptions of institutional legitimacy (Bennett & Livingston, 2018; Oliver, 1992) and also changes the characteristics of institutional fields (Buchanan, Zietsma, & Matten, 2022) by disrupting the routine, everyday interactions within institutional issue fields. We investigate field disruption through the lens of digital materiality (Friedland & Arjali\`es, 2021; Lˆe, 2015) and social media affordances (Leonardi & Vaast, 2017). Numerous material features on socia media platforms facilitate user connections and engagement (Boyd, 2007; Leonardi, 2010). Individual users might make use of a particular feature in a variety of ways due to their intentions (Gibson, 1977). Hence, digital materiality comprises a crucial element in how people use technology to accomplish a particular goal (Fayard & Weeks, 2014; Leonardi & Vaast, 2017). We specifically focus on how digital materiality affords users opportunities to fashion novel, deceptive content and to circulate that content across fast changing, complex social networks.

Adopting an institutional perspective to investigate online disinformation contributes to information systems scholarship and institutional theory in several ways. We gain a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of technology-mediated institutional change (Faik et al., 2020: Powell et al., 2017) and introduce a theoretical model that explains how online disinformation disrupts institutions. Our paper illustrates how the micro-level interactions between human users and technological artifacts facilitates fieldlevel institutional change. In particular, we delineate three material aspects of social media that enable actors to propagate disin formation – modular content, content flow, and manifold network structure. By articulating how digital materiality affords users specific actions, we connect the affordances provided to users to field-level outcomes. Specifically, we contribute to extant theory by articulating the effects of online disinformation on the power centralization, subfield structures, and institutional infrastructure on issue fields. Our theorizing also demonstrates how interventions that alter the material features of technological artifacts might in fluence institutional change. For instance, we are especially interested in how manipulating the features of social media platforms may be useful in reducing the circulation of disinformation. Some scholars suggest that redesigning algorithms that seek to optimize engagement to focus on chronological ranking of content (Gillespie, 2014) or substituting targeted advertisements with friend rec ommendations for products (Nelimarkka, Rancy, Grygiel, & Semaan, 2019) will curb online disinformation. We add to this growing call to redesign social media platforms to dampen the circulation of disinformation in digital discourse.

Our paper proceeds as follows. First, we define disinformation and offer a brief overview of disinformation research, underscoring the need to develop an institutional approach to better understand the societal effects of disinformation. Next, we review the insti tutional change literature with a particular focus on digital materiality. We then introduce three aspects of digital materiality that pertain to disinformation on social media. Following our discussion of digital materiality germane to disinformation, we introduce and specify three disinformation affordances. We then provide three illustrative vignettes of disinformation contexts to illustrate digita materiality and disinformation affordances. Following our vignettes, we offer a theoretical framework that highlights disinformation may cause field-level change and disrupt institutions. We conclude by offering several promising avenues of future inquiry and als discussing policy implications of our research.

## 2. Disinformation and social media

Disinformation – the “purposeful distribution of fake, misleading, fabricated, or manipulated content” – increasingly pervades organizational and social life (Bradshaw & Howard, 2018: 1). Various terms often denote the disinformation phenomenon, including fake news, digital misinformation, propaganda, rumors, and conspiracy theories among others (Kapantai et al., 2021: Lazer et al., 2018). Despite the, at times, imprecise colloquial use of disinformation and its associated concepts (Kapantai et al., 2021), we refer to disinformation as intentional efforts to mislead others and to disrupt the routine functioning of institutions through the sharing of manipulated online content. Intent distinguishes disinformation from misinformation (Guess & Lyons, 2020). Misinformation tran spires when “people hold incorrect factual beliefs and do so confidently" (Kuklinski, Ouirk, Jerit, Schwieder, & Rich, 2000: 792). For example, someone that believes a political falsehood to be true and shares that content on social media is propagating misinformation. Although we acknowledge the deleterious effects of misinformation, we limit our focus to disinformation in this paper due to its intentionality.

Disinformation is not a unique phenomenon to the twenty-first century, yet the prevalence of social media has led to disinformation quickly traversing global, decentralized digital networks (Benkler, Faris, & Roberts, 2018). Social media includes any computermediated technology that facilitates the creation of user-generated content and that occurs largely in public view (Aichner & Jacob 2015; Boyd, 2007). User interactions on social media differ from face-to-face communication in several ways, especially through the persistence, searchability, and replicability of content and that user interactions are visible to invisible audiences (Boyd, 2007). Social media allows user-generated content to reach a vast number of other users (Aichner & Jacob, 2015). For instance, a single social media account may broadcast a message that millions of other people interact with (Powell et al., 2017). Illustrating the potential reach of such messages, in 2021 over 2.85 billion users are active on Facebook, while Twitter counts 206 million daily monetizable users (statista.com, 2021). Individuals that employ the necessary capabilities to craft a resonant message or that understand how to leverage online search algorithms may be especially effective in disseminating disinformation (Alexander, 2021; Allcott et al., 2019; Ferreira, 2021; Xia et al., 2019). Thus, actors – individual or collective – frequently leverage social media platforms to disseminate disinfor mation (Benkler et al., 2018).

While intentionally false information represents a small portion of online information (Guess. Nyhan. & Reifler. 2020). the consequences of disinformation are numerous and include vaccine hesitancy (Dib et al., 2021), distrust in scientific knowledge (Ravenelle, Newell, & Kowalski, 2021), political illiteracy (Jerit & Zhao, 2020) or violent actions (Walsh, 2021). In particular, disinformation often “devalues and delegitimizes the message and voices of public institutions as well as experts” (Koulolias, Jonathan, Fernandez, & Sotirchos, 2018: 9). Following exposure to disinformation, individuals may lose trust in existing institutions (Kapantai et al., 2021) and become less likely to adhere to normative institutional pressures (DiMaggio & Powell. 1983). Citing the turmoil that often besieges institutions that are subject to disinformation, Bennett and Livingston (2018) claim that intentional manipulation of the information environment results in a pervasive disinformation disorder. Accordingly, the potential disruptive effects of online disinformation on societal institutions have led to calls of action by both policymakers and scholars (Cook et al., 2018; Gardberg et al., 2021; Lyytinen & Grover, 2017). To better understand the escalating presence and the societal consequences of online disinformation, we focus attention on institutional issue fields and the emergent research that connects the digital materiality of technological artifacts to the affordances that individuals enact to promote institutional change.

## 3. Institutional change and materiality

## 3.1. Change in issue fields

Issues refer to “events, developments, and trends that members of the industry recognize as having important consequences for the industry and which receive continued attention” (Hoffman & Ocasio, 2001: 427). Fields coalesce where actors “partake in a common meaning system and whose participants interact more frequently and fatefully with one another than with actors outside the field” (Scott, 1995: 56). Fields impart an “orienting principle or goal” among field members (Evans & Kay, 2008: 973), facilitating routine and predictable interactions across various social and economic activities (Wooten & Hoffman, 2008). While early studies of fields were principally concerned with economic exchange such as competition within industries (Zietsma, Groenewegen, Logue, & Hinings, 2017). Hoffman (1999: 351) contends that organizational fields tend to develop “around a central issue – such as the protection of the natural environment – rather than a central technology or market." Issue fields constitute arenas where heterogeneous actors contest existing institutional arrangements, meanings, and practices (Furnari, 2018; Zietsma et al., 2017). Zietsma et al. (2017) further articulate that issue fields form in response to a contested issue or competition over a central issue to influence an exchange field. Issue fields often emerge to address complex and vexing societal issues such as income inequality, climate change, and food deserts (Pothukuchi, 2009).

Institutional scholars distinguish among several characteristics of issue fields that might influence the dynamics of institutiona change (Buchanan et al., 2022; Zietsma et al., 2017). First, issue fields vary by power centralization. Power may be concentrated among a few actors or power may be diffused throughout the field (Greenwood, Raynard, Kodeih, Micelotta, & Lounsbury, 2011). A high degree of power centralization often indicates that a central authority maintains institutional structures and makes high-level decisions regarding interaction, reducing the complexity that field participants experience (Greenwood, Díaz, Li, & Lorente, 2010). Second, issue fields may also possess a variety of subfields that multiple different types of actors – with divergent interests – populate (Faulconbridge & Muzio. 2021). such as a unified field with no subfields. a bifurcated subfield structure or a fragmented subfield structure characterized by more than two subfields (Buchanan et al., 2022). The presence of subfields also likely increases the complexity of the issue field by introducing multiple logics that field participants must negotiate (Goodrick & Reay, 2011) Third, Hinings et al. (2017: 172) define institutiongl infrastructure as the “governance and field-coordinating structures" that facilitate routine interactions within the field. Institutional infrastructure includes “collective interest organizations, regulators, informal governance bodies, field configuring events, status differentiators, organizational templates, categories or labels, and norms” (Hinings et al., 2017: 168). Issue fields where infrastructure is highly elaborated possesses a high degree of institutionalization (Maguire & Hardy, 2009; Zietsma et al., 2017) Institutionalization denotes how taken-for-granted practices within the issue field are embraced by participants (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). For instance. Furnari (2018) notes how unsettled issues may emerge periodically for debate within a field. Thus, practices within issue fields may undergo deinstitutionalization as actors successfully problematize established practices (Hardy & Maguire, 2010) such as when social division occurs, or values change (Oliver, 1992).

Issue fields change when field members contest meanings and practices (Hoffman, 1999; Zietsma & Lawrence, 2010). Field-leve change occurs as actors alter or disrupt “sociocultural constructions that prescribe appropriate organizational behaviors and that shape and enforce patterns of interests and privilege” (Micelotta, Lounsbury, & Greenwood, 2017: 2). Recent studies suggest that information and digital technologies have become a central source for contemporary institutional change (Alaimo, 2022; Dahabiyeh & Con stantinides, 2022; Faik et al., 2020; Ratner & Plotnikof, 2022). Recent scholarship recognizes that digital objects mediate institutiona processes (Ratner & Plotnikof, 2022), and goes beyond only considering technological artifacts as discrete objects that individuals use to assist a particular action, thereby requiring new theorizing (Powell et al., 2017). One theme of these studies investigates how digita technology scales from individual users to influence organizational fields (Alaimo, 2022; Dahabiyeh & Constantinides, 2022) and society (Faik et al., 2020). Digital technology affords actors new ways of interacting with others and with artifacts (Ratner & Plotnikof, 2022). Despite significant progress, a consideration of how technological artifacts influence institutional change remains underex plored (Friedland & Arjali\`es, 2021; Powell et al., 2017). Powell et al. (2017) call on further research to update currently anachronistic theoretical concepts within the institutional literature that predate our digital age. Alaimo (2022) contends that a field-level perspective is necessary to understand some material practices within fields. Finally, Faik et al. (2020) note a lack of theorizing, connecting the features and affordances of information technology, especially disruptive technology, to institutional and societal change. Extant scholarship makes clear that more work remains necessary to better understand the role of information and digital technology in fieldlevel change.

## 3.2. Digital materiality, affordances, and social media

The recent emphasis on technology and issue fields underscores that institutional change is often inseparably connected to the material features of the technological artifacts that human actors use (Faik et al., 2020; Friedland & Arjali\`es, 2021). Yet, institutiona theories often fail to consider materiality as individuals engage in efforts to alter institutions (Alaimo, 2022; Faik et al., 2020; Lˆe, 2015). Responding to the lack of materiality, some scholars have sought to foreground the role of material objects in institutiona practices (Friedland & Arjali\`es, 2021; Lawrence, Leca, & Zilber, 2013; Lˆe, 2015; Raviola & Norback, ¨ 2013). For instance, actors might use technological artifacts as boundary objects, such as selectively using organizational assessment tools to diminish support for practices and allow individuals to redefine their work identity (Sajtos, Kleinaltenkamp, & Harrison, 2018). Similarly, Lˆe (2015) il lustrates how Twitter allows journalists to enact various identities when reporting on the news, by allowing rapid access to share breaking stories. Thorseng and Grisot (2017) reveal how healthcare providers’ use of digital E-health tools can change their per spectives, viewing their diabetic patients as more active, learning, and reflective in their care and contributing to desirable institutional change.

To further emphasize materiality and the material practices that underlie institutional change. scrutiny necessarily turns towards the technological artifacts that make action possible. Materiality encompasses multiple aspects of a given artifact (Orlikowski, 2007). Yet, materiality does not always refer to a tangible object, but instead may be “stuff’, such as data or digital processes (Leonardi, 2010) Humans instantiate digital processes in the physical world where they experience and interact with them (de Vaujany, Adrot, Box enbaum, & Leca, 2019; Leonardi, 2010). Digital materiality emerges from the intersection of physical artifact(s), user interaction with the artifact, and the practices those artifacts mobilize (Casemajor, 2015; Dever & Morra, 2014; Leonardi, 2010; Leonardi, Nardi, & Kallinikos, 2012). Patterns of circulation among technological artifacts (Beer & Burrows, 2013) carry meanings and allow for novelty and renewal (Swalwell, 2007). These patterns afford users on social media platforms opportunities to create and share text, video, audio, and images in a variety of ways, while also instantiating various material properties (Leonardi & Vaast, 2017; Manovich, 2005). Human interaction with content on social media platforms results from a combination of what they and others create, transcending any single actor.

Through interaction, technological artifacts and their material features make certain actions possible (Leonardi & Barley. 2008: Scott & Orlikowski, 2014). Such action potential, or affordances, arise as people make sense of material properties of an artifact and its potential uses for a particular goal (Gibson, 1977; Warren, 1984). Two people may perceive different affordances from the same configuration of physical features. Consider that digital artifacts often consist of both physical (e.g., display screen, keyboard) and digital features (e.g., programming codes, algorithms) that a human designer combines in a relatively stable form (Leonardi et al., 2012); these features make a variety of human actions possible (Leonardi & Vaast, 2017; Vaast, Safadi, Lapointe, & Negoita, 2017). For example, a person cannot use a tablet computer to paint a masterful Fresco painting on intonaco with various colorful pigments, but that person can use software on the tablet computer to create a masterful digital painting or use the tablet computer to sketch a draft of a future painting. Affordances arise in the moment of action at the intersection of actor-artifact-context (Fayard & Weeks, 2014). Thus, “materiality matters for theories of technology and organizing because the material properties of artifacts are precisely those tangible resources that provide people with the ability to do old things in new ways and to do things they could not do before” (Leonardi & Barley, 2008: 161).

Numerous studies document the novel actions that digital technology such as social media affords users (Leonardi &. Vaast. 2017) Web 2.0 enables users to edit content (Riemer & Richter, 2010), allowing them to curate unique social identities (Pan, Lu, Wang, & Chau, 2017). While social media affordances offer users flexible ways to interact with others, social media affordances can also gain scale and stability, increasing their possible macro-level effects (Cinelli, De Francisci Morales, Galeazzi, Quattrociocchi, & Starnini, 2021; Faik et al., 2020). In fact, the concept of connective affordances captures how social media increases interdependence among users that engage in collective action (Vaast et al. 2017). Thus, digital materiality makes possible new affordances that allow users to form highly interactive, interdependent online communities that can amplify collective effort. From this perspective, institutiona change cannot be understood separate from technological artifacts and the material practices that result from human interaction with an artifact’s features (Lˆe, 2015).

Expanding the view that disinformation facilitates field-level institutional change, especially towards disruption and disorder (Bennett & Livingston, 2018), we explore the digital materiality of the technological artifacts that routinely mediate human activity. In particular, we investigate how the distributed and emergent materiality of social media enables the spread of disinformation and ultimately contributes to field-level institutional change.

## 4. Digital materiality

Social media affords users numerous opportunities to interact with others. Yet, many material features of social media platforms do not originate just from a designer’s code that allows a user to write text or share images, but rather emerges from the interconnections among users (Leonardi, 2010; Scott & Orlikowski, 2012). Our focus on digital materiality emphasizes that 1.) users create mutable content on social media, 2.) content on social media flows, circulates, or moves across virtual networks, and 3.) the circulation of content results in new digital social structures. These three aspects constitute the building blocks of social media and recognize, as other have, that online users form an “ecosystem of stakeholders” (Kapoor et al., 2018: 554). Therefore, we view these three aspects of digital materiality as comprising the central features that makes social media valuable to users and distinguishes it from other digita technologies. From this perspective, we advance extant theorizing and offer three specific digital materiality concepts: modular content, content flow, and manifold network structure. We contend that incorporating these elements of digital materiality – and the affordances that follow from their engagement (Leonardi & Vaast, 2017) – allows us to explore how actors leverage these features to effect fieldlevel institutional change. We introduce and discuss each term below, before integrating them within our theoretical framework.

## 4.1. Modular content

Modular content refers to the divisible nature of information on social media. Through the editing features available on socia media platforms, information can be broken apart and reconfigured in numerous combinations (Manovich, 2005; Treem & Leonardi, 2013). Various terms capture the modularity of online content, including collaborative remixability (Manovich, 2005), copy and paste literacy (Perkel. 2010). and editability (Treem & Leonardi. 2013). From this perspective, online content exists as relatively small and readily consumable packets of meaning. Yet, there are limitations to the mutability of social media content. For example, platforms often preserve online interactions in aeternum (Treem & Leonardi, 2013). This property of social media has been referred to by its persistence (Treem & Leonardi, 2013), recordability (Hancock, Toma, & Ellison, 2007), and permanence (Whittaker, 2003). While users are often able to create new content, they are also able to trace the origins of content though search features, ensuring that content remains somewhat fixed to its original context (Murthy, 2012).<sup>1</sup> In this manner, users continually create and remake virtua spaces (Murthy, 2012) that they experience as somewhat stable (Leonardi, 2010; Scott & Orlikowski, 2012). Hence, modularity – as a characteristic of the digital data infrastructure of social media – exerts both “organizing” (e.g., content possess a degree of permanence and allows for the formation of durable communities) and “disorganizing” (e.g., novel content may contribute to new meanings and new interactional patterns) effects (Ratner & Plotnikof, 2022).

Various social media features contribute to modularity. First, platforms often restrict interaction among users in some manner. For instance, a message may be limited to a maximum 280 characters (e.g., Twitter) or videos must be between 15 s to 3 min in length (e.g., TikTok). Second, users may associate short hashtag labels to posts as a means to contextualize their content and attract interest from other users. Finally, features that support editability, such as platform policies that grant users permission to revise one’s own content allows users the ability to readily break apart content and recombine the pieces to construct new meanings (Treem & Leonardi, 2013). Social media platforms often possess built-in editing capabilities for text-entries, images, and video files. For example, Instagram hosts numerous filters to augment images and videos on the platform by altering the color saturation and contrast of visual data to commonly enhance its appeal. However, as previously discussed, modularity denotes a degree of stability and permanence that is critical to the functioning of a social media platform. Taken together, constraints on user-generated content, the permanence of content, and the variety of replicability commands contribute to the modularity of content on social media.

## 4.2. Content flow

Constant movement often characterizes social media networks (Leonardi & Vaast, 2017). As users interact on social media plat forms content flows or moves among a vast number of users. For example, a typical Facebook thread consists of an original post with comments, likes, and shares and also notifications to users that have previously engaged with the content thread. An average user of Facebook engages with 18 posts per month (Kemp, 2021); each post results in content flowing to other users. A user may tag another user in their post to direct the flow of content. Similarly, WhatsApp allows users to forward content a certain number of times to other users (Hern, 2020). As users engage with online content various types of interactions transpire among users, content, and webpages, resulting in emergent information streams (Boyd, 2010). Boyd (2010) contends that “individuals are living inside the stream: adding to it, consuming it, [and] redirecting it” (pg. 26). Engagement-driven algorithms that seek to optimize user interaction also contribute to content flow by making some posts more visible than others to users, suggesting content that users are likely to interact with based on that content’s previous share of clicks, likes, and comments (Vaidhyanathan, 2018). For instance, a friend’s post may become visible on another user’s newsfeed or timeline in an effort to invite greater levels of user engagement. In this way, user actions that occur through engagement-driven algorithms ensure that content circulates on social media. Due to these considerations, we refer to content flow as the circulation and movement of digital content among users on social media platforms

We especially seek to highlight the experiential aspect of content flow; that is some digital spaces appear static and unchanging, while others appear so dynamic that users’ express difficulty making sense of the new information (Pentina & Tarafdar, 2014). How an individual user perceives and experiences content flow may vary, depending on a particular platform’s material features or the size and interactional patterns of the online community on a platform of which the individual user is a participant. Certain platform features, such as receiving notifications about posts or reactions from others in your social network, likely contributes to how a user experiences content flow. Users might experience flow as the speed at which content moves, whether content garners more positive or negative reactions, the growth of online communities, or the novelty of content that they typically encounter. Content flow on social media platforms is also visible to users, through features that make salient various characteristics of movement, such as notifications. Severa factors may increase or decrease content flow. The generation of user-content or republication methods, such as reposting or sharing content, increases content flow. For instance, a controversial political post may induce users to express their views – whether sup portive or hostile (Rathje, Van Bavel, & van der Linden, 2021). In contrast, content flow might decrease as platforms limit the number of times a user can forward content (Hern, 2020) or as a social media platform removes posts or suspends user accounts that violate the platform’s terms of use (Dwoskin & Timberg, 2021), although content may persist through screenshots for other users to share (Boyd, 2007). We expect that most users will perceive content flow to increase at varying rates.

## 4.3. Manifold network structure

Network theorizing typically portrays a single network in two-dimensional space, highlighting connections among actors and possibly illustrating how those connections change over time (e.g., Lawrence, Hardy, & Phillips, 2002; Powell, White, Koput, & Owen-Smith, 2005). However, such theorizing often ignores the complex interconnections among digital content. Social media introduces potentially limitless cross-connections among issue fields; connections that even cross different social media platforms. To account for this likelihood, we contend that interaction among actors in a digital milieu occurs within manifold network structures. Manifolds consist of multiple intersecting points which denote their dimensionality (Freedman & Quinn. 1990). Dimensionality increases as manifolds contain more intersections. We view manifold networks as an extension of Padgett and Powell (2012) concept of trans position that refers to the cross-connection among two or more institutional spheres. Yet, manifold network structures encompass the complex, limitless potential that human actors possess to interact with issue fields across multiple social media platforms. Hence, viewing networks by their connections to other networks – their dimensionality – holds potential to illustrate a variety of digita activity and to reflect how content circulates online.

We draw on the concept of manifolds to refer to online social networks that intersect two or more issue fields and that may also intersect two or more social media platforms. Networks within issue fields host a variety of activity that surrounds a focal topic (Young & Leonardi, 2012). Intersections across issue fields occur as online users engage with digital content through tagged posts, replies, and shares. Several engagement-driven features of social platforms often promote the formation of cross-field ties. For example, a user on one social media platform may post content on several different pages or content threads about numerous topics and do so with ease given platform features that allow for simultaneous posts. In addition, network members can tag others in posts thereby alerting other users of content additions. Moreover, our hypothetical actor may also create content on another social media platform or change their network connections by interacting with entirely new users and content. The number of issues that an actor may choose to interact with online may vastly outnumber those of face-to-face interactions (Treem & Leonardi, 2013). Networks that form online around an issue often contain more intersections and a greater degree of complexity than other types of social networks (Graham, 2010). Social media platforms host as many issue fields as people might imagine, suggesting the importance of viewing interactions as occurring within manifold network structures.

## 5. Disinformation affordances

Together, the preceding material features – modular content, content flow, and manifold networks structures – afford users a variety of actions on social media platforms, including to circulate disinformation. Due to continuous interaction across social media platforms, modular content, content flow, and manifold network structures transcend any single user’s control and become crucia features that afford users many possible actions (Gibson, 1977; Leonardi & Vaast, 2017). We identify three disinformation affordances that result from the combination of social media’s material features and an actor’s intentions to propagate disinformation – crafting, amplifying, and partitioning. While scholars identify other social media affordances that may help to circulate factual information or content (Leonardi & Vaast, 2017; Treem & Leonardi, 2013), we contend that the following affordances capture important aspects of how individuals create disinformation content and disseminate disinformation to online audiences. We limit our focus to disinfor mation, which is the purposeful creation and distribution of false information (Guess & Lyons, 2020). Thus, the intention of the user is important when considering how a user might leverage digital materiality to enact affordances. We introduce and elaborate each disinformation affordance below.

## 5.1. Crafting

Crafting refers to how actors use modular content to craft new meanings from bits of information. As bricoleurs (Christiansen & Lounsbury. 2013), actors often skillfully break apart existing meanings and recombine or retrofit the pieces for novel purposes (Padgett & Powell, 2012). From this perspective, Bloom and Moskalenko (2021) illustrate how disinformation agents filter through user posts within online disinformation communities and select posts or content that might be particularly resonant among individuals within the community to use in subsequent disinformation content. The chosen content is then woven into new disinformation posts, often to construct conspiratorial narratives. In essence, actors that seek to disseminate disinformation break apart user-generated content, identifying certain words or phrases, and retrofit them to assemble new meanings. In this manner, crafting also relies on content flow to create user feedback loops, identifying and incorporating content that is most capable of gaining attention and “going viral” (Partin & Marwick, 2020). Actors might experiment with various versions of a similar disinformation message to observe how audiences react and revise their framing of a particular issue in real-time. Crafting closely corresponds to Treem and Leonardi (2013) editability affordance where individuals might “spend a good deal of time and effort crafting and recrafting a communicative act before it is viewed by others” (pg. 159). Yet, we also note that due to the permanence (Murthy, 2012) and the persistence of online content (Treem & Leonardi, 2013) crafting involves both recent content and also past content. In this manner, persistence may be especially important for disinformation that invokes historical or nostalgic sociocultural images and phrases (Bennett & Livingston, 2018).

## 5.2. Amplifying

Amplifying refers to how digital materiality affords opportunities to increase the size of a potential audience for disinformation content. Although disinformation agents may rely on inauthentic accounts to astroturf' virtual spaces (Ortutay. 2020: Xia et al., 2019). several other actions that make use of the content flow aspect of social media also increase the reach of disinformation. For instance, Partin and Marwick (2020) highlight that when users within an online community help create disinformation, other users are more likely to share that disinformation content. In particular, co-creation of disinformation – between disinformation agents and members of online communities – forges close-knit ties among community members and fosters strong emotional attachments. Disinformation that activates salient social distinctions (e.g., political affiliation, vaccine attitudes) increases user engagement (Lazer et al., 2018). Disinformation that elicits emotional responses, especially negative affect, such as anger, fear, or suspicion contributes positively to an individual user’s decision to share disinformation on social media (Marwick & Lewis, 2017). In this manner, disinformation agents use emotional language as clickbait to induce greater degrees of audience engagement and amplify their reach (Paolillo, 2018). These tactics demonstrate how purveyors of disinformation cultivate a critical mass of users to engage with disinformation and share disinformation content. Actors may direct other users – directly or indirectly – to post content in a coordinated effort to drown out dissidents and shape online discourse. By influencing user perceptions of content flow (i.e., manipulating the visibility of likes and shares, or crafting new content), disinformation agents may make a claim or grievance appear important or legitimate. Illia, Colleoni, Etter, and Meggiorin (2022) illustrate how the volume of posts on social media and refracted attention create tipping points that undermine the legitimacy of actors; while their study examines user-generated content, our model suggests that a disinformation agent could coopt this process, amplifying disinformation content.

## 5.3. Partitioning

Partitioning refers to actions that facilitate the creation of disinformation communities on social media platforms. Disinformation agents may exploit the manifold network structure of social media as they either magnify or shrink the differences among users, activating in-group and out-group dynamics (Tajfel & Turner, 1979). For instance, online disinformation content frequently activates ‘faultlines' – a distinguishing characteristic (e,g., ethnicity, political party affiliation, beliefs) among members of a social group (Lau & Murnighan, 1998). Faultlines in the context of disinformation can form around beliefs and attitudes, but can also intersect with race, ethnicity, and gender (Riedl, Joseff, Soorholtz, & Woolley, 2022). In this manner, disinformation content often appeals to selfperceptions of social identity (Jensen, 2018). Connections to different issue fields on a platform or across platforms allows pur veyors of disinformation to manipulate which aspects of an individual’s social identity become salient by linking content across online communities, such as through the placement of hashtags. For example, if a user scrolls through a Twitter thread about the latest achievement by NASA and views a tweet that includes the hashtag #nasalies, clicking on that hashtag will likely take that user to conspiracy content about the 1969 moon landing or flat Earth theory. For individuals that already distrust NASA or other scientific authorities, this content likely reinforces the individual’s social identity. By exploiting or creating new linkages among issue fields (e. g., aerospace science and flat Earth beliefs) through hashtags, disinformation agents may increase user exposure to disinformation content (Soares & Recuero, 2021) and activate social identities (Partin & Marwick, 2020)

Following from our social identity perspective, the concept of resonance is crucial to understand how disinformation agents partition audiences (Bradshaw & Howard, 2018). Resonance refers to the ability to “capture or activate powerful distinctions along social, ethnic, religious, economic, political, and cultural lines” (Baron, 2004: 11). Giorgi (2017) identifies two types of resonance: cognitive and emotional. Cognitive resonance captures the perceived alignment of a message or frame with “understandings or beliefs that are central or salient to a particular audience” (Giorgi, 2017: 717). For example, disinformation agents cultivating a vibrant community of participants in the creation of new disinformation content (Partin & Marwick, 2020), illustrates the cognitive dimension of resonance. When users co-create elaborate conspiratorial realities with others, they create detailed alternative accounts of their lives and world events. On the other hand, emotional resonance refers to the felt alignment between an audiences’ affect and frame (Giorgi, 2017). Disinformation often evokes strong emotional responses (Barfar, 2019; Zaeem, Li, & Barber, 2020) when social faultlines become salient (Martel, Pennycook, & Rand, 2020), such as anger towards those that belong to an out-group (Miller, 2021). Out-group bias manifests as members of disinformation communities denigrate others that hold divergent beliefs, referring to them as being asleep, sheeple or evil (Rathje et al., 2021). In fact, outrage often motivates individuals to further engage with online disinformation communities (Bloom & Moskalenko, 2021). Thus, interaction with disinformation offers users an occasion to enact a social identity guided by disinformation agents. In this manner, partitioning captures how disinformation agents magnify the differences between field participants to activate group dynamics (e.g., Bloom & Moskalenko, 2021).

We contend the affordances that we outline contribute to the dissemination of disinformation across social media networks. In dividuals that create and spread disinformation make use of the material features – modular content, content flow, and manifold network structures – to craft new disinformation content and amplify disinformation. Disinformation agents also partition online communities, ensuring that some social media users will accept and share nascent disinformation content. Finally, we also suggest that the link between digital materiality and affordances results in macro-level societal changes. Institutions may change as disinformation circulates (Bennett & Livingston, 2018). In the next section, we use three example cases to illustrate how each aspect of digital ma teriality – modular content, content flow, manifold networks – affords the enactment of each disinformation affordance. We also highlight the societal and institutional consequences of disinformation in each case.

## 6.1. QAnon disinformation

On October 28, 2017 an anonymous user(s) – Q Clearance Patriot or Q – on 4chan posted the thread “Calm Before the Storm” foretelling the impending arrest of former presidential candidate Hillary Clinton and other political elites for child sex trafficking. Subsequent threads consisted of cryptic messages and the purported activities of law enforcement as they sought to indict and arrest various Democratic politicians and politically left-leaning celebrities and business leaders. For the next three years posts by the titular Q account would ambiguously weave together then-President Donald Trump’s remarks (“Calm before the storm” was a phrase President Donald Trump used to refer to a meeting of military officials earlier in 2017), prophetic statements, and politically con servative rhetoric into enigmatic posts on several social media platforms. Followers of QAnon (i.e., Anons) took to these online forums to parse meaning and predict the day that “The Storm” would occur when then-President Trump would expose widespread crimina activity and lead law enforcement agencies to arrest the global cabal of Satanic child sex traffickers.

QAnon represents one of the most prevalent and far-reaching disinformation campaigns of 2020, seeping into the lives of millions of Americans and contributing to a violent insurrection at the United States Capitol on January 6, 2021 (Bloom & Moskalenko, 2021). QAnon disinformation thrusts individuals into a continually evolving narrative that depicts political opponents as grandiose villains that commit horrific crimes and are to be defeated by any means (Conner & MacMurray, 2022). The generation and propagation of QAnon disinformation occurs through interdependent contributions among individual users and disinformation agent(s) on socia media platform 4chan before transitioning to 8kun (Partin & Marwick, 2020). Q enacts the crafting disinformation affordance through a complex, recursive interplay among their account and members of the online community that adheres to QAnon beliefs. In particular the titular Q often interlaces together pieces of content (e.g., phrases, sentences, and concepts) taken from multiple posts by anony mous users within the 4chan/pol subforum (Zaeem et al., 2020) to craft new messages – a process known as baking (Bloom & Mos kalenko, 2021). Q’s posts or Q drops frequently consists of cryptic messages, seemingly random references to people, places, or objects, and incoherent sentences (Papasavva et al., 2022). Anonymous users then attempt to decipher the Q drops, posting their interpretations for other members of the subforum to view (Partin & Marwick, 2020). Finally, Q frequently selects content or concepts from com munity posts that receives a great deal of attention or is interesting and uses that content to craft future Q drops (Marwick & Partin, 2022; Partin & Marwick, 2020). Hence, the Q account relies on user-content feedback loops to amplify content. In this manner, QAnon disinformation evolves simultaneously alongside the worldviews and expectations of the disinformation community, sustaining the group identification among individual users.

The titular Q account enacts the partitioning disinformation affordance by framing political adversaries – actors belonging to the political center and left – in both negative and apocalyptic terms (Bloom & Moskalenko, 2021). For instance, QAnon disinformation often claims Hillary Clinton and other Democratic politicians “steal the essence of children as a sort of youth elixir” (Conner & MacMurray, 2022: 1051). In addition to making faultlines surrounding political affiliation salient, QAnon disinformation also activates religious, ethnic, and racial faultlines. Specifically, QAnon disinformation incorporates antisemitic beliefs by targeting Jewish poli ticians and political donors and implicating them in conspiracies of world domination (Riedl et al., 2022). The Storm invokes Judeo-Christian apocalyptic rhetoric. casting the actions of O and their followers as a form of righteous warfare against an evil global cabal (Conner & MacMurray. 2022). Thus. Anons do not attempt to change the issue field of political democracy by advocating for certain policies or legislation, instead QAnon disinformation denigrates individual politicians. By dehumanizing political adversaries through unfounded accusations of involvement with Satanic cults and pedophilia, QAnon disinformation encourages users to engage in nonnormative political tactics and advocates illegal methods of taking power away from political adversaries (Moskalenko, Burton, Fern´andez-Garayzabal ´ Gonzalez, ´ & Bloom, 2023).

In addition to focusing on political adversaries, QAnon content also relies on manifold network structures that spans both issue fields and social media platforms to amplify disinformation (Papasavva et al., 2022). QAnon disinformation often incorporates apolitical people, objects, and events to exploit engagement-driven algorithms (Forberg, 2022). For instance, in their effort to uncover an imagined global child trafficking cabal. OAnon supporters coopted the hashtag #savethechildren. Members of legitimate children's aid organizations, such as Save the Children, complained that the trending hashtag hurt their organization’s legitimate efforts of administering humanitarian aid and addressing issues stemming from childhood poverty (Roose. 2020). Online searches that were contaminated by OAnon content led to confusion for those that sought out information or wanted to make monetary donations to the non-profit, Save the Children. In this manner, QAnon disinformation spilled over from the political democracy issue field and under mined efforts of humanitarian aid. In a different context, when the Ever-Given cargo ship ran aground in the Suez Canal in 2021, QAnon adherents falsely claimed that the cargo ship was carrving victims of human trafficking, obscuring the true nature of the shipping accident (Gilbert, 2021). These instances illustrate how QAnon disinformation interjects false content into the broader online infor mation environment. frequently resulting in online search activity that is rife with disinformation and results in confusion about real events.

OAnon disinformation primarily targets the issue field of political democracy within the United States of America (Bloom & Moskalenko, 2021; Moskalenko et al., 2023; Partin & Marwick, 2020). The political democracy issue field consists of local, state, and federal politicians, political consultants, political action committees (i.e., PACs), think tanks, donors and activists. The societal con sequences of QAnon disinformation are far-reaching. Individual users experience the disruption of the political democracy issue field through the erosion of democratic governance beliefs and norms. While Garry, Walther, Rukaya, and Mohammed (2021) note that conspiratorial beliefs take hold in society when citizens already distrust government and dominant institutions, QAnon disinformation has exacerbated such mistrust. Shanahan (2021) documents that as many as 14% of U.S. adults may have held some OAnon beliefs during the peak of its online circulation. Other polling suggests that over one third of U.S. adults believe that elites in Hollywood, the media, and politicians are possibly engaged in child trafficking and abuse (Kight, 2020). These survey results illustrate how disin formation erodes perceptions of institutional legitimacy among individuals. Further, QAnon online disinformation led to nonnor mative political action. The January 6th insurrection at the U.S. capital represents, in part, the culmination of QAnon disinformation and its most visible effect on political democracy. The purpose of the actions on January 6th by members of political far-right groups were to disrupt the certification of Joe Biden's electoral victory (Bloom & Moskalenko. 2021). The events of January 6th. alongside other violent acts (e.g., the plot to kidnap Michigan governor, Gretchen Whitmer) illustrates how QAnon supporters justify the seizure of power through illegitimate means (Conner & MacMurray, 2022; Bloom and Moskalenko, 2021). In this manner, QAnon disinfor mation denigrates individual politicians and undermines their legitimacy thereby disrupting the existing issue field. Moreover, power within the issue field has shifted as political candidates with ties to QAnon disinformation have run for local and federal offices in the United States and in some cases won their respective races, elevating QAnon content in public discourse (Tull-McManus, 2020).

## 6.2. Anti-Vaxx disinformation

“Yes, The Vaccine Changes Your DNA. A Tiny Bit. That’s a Good Thing.” Is the title that a professor of biomedical engineering at Johns Hopkins University chose for an article that Forbes published on November 29, 2021. The article describes how mRNA vaccines offer protection against COVID-19 infection and severe disease. Yet, users across various social media platforms shared the article, particularly the title, as evidence to support the conspiratorial claim that COVID-19 vaccines alter human DNA in harmful and un predictable ways. Hours later the author changed the title of the article to “COVID Vaccines Don’t Alter Your DNA – They Help Choose Cells to Strengthen Your Immune Response.” Some users of social media pointed to the change as evidence for something more sinister. One purvevor of disinformation wrote on Instagram following the title change that “Mainstream media almost told the truth for once.” Disinformation and misinformation have surrounded COVID-19 vaccines since they have become available to the general public Conspiratorial beliefs include: that the government uses vaccination to subdue the populace, that vaccination causes infertility, that vaccination dangerously alters DNA, or that vaccines insert a microchip into the body. Social media posts appear daily where users continuously add to this frightening list of vaccine hazards.

The preceding vignette illustrates how social media users contort truthful information from authoritative actors (see healthfeedback,org) to alter the issue field surrounding vaccination. By enacting the crafting disinformation affordance, users on social media often weave together actual medical facts with alternative medical beliefs (e.g., new age medicine), and other conspiracies (e.g., 5G telecommunication towers cause COVID-19). During the COVID-19 pandemic, disinformation agents sought to sow doubt on the legitimacy of participants in the vaccine issue field by framing vaccines as dangerous (Grimes, 2021). These claims often contend that medical professionals know very little about viruses and disease, underestimate the risks of vaccination and therefore vaccines actually harm patients (Smith & Graham, 2019; Swire-Thompson & Lazer, 2020). As such, anti-vaccination disinformation content asserts that doctors do not know everything or even that doctors’ actions should be suspect as they routinely place profits above patient safety. Surprisingly, medical professionals also engage in anti-vaccine disinformation; for instance, one medical doctor told audiences during a livestream on social media to avoid vaccination and instead to schedule a \$90 telehealth appointment to receive access to alternative COVID-19 drugs (Brumfiel. 2021). Anti-vaccination disinformation content also frequently overlaps with other alternative medicine beliefs, such as the contention among supporters of New Age medicine that mineral crystals possess healing properties capable of curing COVID-19 (Kale, 2021). Disinformation agents also enact crafting by articulating connections between COVID-19 cases and geospatial maps of 5G telecommunication towers, contending that 5G towers cause COVID-19 and vaccinations are ineffective or used for an ulterior sinister purpose (Flaherty, Sturm, & Farries, 2022). Taken together, each of these examples of crafting illustrates how disinformation agents use manifold networks to connect disparate information and issues together to create anti-vaccination disin formation content.

Anti-vaccine disinformation often appeals to fears of bodily harm and violation of bodily autonomy. The act of an injection puncturing the skin and pushing foreign material into the body activates powerful emotions especially of fear, anger, and suspicion. In this manner, disinformation agents frequently use negative emotion to amplify false content (Williams & Dupuis, 2020). Antivaccination disinformation is typically broadcast on social media platforms in the form of images and short videos that depict med ical procedures or grotesque side effects of vaccination. Moreover, such disinformation content routinely portrays COvID-19 deaths as overreported, while vaccine deaths remain underreported, despite no supporting evidence for either claim. Exposure to antivaccination disinformation often results in an incongruence of frames between various official government claims. local vaccine heroes (i.e., personal doctors, nurses, and community leaders), and acquaintances in one’s interpersonal network (Chan et al., 2022). Such incongruence suggests that individuals experience recurrent strain (see Oborn & Barrett, 2021) as a result of frequent interaction with disinformation content. Persistent negative affect that surrounds the vaccination issue field invites greater user-engagement, diffusing disinformation content widely. In a countervailing way, recent work demonstrates that associating the benefits of vaccination with positive emotions helps to debunk fake news pertaining to vaccination (Castello, ´ Joachim, & Parry, 2022).

Anti-vaccine disinformation agents enact the partitioning disinformation affordance by provoking members of online communities (e.g., an anti-vaxx Facebook group or subreddit) to harass or troll other social media users. Anti-vaxxers routinely harass medica providers online, targeting their personal accounts and also business pages. Purveyors of disinformation also target social media users that both promote vaccination and are relatively neutral to vaccination with toxic and negative comments (Mivazaki. Uchiba. Tanaka. & Sasahara, 2022). Anti-vaccination proponents also attempt to partition users by invoking notions of a “good parent,” “a mother’s intuition,” and “individual rights,” presenting children as innocent victims (Baker & Walsh, 2022). In particular, a good mother is one who refuses unproven and potentially dangerous vaccines for her child, while bad mothers unquestioningly accept a medical doctor’s vaccine recommendations (Baker & Walsh, 2022).

Vaccine disinformation has led to several consequences for the vaccine issue field. The vaccination issue field consists of medica practitioners, public health experts, biomedical scientists, government officials, public health non-profits, and health activists. These field participants formulate rules and procedures to safely develop vaccines and also enact policies to equitably distribute vaccines to citizens within their communities. In the United States as many as 78% of adults have been exposed to online COVID-19 misinfor mation (e.g., the U.S. government conceals deaths due to the vaccine, the vaccine causes COVID-19 infection) and also either believe the misinformation to be true or are unsure of the information’s veracity (Hamel, Kirzinger, Munana, & Brodie, 2020). Relatively high rates of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy also characterize many European countries, such as France, Poland, and Croatia (de Figueiredo & Larson, 2021). To be sure, accepting conspiratorial beliefs and vaccine hesitancy represent complex phenomena (de Figueiredo & Larson. 2021: Dror et al., 2020: Troiano & Nardi, 2021). Yet. the harmful effects of vaccine disinformation are widespread, especially to those individuals that cannot receive the vaccine for medical reasons or for individuals that reside in areas where vaccine distri bution is difficult. The circulation of falsehoods led the World Health Organization to declare an infodemic where “there is too much information including false or misleading information in digital and physical environments during a disease outbreak. It causes confusion and risk-taking behaviors that can harm health.” For example, Hansen and Schmidtblaicher (2021) document how a TV documentary’s portrayal of anti-vaccination content led to a significant decline in HPV vaccinations in Denmark, undermining the Danish HPV vaccination program. In this manner, disinformation weakens the power of public health officials in the vaccination issue field, as citizens question the motives of health care providers and the efficacy of vaccines.

## 6.3. Flat Earth disinformation

Scholars and thinkers from many ancient civilizations set forth that the Earth exists as a flat plane. Yet, Grecian philosophers Pythagoras, Plato, and Aristotle rejected the notion of a flat Earth, offering theories and evidence for a spherical Earth. Nevertheless, people in the 21st century form online communities on social media platforms to espouse their beliefs in a flat Earth model. Online users often claim that the United States’ National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) alters images and fakes video to conceal that the Earth is actually flat and that a physical barrier (e.g., an ice wall that is hundreds of meters in height) prevents humans from traversing to the edge of the world. Within Facebook groups, Reddit threads, and YouTube videos “flat Earthers” debate the actua shape of Earth, casting aspersions on scientific associations and national space agencies for conspiring to keep reality hidden from them. Users often advocate passionately in favor of the scientific method and report their findings from experiments to other online community members. Usually, such experiments involve measuring the distance between two objects and perceiving no visible change in altitude or visibility between those objects, reinforcing their rejection of a spherical planet.

Flat Earthers craft disinformation by navigating the manifold network structure of social media platforms to take pieces of content from seemingly unrelated social spheres and weave them into a grand tapestry of global conspiracy (Paolillo, 2018). In particular, Flat Earth content enmeshes conspiracies (e.g., NASA conceals the flat nature of the Earth from the general public) with scientific discourse, popular media, such as the film The Matrix, religious fundamentalism, and political rhetoric (Landrum & Olshansky, 2019; Paolillo, 2018). For instance, disinformation agents draw on the films that depict dystopian futures (e.g., The Matrix, The Truman Show) to illustrate to their audience that a global elite has fooled the general populace into believing the “Global Model.” (Paolillo, 2018). Their content offers a red pill (e.g., The Matrix) to wake up and understand the extent of this conspiracy (Landrum & Olshansky, 2019; Paolillo. 2018). By using the features of YouTube to splice video clips together and selectively edit legitimate information, users craft narrative accounts that prove the existence of a flat Earth. Flat Earth YouTube videos and other disinformation content invites users of social media into an exciting and seemingly scientific alternative worldview: a worldview where the audience member has put together for themselves a big secret that only themselves and a handful of people know.

While flat Earth conferences and conventions are held annually around the globe (Brazil, 2020), there is a seamless crossover between these in-person events, often drawing hundreds of participants, and the numerous YouTube videos that detail models of a flat Earth and offer “scientific" evidence for the flat Earth proposition (Brazil, 2020). A vibrant community of content creators on YouTube constantly uploads conspiratorial videos and invites audience participation through live chats (Paolillo. 2018). People often first learn of flat Earth beliefs from a friend or family member and then are directed to YouTube videos for in-depth explanations of flat Earth science (Landrum & Olshansky. 2019). Participation in livestreams and online chats makes use of content flow across social media platforms, in attempts to reach more online users and make flat Earth content trend or go virgl. Cross-collaborations among hosts on YouTube videos extend the appeal to new viewers and increases user engagement (Paolillo, 2018). Interconnections among content creators and YouTube channels demarcate the flat Earth community, where users know who to trust and what sort of information each content creator offers. For its part, YouTube did alter their engagement-driven algorithms in 2019 to reduce the amount of conspir atorial content recommended to users (Brazil, 2020). Through YouTube livestreams and cross-collaborations, flat Earthers enact the amplifving disinformation affordance by making use of content flow (e.g., user-interactions on livestreams) and manifold networks (e. g., bringing the varied audiences of YouTube content creators together via livestreams). Finally, Flat Earthers enact the partitioning disinformation affordances in a variety of ways. Flat Earth disinformation erects discursive boundaries to distinguish the flat Earth online community by incorporating religious texts that purports a stationary geocentric universe (Olshansky, Peaslee, & Landrum, 2020; Paolillo, 2018). Flat Earthers often claim that by placing the Earth at the center of the universe they are able to discredit scientific theories such as the Big Bang and Darwinian evolutionary theory, giving those in the flat Earth community a sense of purpose (Olshansky et al. 2020). Beliefs in other conspiracies, such as the fake Moon landing, often serves as a litmus test for membership into the flat Earth community. While religious fundamentalism and conspiratorial beliefs demarcates group membership within the genera flat Earth online community, flat Earthers also compete intensely among themselves. Content creators incessantly attempt to discredit other flat Earth YouTubers and will frequently loot content from channels that are taken down when a content creator deactivates their YouTube account and use that looted content in their own videos (Paolillo, 2018).

Flat Earth disinformation targets the issue field that encompasses scientific research and education, especially pertaining to geology and astronomy. Members of the issue field include: university and college professors, K-12 teachers, non-profit organizations that promote science literacy, and science journalists. Unlike the issue fields in the preceding vignettes, little formal interaction exists among members of the scientific community and flat Earthers (Břízová. Gerbec. Šauer. & Šlégr. 2018). While some scientists do use social media to dispel flat Earth disinformation, the scientific community largely ignores such disinformation. As such, flat Earth beliefs have not so far been successful in effecting field-level change. One difference between flat Earthers and other disinformation campaigns, is that flat Earthers have largely been unable to infiltrate and gain support from the scientific community. For example, some politicians support QAnon disinformation and some health care professionals engage in anti-vaccination disinformation, yet the flat Earth community largely lacks the support of teachers and researchers that comprise legitimate educational and scientific in stitutions. Moreover, flat Earth beliefs are diverse (Brazil, 2020). Popular models include a flat Earth where the Antarctic ice wall circumscribes the known land masses whereas another details how the flat Earth exists within a snow globe like structure with the sun acting like a spotlight by making some parts day and others night. Little consensus exists among flat Earthers. Barrett, Heracleous, and Walsham (2013) describe similar dynamics within the free/open-source software community where multiple groups may unite in their opposition to proprietary software, but differ on their vision for how to implement and sustain free/open-source software practices. Similarly, the various flat Earth online communities espouse different explanations to account for a flat Earth model, yet they frequently tolerate these divergent interests and beliefs through interdependent actions (e.g., by collaborating on YouTube videos) in order to marshal opposition to mainstream scientific thought. These internal tensions and divisions among flat Earthers regarding the competing and divergent details of a flat Earth contribute to an active and lively discourse on online forums and in the comments section of YouTube (Paolillo, 2018), yet prevent flat Earthers from forming a consensus and establishing a disinformation disorder (Bennett & Livingston, 2018) and exerting their collective power to alter scientific and educational institutions.

## 7. Discussion

## 7.1. Contributions

By articulating the relationship between digital materiality and disinformation affordances, we contribute to extant theorizing on the circulation of online disinformation and the organizing processes that create and sustain disinformation communities (Lazer et al., 2018; Paolillo, 2018; Partin & Marwick, 2020; Xia et al., 2019). Through the enactment of disinformation affordances – crafting, amplifying, and partitioning – disinformation agents engage online communities with the intent to propagate disinformation content (crafting and amplifying) and to differentiate the online community’s boundaries from those of other online groups (partitioning). Together, these disinformation affordances contribute to an online disinformation community (Ruiz & Nilsson, 2022). Crucially, online disinformation activates in-group biases, where users are more likely to accept distorted assertations and false claims as fact (Bloom & Moskalenko, 2021; Marwick & Partin, 2022; Paolillo, 2018). Our vignettes also illustrate how digital materiality – modular content, content flow, and manifold networks – provide opportunities for individual actors to enact disinformation affordances. Hence, our paper unpacks the processes that contribute to “disinformation orders” on social media platforms (Bennett & Livingston, 2018) by exploring how people use information and digital technology to spread false content.

Our paper contributes to institutional theory by considering how technological artifacts and their material features make new institutional processes possible, addressing concerns regarding the dearth of technology and materiality theorizing in institutiona analysis (de Vaujany et al., 2019; Friedland & Arjali\`es, 2021; Lˆe, 2015). Although institutional scholars have long recognized the material foundation of meanings and social practices, recent work at the intersection of technology and institutions reveals a bewildering munificence of possibilities for action that results in new institutional processes (Powell et al., 2017). We contribute to this emergent research stream by focusing on several aspects of materiality that are relevant for social media. Thus, we complement scholarship that documents how the features of digital technology and their affordances promote change processes (e.g., Alaimo. 2022: Faik et al., 2020). By focusing on the connections among digital materiality and technology affordances, we offer a useful framework to explore other institutional processes that occur within virtual spaces.

Our digital materiality-disinformation affordance perspective also contributes to recent scholarship that explores the societal consequences of online disinformation. In particular, extant research (e.g., Bennett & Livingston, 2018; Bradshaw & Howard, 2018) and two of our three descriptive vignettes (i.e., QAnon and Anti-Vaccination) suggests that online disinformation may result in fieldlevel change by disrupting institutions. Our vignettes document how actors create and disseminate online disinformation to disrupt the routine functioning of an institutional issue field. Routine functioning indicates that field participants “agree on a common framework” to address the central issue of the field (Litrico & David. 2017, pg. 988). From this perspective, online disinformation often attempts to undermine the legitimacy of dominant practices within the field (e.g., vaccinations protect against disease) and question the authority of elite actors within the field (e.g., medical doctors, government regulators). Therefore, online disinformation deinstitutionalizes (Oliver, 1992) various elements of a field’s institutional infrastructure (Hinings et al., 2017).

Our vignettes and extant institutional theory suggest that online disinformation likely disrupts issue fields through changes to one or more field-level characteristics – power centralization, subfield structures, and institutional infrastructure (Buchanan et al., 2022; Wooten & Hoffman. 2008: Zietsma et al. 2017). We choose to elaborate online disinformation's effect on these three characteristics because each characteristic contributes to a field's degree of settlement (Buchanan et al., 2022). which helps actors to establish shared frameworks for collective action (Furnari, 2018; Litrico & David, 2017). Therefore, these field-level characteristics represent critica elements that shape the capability of institutions to address shared problems. Yet, we also recognize that there are other field char acteristics (e.g., Zietsma et al., 2017), such as field age, that require further consideration and empirical exploration

First, elite actors often wield power to maintain the dominant institutional structures that confer their advantageous positions through the enforcement of norms and rules (Furnari, 2018). Institutional scholarship often denotes elite actors as individuals or organizations whose legitimacy allows them to influence the action of other field members, such as a social media influencer that commands many followers or an international non-governmental organization that advocates for technology regulations (Powell et al. 2017). Yet, as purveyors of disinformation enact disinformation affordances to sow doubts among the members of targeted socia groups regarding the capability of existing institutions to address their needs (Allcott et al., 2019; Del Vicario et al., 2016; McKay & Tenove, 2021) and frequently casts aspersion on the legitimacy of elite actors (Bennett & Livingston, 2018), online disinformation may shift the locus of power within an issue field. Online disinformation that undermines the authority of elite actors may redistribute power among other field participants. Through crafting and partitioning, disinformation agents create content to erode trust and polarize attitudes towards elite actors (Bennett & Livingston, 2018). For example, propagators of anti-vaccination disinformation may intentionally misrepresent the results of clinical trials for a particular vaccine or exploit manifold network structures to tie vaccination to other conspiratorial beliefs (e.g., Covid-19 vaccination and 5G telecommunications infrastructure) to discredit scientists and other medical professionals (Grimes, 2021).

Second, the presence of online disinformation likely contributes to the emergence of new subfield structures within the issue field – where one or more actors pursue common interests and engage in similar practices that are distinct from other groups of actors within the issue field (Faulconbridge & Muzio, 2021). For instance, among members of the flat Earth disinformation community little agreement exists on what constitutes an accurate representation of a flat Earth, leading to a splintering of the disinformation com munity into competing factions, each engaging in different practices to demonstrate to their followers how the Earth is flat (Brazil, 2020). Understanding the dynamics of subfields is important because a proliferation of subfield structures also increases the complexity of the issue field (Buchanan et al., 2022) Actors from different subfields that vie for limited resources are likely to introduce multiple – and at times competing – institutional logics (Greenwood et al., 2011; Zietsma et al., 2017). As the heterogeneity of the issue field increases elite actors may experience greater difficulty when attempting to coordinate collective action among field members (Buchanan et al., 2022; Greenwood et al., 2011), undermining the ability to manage field-level boundaries (Hinings et al., 2017).

Finally, online disinformation might change the infrastructure of an issue field. An issue field that consists of an elaborate and coherent institutional infrastructure conveys the norms, rules, and regulations that govern activity among field participants (Buchanan et al., 2022). The various elements of the institutional infrastructure provide templates for appropriate interactions (Hinings et al., 2017). Yet, disinformation often frames existing rules as ineffective to respond to contemporary challenges (Bennett & Livingston, 2018). Disinformation content is often especially effective when progress towards addressing the collective issue that prompts field formation is perceived as slow or is difficult to evaluate by field participants (Bennett & Livingston, 2018). In such circumstances, dissatisfaction with the status quo may catalyze people to explore alternative infrastructure elements. From this perspective, citizens that perceive public policy as not addressing their concerns or needs are especially likely to believe in political disinformation (Garr et al., 2021). In the QAnon vignette, the tendency for individual users to forgo legitimate political processes and instead advocate for violence to achieve political goals (Bloom & Moskalenko, 2021), illustrates a decrease in the degree of coherence of the institutiona infrastructure of the political issue field (Hinings et al., 2017)

However, additional questions remain regarding how actors use digital technology such as social media to spur field-leve institutional change (Faik et al., 2020). Our paper addresses this quandary by reframing disinformation as the intentional disrup tion of the routine functioning of an issue field. We focus attention on three material features of social media and the affordances that they provide to propagate misleading content, although we recognize that these features may also support the dissemination of legitimate information. By considering the intersection of institutional change and digital materiality, we suggest three possible pathways whereby online disinformation disrupts institutional issue fields – power centralization, subfield structure, and institutiona infrastructure. Finally, our theorizing offers guidelines for policymakers to tamp down the quantity of circulating online disinfor mation. We summarize our contributions to institutional scholarship in Table 2 and also present several research questions that merit further exploration. We elaborate on some of these research questions below

Our research suggests several avenues for future scholarship that contributes to our understanding of online disinformation and social institutions. We encourage further exploration of how social media disrupts institutions. For example, our OAnon vignette suggests that online disinformation not only had a disrupting effect on the political issue field, but perhaps a longer lasting effect. From this perspective, disinformation may fracture institutions, resulting in a widening and deepening of the faultlines (Lau & Murnighan, 1998) and in-group dynamics that makes disinformation so potent and capable to disrupt institutions (Partin & Marwick. 2020). Accordingly, an investigation of institutional fracturing is beyond the scope of this paper, but additional theorizing needs to focus on the temporal dimension – especially the permanence – of the institutional consequences of online disinformation. Additionally, our research theorizes how digital materiality influences and alters social identity dynamics. Our model considers the role of affect within online disinformation communities, addressing calls for further research both on the relationship between digital technology and institutional change (Faik et al., 2020) and also requests for considering how emotions contribute to institutional processes (Ashforth & Humphrey, 2022). Yet, much is left to explore regarding how actors institutionalize emotions within digital environments and how these types of actions influence field-level change.

We also encourage future studies to explore the processes through which field characteristics change. For instance, our vignettes suggest that online disinformation disrupts institutional issue fields at varying degrees and paces (Micelotta et al., 2017). For instance, radical and revolutionary institutional change – as the QAnon vignette suggests – results, in part, from the social media affordances for scalability (Faik et al., 2020) or the presence of echo chambers (Cinelli et al., 2021) that contributes to positive feedback loops of content. Future research could explore how positive feedback loops among elements of an issue field alter field characteristics, such as power centralization or subfield structures. Yet, our vignettes also illustrate that no observable change might occur following the introduction of disinformation (e.g., as was the case in the flat Earth vignette). One possible avenue for further exploration is how the type of field influences how disinformation disrupts institutions. For instance, we investigate issue fields, yet many organizations operate within exchange fields that form around technologies or products (Hoffman, 1999). Additionally, fields may be emergent or mature (Zietsma et al., 2017). Future scholarship might illuminate how disinformation effects differ in various types of fields.

We recognize that differences exist among social media platforms with respect to volume and dispersion of disinformation present within their users' content or exchanges. But why? Is it the material features of a specific platform or perhaps company policies, and proprietary algorithms, that establish boundaries for user contributions or exchanges? Relatedly, how do users perceive the material features and affordances of social media platforms? Within-person variance regarding perception of the saliency of social media features may offer insight into the micro-foundations of disinformation affordances. Future theorizing and analysis of the distinctive features or control mechanisms across social media platforms may offer a sort of negative case analysis to the framework presented in our paper. For instance, how frequently does a single user copy disinformation content to different platforms and what features afford users easy cross-platform interactions? Such an approach would reframe disinformation research to focus on the material foundations of false content.

Our framework also raises questions about the relationship between online interactions among issue field participants and the offline material practices of organizational leaders, activists, and consumers (Young & Leonardi, 2012). How exactly does social media influence non-digital contexts? Online disinformation may become part of the vernacular and influence what individuals with no socia media presence say and do. Considering how events and practices gain attention within a field may offer an initial starting point for such theorizing (Nigam & Ocasio, 2010). In this manner, online disinformation may illuminate a certain characteristic of an issue that had previously been overlooked. Hence, online disinformation may shift field-level attention to new problems – real or imagined Social media use may also influence the permanence of institutional meanings and practices through the persistence of information (Treem & Leonardi, 2013). Although our framework does not unpack this feature directly, we speculate that persistence suggests that disinformation content never disappears from public discourse. Hence, disinformation agents may begin to circulate dated disinfor mation anew, relying perhaps on nostalgia to increase resonance among audiences (Giorgi, 2017). To better explore these empirica quandaries, we encourage the development of a methodology that can account for the complex nature of disinformation; a meth odology that leads to “convergent and holistic triangulation" of multiple methods (Turner, Cardinal. & Burton. 2017: 1). A wealth of data exists on social media platforms. Scholars may turn to web crawler software to scrape social media platforms for user-generated content (Landers, Brusso, Cavanaugh, & Collmus, 2016) to gain insight into newly emerging disinformation actions. Machine learning techniques also may be beneficial in navigating complex issue fields and helping researchers uncover digital processes or patterns of actions that may be difficult to observe.

Our research also touches on previous assertions regarding the nature of contemporary information. Disinformation may be a permanent aspect of the digital age due to our reliance on technology-mediated communication and information capitalism (Marshall, 2017). Scholars contend that diminishing trust in basic institutions may be ushering in a post-truth society (Iyengar & Massey, 2019), where facts are malleable and subiect to change without notice (Sismondo. 2017). These observations challenge the dichotomy be tween information and disinformation. For instance, how does information become labeled as misinformation or disinformation? The debate surrounding the origins of COVID-19 – zoonotic spillover or accidental lab leak – illustrates the murky nature of distinguishing between information and disinformation. Specifically, early in 2020 the possibility of an accidental lab leak scenario quickly became conspiratorial content (Calisher et al., 2020) and labeled misinformation by social media platforms (Dwoskin, 2021). Yet, in 2021 new evidence suggested that the lab-leak hypothesis was worth investigating and Facebook ceased censoring lab-leak content (Dwoskin, 2021). The ability to designate what is and what is not disinformation resides among actors that wield the power to bestow legitimacy on a given claim. The perceived reality of an event or object may change through various social actions (Murray, Rhymer, & Sirmon, 2021). Although our theorizing emphasizes the importance of power and agency, future studies need to scrutinize these concepts further to better understand their function in the context of disinformation. Moreover, such explorations can also inform how we identify and remove disinformation, since some disinformation may never even become identified as such.

## 7.3. Practical implications

Our research joins the expanding effort to reduce the quantity and transmissibility of online disinformation (Bliss et al., 2020; Guo, Ding, Yao, Liang, & Yu, 2019; McQuillan, McAweeney, Bargar, & Ruch, 2020). Although removal of disinformation ex post facto (i.e., a content-based approach) does improve the quality of the social media information environment (Dwoskin & Timberg, 2021), our focus on digital materiality suggests a different approach to deter disinformation. We join others (Nelimarkka, Laaksonen, & Semaan, 2018) and advocate for public policy that emphasizes the redesign of social media platforms and their material features as a means to combat the spread of disinformation. Specifically, policymakers and designers might wish to alter the material features of social media platforms that prevent or limit users from enacting disinformation affordances, such as crafting, amplifying, and partitioning. Yet, we also acknowledge that formulating new public policy guidelines will require nuance to ensure that social media can continue to effect positive social change, such as the use of social media to promote democratic elections (Leong, Faik, Tan, Tan, & Khoo, 2020) or to help users organize social movements to address societal injustices (Kudesia, 2021). Hence, the following suggestions are offered merely as general guidelines that are informed by our theoretical model.

## 7.3.1. Countering crafting and amplifying affordances

To reduce online disinformation, possible redesign tactics may include modifications that reduce an actor’s ability to enact crafting and amplifying disinformation affordances. To interrupt the emergence of these disinformation affordances, we suggest redesign ef forts that alter aspects of the digital materiality of social media platforms. In particular, platforms may adopt temporary waiting periods to interact with new content with the aim to decrease content flow. A temporary waiting period may be relatively brief – a few seconds to a few minutes – before new user content becomes visible to other users. Similarly, WhatsApp’s policies of limiting the number of times a message can be forwarded or labelling a message that has been forwarded multiple times decreases content flow and hinders the enactment of the amplifying affordance to counter disinformation (Hern, 2020). Another redesign technique would be to limit the number of posts that a user can view per day to slow interactions among users (Ewing, 2023). By preventing immediate interaction, content flow would likely decrease, preventing most or a significant portion of disinformation content from pervading the information ecosystem. Additionally, social media platforms may consider altering how users can share content or limit the sharing of content across platforms with the goal of reducing the size of manifold networks that disinformation agents can use to expand the reach of their disinformation. By decreasing the quantity of circulating online disinformation, the amount of false content within an issue field may not reach the critical threshold that threatens institutional or organizational legitimacy (Illia et al., 2022). However, we also recognize that not all institutional or organizational legitimacies need to be protected, especially institutions that perpetuate inequities (Amis, Mair, & Munir, 2020).

## 7.3.2. Countering the partitioning affordance

The disinformation affordance of partitioning suggests that countering negative emotions, such as anger and fear, may be especially helpful when attempting to tamp down the spread of disinformation. In particular, actors routinely frame disinformation content with negative emotions to partition online communities and to increase user engagement (Williams & Dupuis, 2020). Disinformation agents often take advantage of a platform’s algorithmic ranking of content by creating or sharing content that elicits negative affect. One option to minimize disinformation agents’ exploitation of negative affect might be to redesign algorithmic rankings to account for affect or de-emphasize negative affect. For example, interventions that employ positively framed emotional content have been shown to undermine the potency of anti-vaccination disinformation (Castello ´ et al., 2022). Exploring how to instill positive affect in online space might also help to rupture the pervasive negative affect that characterizes many online disinformation communities and sustains in-group dynamics and disinformation echo chambers (Ruiz & Nilsson, 2022). Yet, designers must also exercise caution in the context of social movement organizations and especially regarding content surrounding marginalized communities to not suppress social media affordances that allow users to coalesce anger concerning actual inequalities into collective action (Kudesia, 2021).

Focusing on the materiality-affordance relationship has the potential to yield important insights towards a set of design principles for social media platforms that reduces disinformation. As a final comment, government policy that encourages social media redesign should consider the interests of community and societal stakeholders, in addition to the business interests of social media companies (Nelimarkka et al., 2019). A diversity of opinions and perspectives may lead to a set of social media design principles that empowers actors to harness the digital materiality inherent within social media for productive ends (Nelimarkka et al., 2019; Ruiz & Nilsson, 2022). By including diverse stakeholders, such as policymakers, scientists, software engineers, and consumer advocates, during interventions institutions might become more equitable and just (Amis et al., 2020).

## 7.4. Conclusion

In closing, we encourage further exploration of how individuals use digital technology, such as social media, and how individuals use of digital technology influences field-level institutional change. Social media profoundly alters human interaction and group-leve processes (Leonardi, 2010; Leonardi & Vaast, 2017), suggesting that scholars may need to revisit prior institutional theorizing (Powel et al., 2017). Future scholarship should not bracket off digital artifacts from their theorizing, but instead must account for how human agents interact with technological artifacts (Treem & Leonardi, 2013). In this effort, we hope that our development of a materialityaffordance perspective of disinformation enriches institutional scholarship and provides practical insights for policymakers that seek to minimize the deleterious effects of false information. Specifically, we believe that institutional change and a focus on materiality can supplement extant perspectives on disinformation (Bennett and Livingston, 2018) and contribute to redesigning social media for productive information exchange (see Nelimarkka et al., 2019). Finally, considering the fast pace of contemporary technological innovation, we hope that information systems and organization scholars continue to make strides in discovering and understanding how technology, especially digital technology, transforms organizing so that it may be both a productive and equitable tool for col lective action.
