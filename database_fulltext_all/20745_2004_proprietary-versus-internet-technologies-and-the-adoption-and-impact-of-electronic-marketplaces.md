# Proprietary versus internet technologies and the adoption and impact of electronic marketplaces

Ellen Christiaanse<sup>a,\*</sup>, Tonja Van Diepen<sup>b</sup>, Jan Damsgaard<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>University of Amsterdam, The Netherlands and ESADE, Avenida Pedralbes 60-62, E-08034 Barcelona, Spain

<sup>b</sup>University of Amsterdam, Roeterstraat 11 1018 WB, Amsterdam, The Netherlands

Received 31 March 2002; accepted 25 February 2004

Available online 24 April 2004

## Abstract

In this paper we provide a trans-disciplinary view on electronic markets and their impact beyond the dyadic use of EDI. Electronic marketplaces and business-to-business (B2B) exchanges are proliferating now that the Internet has made cooperation and collaboration easier and cheaper. The key question addressed is: will these newer forms of electronic markets lead to other mechanisms of integration and governance than the old EDI-based e-markets and Interorganisational systems? We argue that the characteristics of pre-internet electronic marketplaces structurally differ from those of internet-based electronic marketplaces, and that this results in different adoption and integration patterns. We provide a model and a set of integrative propositions.

## 1. Introduction

Despite the considerable investments being made and the promises of efficiencies to be gained and support being offered by collaborative integration, electronic markets so far are no big generators of liquidity (Wise and Morrison, 2000). Although ‘a widely held position observed through the lens of transaction cost theory has been the role of information technology in decreasing transaction cost between buyers and suppliers and in creating more market based governance structures.’ (Grover et al., 2002), recent observations do not support this contention.

Electronic markets are not new (Bakos, 1991a,b; Bailey and Bakos, 1997; Malone et al., 1987). But, the open infrastructure of the internet provides internet-based marketplaces with fewer of the traditional barriers of point-to-point electronic connections (such as upfront, highly asset-specific investments in joint infrastructure). We argue that the characteristics of pre-internet electronic marketplaces structurally differ from those of internet-based electronic market places, and that these differences result in changing governance mechanisms and causing substantial effects on channel structures (Bakos, 1998). In particular the role of asset-specific investments underlies these changes. The main questions underlying the theoretical analysis in this paper are: (1) To what extent will the different nature of the technology applied have a pervasive impact on the governance of inter-firm activities; (2) What will be the effect of open internet-based technologies on inter-firm governance mechanisms traditionally assumed to be affected by transaction costs and the role of asset specific investments; and (3) What will be the implications for electronic markets when the focus shifts away from getting the technologies in place, to building upon the potential collaborative capabilities these technologies facilitate (Holland and Lockett, 1997; Christiaanse and Venkatraman, 2002).

We will address these questions from a multidisciplinary perspective and identify the gaps in the literature concerning the impact of technology on relation-specific investments and transaction costs traditionally associated with quasi-integration (Subramani and Venkatraman, 2003) and/or with relational integration (Grover et al., 2002). Thus, the general research questions guiding this paper are:

1. How do open internet technologies, instead of proprietary technologies, influence the antecedents to the formation of these marketplaces (adoption, ownership) in this new post-internet era?

2. Do ‘open technologies’, as a result of the reduced asset-specific investments required, lead to different consequences (governance and relational integration) of electronic markets?

First, we would like to point out that we define electronic marketplaces as electronic networks where buyers and sellers meet to engage in buying and selling as well as other activities, such as collaborative planning, logistics, transportation arrangements and fulfilment-like the functions that traditional marketplaces have been fulfilling for thousands of years (Headrick, 2000). We therefore use the term electronic marketplace for all kind of Inter-organisational system (IOS) arrangements involving multiple firms. In this we differ from other authors, like Bakos (1991a,b, 1997), who initially just used the term to indicate networks where buying and selling took place. We argue that the governance effects of these marketplaces in particular industries are defined by the focus of the marketplace: either purely transactional (focusing on the sales transaction and the price), or focused more on relational exchange (e.g. supply-chain management solutions, fulfilment, and collaboration).

The paper is structured as follows: Section 2 addresses dyadic transaction-cost approaches to research on electronic marketplaces (EMPs). Section 3 then discusses in more detail the role of asset specificity as an antecedent to the formation of EMPs, and shows why we believe this to be a key construct to demonstrate why internet-based electronic markets, as opposed to dyadic EDI-based networks, have properties that allow for a new range of consequences. Section 4 presents a model and a set of propositions to integrate views from transaction-cost analysis (TCA) with relational exchange and strategic network paradigms. Section 5 presents the conclusions with their implications, as well as suggestions for further research.

## 2. Transaction-cost analysis based approaches

Research has predominantly adopted a transaction-cost standpoint regarding electronic markets. In line with other research (Grover et al., 2002; Markus and Christiaanse, 2003), we argue that this approach poses several problems. The first problem is that, nowadays, there are many species of electronic marketplaces (Lennstrand et al., 2001; Markus and Soh, 2002), each with different characteristics and with different effects on existing channel structures. For instance, in the chemical industry, Elemica can be seen as a collaborative marketplace (Christiaanse et al., 2003), while Endorsia.com and Alibaba. com focus only on facilitating buying and selling. Covisint, in the automotive industry, is moving increasingly from a transaction-oriented marketplace towards a collaborative one (Dyer and Singh, 2000). The nature of the governance structure for electronic marketplaces depends on a number of factors and in our view might be strongly affected by the technology characteristics.

The second problem with applying transaction-cost logic to the adoption of electronic markets is that the (sales) transaction is the unit of analysis, instead of the exchange relationship. A too narrow focus on sales transactions, as opposed to other aspects of inter-firm relationships, has characterized IS research for decades (Grover et al., 2002). As firms function in supply-chain relationships, and not in ‘transaction vacuums’, we argue there are clearly many functions that electronic markets can perform beyond the sales transaction itself (e.g. fulfilment, logistical and transportation arrangements), and that these functions obviously depend on the nature of the transaction and the relationship. As the negotiation and transaction phases have been the main focus in most electronic market research, a natural tendency to focus on these transaction-oriented EMPs has characterized research in this area (Bakos, 1991a,b). TCA-based antecedents, like asset-specific investments, transaction frequency, and performance ambiguity— often the focus of IS-based approaches—are too limited to help us understand both the complex relationship structures and the economic and political consequences of electronic markets.

A third issue is that the focal role of asset-specific investments in transaction-cost based analysis might increasingly lose its power as a factor to explain integration effects. We will deal with this issue in more detail in Section 3.

## 2.1. Proprietary technologies and conflicting evidence on the role of asset-specific investments

Over the last two decades, many studies have focused on the factors that define the success and failure of large-scale IOS initiatives (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 1998; Christiaanse and Damsgaard, 2001; Markus, 1983; Robey et al., 1993; Kwon and Zmud, 1987; Keil and Montealegre, 2000; Christiaanse and Huigen, 1997; Christiaanse and Venkatraman, 2002). Before the commercial application of the World Wide Web and the Internet, EDI was the dominant way for businesses to exchange documents electronically. The proprietary nature of the EDI-based systems involved was often found to be inhibiting successful cooperation (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 1997, 1998, 2001).

Asset-specific investments, as required by older communication technology applications such as EDI and other proprietary networks, have long inhibited the prediction that firms would move to more market-type coordination structures that use new intermediaries (Sarkar et al., 1995; Clemons et al., 1993). Only after the advent of Internet-based electronic markets did the real opportunities for on-line transactions open up across space and over time (Dai and Kauffman, 2001).

It is important to realize that most of the marketplaces reported on by Bakos (1991a,b, 1997) were based on proprietary systems, requiring relatively high upfront investments in assets and process coordination (Holland and Lockett, 1997) when compared to today’s more open, (non-proprietary) Internet-based systems. Communication over the Internet provides firms, at least in the pre-transaction information and negotiation phases, with the opportunity to get in touch with other firms without having to make high upfront asset-specific investments. Now that the technology is maturing and infrastructures are in place, firms can start building upon the capabilities gained during these inter-firm collaboration processes and start to coordinate their transactions and redefine traditional supply-chain relationships in a dramatically different manner (Clemons et al., 1993; Choudhury et al., 1998; Christiaanse and Kumar, 2000).

It follows that, as firms no longer have to invest in complex technologies to communicate with each other, the argument could be made that asset-specific investments are lower for firms in the internet era than for firms in the 1980s and 1990s, when proprietary systems were the dominant way of inter-firms communications.

One of the core propositions of transaction-cost economics is that the presence of higher levels of vertical integration in channel environments requires higher transaction-specific assets. Asset specificity refers to the relative lack of transferability of certain assets, intended for use in a given transaction, to other uses. Williamson (1985, 1989) has suggested six main types of asset specificity: site specificity, physical asset specificity, human asset specificity, brand names, dedicated assets, and temporal specificity. Highly specific assets represent sunk costs that have relatively little value beyond their use in the context of a specific transaction. In the IS field it was argued that dedicated IT-related assets lead to forms of quasi-integration (Kambil and Short, 1994; Grover et al., 2002).

This general view has been supported in many earlier studies of marketing channels (cf. Anderson and Schmittlein, 1984; Anderson, 1985; Anderson and Coughlan, 1987; John and Weitz, 1988). A recent study by Christiaanse and Venkatraman (2002) found however, that business-process asset-specificity did not emerge as a significant predictor for the level of electronic integration. This is in contrast with the results of an earlier study on the insurance industry by Zaheer and Venkatraman (1994), that did find businessprocess asset-specificity to be a major determinant of electronic integration in the insurance industry. Rangan et al. (1993) observed however that asset-specific investments may play a different role for hybrid channels (tasks shared between ‘direct’ and ‘indirect’ channels) and in channels that are in transition (i.e. evolving from one form to another).

Malone et al. (1987) argued that IOSs would reduce asset-specific investments, which in turn would reduce the electronic integration effects. Findings by Holland and Lockett (1997) indicated that IOSs have indeed reduced asset-specific investments and led to more integration and interdependence between firms, which has caused firms to move to mixed-mode network structures.

Alternative theories, like the move-to-the-middle theory of Clemons and Row (1992), the mixed-mode network structures theory of Holland and Lockett (1997), and mixed-mode structures described by several other scholars (Powell, 1987; Hennart, 1993; Lynn and Rao, 1995), each predicted different impacts of asset-specific investments.

Malone et al. (1987) and, subsequently, Malone and Rockart (1992) asserted that information technology will reduce the role of asset-specific investments. Holland and Locket, on the contrary, have argued—and illustrated, through the use of case studies—that: “Rather than use IOSs to decrease the level of asset specificity and deal with larger number of suppliers, organization are implementing IOSs that increase the level of asset specificity, either as part of an explicit strategy to tie-in customers or as a result of improving the coordination of hierarchical business relationships.” (Holland and Lockett, 1997: 485).

Empirical research on this topic has focused on the role of physical asset specificity due to the presence of dedicated systems (Venkatraman and Zaheer, 1990; Zaheer and Venkatraman, 1994; Nault, 1997; Artz and Brush, 2000), some other studies have gone further focussing on the role of systems in creating process specificity (Zaheer and Venkatraman, 1994). It is important to realize that the main objective of some more recent IOSs or EMPs is no longer to lock-in vertical channel members by forcing them to make asset-specific investments, as was often the objective of earlier proprietary systems like Baxter’s ASAP and SABRE. As in the early SABRE years (cf. American Hospital Supply and many other well-known examples from the IS literature), a dedicated dumb terminal obviously involved higher asset-specific investments by the customers than did an open systems infrastructure allowing access to a variety of electronic markets. We argue that these developments have clearly reduced the level of business-process asset-specificity. Recently, ownership and asset-specific investments have been demonstrated to be less required to monitor, influence and integrate trading partners (Venkatraman and Kambil, 1991; Zaheer and Venkatraman, 1994; Bensaou and Venkatraman, 1995) and, in combination with the open nature of our present communication infrastructure—the Internet—new flexible trading mechanisms are now becoming pervasive. This leads to our main proposition:

## 3. Beyond dyadic EDI

In order to achieve a certain level of electronic integration, investments in electronic data interchange (EDI) systems require a close coordination (Venkatraman and Zaheer, 1990). Inter-organisational systems (IOS) like EDI devices permit firms to exchange information on a more timely and frequent basis. Investment in EDI systems required close coordination and mutual adjustment to achieve a certain degree of electronic integration. Therefore, the fact that firms are now able to exchange documents without engaging in the complicated, relation-specific investments required by the older EDI applications has several important consequences:

1. Significant lower costs;

2. A lower dependency on proprietary standards;

3. Less mutual adjustment.

We will briefly discuss these consequences below.

The costs involved for a group of firms using proprietary systems have always been considerable. The coordination with proprietary systems always was pointto-point, on a one-to-one basis, and therefore involved considerable costs (Markus et al., 2000) and substantial standardization problems (Besen and Farrell, 1994). Inter-firm coordination, where proprietary information is shared on a one-to-one basis, could only be sustained through high transaction-specific investments to implement these systems.

Kindleberger (1983) explains how standards exist primarily to reduce transaction costs, because they function as a common backdrop for the trade of goods. Since the mid-1980s, when the United Nations announced their commitment to EDI, vast resources and much symbolic power have been poured into developing common standards EDI. During the 1990s, two alternative EDI standards emerged: (1) ANSI X.12 for the American continent and Japan, and (2) the UN-supported EDIFACT (EDI for Administration, Commerce and Transport) standard for the rest of the world. In addition, a myriad of local implementation guidelines (and subsets) of these standards exist. This has resulted in a slowdown of the adoption process, as companies are struggling to figure out which standard, subset, and version best fit their specific needs (Damsgaard and Truex, 2000a,b). In most cases, these competing and overlapping alternatives did not lead to an increased adoption of inter-organizational systems. Vital in this respect are the constant modifications and fluid interpretations of these systems, which also pose a significant barrier to EDI diffusion. This leads to our next proposition:

Proposition 2. Due to the use of technologies based on open standards and lower asset-specific investments, adoption will be higher and more rapid.

We argue that the reduced costs and the different role of standards, as discussed above, have various consequences for the ways in which firms may work together. We further argue that, in today’s Internet-based electronic markets, asset-specific investments are reduced to such an extent that the structure and governance of the channel relationships involved are fundamentally changing. Section 4 discusses these governance effects.

## 4. From transactions to relational exchange: the consequences for governance

Electronic markets are said to affect buyer/seller relationships in several ways (Bailey and Bakos, 1997; Choudhury et al., 1998; Dai and Kauffman, 2001). But few studies have addressed or tested the differential role that asset-specific investments may have on the governance and integration of participants in different types of electronic markets (Holland and Lockett, 1997). In addition, a major gap in the literature is that the effects of ICT, as well as its role in the broader context of relational structures, is insufficiently understood (Grover et al., 2002).

Recently, it has been argued that, relational and social-context factors should be taken into account to explain the influence of ICT on inter-organizational relationships (Grover et al., 2002; Subramani and Venkatraman, 2003; Christiaanse, 2004). These arguments are mainly based on relational exchange theory (RET) (Macneil, 1974), in which a contract is considered to consist of the future relations among individuals participating in exchange transactions (Macneil, 1980). The genuine concern of RET is to examine the importance of contracts for the governance of exchange transactions. Relational integration refers to the extent to which firms have integrated their business processes beyond contractual agreements. ‘Transaction discreteness’ is a central concept in relational exchange marketing.

Relationship marketing as a paradigm became popular in the mid to late 1980s. Based on work by Arndt (1979) and further developed by Berry (1983) and Gronroos (1990), it was described as a means ‘(· · ·) to establish, maintain, enhance and commercialize customer relationships (· · ·) by a mutual exchange and fulfilment of promises (Gronroos, 1990: p. 5). This refers to the fact whether or not a transaction occurs as a stand-alone event. Macneil’s work in this area (Macneil, 1980) has been used by Dwyer et al. (1987) to describe indicants of non-discrete transactions. These indicants include (among other things) expectations that an exchange relationship will endure over time, that benefits and burdens will be shared, that partners will share mutual trust, and planning for future transaction will take place. For decades, firms have been performing several different functions in the marketing channel (e.g. design, logistics, and manufacturing), initiated and guided by a leading company (Achrol, 1991). These new organizational forms may adopt various forms, mainly depending on the time span of the cooperative agreements. Nevertheless, as Anderson and Hakansson (1994) clearly state: “(· · ·) if business networks are to possess advantages beyond the sum of the involved dyadic relations, this must be due to considerations that take place within dyadic business relationships about their connectedness with other relationships.” (1994: p. 37).

Grover et al. (2002: 220), in a very recent paper based on RET, define ‘relationism’ as ‘reflecting cooperative behaviour with long-term implications’. The concept mirrors the notion of ‘relational embeddedness’, which captures the degree to which exchange parties consider each others’ needs and goals (Granovetter, 1985). The empirical study by Grover et al. found support for a strong negative relationship between perceived transaction costs and relationism, indicating that, in conditions of perceived high transaction costs, relationism within the supplier – buyer dyad is low. In line with the studies mentioned above, we argue that the combination of lower asset-specific investments and lower transaction costs will lead to higher levels of relational exchange, resulting in EMPs with different functions. This leads to our final propositions:

Proposition 3. Lower asset-specific investments allow for higher levels of relational exchange.

Proposition 4. EMPs will increasingly be oriented towards the facilitation of collaboration at the network level, instead of at the dyadic level.

## 5. Conclusions

In this paper we addressed the decreasing importance of asset-specific investments and the consequences for governance of the adoption of electronic marketplaces. We argued that, due to changing technological characteristics, asset-specific investments are no longer as high as they were before, and therefore allow for a more rapid adoption and a more relational type of exchanges of governance mechanisms. We presented a framework which explained the relative influence of constructs such as asset specificity as antecedents of the adoption and governance of electronic marketplaces before and after the introduction of the Internet. We further provided a set of propositions concerning the impact that the antecedents of EMP formation and adoption will have on governance consequences.

## 5.1. Technology-related asset-specific investments

In the current situation, many industries seem to be trapped in their own information technology infrastructure and power dependency. The web of networks, systems, computers, programmes and very high asset-specific investments weighs heavily on their investment capacity, without bringing about any real positive results. In many industries, technological innovations on the Internet and their fast adoption pace, are rearranging the provision of IOS in many industries. For IOS owners the change is even more radical, because the Internet is eroding their business foundation. The Internet is replacing the service providers as the primary means of carrying electronic messages, and innovations in WWW technology are challenging the systems that the electronic markets and their logistic service providers are offering. The user-friendly interface and the low costs of access to the Internet are also opening the gates to Internet-based marketplaces for a number of players who until now could not afford, and/or were lacking the skills, to operate proprietary systems. Based on Internet and XML technologies, for example, the recent emergence of numerous vertically organized cargo exchanges seems to indicate that collaborative systems with low asset-specific investments were needed to achieve the adoption rates necessary to operate a successful market in this industry.

## 5.2. The relational-exchange focus of electronic marketplaces

Nowadays, with the emergence of Internet-based public and private marketplaces (Kaplan and Sawhney, 1999, 2000; Kauffman and Walden, 2001), we see that another major shift in the organization of business activities is taking place in many industries, thereby creating new opportunities for coordination and decision-making throughout the supply chain (Mowshowitz, 1997; Klobas, 1998; Kumar et al., 1998; Christiaanse and Kumar, 2000). In turn, the reduction of uncertainty accompanying these increasingly accurate, rich and instantaneous information flows, now visible throughout supply chains, is again leading to further radical shifts in the coordination mechanisms of production, distribution, and sales. The combination of real-time information, reducing search costs to a minimum, affecting the reduction of switching costs between suppliers, and connecting downstream demand flows with upstream capacity, has in some industries already led to massive restructuring of the coordination structures among adjacent members of supply chains (Lee and Billington, 1992; Wouters et al., 1999; Premkumar, 2000).

Recent phenomena like business-to-business (B2B) exchanges and hubs (vertical or horizontal) have appeared in many industries to coordinate supply and demand for manufacturing, maintenance, and operating goods (Lee and Whang, 2001). The costs of switching or searching for trading partners are expected to decrease as these electronic markets virtually coordinate supply and demand. Although many of them have disappeared in the recent dot.com shake-out, experience with some types of electronic market coordination mechanisms has in some industries demonstrated that a massive restructuring of supply chains and industry structures can be the result (Dai and Kaufmann, 2001).

As a consequence of these dramatic reductions in coordination and search costs, supply chains are reorganizing themselves into flexible interactive trading mechanisms, with advanced optimisation and matching algorithms that link customers with suppliers in order to target transactions (Lee et al., 1997; Lee and Whang, 2001). The IOSs described by Barret and Konsynski (1982) where based on proprietary systems, requiring significant asset-specific investments. Since then, and in addition to the reduced role of asset-specific investments, the role and impact of relational integration and systems integration along the supply chain is becoming visible in more and more industries (e.g. Clemons and Row, 1989, 1991; Zaheer and Venkatraman, 1994; Short and Venkatraman, 1992; Bakos, 1991a,b). As a result of the factors discussed in this paper, we expect to see structural changes in many industries. The role of electronic markets in a particular context may heavily depend on the strategy that the market aims to execute (Markus and Soh, 2002). We hope that the conceptual framework and the propositions provided here will inspire and provoke other researchers in their analyses of electronic markets and exchanges.
