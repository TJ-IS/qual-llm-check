# Strategies for value creation in electronic markets: towards a framework for managing evolutionary change

Ray Hackney<sup>a,\*</sup>, Janice Burn<sup>b</sup>, Angel Salazar<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Business Information Technology, Manchester Metropolitan University, Aytoun Building Aytoun Street, M1 3GH Manchester, UK

<sup>b</sup>Edith Cowan University, Joondalup, WA, Australia

Received 30 May 2002; revised 1 September 2003; accepted 1 February 2004

## Abstract

This paper reviews the theoretical relevance of strategic analysis in eMarkets and advocates for a co-evolutionary approach to value creation and the management of change. A view of strategy is recognized which includes an evaluation of the stages and processes of evolution for these markets. A framework encompassing critical elements such as e-market ecosystems, e-alliances, e-knowledge and e-systems, is developed and advanced for further strategic analysis. The model is believed to be of value for conceptual assessment of virtual business communities where unique attention to continuous innovation and dynamic organizational adaptation are considered imperative for competitive success. This is increasingly characterized by paradoxical issues, such as competing in the present time and building long-term strategic competences for the future. The model is validated with relevant examples and a substantive case study.

## 1. Introduction

The study of strategies for value creation in electronic markets, or eMarkets, has been considered by a number of scholars and has been variously associated with concepts from value chain frameworks (Porter, 2001), resource-based view of the firm (Barney, 1991), transaction cost economics (Sarkar et al., 2002), strategic network theory (Dyer and Singh, 1998) intangible assets (Subramani and Walden, 2001) and ‘value drivers’ (Amit and Zott, 2001). Most of this body of research has, however, emphasized the structure and outputs of electronic marketplaces, instead of focusing on the process of co-evolution within an environment of constant change (Burn and Hackney, 2002; Hackney and Burn, 2001).

It may be argued that these frameworks are inappropriate and redundant in the post-net era. Beinhocker (1999a) suggests that what is needed is a model of a world where innovation, change and uncertainty are the natural state of competitive engagement. Strategy may be associated with many contradictions and dilemmas as evidenced by the Red Queen effect (Kauffman, 1995). The Red Queen in Through the Looking Glass remarks “It takes all the running you can do to keep in the same place”. In a system of co-evolution, when the predator learns to run faster, the prey starts to climb trees and then the predator develops alternative means of pursuit. Long-term sustainable advantage is not possible without continual adaptation (Venkatraman, 1994; Venkatraman and Henderson, 1998; Iansiti and Levien, 2004).

Advantage tends to be competed away quite quickly and increasingly in sophisticated new global markets. A study of the performance of more than 400 organisations over 30 years reveals that companies find it difficult to maintain higher performance levels than their competitors for more than about 5 years at a time (Beinhocker, 1999b). In an ecosystem of co-evolution, adaptation can be seen as the attempt to optimise systems riddled with conflicting constraints. It is therefore critical to reconcile opposing issues of tension, dilemmas or polarities. Traditional strategic approaches are incomplete since they over emphasise executives’ abilities to forecast and predict in a highly competitive, high-velocity market and under emphasise the challenge of actually creating effective strategies and the decisive role of human agency within this process (Peppard and Breu, 2003). Given uncertain environments, strategies must also be robust and allow for the organisation to pursue a package of potentially paradoxical issues simultaneously, such as competing in the present time and building long-term strategic competences for the future (Zott et al., 2000; Hackney and Burn, 2001; Brunn et al., 2002; Salazar et al., 2003; Monge et al.).

## 2. Towards a framework for managing evolutionary change

The framework shown in Fig. 1 offers a model to conceptualise the eMarket strategy in an organisation and focuses on value creation and managing change in electronic markets. The features within Fig. 1 represent an articulation of the continuous evolution of organizations and their inter-relationships within eMarkets. The degree to which virtuality can evolve in the organisation will relate to the extent to which the strategic change management factors, such as market ecosystem, alliances, knowledge and systems, are congruent. When these are not aligned then the organisation will find itself dysfunctional in its exploitation of the virtual marketplace and so be unable to derive the maximum value benefits from its strategic position.

The path for evolution is not necessarily rigid. Firms can follow various alternative paths for evolution. They can adopt an ‘exploiter’ strategy focusing on maximising the effectiveness of business processes along the value chain through ICT; or follow an ‘explorer’ strategy to extend market reach by strengthening structural alliances and interorganisational partnerships along the supply/demand chain; or they could choose an ‘innovator’ strategy to attempt to integrate all these processes into a virtual value chain and to move into new eMarkets. (For a more elaborate explanation on these alternative paths see Burn and Hackney, 2002.)

## 2.1. eMarket ecosystems

The post-net era is driven by such phenomena as the World Wide Web, mass customisation, compressed product life cycles, new distribution channels and new forms of integrated organizations. The most fundamental elements of doing business are changing and a totally new business environment is emerging.

Electronic markets have evolved into interconnected one-stop shops, providing specialized services with affiliate firms. Worldwide Internet Commerce, both B2B and B2C, will reach \$12.8 trillion in 2006 as estimated by Forrester Research (2001). North America would represent a majority of this trade, but its dominance will fade, as some Asian-Pacific and Western European countries should reach hyper growth in 2002. There are three types of marketplace: those controlled by sellers, those controlled by buyers, and those controlled by neutral third parties (Berryman et al., 1998). Marketplaces controlled by sellers are usually set up by a single vendor seeking many buyers. Its aim is to create or retain value and market power in any transaction. Buyer-controlled marketplaces are set up by or for one or more buyers with the aim of shifting power and value in the marketplace to the buyer’s side. Electronic markets are expanding to almost every industry sector, including retail, manufacturing, aerospace and defense, healthcare and pharmaceuticals, and chemicals.

These electronic business models have evolved from basic electronic procurement and ecommerce into what is referred here as an eMarket Ecosystem. This eMarket environment is characterised by rapid exchange of information within a virtual network of customers and suppliers working together to create value-added processes (Wigand and Benjamin, 1995; Burn and Barnett, 2000). Described here as the eMarket, it brings with it new forms of IT-enabled intermediation, virtual supply chains, increasing knowledge intensity and information-based business architecture strategies. Core business processes may need to be rethought and redesigned, new organisational forms and interorganisational structures may need to be developed and the emphasis will be on collaboration rather than competition.

Moore (1993, 1997) suggests that businesses are not just members of certain industries but parts of an ecology that incorporates different industries. The driving force is not pure competition but co-evolution. The term co-evolution originated in biology. It refers to successive changes among two or more ecologically interdependent but unique species such that their evolutionary trajectories become intertwined over time. As these species adapt to their environment, they also adapt to one another. The result is an ecosystem of partially interdependent species that adapt together. This interdependence is often symbiotic (each species helps the other), but it can also be commensalist (one species uses the other). Competitive interdependence can emerge as well: one species may drive out the other, or both species may evolve into distinct, noncompetitive niches. Interdependence can change, too, such as when external factors like the climate or geology shift. An organisation which undertakes strategic planning without understanding the impact on the ecosystem as a whole is ignoring the reality of the networked environment in which it operates (Iansiti and Levien, 2004).

The eMarket ecosystem is seen as “an economic community supported by a foundation of interacting organisations and individuals. Over time they co-evolve their capabilities and roles, and tend to align themselves with the direction set by one or more central companies” (p. 26). The ecosystems are identified to evolve through four distinct stages and at each of these phases the ecosystem faces different leadership, cooperative and competitive challenges. This ecosystem can be viewed as the all-embracing eMarket culture within which an organization maintains equilibrium, as shown in Fig. 2.

## 2.2. eAlliances

Interest in strategic alliances has led to a growing stream of research by strategy and organizational scholars who have examined some of the causes and consequences of such partnerships. In this article, the authors do not intend to review the vast and growing field of research (for a review see Koza and Lewin, 1998; Gulati, 1998; Wilkinson and Young, 2002; Peppard and Breu, 2003). Instead we will review relevant empirical evidence to illustrate that, with the appropriate organizational conditions and competitive environment, firms would tend to follow a continuous process of co-evolution and adaptation, where information and expertise is coherently managed and exploited to support future eBusiness initiatives and enhanced virtual alliances.

For example, most of the big players in the \$400 billion aerospace and defense business are embracing emarkets, and industry-wide alliances are increasingly becoming an important element for long-term survival. A specific example is Aeroxchange.com, which streamlines the purchase of airline supplies and spare parts and allows members to see one another’s inventories. Launched in February 2001, its 33 members include Northwest Airlines and Federal Express, representing a total of \$50 billion in annual purchases. Boeing, United Airlines and KLM have launched consortiums, in an attempt to attract customers. Cordiem, which launched in 2001, connects airlines and their suppliers in one integrated exchange. Cordiem is the result of the merger of two other exchanges: MyAircraft.com run by Honeywell, United Technologies and Goodrich Aerospace; and AirNewCo, founded by nine airlines including United, American, Delta and British Airways. Geared toward the defense side of the industry, Exostar, launched in 2000, is run as a separate company by BAE Systems, Boeing, Lockheed Martin, Raytheon and Rolls-Royce. In AVSupport Online, buyers and sellers can connect through this online marketplace. Buyers can cross-reference purchasing data, read government reports and manage all their RFQ transactions. Through subsidiary PartsLogistics.com, 540,000 active buyers find solutions to their logistics needs. Alliances with EBay, to participate in aircraft-part auctions, and Google, for career listings, expand the company’s services.

In the healthcare industry, Verticalnet.com and Premier Inc., a healthcare purchasing alliance owned by more than 200 of the nation’s hospitals and health-care systems in the US, formed an alliance to manage contract sourcing and administrative processes on behalf of its nearly 1600 members and other supply chain partners. Drug chains such as CVS and Drug Emporium, which have established partnerships with medical insurance providers in the US, can offer a better price and prescription benefits package to consumers.

Pharmaceutical companies can excel in establishing long-term relationships with users of their drugs via the web by providing personalized information about their medications. Many pharmaceutical companies have been strong in using web technology to streamline the process of ordering supplies. For Bristol-Myers Squibb (BMS), with revenue of \$18.22 billion, web technology is an integral part of the 115-year-old drug company’s efforts to reduce inventories, improve customer service, slash order-cycle time and contain expenses. In 1999, e-business achievements included more than \$90 million in savings, the result of deployments of Ariba’s Buyer e-procurement software. It streamlined ordering of PCs and other equipment across business units worldwide. In 2000, BMS extended the relationship with Ariba to let its 30,000 buyers companywide access a broad range of suppliers and compare different offers in real time through Ariba’s Sourcing network.

In the Chemical Industry, a good example of purposeful partnership is Chemdex and DuPont. Chemdex coupled with Dupont’s buying power created Industria, an eMarketplace for the fluid processing industry. Instead of building a Net market from scratch, this new venture is smartly assembling its firm from existing resources. A more recent example is a consortium of 12 major chemical companies which created a global e-commerce exchange aimed at lowering transaction costs for suppliers and clients. The group, which includes BASF AG, BP Amoco, Dow Chemical and Dupont, invested \$150 million formed a separate company, which operate an online marketplace that addresses supply chain inefficiencies and offers integrated products and services for buying and selling chemicals. The size of the targeted market is estimated to be \$400 billion. The number of eMarkets worldwide is multiplying, but as these multi-firm sites begin to collide within industries, they have started to interconnect with other marketplaces to survive (Nambisan, 2000).

Some scholars point out that these new trends on collaboration in eBusiness are actually counterintuitive and that collaboration does not naturally lead to synergy (Eisenhardt and Galunic, 2000). Where synergies are achieved the managers have mastered the corporate strategic process of coevolving. These managers routinely change the web of collaborative links—everything from information exchanges to shared assets to multi-business strategies—among businesses. The result is a shifting web of relationships that exploits fresh opportunities for synergies and drops deteriorating ones, as shown in Fig. 3.

## 2.3. eSystems

There is a symbiotic relationship between the elements of the model already discussed and the web systems element. Alliances and knowledge enable partner firms to devise and upgrade their web-based systems, which would be then aligned to the enhanced business strategy for value creation. The development and alignment of e-systems with strategic alliances can contribute to understanding the future requirements for information and dependence of partners on each other.

In today’s marketplace, e-Systems are increasingly important to support the growing web of interorganisational relationships and strategic alliances. Current e-Systems have not only evolved into sophisticated technological platforms that integrate ERP, SCM and CRM systems, but also enable strategic partner alliances, market positioning and knowledge dissemination and creation (Ash and Burn, 2003; Rashid et al., 2002; Mandal and Kim, 2002; Yamada, 2003).

## 2.4. eKnowledge

In today’s eMarkets, knowledge, particularly knowledge networks, has become one of the most strategically important resources and learning the most strategically important capability (Larsson et al., 1998; Zack, 1999; Macleod, 1999; Hansen et al., 1999; Buchel and Raub, 2002). However, initiatives being undertaken to develop and exploit organisational knowledge are of little value if they are not explicitly linked to the overall business strategy directed at creating value. In turn, the strategic process must reflect the continual learning capabilities of the organization, which are often acquired and strengthened through strategic partnerships. One solution is to apply a perpetual strategy process which will embed knowledge and competitive intelligence into a continual monitoring of the external and internal environments and induce continual re-engineering of the organisation in line with shifting demands (Tyson, 1998). Such a strategy should be resource-based emphasising distinctive, firm-specific and hard to copy assets, skills and knowledge. These are generally referred to as core competencies or distinctive capabilities that confer competitive advantage on the business (Pitt and Clarke, 1999). Strategic management or management of strategic innovation is the purposeful orchestration and directed application of such organisational skills and knowledge. Such strategies, however, are not so easily implemented in a virtual community where concepts such as assets, skills and knowledge may not be firm-specific but rather stem from the synergistic coalescence of multiple organisations networked in the virtual chain. It is nevertheless vital that as organisations become more virtual, experience, information and expertise is coherently managed and used to support future eBusiness initiatives and enhanced virtual alliances (Burn and Hackney, 2002; Hackney and Burn, 2001; Burn and Barnett, 1999).

Competitive strategy must drive the firm’s knowledge management strategy but categorising what an organisation knows and should know about its industry or competitive position is complex (Zack, 1999; Hansen et al., 1999; Havens and Knapp, 1999). If it were easy then competitive advantage would be unsustainable. As a first step the organisation needs to determine the value of knowledge to its business (Malhorta, 2000). In other words it must align its knowledge resources and capabilities to the intellectual resources of its strategy. This should be measured against two dimensions and related to knowledge aggressiveness. The first dimension addresses the extent to which an organisation is primarily a creator or user of knowledge and the second addresses whether the primary sources of knowledge are internal or external. These together will provide the strategic framework in which knowledge management strategy needs to be developed. Internal knowledge is obviously especially valuable and should be exploited but as witnessed by insider trading deals, this needs to be conducted in an ethical and legal manner. Further, in today’s competitive markets such niche or monopolistic positions are seriously challenged. In the virtual organisation exploitation of external knowledge can take place through the value network of strategic alliances to create knowledge advantage within a bounded rationality. This can be further extended along the supply chain into unbounded environments to include customers in knowledge exchange.

Exploration and exploitation of knowledge typically occur in different parts of the organisation and are often separated temporally and culturally. Balancing these requires a knowledge culture, transfer and integration capability, which is itself strategic and subject to constant reevaluation. The choice of exploitation, exploration or innovation reflects the overall competitive business strategy of the organization and the level of maturity and specialisation of the particular industry (Macher et al., 2002). Strategic positioning within this framework reflects the knowledge management strategy in alignment with the business strategy. These two together can radically change the organisation and the way it is positioned within the marketplace.

Knowledge is therefore dependent on individuals so that a rigid distinction between strategy and organisation is inappropriate and, indeed, successful knowledge strategies involve almost every aspect of a company’s organisational design. This is not something that can be lightly undertaken but it can be part of a staged growth development, which should be implemented through an iterative and parallel development process rather than linearly.

## 3. Validating the framework with a substantive case study: Biospace Inc

Fig. 4 illustrates the co-evolution of alliances, systems and knowledge and the resulting value creation at BioSpace Inc. BioSpace is a specialized provider of web-based products and information services to the life sciences. BioSpace had established a series of strategic alliances and collaborations to innovate products and services from its conception. The company’s premier Website, www.biospace.com, offers both public and subscriptionbased life sciences information and resources, including its competitive intelligence offering, the BioSpace CCIS<sup>e</sup> Solution. In 1998 the website experienced 295,000 unique monthly visitors and 1.6 million page views, compared to 2002 with over one million unique monthly visitors and more than seven million page views (Salazar et al., 2003).

In partnership with National Institutes of Health, BioSpace developed the NIH IntraMall, an enterprise-specific portal and electronic marketplace. The NIH IntraMall, operated by BioSpace since 1999, was the first of a suite of Enterprise Portal Services developed by BioSpace to enhance information management and knowledge sharing in the life sciences industry. The NIH IntraMall was designed to positively impact the agency’s supplies expenditures by streamlining the procurement process and significantly lowering administrative costs. With BioSpace’s efforts, the IntraMall product database has grown from 100,000 to over 1.8 million, and new technology was implemented to automate purchase card reconciliation.

BioSpace’s Enterprise Solutions are led by an information management software application, the BioSpace LInKS<sup>e</sup> Solution (Life Sciences Information and Knowledge Solution). This commercial application of BioSpace LInKS<sup>e</sup> Solution has been purchased and deployed by leading pharmaceutical companies where it allows research teams and individuals to access, integrate, rate, share and organize via the company Intranet disparate information easily and quickly. The user views this content through a personalized, single interface that is designed to facilitate knowledge sharing. Using the speed and reach of the Internet, coupled with the security of the corporate intranet, this tool improves productivity and efficiency for life sciences researchers and business professionals. The system is designed to enhance the organization and sharing of new information at all stages of product development, thus accelerating the overall pace of innovation within a single pharmaceutical or global cluster of biotechnology companies.

BioSpace had also established a strategic alliance with Acurian, a leader in accelerating clinical trial launches for the biopharmaceutical industry. Acurian licensed BioSpace’s Clinical Competitive Intelligence System technology (CCIS<sup>e</sup>) to use with their solutions that accelerate clinical trial launches. BioSpace’s clinical development database tracks drugs in clinical development from more than 700 biotechnology and pharmaceutical companies worldwide. The BioSpace database lists more than 7000 trials in 350 disease targets and is updated daily (Salazar et al., 2003).

BioSpace in partnership with Sopheon, an international knowledge management software and services provider, offer a co-branded network of scientific and technical experts for the life sciences community. Through the co-branded site, clients can consult with a certified network of experts via telephone or e-mail, or purchase documents composed by these experts to obtain timely, high-quality, confidential answers to pressing technical and business questions. Additionally, the experts are available for on-going client consulting engagements. The network of experts is made up of specialists recruited from universities, national laboratories, consulting groups and the ranks of recent industry retirees.

BioSpace, in collaboration with StartUp University, helps entrepreneurs navigate the first steps of creating a venture-backed company. BioSpace and StartUp University have produced a series of live ‘how to’ seminars for biotechnology and life science entrepreneurs. BioSpace also offers online Regional Campaigns. These bi-annual, online cooperative marketing campaigns serve the key biotech/life science ‘clusters’ such as Biotech Bay, Biotech Beach, BioNC, BioCapital, Genetown, and other regions competing to attract capital, people and resources.

## 4. Discussion

In response to the staged nature of evolution of the ecosystem, organisations may need to cultivate evolving populations of strategies. Kauffman (1995) refers to this evolutionary process as the development of fitness landscapes where the corporations will search for the high points of their competencies and objectives. This process of evolutionary search is continuous but should also employ parallelism with multiple landscapes and strategic teams employing different techniques to explore the terrain. Such strategies force people to deal with ambiguity and accelerate constructive conflict (Eisenhardt et al., 1997; Beinhocker, 1999a,b).

Successful adaptation also implies co-evolution between the organisation and the strategy model. Not only must strategy models be adapted to fit the unique characteristics of an organisation but also organisations need to evolve to benefit from the lessons incorporated into the strategic model and so both the organisation and model continually change and learn (Romano and Fjermestad, 2002). Iansiti and Levien (2004) suggest that the strategy choice for a company is governed primarily by the type of company it aims to be but also the business context in which it operates: the general level of turbulence and the complexity of relationships within the ecosystem. This choice revolves around three key strategies: niche strategy, keystone strategy or physical dominator strategy and will be the basis of continual review. This perpetual co-evolutionary process takes place within an ecosystem of evolving markets. The successful firm will be the one who maximises the value, which can be obtained from its strategic interorganisational alliances, leveraged by their electronic systems, and their understanding of knowledge co-creation (Bierly and Chakrabarti, 1996; Zack, 1999).

Therefore, firms must:

1. Continually review their current market and technology alliances, customers, suppliers and competitors,

2. Understand the implications of knowledge for organisational and network design,

3. Adjust the organisation’s external posture and conduct and build value through innovation.

## 5. Conclusion

The paper has outlined a range of approaches to organizational strategy in the post-Net era. It has considered the nature of eMarkets and has observed how these environments are clearly dynamic, complex and ambiguous. Traditional approaches to strategic analysis is therefore of limited use where attention is necessary to virtual competition and collaboration. The emphasis is upon innovation, adaptation and attention to rapidly changing environments. Under these conditions, driven by post-Net facilities and capabilities, organizations need to nurture their knowledge and capabilities, strengthen and renew strategic alliances, align information technology and systems, in order to sustain the value generation and facilitate business services innovation to meet the demands of dynamic global competition. Traditional, functional and prescriptive strategy models are clearly redundant in these dynamic circumstances. A number of exploratory empirical examples illustrated the co-evolutionary nature of strategy within an eMarket environment. Further longitudinal empirical research is being carried out by the authors to assess the adaptation and evolution processes within specific electronic marketplaces.
