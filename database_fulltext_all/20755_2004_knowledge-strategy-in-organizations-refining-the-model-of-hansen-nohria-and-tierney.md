# Knowledge strategy in organizations: refining the model of Hansen, Nohria and Tierney

Rens Scheepers<sup>\*</sup>, Krishna Venkitachalam, Martin R. Gibbs

Department of Information Systems, The University of Melbourne, 111 Barry Street, Carlton, Vic. 3010, Australia

Received 4 June 2004; revised 22 August 2004; accepted 24 August 2004

## Abstract

We assessed the model of knowledge strategy and IT support proposed by Hansen, Nohria and Tierney. Their main argument is that, in order to enable effective use of knowledge, organizations should select an 80/20 knowledge strategy mix of codification and personalization with one of these strategies dominant. We examined the knowledge strategy emphasis (codification or personalization) in each of four case organizations. These organizations represented different combinations of the codification and personalization knowledge strategy mix. Our cases indicate some support for Hansen et al.’s main argument, but also highlight the need for refinements to their model. Based on our assessment, we conclude that the advice of Hansen et al. is useful for deciding upon an initial strategic direction and setting suitable priorities. We propose the following refinements to the model. First, organizations may find it necessary to evolve their knowledge strategy mix over time, augmenting the alternate strategy as a result of the intertwined nature of knowledge processes. Second, we propose two strategic pathways that organizations could adopt on their journey towards effective use of organizational knowledge. Lastly, we identify additional considerations for deciding upon a particular knowledge strategy direction.

Keywords: Knowledge strategy; IT infrastructure; Codification; Personalization

## 1. Introduction

Many contemporary organizations view knowledge as one of their most important strategic assets. Accordingly, the choice of an effective knowledge strategy, and its implementation, are widely regarded as issues for the contemporary managerial agenda (Zack, 1999a; Earl, 2001). Tied to this are decisions about effective information technology (IT) infrastructure to support the chosen organizational knowledge management approach (Davenport and Prusak, 1998; Alavi and Leidner, 1999, 2001; Alavi, 2000).

The choices of knowledge strategy and appropriate supporting IT infrastructure are especially critical for knowledge-intensive organizations such as consulting and research firms. For these organizations, knowledge strategy and IT infrastructure decisions have a direct impact on their competitive position. Hansen et al., (1999) (hereafter HNT) have proposed a model to inform the choice of knowledge strategy and IT support for knowledge-intensive organizations. The HNT model is widely cited in the literature (Soliman and Spooner, 2000; Earl, 2001; Kautz, 2002; Maier and Remus, 2002) and was developed based on Hansen et al.’s work with organizations in the consulting sector. HNT have, however, claimed that “the strategy does not apply only to the world of consulting; we found that providers of health care and manufacturers of computers also need to choose a knowledge management approach that fits their needs and goals (e.g. as observed in HP, Dell and Access Health)” (Hansen et al., 1999, p. 110).

The HNT model suggests a choice between two alternative approaches to knowledge strategy; codification or personalization. Codification is a knowledge strategy that seeks to capture and store knowledge in explicit form (e.g. in documents and information systems), for subsequent transfer and (re)use by others within the organization. Within this approach, the role of IT is to support the storage and retrieval of this form of knowledge by people throughout the organization as and when required (Hansen et al., 1999). Personalization, on the other hand, is a knowledge strategy that seeks to link people to each other to foster ‘person-to-person’ sharing of knowledge rather than storing it (Hansen et al., 1999). In a personalization strategy, IT is used to extend interpersonal networks and the ability to connect and communicate with one another (Hansen et al., 1999). HNT’s central argument is that, to enable effective use of organizational knowledge, depending on the nature of their business, organizations should predominantly pursue either a codification or personalization strategy, but still draw on the alternative strategy in support. Investments in supporting IT infrastructure should reflect this strategic choice.

While the HNT model is often cited, their advice appears to address mainly practical aspects of knowledge strategy decisions at the onset of a knowledge management initiative. In this paper, we question whether their model is adequate given the dynamics of knowledge strategy in organizations. We addressed this question using a case study approach with four organizations from various industries. The four case organizations represent different combinations of HNT’s knowledge strategies and supporting IT infrastructure. The first case is a global management consulting organization that has an extensive investment in IT infrastructure to support a predominantly codified knowledge strategy. The second case is a small Australasian consulting organization in the educational sector where the investment in IT infrastructure is minimal and the knowledge strategy is predominantly personalized. The third case is an Australian manufacturing organization that has a moderate investment in IT infrastructure; their knowledge strategy is implicit and has no strong emphasis on either codification or personalization. The final case is a public-sector organization in Australia that provides research and statistical data for economic policy planning and development; this organization has an extensive investment in IT infrastructure to support both codification and personalization.

The structure of this paper is as follows. We review the pertinent literature on business strategy, knowledge strategy, and IT infrastructure support to manage knowledge. We then explain the concepts of HNT’s model for knowledge strategy, implications for supporting IT infrastructure, and effective use of organizational knowledge. Following that, we outline our research methodology and present the four case studies. We compare HNT’s arguments with the findings from the cases, which indicate some support for the model in the first three of our four cases. Based on the findings from our cases, we propose several refinements to the original HNT model.

## 2. Organizational knowledge and supporting IT infrastructure

Before we discuss HNT’s model of knowledge strategy and its implications, we review literature related to HNT’s ideas with respect to knowledge in organizations, knowledge strategy, and supporting IT infrastructure.

There have been numerous efforts in the literature to define knowledge and knowledge management in organizations (Prusak, 1997; Davenport and Prusak, 1998; Alavi and Leidner, 2001; Muller-Merbach, 2004; Patriotta, 2004). Alavi and Leidner’s (2001) define knowledge as ‘the potential to influence action’ and knowledge management as ‘building core competencies and understanding of strategic knowhow’. Many authors (including HNT) distinguish between tacit and explicit knowledge (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995; Sveiby, 1997; Hansen et al., 1999; Alavi and Leidner, 2001). Explicit knowledge can be expressed in words and numbers and shared in the form of data, formula, and specifications (Nonaka, 1994; Nonaka and Konno, 1998; Zack, 1999b). This kind of knowledge can be formalized and articulated in documents, and shared electronically through IT infrastructures such as intranets (Scott, 1998; Damsgaard and Scheepers, 2001). Tacit knowledge on the other hand is personal and deeply rooted in an individual’s actions, skills, and experience as well as in their ideals, values, and emotions (Polanyi, 1966; Nonaka, 1994; Sveiby, 1997; Stenmark, 2000; Whitley and Hedesstrom, 2000). Compared to explicit knowledge, tacit knowledge is difficult to formalize, and hence harder to articulate and share with others. Avenues for sharing tacit knowledge include apprenticeships and ‘story-telling’ in organizations (Brown and Duguid, 1991; Nonaka and Konno, 1998). In addition to individual perspectives on knowledge, the community perspective on knowledge suggests that much of the organizations’ knowledge that is created and shared via ‘communities of practice’(Brown and Duguid, 1991; Wenger, 1998; Wenger and Snyder, 2000). Such knowledge is typically shared socially and informally and hence is more challenging to ‘manage’ organizationally. IT support for the facilitation of communities of practice includes discussion tools and other interactive technologies (Brown and Duguid, 1991; Wasko and Faraj, 2000).

The choice of a knowledge strategy has been closely linked to the organization’s competitive positioning (Zack, 1999a). As such it is argued that an organization’s knowledge strategy should be considered a key component of its business strategy (Grant, 1996; Hansen et al., 1999; Zack, 1999b; Earl, 2001). In addition, the organization’s knowledge strategy should provide direction in determining how information technology can enable and support knowledge work in the organization (Davenport et al., 1996; Dunford, 2000; Earl, 2001). Despite these benefits, it has been noted that organizations often do not explicitly state their knowledge strategy (Zack, 1999a; Maier and Remus, 2002). In this respect, HNT advocate an explicitly stated and deliberate knowledge strategy.

Much debate surrounds the role of IT infrastructure in supporting organizational knowledge processes (Davenport and Prusak, 1998; Alavi and Leidner, 1999, 2001; Damsgaard and Scheepers, 2001). While the use of e-mail and other Internet-based technologies has enabled the vision of knowledge sharing in organizations, the challenge of managing this process and leveraging knowledge assets in organizations remains a concern (McDermott, 1999; Newell et al., 2001). Some authors have argued for a strategic emphasis on IT investment, including the IT infrastructure necessary to support knowledge management initiatives (Weill and Broadbent, 1998). Here again HNT’s ideas hold specific implications for the level of investment in IT, depending on the choice of knowledge strategy.

## 3. The HNT model: codification and personalization knowledge strategies

HNT propose two main organizational knowledge strategies, codification and personalization. They developed these concepts based on their experience in the consulting sector, particularly with management consulting and strategy consulting organizations and have argued that the alternatives in their model are the central choices “facing virtually all companies in the area of knowledge management” (Hansen et al., 1999, p. 107). We now summarize HNT’s definitions of these two strategies and the required IT investment to support each strategy.

A codification strategy aims to capture and codify knowledge in explicit form (e.g. in documents, databases) and make these available to everyone in the organization for further reuse. In this manner, organizations invest once in the development of explicit knowledge, store it, and are able to reuse it as often as required. As a result, this ‘reuse economics avoids the cost associated with the ‘reinvention’ of knowledge assets.

The IT investment required to support a codification knowledge strategy is significant. HNT advise that organizations should invest in an extensive IT infrastructure to codify knowledge. The IT infrastructure may include sophisticated electronic repositories and powerful search engines to access codified knowledge. The investment in IT infrastructure should enable people easy access to reusable codified knowledge. HNT suggests that this is the ‘people-to-documents’ approach to knowledge management.

A personalization strategy draws on interpersonal relationships to mobilize and share knowledge in tacit form across the organization. Thus, the organization aims to create and facilitate networks between people to share and learn from their individual skills, experiences, and expertise. This results in an ‘expert economics’ approach that allows people easy access to various experts in the organization.

Compared to the codification approach, in the personalization strategy, organizations need only a moderate investment in IT infrastructure to connect various experts in the organization. This infrastructure may include e-mail systems, videoconferencing, online discussion networks and other collaborative tools. The investment in IT infrastructure can hence be typified as a ‘people-to-people’ approach to knowledge management.

We believe HNT’s intent was to develop a normative model for knowledge strategy choice in organizations; their argument is that, depending on the nature of business, organizations should choose a particular strategy mix to use knowledge effectively. This means that organizations should pursue either codification as the predominant strategy and personalization in support or vice versa. HNT warn organizations against a dual emphasis on both strategies at the same time. Instead, they argue that these strategies should be implemented in an 80–20 mix; 80% of the total knowledge management efforts should focus on the dominant strategy and 20% on the alternative. This knowledge strategy mix should also be mirrored in terms of suitable IT infrastructure to support the predominant strategy.

HNT put forth some specific recommendations for an appropriate knowledge strategy mix. Organizations should consider the nature of their business, how they serve their customers and their business economics in deciding upon their knowledge strategy mix. HNT use the example of management consulting firms to illustrate the choice of a dominant codification strategy. If the organization is involved in providing ‘standardized’ products or solutions to its clients, a predominant codification strategy (with personalization in support) should be chosen. This choice would leverage the reuse economics of the organization’s knowledge. HNT compare this choice to the example of strategy consulting firms. These businesses develop non-standard solutions to address unique client problems. Such organizations draw on their consultants’ experience and interpersonal interactions in coming up with a new solution. For these consulting organizations, a dominant personalization strategy (with codification in support) would be more appropriate. This underpins the expert economics of the organization.

In summary, HNT have proposed the following:

HNT 1. The organization’s nature of business (and their business economics) should determine their knowledge strategy choice (predominantly personalization or codification) with the alternative strategy in support (80/20 mix).

HNT 2. The organization’s knowledge strategy mix should determine the level and focus of the investment in supporting IT infrastructure.

HNT 3. Organizations which pursue an appropriate knowledge strategy choice should have effective use of knowledge (in line with HNT 1 and HNT 2).

In this paper, we assess the applicability of each of these normative arguments in four case organizations. Section 4 explains the methodology we used to assess the HNT model and its advice.

## 4. Methodology

We used a case study approach to examine the knowledge strategy and supporting IT infrastructure in four organizations. The case study approach enabled us to assess the nature of each organization’s business, their knowledge strategy and supporting IT infrastructure, and use of organizational knowledge in depth (Benbasat et al., 1987; Eisenhardt, 1989; Galliers, 1991). We decided upon the four organizations after a series of preliminary interviews in which we explored each organization’s knowledge strategy emphasis (codification or personalization) and supporting IT infrastructure.

We then assessed the effect of HNT’s different knowledge strategy emphases (codification/personalization), and supporting IT infrastructure, on the reported use of knowledge in each organization. As such we developed a multiple-case study design that enabled us to compare the effect of the different knowledge strategy emphases in similar and distinct business contexts (Neuman, 2000; Yin, 2003). As described in more detail below, in each case we analyzed the organization’s knowledge strategy, taking into account a range of evidence (Walsham, 1995). Our research design is depicted in Table 1.

We use the pseudonyms, ConsultCo, EduCo, ResearchCo and ManufactCo for the case organizations. These cases were chosen for the following reasons:

(a) Each organization had a stated desire to make effective use of its knowledge (e.g. in mission or vision statements).

(b) Each organization’s knowledge strategy could be related to a different emphasis on HNT’s codification and personalization strategies as shown in Table 1. This allowed for a ‘literal replication’ (Yin, 2003, p. 47) of the model.

(c) The four organizations had different levels of IT infrastructure to support their knowledge strategies.

Data was collected from multiple sources including formal and informal interviews, documents, and by inspection of systems. Interviews were our key source of evidence. We conducted a total of 32 formal interviews, including six follow-up interviews as shown in Table 2. The number of follow-up interviews in each case appears in parentheses in the table.

Formal interviews were semi-structured using an interview guideline (Neuman, 2000; Yin, 2003). The interview guideline was developed from themes derived from the research literature on knowledge strategy and IT infrastructure. On average, the interviews lasted for around an hour. The interviews were recorded and transcribed. The transcribed data were subsequently verified with the interviewees to check for transcription errors (Neuman, 2000) and to improve the internal validity of data collection (Yin, 2003). Separate notes were taken during the interviews and formally documented thereafter.

Follow-up interviews were used to clarify our understanding of the knowledge strategy, IT infrastructure, and knowledge use in each organization. They were also used to confirm our analysis of material collected in the earlier interviews in line with the principle of dialogical reasoning as suggested by Klein and Myers (1999).

In assessing the effectiveness of knowledge use, given the choice of knowledge strategy in a case, we drew on statements from our interviews that reflected (either positively or negatively) on the effectiveness of knowledge processes and supporting IT infrastructure. For example, if mostly positive statements were made in these respects, we would assess this as being reflective of effective use of knowledge in the organization (and vice versa). In this regard, we considered statements that referred to individuals’ use of codified knowledge as well as statements about knowledge exchange between individuals and groups (an approach consistent with, e.g. Murray, 2002).

## 5. Case studies

In this section, we present the four case studies. The cases describe the knowledge strategies, supporting IT infrastructure and the effectiveness of knowledge use in the organizations.

## 5.1. Case 1—ConsultCo

## 5.1.1. Nature of business and economics

ConsultCo is a large, multi-national consulting organization with offices around the world. They provide consulting services in the areas of management and technology. Knowledge is regarded as their principal asset and main revenue engine. As a consulting organization, ConsultCo’s main business is the codification, reuse and sale of knowledge.

## 5.1.2. Knowledge strategy

ConsultCo has an articulated knowledge strategy and a dedicated knowledge management division. The staff of the knowledge management division plays a variety of roles and responsibilities such as knowledge taxonomists, knowledge analysts, and knowledge managers responsible for different regions of the business (e.g. Australia, Europe, and North America). Knowledge work at ConsultCo is highly codified and strong emphasis has been placed on knowledge processes such as capturing, storing and reusing knowledge in explicit forms.

## 5.1.3. Supporting IT infrastructure

ConsultCo has made large investments over many years in their IT infrastructure to support their knowledge management efforts. Their IT infrastructure includes search engines to locate and map information on prior projects, numerous databases, high bandwidth networks and an intranet. They also developed a ‘knowledge portal’ with rich features to enable quick navigation and access to codified knowledge. In addition, research databases provide capabilities for internal research and integration with external sources of information and knowledge.

ConsultCo’s IT infrastructure provides strong support for knowledge reuse within the organization. This enables consultants to work on client problems and draw on the codified knowledge of other consultants who have developed solutions for similar problems elsewhere in the world. The organization’s IT infrastructure provides consultants with fast access to regularly updated information such as market trends, business intelligence, industry performance and product portfolios.

Despite its sophistication, the IT infrastructure at ConsultCo was mainly geared to support codification. However, some facilities did exist that could support person-toperson knowledge exchange.

## 5.1.4. Effectiveness of knowledge use

Even though they had a sophisticated IT infrastructure, interviewees commented on the difficulties they encountered when sharing knowledge on an interpersonal basis. In fact, at the time of our study the organization was taking steps to address these difficulties, such as recruiting more people to liaise between knowledgeable individuals across the organization. As a regional ConsultCo Technology Leader commented:

Certainly, a consulting firm cannot share knowledge without technology. You can create great things on the greatest technology, but if people don’t care to share knowledge, then it is difficult for the organization.

At the time of our study, the role of Knowledge Market Leader had recently been created. An important part of this role was to ‘connect’ people across the organization and to facilitate and encourage the formation of interpersonal networks that would foster knowledge exchange across the globe. The Knowledge Market Leader remarked:

The most important thing for knowledge management to work is building knowledge networks and sharing relationships. My philosophy is, building networks [is] are important in consulting. In the end the bigger the knowledge networks are, the more beneficial it is for organizational use of knowledge. Systems cannot do everything and we are limited by that.

A regional Knowledge Manager pointed out some difficulties in exchanging knowledge between groups in the organization:

It is all very much, sort of, everyone putting things into the knowledge management system and then pulling things out. But there is no real discussion between groups. And this is why we need to get everybody together and make use of knowledge more often. For example, I might be doing a SCM project and I might want to tap into SCM knowledge that is not in the portal<sup>.</sup> which means that my answer is probably out there somewhere but I cannot even look to see if it is there or not.

A regional representative made a similar observation: “It always comes to a situation where we need to do something more than codification.”

## 5.2. Case 2—EduCo

## 5.2.1. Nature of business and economics

EduCo is a non-profit organization that provides support and counseling services for international students studying in Australia and New Zealand. It is a member-based organization comprised of a large network of student advisers and counselors based in these two countries. The organization provides a range of customized services such as advice to international students about life and study abroad, counseling and orientation for students, and advice to universities on issues associated with international students. EduCo seeks to disseminate relevant information and knowledge between its members to ensure that necessary expertise is available as and when required. EduCo had a significant increase in the size of its member base in recent years.

## 5.2.2. Knowledge strategy

EduCo does not have an explicitly stated knowledge strategy. However, the organization’s mission statement reflects the importance of knowledge sharing between its members. The work done at EduCo is highly variable; most clients require a customized solution, but there are also some common aspects to solutions. Knowledge exchange relies strongly on interpersonal networks and there is a culture of knowledge sharing within the organization. Much of the organization’s documentation exists only in hardcopy format and is distributed between offices via the postal system.

## 5.2.3. Supporting IT infrastructure

The IT infrastructure at EduCo is minimal; the organization has an e-mail system and a website. The student advisers and counselors rely on e-mail to share knowledge with each other and interact with their clients. Due to the increase in its member base and minimal IT support, some knowledge problems have been identified. The Vice President for Policy, Planning and Development remarked:

One of the problems that we have had is, although we use the technology to share the information, we have not necessarily used the technology to store the information. And over the years that gets lost. That’s a huge problem for us as an organization because strategically we want to be able to capture the information and not reinvent the cycle all the time.

## 5.2.4. Effectiveness of knowledge use

At the time of our study, EduCo was experiencing some problems with their use of explicit knowledge. These problems seemed to be related to the lack of support for codified knowledge storage and reuse. The President explained:

There were particular instances where people would pass on their personally stored files. And there were often delays in sending that information and it was not very effective. [We want] central access to the knowledge in the organization, and an IT system to access files and records so that the organization [can] use its available knowledge efficiently.

EduCo was planning to invest in a suitable IT infrastructure to better support knowledge use across the organization. The Vice President for Professional Development outlined the rationale for this plan:

Technology is to help us manage better. There are pockets of knowledge everywhere in Australia. [We need] a centralized Web-based database to which members can contribute, and not re-invent the wheel.

## 5.3. Case 3—ManufactCo

## 5.3.1. Nature of business and economics

ManufactCo is an Australian firm involved in the manufacturing and sale of a chemical compound to the local and overseas market. The organization is comprised of process engineers, chemists, technology managers, operational staff and other technical specialists in chemical manufacturing. The process of manufacturing the chemical compound is knowledge-intensive and relies on the integration of expertise from different divisions of the organization.

The market demand for the compound in Australia is higher than ManufactCo’s current productive capacity. In the face of low-cost imports from overseas, the organization’s business strategy is to leverage the benefits of being a local producer and focus on cost reduction and production efficiency. Given the dynamics of their market, the organization is seeking to harness the knowledge and experience of its specialist employees so as to maximize the productive output of the plant.

## 5.3.2. Knowledge strategy

ManufactCo does not have an explicit knowledge strategy but there is a reference to knowledge in their mission statement. The organization is keen to formulate a specific knowledge strategy with the aim of increasing the productivity of the plant and reducing the existing redundancies and inefficiencies in their manufacturing and related business processes.

## 5.3.3. Supporting IT infrastructure

ManufactCo has a wide range of systems and applications in their IT infrastructure. This IT infrastructure has supported cost minimization objectives and also automated certain manufacturing processes. The IT infrastructure consists of databases, control and production scheduling systems, enterprise systems, an intranet and a website. They also have a sensor-based communications infrastructure at their clients’ premises that remotely detects the level of compound in stock and automatically alerts ManufactCo to re-supply them when stock drops below an agreed-to level.

From a knowledge process perspective, the IT infrastructure at ManufactCo lacks integration. The organization uses a number of applications on a variety of different computing platforms. Most applications are standalone systems used to support a discrete manufacturing or business process. Due to this lack of integration in the IT infrastructure, ManufactCo has problems with capturing, storing and using knowledge effectively. The organization does not effectively manage the codification and reuse of knowledge related to their manufacturing processes. The General Manager of Technology commented on the dispersed nature of their different systems and the lack of reusable, codified knowledge in their IT system:

Lots of the early work tends to die within the IT system. This is referring to the files sitting somewhere, no longer accessible conveniently, no longer accessed and known about by the new people in the organization. They don’t have a clue that the information exists. This eventually leads to knowledge reinvention.

## 5.3.4. Effectiveness of knowledge use

Problems with knowledge codification and reuse knowledge are not the only knowledge management problem faced by the organization. They also experience inherent difficulties in sharing knowledge due to the nature of the manufacturing environment. The CEO noted:

In a manufacturing plant getting one shift to talk to the next shift, talk to the technical guys, talk to the maintenance crews and actually pool their resources from past to current experiences for an optimal solution is actually quite difficult, particularly as [night] shift blokes are only on site a few days a month during daylight hours. During the rotations they don’t share very often. So in the last 8 years that I have been involved in this plant, they keep solving the problem over and over again.

The CEO further explained how ineffective sharing of knowledge led to production inefficiency:

We lost 5000 tons in production through the operating group last year. They got it wrong and all those things [that went wrong] are known and should not have occurred. So in a [worker’s] whole lifetime, a specific problem may only come up once. When it comes up, they have never seen it. But it has happened 15 years ago and someone else knew the answer. That’s the challenge we have to get beyond.

## 5.4. Case 4—ResearchCo

## 5.4.1. Nature of business and economics

ResearchCo is an Australian-based organization that provides a range of services including research data, statistical content, advice for policy development, and economic planning for its clients. ResearchCo has offices in every state capital city of Australia. The organization is comprised of researchers, economists, statisticians, and technology specialists. The business is primarily focused on the production and communication of pertinent research reports and economic analyses.

## 5.4.2. Knowledge strategy

ResearchCo has an articulated knowledge strategy and staff dedicated to knowledge management. These staff roles include Knowledge Director, Knowledge Analysts and dedicated IT Support personnel. Their knowledge strategy addresses codification processes such as the capture, integration and reuse of knowledge generated within and outside the organization in explicit forms. Their knowledge strategy also addresses the facilitation of interpersonal networks, collaboration and person-to-person knowledge exchange.

ResearchCo has been fostering a knowledge sharing culture over many years. This was reflected in the comments of a Senior IT Specialist working in the Knowledge Management Division:

Our CEO and his predecessor, they both and nearly all our people are well educated in knowledge management and we regard it [as] absolutely fundamental to the organization<sup>.</sup>the organizational culture has a very good influence in designing the knowledge strategy and what IT applications the organization should look for.

## 5.4.3. Supporting IT infrastructure

ResearchCo has invested in an IT infrastructure to support knowledge processes for more than two decades. This includes extensive networks, groupware and communication tools, stored audio and video clips, an intranet, and multiple databases. Knowledge is codified in these various repositories and integrated across the different components of the whole IT infrastructure by means of meta-data. For example, when an analyst develops a new report for a particular industry, s/he is automatically alerted to related documents such as past analysis, policy recommendations; audio and video clips and so forth, via metadata links.

In addition, sophisticated IT support exists for person-to-person and even workgroup-to-workgroup collaboration. These include instant messaging, videoconferencing, workflow applications, and collaborative workspaces where groups can exchange ideas and engage in online discussions with various subject experts across the organization.

## 5.4.4. Effectiveness of knowledge use

The IT infrastructure at ResearchCo supports both knowledge reuse and interpersonal sharing within the organization. This enables researchers, statisticians and economists to draw on the codified know-how of their counterparts working in other Australian cities. Policy documents, economic analysis, and other reports could all be accessed and used elsewhere in the country at any time. Moreover, the organization’s IT infrastructure provides the staff with quick access to regularly updated information such as global economic trends, statistical analysis on industries, business performance reports and other economic indicators such as inflation and unemployment rates.

The Director of Knowledge Management at ResearchCo elaborated:

The current IT infrastructure is dependent on our strategy and everything we have been working on in the last few years is towards improving the processes of getting something from one end to another<sup>.</sup> from the start to finish the processes are essentially linked to systems<sup>.</sup> Also, statistical reports published in one department can be accessed and used in another department<sup>.</sup> with the help of systems that links them through. So, IT is doing a lot to assist the processes.

In this way, the organization focuses on codifying the knowledge embedded in the business routines and processes of the organization. In addition, their IT infrastructure provides rich support for interpersonal knowledge sharing. The Director of Knowledge Management explained:

We have our databases, our information databases which are primarily in the Lotus Notes environment for explicit knowledge, but we also have support for effective tacit communication processes. We do this through mentoring programs and extensive development programs to share knowledge; and primarily encourage staff to contribute explicitly and tacitly through the system.

Other factors seem to contribute to their effective use of organizational knowledge. Employees tend to remain with ResearchCo for many years, senior management strongly supports and resources knowledge management initiatives, and a general awareness of the importance of knowledge sharing seems to prevail in the organization. As part of their induction, new employees are made aware of ResearchCo’s knowledge management approach, and how this can deliver value to the entire organization.

Over the years, the organization has experimented with different knowledge strategy approaches and IT support in an effort to enable effective use of their knowledge. As such they have learnt from past mistakes and improved their approach for supporting the knowledge processes within the organization. A senior IT specialist commented:

We restructured the knowledge according to what we knew people were looking for rather than what we thought they might look for<sup>.</sup> We arranged and re-arranged about four or five times in the last 12 years. And now we know that what we got at the moment actually works for us better than before<sup>.</sup> We go out and talk to people, watch them how they do things, then rearrange the information to make it easier for them<sup>.</sup> So, we have done this many times now and we have been on a continual improvement process for a long time.

ResearchCo’s Director of Knowledge Management reflected on the current use of knowledge in the organization:

We have these behavioral and technical mechanisms work for us better now to manage information and knowledge in the organization. We think we can just sit there and continue to manage pretty well as we are for a while.

## 6. Analysis and discussion

In this section we analyze the applicability of HNT’s model in each of the four case organizations. A comparative summary of knowledge strategy emphasis, IT support and effectiveness of knowledge use in each of the cases appears in Table 3. A summary of our findings in terms of HNT’s main claims appears in Table 4. Following the analysis, we raise some concerns with HNT’s advice in terms of knowledge strategy evolution.

ConsultCo’s knowledge strategy resembles HNT’s strategic 80/20 mix, with a dominant emphasis on codification, and personalization in support. Their choice of strategy is reflective of the nature of their business and the business economics of the organization (in particular the reuse economics). Thus, in this case, we find support for the claim HNT 1. ConsultCo has an extensive investment in IT infrastructure that supports its knowledge strategy of predominant codification. As such, the case also supports the claim HNT 2. At the same time, we observe that the organization is experiencing some challenges in facilitating person-to-person knowledge exchange. This could be attributed to an over-reliance on codification, and too little attention to personalization in support. The fact that ConsultCo was addressing these difficulties by creating new organizational roles to help facilitate interpersonal knowledge networks seems to underpin this observation. Hence, this case supports HNT 3 in the sense that the ineffective knowledge use in this case could be attributed to the lack of attention to personalization in support of their (dominant) codification approach. At the same time, the case highlights the difficulty that organizations can face in implementing the 80/20 knowledge strategy mix advocated by HNT. The situation at ConsultCo illustrates that the dominant strategy can ‘crowd out the supporting strategy to the extent that other knowledge management problems appear.

In contrast, EduCo has placed a dominant emphasis on personalization, with codification in support. Again, given the nature of their business, their knowledge strategy mix is in line with the claim HNT 1 in terms of the expert economics approach of personalization. The IT investment in EduCo is minimal, but seems to be reflective of a predominant personalization strategy as suggested by the claim HNT 2. However, as in ConsultCo, we note that the dominant strategy (personalization in this case) seems to draw attention away from the supporting strategy (codification). Hence, this case supports HNT 3; the lack of codification (to support the dominant strategy) in this case accounts for the knowledge ‘reinvention’ problems (student advisors keep solving similar problems again and again) encountered within the organization. As in ConsultCo, EduCo was aware of these problems and was seeking to address this shortcoming by planning more investment in IT support for codification.

ManufactCo does not have a specific emphasis on either codification or personalization. Based on the HNT model, the repetitive (process) nature of their business would suggest the need for a dominant codification strategy. ManufactCo has a moderate investment in IT infrastructure support, but this is not well integrated or aligned with the types of knowledge problems they face. As a result they seem to have difficulties in reusing and accessing knowledge. The organization is also experiencing challenges in sharing knowledge on an interpersonal basis (e.g. between shift workers). In these respects we have no reason to refute the advice of HNT; it could be argued that the examples of ineffective use of knowledge in this case (the flipside of HNT 3) could be attributed to the lack of a knowledge strategy in line with their business economics (HNT 1) and inadequate supporting IT infrastructure (HNT 2).

The knowledge strategy of ResearchCo seems to encompass both codification and personalization without a dominant focus on either approach. While ResearchCo’s business economics might suggest a reuse economics approach, they also strongly address the expert economics facet of their business in their knowledge strategy. HNT’s model would caution against such a dual approach (HNT 1). Furthermore, ResearchCo has made an extensive investment in a large, integrated IT infrastructure with rich capabilities for both codification and personalization support. Not only does their IT infrastructure enable employees to access and reuse codified knowledge across their branches, but it also has rich support for person-to-person and even workgroup-to-workgroup knowledge processes. In this respect, the case seems to refute both HNT 1 and HNT 2. Despite this, we do find evidence to suggest that ResearchCo is making effective use of their knowledge. While it can be argued that this questions the claim HNT 3, a number of contextual factors should be noted. ResearchCo has refined their knowledge strategy over many years. Their senior management has been highly supportive of (and personally committed to) knowledge initiatives in the organization. A climate of continuous learning and improvement seems to prevail in the organization. As such, the ResearchCo case illustrates that it is indeed possible for codification and personalization to be pursued in tandem without necessarily placing a dominant focus on either strategy. At the same time, such a tandem approach seems to be strongly contingent on the contextual factors noted in the case.

While the analysis of the ConsultCo and EduCo cases (and ManufactCo to some extent) seems to indicate support for the HNT model, the continuous evolution of knowledge strategy in the ResearchCo case raises issues for further consideration.

The HNT model implies that the 80/20 strategic mix (of either codification or personalization) remains constant over time. Based on our cases (especially the ResearchCo case), and also other literature sources we question this advice. For example, some scholars have painted a more dynamic picture of knowledge management processes in organizations (Bohn, 1994; Nonaka, 1994). As such, organizations may find it useful (if not necessary) to revise their knowledge strategy focus to ensure effective use of the knowledge. Others have also stressed the links between the organization’s knowledge strategy, business strategy, IT strategy and the dynamics of the organization’s competitive positioning (Mata et al., 1995; Grant, 1996; Hansen et al., 1999; Zack, 1999b; Earl, 2001; Murray, 2002).

As such, the support that we found for the HNT model could merely reflect the organizations’ knowledge strategy focus at a particular point in time (especially the EduCo and ConsultCo cases). Apart from the ManufactCo case, we noted that as the organizations grow in terms of their knowledge needs, they seek to expand their knowledge strategy focus (augmenting their strategies to address shortcomings). Strategy evolution may, however, continue over time. In fact as the ResearchCo case illustrates both the codification and personalization aspects may mature in the long run (e.g. a 60/40 combination). This suggests that HNT’s advice may be useful during the early stages of strategy development (e.g. to prioritize initiatives), but become less applicable over time. In fact, we see a risk in slavishly following HNT’s 80/20 rule; the organization could fixate on the dominant choice and ‘lock’ itself in strategically.

## 7. A refined model of organizational knowledge strategy evolution

Our cases do not suggest a ‘wholesale’ rejection of the HNT model; in fact the support that we find for aspects of the HNT model confirms its managerial value. In this section we propose a number of refinements to the original HNT model and its advice in terms of knowledge strategy choice, based on our assessment as discussed above. Our proposed refinements are depicted in Fig. 1.

We concur with HNT in advocating for either a dominant codification or personalization strategy, with the alternate strategy in support. In most of our cases organizational resource constraints would in fact necessitate the choice of a dominant focus. In this respect, we also support HNT’s caution that an equal emphasis on both personalization and codification during the initial stages of strategy implementation represents a high-risk approach. However, we believe that such a dominant emphasis on either strategy is only of concern at the onset of strategy implementation.

Fig. 1. A refined model of organizational knowledge strategy evolution.

We have argued that firms may find it necessary to evolve their knowledge strategy mix over time, augmenting the alternate strategy as a result of the intertwined nature of knowledge processes (and to avoid crowding out by the dominant strategy). In this respect, we noted that both EduCo and ConsultCo were planning more investment to support their alternate strategies. Hence, instead of a constant 80/20 strategy mix as HNT suggest, we propose two strategic pathways on the ‘journey’ towards effective organizational use of knowledge. In Fig. 1, Pathway 1 represents the scenario where an initial dominant emphasis is placed on personalization with some codification in support. As the strategy unfolds and the initial personalization priorities are met, more attention should be placed on codification (moving beyond the 80/20 mix as HNT suggests). Over time the strategy mix can shift even further, culminating in the scenario (as illustrated by the ResearchCo case) where a dual strategic emphasis results. Pathway 2 represents the evolution of the converse scenario over time, following an initial prioritization of codification.

We also refine the advice of HNT in deciding upon a particular strategic pathway. Our cases (especially ResearchCo) suggest that other factors beyond merely the nature of the business and business economics should be considered. The organization’s political and cultural contexts represent an important influence on the choice of strategic pathway. For example, Nicolajsen and Scheepers (2002) have attributed the lack of (codified) knowledge exchange within a pharmaceutical company to confidentiality concerns when project groups compete against each other for research funding. In that study, project group members were wary to enter the details of their project on a Web-based support system; instead they used the system to direct users to contact project members personally (via e-mail/phone). Other factors, such as the organization’s size and geography, could also influence their choice of strategic pathway. For example, a small firm where the workers are in close proximity could decide to prioritize codification (Strategic Pathway 2 in Fig. 1) despite their nature of business, since the workers may already be able to draw readily on each other’s personal expertise and skills. In addition, the peculiarities of the industry and organization’s task environment may restrict its knowledge strategy options; the ManufactCo case illustrates the constraints that shift work in the manufacturing sector could impose on the effectiveness of, say, a personalization strategy. All these examples illustrate that, although the nature of business might indicate a particular knowledge strategy emphasis (as HNT have argued), other factors come into play that could influence the choice and effectiveness of knowledge use following such a decision.

While it is beyond the scope of our present case study data, organizations that comprise of semi-autonomous units (divisions, subsidiaries) or those that have recently merged could conceivably be pursuing different knowledge strategy paths—a view that is consistent with HNT. As such, different knowledge strategies could co-exist/evolve in the different parts of these organizations (Perrow, 1986). Such scenarios need to be explored in further research (e.g. in terms of their potential merits, and problems arising due to a lack of integration or loss of synergy).

## 7.1. Limitations of the study

In terms of the choice of a strategic direction, we have listed a number of additional considerations (e.g. politics, organizational culture, size, geography) which go beyond HNT’s mainly rational ‘nature of business’ argument. Given the scope of this paper, we do not claim this list to be exhaustive, nor have we explored the influence of these factors on the choice of strategic direction in depth. We believe such considerations are important and should be explored in follow-up research.

Our study has focused specifically on organizations where codification and personalization knowledge strategies were considered important. These organizations were all knowledge-intensive and the effective use of knowledge was seen as essential for their overall business. In our assessment, we have focused on aspects of the HNT model such as the knowledge strategy mix, supporting IT infrastructure and effectiveness of organizational knowledge use. We did not assess other factors that HNT address such as staffing and incentive schemes that relate to the implementation of knowledge strategies.

From a combinatorial logic perspective the inclusion of additional cases would have been ideal. We did not examine combinations such as a dominant codification strategy with low codification in terms of IT infrastructure; and a low personalization knowledge strategy with high personalization in IT infrastructure. While we expect such combinations to be rare, they are still worthy of further investigation.

The proposed knowledge strategy pathways as depicted in Fig. 1, are derived from our assessment of the initial and expected knowledge strategy directions of EduCo, ConsultCo and ManufactCo, as well as the long term position of ResearchCo. In this respect, we see a need for longitudinal studies to closely track the evolution of organizational knowledge strategies over time.

## 8. Conclusion

In this paper, we have assessed and refined the model of knowledge strategy and IT infrastructure support as proposed by Hansen, Nohria and Tierney (1999) in four case organizations. We found support for the HNT model’s main argument in three cases, while the fourth case questions HNT’s advice of a constant 80/20 mix of knowledge strategy over time. We conclude that the HNT’s advice is useful for deciding upon an initial strategic direction and setting suitable priorities. Our assessment, however, indicated the need for some refinements to this advice. We have argued that organizations may find it necessary to evolve their knowledge strategy mix over time, augmenting the alternate strategy as a result of the intertwined nature of knowledge processes. In this respect, we proposed two knowledge strategy pathways that organizations could adopt on their journey towards effective use of organizational knowledge. Our cases also highlighted factors beyond the nature of business and business economics that should be considered in deciding upon a particular knowledge strategy direction.
