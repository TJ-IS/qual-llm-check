# An institutional perspective on sources of ERP package–organisation misalignments

Christina Soh<sup>\*</sup>, Siew Kien Sia

Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University, S3-1A-28, Nanyang Avenue, Singapore, Singapore 639798

## Abstract

Organisations often appear to be unaware of fundamental differences that exist between their organisational context and that which is assumed by ERP package developers. This has undermined expected benefits from ERP implementation, and in extreme cases of package-organisational misalignment, has led to project and even organisational failure.

In this study, we explain package-organisational misalignments as the result of differences between the structures embedded in the package and those embedded in the organisation. These differences reflect the different institutional context of the package developers and implementing organisation. Some organisational structures are imposed while other structures are voluntarily adopted. The organisation has less control in the case of imposed structures and misalignments associated with imposed structures are therefore more likely to be resolved through package customization.

We apply the framework to three hospitals implementing the same ERP package, identify the structural sources of the misalignments, and analyze the relationship to the type of resolution (package customization or organisational adaptation). We found that imposed structures were indeed overwhelmingly resolved through package customization, while most voluntarily acquired structures were resolved via organisational adaptation. We identified the different context specific sources for these structural differences and contribute to the research on ERP implementation, institutional theory, as well as draw implications for practice in managing package–organisation misalignments. q 2004 Elsevier B.V. ## 1. Introduction

Many organisations have implemented ERP packages. A significant number, however, have not experienced the level of expected benefits. This has led to a stream of research on the critical success factors associated with ERP implementations (Koh et al., 2000; Sumner, 2000; Nah et al., 2001; Sarker and Lee, 2003). One frequently noted shortcoming in the management of many projects has been that organisations underestimate the amount of commitment and effort required for the project, and this translates into inadequate time and resources for design, testing and change management (Girard and Farmer, 1999). The optimistic estimates of project resources may be due, in part, to the myth that packages are flexible and offer complete, ready to implement solutions to organisations’ problems with systems legacy and integration (Alvarez, 2000; Pozzebon, 2001), and somewhat contradictorily, that organisations should in any case modify their business processes to fit the ‘best practices’ embedded in the ERP package (Pereira, 1999).

Our reading of several well documented ERP failures, such as AeroGroup and Fox Meyer, provides a less sanguine view. The AeroGroup found that the SAP R/3 Apparel Footwear solution was unable to meet the uniqueness and complexities of its footwear business, and dropped the package half-way through the implementation. Similarly, Fox Meyer found that its ERP would not meet the requirements of its wholesale distribution business, and attributed its bankruptcy to the problems with the ERP package (Cardell and Tiffin, 1999). While ERP packages may be configured to meet a range of organisational requirements, these cases suggest that in some instances, the mismatch between the context of the implementation environments and the context embedded in the package can be significant (Soh et al., 2000). Unfortunately, organisations often realize the extent of the misalignment too late into the implementation process to deal effectively with it.

We believe insufficient attention has been paid to the problem of fundamental misalignments between the package and the organisation. We define misalignments as differences between the structures embedded in the organisation (as reflected by its procedures, rules and norms) and those embedded in the package. Ideally, organisations should be able to assess, prior to implementation, the extent of the misalignment, so that they may better manage the process of bringing the organisation and package into alignment. We believe that an important first step is to understand the sources of misalignments.

In this paper, therefore, we propose a conceptual framework, built on key concepts from theories on the institutional and structurational properties of technology (Orlikowski, 1992; DeSanctis and Poole, 1994; Volkoff, 1999; Orlikowski, 2000), for identifying the sources of package–organisation misalignment and their influence on the organisational choices for resolving the misalignments. We examine three hospitals that implemented the same ERP package, and use the framework to analyse the several hundred instances of misalignments, and to assess the influence of the type of misalignment on the organisation’s response in terms of organisational adaptation or package modification.

## 2. Embedded structures in packages and organisations

Technology has been conceptualized as the product of human action, both during its creation by developers, and when it is subsequently appropriated and modified by users (Orlikowski, 1992, 2000). Much research has focused on technology in use, and there is concern that technology in use has been treated as being interpretively flexible, with insufficient recognition of the inherent structural properties of the technology (DeSanctis and Poole, 1994). Technology creators, such as the developers of packaged software, inscribe their vision or view of the world in the technology that they create (Latour, 1992). Their view of the world is influenced by the institutional properties of their particular setting and draws on the knowledge, resources and norms that are a part of their institutional context when developing the technology. Technology developers’ institutional context influence the ‘spirit’ of the technology and this is reflected in the values and beliefs underlying the structural features of the technology. These structural features incorporate institutional structures such as reporting hierarchies, organisational knowledge and standard operating procedures as rules and capabilities in the technology (DeSanctis and Poole, 1994).

Much of this is true of ERP packages which seek to automate and informate core organisational processes. They are developed by vendors who draw on their existing sources of knowledge, resources and norms. These would include the developer organisation’s own business strategies and prevailing norms about what constitutes best practice. In general, the ‘spirit’ of ERP packages reflects beliefs about the value of having a single enterprise-wide system, and hence tend to have features that are aligned with cross-functional process integration and enterprise-wide data sharing (Markus and Tanis, 2000; Soh et al., 2003). Developers will also need to model likely organisational requirements in order to design the system, and will draw on the network of organisations to which they have access. These ‘referent’ organisations are usually those from their home market and other markets in which they have a major presence. Such markets are likely to be defined by national and industry boundaries. The structures embedded in the resultant package will therefore reflect the context of the group of companies that the developers interacted with most closely during the creation of the software.

The extent to which the package embedded structures differ from those of the implementing organisation will depend on differences in their institutional contexts. In the case of ERP packages, the developer’s context would be different from the context of many other potential implementers, particularly if these implementers are from different countries and industries than the original group of referent organisations. Even within the same country and industry, organisations are likely to differ in their goals and practices due to differences in history, and differences in responses to competitive pressures. The resultant institutional structures persist in part because they arise from cumulative action over time as the organisation interacts with its environment, and serve to provide meaning (signification and legitimation) and access to necessary resources (domination) from the environment (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983).

Where such contextual differences between the package and implementing organisation exist, it is important to explicitly consider the sources of contextual differences that contribute to package–organisation misalignments. Unreflective use of the technology, where there are major mismatches between the assumptions and rules embedded in the technology and in the use context have resulted in disastrous outcomes, such as the methane explosion in a Lancashire water tunnel, and the Challenger space shuttle O-ring malfunction (Wynne, 1988; Orlikowski, 1992). Such unreflective use occurs because technology developers are often separated in time and space from users of the technology, and users are usually unaware of developers’ context and the embedded assumptions and rules (Orlikowski, 1992). In addition, the emphasis on the benefits of ‘vanilla implementations also means that little effort is expended on identifying differences in contextual assumptions. Fundamental differences are often not discovered until late in the implementation process or after the system goes live.

## 3. Imposed and voluntarily adopted organisational structures

While it is clearly important for organisations to surface the areas in which their embedded institutional structures are different from those of the package, they may find it difficult to know where to start. In addition, once they begin listing differences, they will often find themselves swamped by a host of differences. It would therefore be helpful to provide theoretically grounded guidelines about potential sources of misalignments, and about the likely influence of the different types of misalignments on organisational actions towards achieving alignment through package modification or organisational adaptation.

One important consideration is whether the misalignment arises from organisational structures that are imposed on the organisation by external authoritative pressures, or voluntarily adopted by the organisation (Scott, 1988). While all organisational structures are adopted over time in response to the external environment, the degree of organisational volition can vary. This distinction is important. While we recognize (and discuss in a later section), that the process of achieving alignment is not deterministic, and involves ‘negotiation, persuasion and debate’ (Orlikowski, 2000, p. 405) among the various parties involved in the implementation, we will argue that organisations are likely to have more leeway in adapting to package embedded structures where the organisational structures that need to be changed have been voluntarily acquired. Where the organisational structures have been imposed by external institutions, the organisation may not be able to adopt the package embedded processes and still comply with external authority requirements. ## 3.1. Imposed organisational structure

Some institutional structures are imposed on organisations by authoritative sources in the organisation’s environment. Two types of authoritative sources have been identified— the coercive authority of nation-states, and the more normative authority of professions (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). Countries exert coercive pressures, through laws and regulations, and organisations adopt the requisite structures in order to comply and remain in business.

Professional and industry institutions are more likely to exert normative authority, for example through guidelines on professional conduct and industry accreditation or recognition of organisations. To remain in good standing, organisations adopt forms and procedures that are appropriate to their particular type (Meyer and Rowan, 1977; Barley and Tolbert, 1997).

Organisations operating in different countries face different institutional pressures, and will evolve different structures. If the implementing organisation is from a different country than that of the ERP package’s main markets, country level differences need to be identified. There are myriad ways in which countries differ from each other, and not all of the differences will be relevant when considering potential sources of misalignments in an ERP implementation. Since ERP packages are meant to support the processes of economic entities, differences in national economic institutions should be considered. The economic institutions and the laws and regulations imposed by each country on the organisations operating within its boundaries reflect its values and norms. Some countries have a larger governmental role in economic activity, and ERP packages developed in or for such markets will have rich features for government funding, reporting and regulation. Package implementers should therefore consider the extent to which national level laws and regulation pertaining to accounting, banking and other funding related matters, labor and employment, and trade between organisations and between organisations and consumers are different from those assumed by package developers from their context.

Similarly, organisations operating in different industries will face different institutional contexts. Industry and professional institutions can impose structures on organisations through certification and membership requirements. For example, hotels that wish to obtain and maintain a certain level of rating from their industry association would need to adopt prescribed practices pertaining to staff training, customer service, quality of hotel amenities and so on. These industry imposed requirements reflect best practice norms in the execution of value activities within organisations as well as interactions across the industry value chain (Porter, 1980). Patterned ways of transacting within the industry emerge, and over time define expectations regarding acceptable industry practice with regard to dealing with customers, suppliers, competitors and regulators.

These industry differences influence the structures that are built into ERP packages, as well as the structures that are embedded in organisations. There are examples where imposed industry context differences between the package and organisation structures have contributed to organisational failure. For example, Fox Meyer, who implemented SAP in the mid-1990s, found that the package, which at that point was largely designed for and used by manufacturing firms, was unsuitable for its drug wholesale distribution business. The different pattern of interactions with customer and supplier caused significant operational problems. As a wholesale distributor, dealing with many retail businesses, Fox Meyer had a much higher volume of daily transactions than the system could handle (Key, 1998). The role of a wholesale intermediary also differs from that of a manufacturer, in that the wholesaler must have the capability to quickly source from many suppliers in order to fill a customer’s order. Often, this means breaking orders up into multiple shipments. Fox Meyer found that the system did not have the features needed for correctly invoicing incomplete shipments and the ensuing confusion resulted in the loss of millions of dollars (Caldwell, 1998).

The resource based view (Wernerfelt, 1984; Barney, 1991) also provides the important insight that industry boundaries and differences reflect, in part, the heterogeneity and immobility of some types of resources. Differences in resource types will give rise to differences in routines and structures for acquiring, deploying, maintaining and disposing of the resources. An ERP developed in the context of industries with lower value capital assets may not have the necessary routines and structures to support the processes for an industry where the cost of capital assets is very high. For example, while trucking and airline companies provide transportation services, and have significant capital investment in transportation vehicles, the cost of a single airplane is many times the cost of a truck. There are many implications with regard to what is acceptable down time (it costs a company far more if an aircraft is not operational), and what are acceptable levels of safety. The logistics and engineering and maintenance modules of an ERP package for the airline industry therefore are likely to require more complex features that support the current routines and structures of maintenance and safety certification, than those developed for a trucking industry.

## 3.2. Voluntarily acquired organisational structures

Traditional institutional theory, however, has been criticized for placing too much emphasis on organisational passivity, focusing on how organisations are influenced by external pressures to adopt similar institutional structures (Perrow, 1985). More recent work has extended the theory to accommodate a variety of strategic responses to the institutional environment, recognizing that the degree of choice is not invariant across all institutional conditions and that organisational self interest and active agency can play a role in varying organisations’ responses to institutional pressures and expectations (Oliver, 1991). In particular, Oliver (1991) notes that institutional pressures toward conformity can vary, and with it the amount of leeway that organisations have in acquiring structures. For example, firms are less likely to conform when environmental uncertainty is low, or when the firms are less interconnected. This extension of traditional institutional theory helps to explain how organisations operating within the same institutional environment can acquire and retain different structures. We therefore argue that in contrast to imposed structures that contribute to conformity across organisations, organisations also voluntarily acquire different structures that over time differentiate them from each other.

Organisations are likely to acquire different structures for a variety of reasons. A major cause is different organisational experience and perceptions of routines associated with efficient acquisition and utilization of resources (Scott, 1988). These choices can vary from being at the level of competitive strategy to more operational routines. Oganizations make strategic choices over time as they seek favorable positions within their environment. Organisations do so by targeting different market segments within the industry, offering distinctive value propositions, and implementing routines and structures in their interaction with customers, suppliers, and other industry players (Porter, 1980, 1996). From a strategic perspective, the resource-based view theorizes that such organisational differentiation is rooted in heterogeneous resource endowments. Hence, organisations will also evolve distinctive routines and structures for acquiring and managing their strategic organisational resources. Indeed, much of the recent work on capabilities (as embedded in organisational routines) has noted that the ability to combine and leverage resources, rather than the resources per se is more important in today’s competitive environments (Teece et al., 1997; Eisenhardt and Martin, 2000).

Other voluntarily adopted structures are more operational in nature, and reflect the organisation’s particular experience and history, as well as management preferences. For example, in the absence of strong external requirements, organisations evolve ways to deal with recurring problems such as bottlenecks and resource constraints, and over time these solutions become part of organisational routines. Organisations also often face a multiplicity of external and contradictory pressures, and management teams of different organisations are likely to have different responses to balancing and trading-off the demands of multiple external pressures (Oliver, 1991). Leaders also influence the adoption of structures that support their particular needs for information and levels of risk tolerance. For example, some organisations have a much lower tolerance for risk than others, and these organisations are likely to have adopted structures that enable a high level of internal controls, as evidenced by norms and routines for detailed analysis, multiple approvals and so on.

Voluntary structure misalignments, if not surfaced and appropriated handled can also result in significant deterioration of organisational performance. For example, in the candy industry, customer demand is cyclical, with Halloween being a period of strong demand. This exerts pressure on candy manufacturers who need to meet the demand. Hershey had evolved its own organisation specific routines for managing its storage resources in order to deal with the sharp rise in output in the run up to Halloween. It hired temporary storage facilities, and also converted rooms in its manufacturing premises temporarily for storage. The implementation of an ERP package, however, cost Hershey \$150m in lost sales because of misalignments in the way the system tracked inventories and the way that the organisation had dealt with inventory during peak periods. The temporary locations were not recorded as storage points in the ERP’s data model, and hence Hershey lost track of its inventory so badly that it could not fulfill many orders (Carr, 2002).

## 3.3. Organisational responses to misalignments

It is therefore important for organisations to identify the instances of misalignments between the institutional context embedded in the package and the organisation, and to minimize negative consequences through appropriate organisational responses. When a misalignment is surfaced, organisations can respond through package customization or organisational adaptation. In the context of ERP implementation, package modification can range from customizing the package code, to interfacing with custom developed modules or modules from other vendors. On the other hand, organisational adaptation can take the form of a conscious redesigning of organisational processes and structures accompanied by substantial change management, to more piecemeal, evolutionary workarounds as individuals adapt to the package and their practices become part of the new organisational structures.

Typically, in any given ERP implementation, there will be a mix of both organisational and package adaptation. The organisational response to each misalignment is the result of a process of interaction and negotiation among various parties, including management, users, IS personnel, and consultants. Prior research suggests that such interaction processes are ‘messy’, and do not always simply reflect management’s directives (Ciborra et al., 2001). The negotiations to handle misalignments are likely to be ‘deeply embedded in the respective contexts’ (Scott and Wagner, 2003) of the different parties involved in the implementation. Various actors will appeal to elements in their respective institutional context to support their view of what should be left behind, and what should be brought forward. Vendors and consultants tend to promote the adoption of the package embedded structures with normative arguments that these structures represent best practice, and that customization will make future upgrades more difficult. Management, concerned about the cost and risks associated with customization, may also support the adoption of package structures with minimal customization. While there are many good reasons for recommending this ‘vanilla’ approach to ERP implementation, it tends to assume away the persistence of current institutional structures within the organisation that have evolved over time (Barley and Tolbert, 1997).

In negotiating the resolution of these misalignments, users appeal to the imposed and voluntarily acquired institutional contexts in defining the problems and in convincing and persuading others to embrace their desired course of action. Where the misalignment arises from an imposed structure, users are likely to point to the external authority that gave rise to the imposed structure. Since the organisation is usually unable to change government regulations or industry norms, the institutional structure is likely to prevail. Unless a transaction is relatively infrequent (e.g. annual reporting to a regulatory authority), the decision will usually be to modify the package to support the existing institutional structure.

Relative to voluntarily acquired structures, misalignments arising from imposed structures offer a stronger basis for the rhetoric justification of ritual, authority, pragmatism or scientific rationality across multiple constituents (Pentland, 1995), and hence the likely course of package customization. Latour (1999) noted the importance of the rhetoric justification to be seen as an organisational ‘matter of fact’ in sustaining the course of action, i.e. something so ingrained within a community as a result of extended negotiations, that its presence is indisputable and obvious.

The process of resolving voluntary structure misalignments is likely to be less clear-cut, and to have greater variability in the organisational choices. On the one hand, organisations appear to have more leeway in making changes to voluntary structures than imposed structures, and therefore appeals to package embedded best practice and the cost of modification are likely to sway the negotiations towards the decision of organisational adaptation. However, some voluntary structures themselves can be deeply embedded and difficult to change, e.g. those structures that contribute significantly to an organisation’s identity and performance. For example, Nike’s success was in part due to its strategy (and the accompanying structures) of providing a large range of product variety, and having a supply chain that spanned from the Far East (tapping lower cost suppliers) to retail channels in many parts of the world. Hence in its ERP implementation, Nike decided not to adopt the package’s pre-configured industry templates, as it felt that the templates were not well aligned with its concurrent efforts to integrate with its global network of suppliers via a private marketplace (Ericson, 2001).

The process by which an organisation arrives at a consensus of what constitutes best resolution, depends on the different perceptions of the necessity of the ‘voluntary acquired’ structures by different constituents within the organisations. Such negotiations are often fluid and complex, and hinge partly on the relative power position of the different constituents during implementation (e.g. consultant-led, IS-led, finance-led, or user management-led). It is harder to sustain the articulation of the ‘matter of fact’ for these voluntarily acquired misalignments given the heterogeneity of local interests, and hence, the propensity towards organisational adaptation as the best course of action.

In summary, while the complex interactions and negotiations among the parties involved in the implementation introduce a probabilistic element to the resolution of misalignments (particularly for voluntary misalignments), we argue that the nature of the misalignment itself also exerts a strong influence on whether the package structures would be more likely to prevail (i.e. the organisation would adapt to the package), or the organisation structures would be more likely to prevail (i.e. the package would be customised via the addition of supplementary modules or modification of package code).

## 4. Methods

We examine the phenomenon of package-organizational misalignment, its institutional sources and influence on organizational adaptation or package change by studying the ERP implementation in three large (800–1500 beds), public sector hospitals in Singapore. The three hospitals had each been established for about a hundred years, and had evolved distinct organizational cultures and structures over time, due in part to having different origins and missions. Hospital A was a long-established tertiary care, teaching hospital, Hospital B was founded by a Chinese philanthropist as a hospital for the poor and later converted to a government funded general hospital, while Hospital C was an established women’s and children’s hospital.

When the Singapore government decided to introduce some measure of privatization to the healthcare sector in the 1980s, hospital management became an increasingly professionalized and specialized function, with independent management boards and executive management teams being set up in each hospital. In the latter half of the 1990s, the management in the three hospitals had decided to move to an ERP package as their previous systems were not Y2k compliant. The implementation took place in 1998 and 1999. Other than some informal knowledge sharing, the respective project teams worked separately in the identification of misalignments and the resolution of these misalignments.

The three ERP implementation sites offered many advantages for the study of package– organisation misalignments. First, the hospitals were implementing both front-line operations modules (patient management) as well as back-end support activity modules (accounting and finance), thus providing a variety of misalignment types. Second, the cases provided both theoretical replication and variation (see Table 1). With regard to theoretical replication, all three cases were from the same country (Singapore) and industry (public sector healthcare), enabling us to identify common misalignments that arose from country and industry imposed structures. With regard to theoretical variation, the hospitals differed in their strategic positioning and history, allowing an examination of variations in voluntarily acquired structures. Third, a high degree of access to project documentation and personnel also allowed us to trace the misalignments to their resolution.

We were in the field for a year reviewing documentation (e.g. contracts, project files, meeting minutes, and issue logs) and interviewing key players (15–20 project team members, comprising senior managers, user representatives, hospital IT personnel and consultants per hospital), acquiring knowledge about the healthcare industry and the ERP implementation. After eliminating purely technical issues (e.g. technical integration with existing platform, hardware performance), we arrived at the total number of 190, 121, and 136 distinct instances of misalignments, respectively, for hospital A, B, and C. We also tracked the resolution for each misalignment through sources like issue logs, interviews, minutes of meetings, and engineering change requests. Where information gathered was insufficient, follow-ups were made to clarify the context of change requests and the decision rationale for their resolution.

The final tabulated misalignment lists were then categorized into imposed and voluntary structure misalignments. Typical imposed structure misalignments arose from mandatory requirements from external authority through laws and regulation as well as strong industry practices and norms. Their manifestations were often consistent across all three hospitals. On the other hand, voluntary structure misalignments arose from the unique history/organisational experiences or the idiosyncratic management and user preferences of the specific hospital. There were often wide variations in the ways such misalignments were surfaced and responded to across the hospitals. The categorization into imposed and voluntary structure misalignments was performed iteratively as we developed more in-depth understanding of the organisational or industry context in which they arose. The final categorization by the first author was then reconciled with that of the second author, who had also independently undertaken the same categorization analysis. Categorization inconsistencies were discussed to clarify the underlying rationales. Inconsistencies were largely due to the inadequate contextual understanding of the institutional or industry requirements and norms for the healthcare business. They were eventually clarified and agreed upon through further dialogue with the respective hospital personnel. We also observed that many of these misalignments were related and could be traced to a smaller set of issues. Thus, we qualitatively clustered these misfits thematically to surface the deeper level of structural forces for the imposed and voluntary structures.

Finally, the organisational responses to these misalignments (package modification or organisational adaptation) was systematically tabulated. Package modification was evidenced by contract annexes of agreed modification, engineering change request (consultants), and formal request for change (signed and approved). On the other hand, evidence or organisational adaptation include the rejection/lack of follow-ups of RFCs and the discussion of the related change and workaround efforts in the project minutes and interview transcripts. ## 5.1. Imposed structure misalignments

Consistent with expectations, the findings across the three hospitals show that the organisation responded to most of the imposed structure misfits by modifying the package (A: 89%, B: 93%, and C: 92%). The organisations adapted to imposed structure misalignments in only a handful of instances, and these were typically related to infrequent transactions and the functionalities were usually partially fulfilled by the package. Table 4 shows the major clusters of imposed structure misalignments arising from country and industry sources.

Our thematic analysis of the imposed structure misalignments showed that at the country level, these misalignments could be traced to a small set of fundamental sources that were misaligned with the package structures. Two such prominent sources of structural differences are the government policy of co-payment (where each patient’s share of the treatment costs depends on their choice of bed-class) and the use of a national identification number of every citizen.

The principal of co-payment is widely implemented in Singapore government policies ranging from education (highly subsidized but not free), to worker training, to public housing, to healthcare. In the case of healthcare funding, the co-payment principle had developed into a complex set of rules and practices for funding patients in hospitals. Patients can opt (depending on their ability to pay) for ‘A’, ‘B’ or ‘C’ bed-class wards with differing levels of room amenities and with increasing levels of government subsidy. Patients in ‘A’ and ‘B’ class wards pay substantial portions of the bill themselves, either in cash/credit card/cheque, or through deductions from their Medisave accounts (which are from government mandated employer and employee monthly contributions to the Central Provident Fund). The ERP package, however, was largely developed for the European and US markets. The policies and practices contrast sharply with the institutional context of European countries (where healthcare is more highly subsidized) and the United States (where there is a much higher reliance on healthcare insurance providers). The concept of multiple bed-class is also not widely practiced in these markets.

A slew of package–organisation misalignments arose from this fundamental institutional difference in national healthcare funding norms and policies. For example, the package did not have the ability to handle the complexities of collecting from private individuals, such as over-the-counter payment collection, and Medisave claims. Similarly, the package could not handle the complexities of billing and collection for the different bed-class. Not only were different subsidy formulas required for computation, but the system also needed to track patient bed-class upgrades and downgrades in order to arrive at the correct bill amount. Bed-class upgrades were fairly common occurrences as patients were sometimes admitted temporarily to a lower bed-class because higher bed-class rooms were not available, and downgrades sometimes occurred when patients’ stay were extended and patients needed to reduce their medical costs.

The other fundamental source of country imposed misalignments was the country-wide practice of using a national identification number as the unique identifier for every citizen in all interactions with the government. The hospitals, like other government organisations, had been using the national identification number to register and track patients, as well as to interface with other government systems (e.g. for Medisave claims from the Central Provident Fund agency). The ERP package, on the other hand, generated a unique identifier number for each patient. This reflects the country institutional context of its main markets, where citizens were not assigned national identification numbers, or where these were not as heavily relied on.

At the industry level, the misalignments could similarly be traced to key industry operating models, norms and practices among the hospitals. A substantial portion of these misalignments arose from the reporting requirements imposed by the Ministry of Health, the main industry regulator. These include key statistics in tracking diseases (e.g. HIV and MRSA indicators) and the efficiency and effectiveness of healthcare provision (e.g. reporting of hospital expenses, accident and emergency readmissions, diagnosis code, cause of death). In addition, being public hospitals, more stringent compliance procedures were necessary, e.g. in tracking purchases of medical equipment from specific donated funds. These requirements are specific to the healthcare industry, and the finance and accounting module, which was not developed particularly for the healthcare industry did not have the ability to provide the analysis required for the reports.

There were also industry norms and practices that imposed certain structures on the hospitals. For example, the country policy of co-payment meant that bad-debts arising from non-payment by individuals was an industry-wide problem. The hospitals spent a substantial amount of resources in monitoring overdue payments from individuals. Over time, the industry had developed the practice of handing more difficult collection case over to professional collection agencies. This was practiced by all three hospitals. The ERP package, developed in an institutional context where collection from patients was not as significant, did not have any functionality for collection agency management.

Misalignments were mostly surfaced during the ‘as-is’ and ‘to-be’ phases of the implementation, and each was handled through an iterative process of negotiation, persuasion and debate among the users, consultants, project manager and IS staff. The rationale for customizing these imposed structure misalignments were discussed as a ‘matter of fact’. Users and management took it as given that the imposed structures would continue, and their arguments for modifying the package to conform to imposed organisational structures emphasized the pervasive effects caused by the misalignments. For example, one manager provided a detailed explanation of the chain of additional work, errors and poor customer service that would result if the package was not modified to accurately capture, track and report installment payments. Users and project manager also marshaled arguments relating the cost of the particular modification relative to the impact on the organisation’s operations. For example, in supporting the RFC for installment plan and bad debts monitoring, the project manager noted that they had “a significant amount of bad debts and installment plans to manage, and providing the business office with the ability to manage these plans and payments automatically would be a productivity boost for them.” Subsequently, analysis was provided to show that there were several thousand cases of installment and bills submitted to collection agencies, whose annual value was 70 times the amount required for the RFC. In the interactions, while consultants may occasionally offer suggestions of workaround solutions, they appeared to accept the ‘imposed’ nature of the misalignment, and there was relatively little effort to dissuade the organisations from customizing the package.

These interactions led to decisions to modify the package for most of the imposed structure misalignments, with the creation of new entities in the package (e.g. Medisave schemes, bed-class) and processes (e.g. counter collection, subsidy rules validation, Medisave claims or refunds, installment payment and bad debt monitoring). Modifications and refinements were also needed on existing entities (e.g. more detailed patient information like Central Provident Fund account numbers for Medisave claims) and processes (e.g. complex business rules for billing computations).

## 5.2. Voluntarily acquired structure misalignments

In contrast to imposed structure misalignments, a much lower proportion of misalignments arising from voluntarily acquired structures resulted in package customization (A: 23%, B: 35%, C: 25%). In the majority of instances, the organisations adapted to the package instead. Table 5 shows the major clusters of voluntary structure misalignments resolved through package customization and organisational adaptation.

Most of the voluntary structure misalignments were about management information, internal control, and ease of use, and covered a broad range of functional areas. Unlike imposed structure misfits, such misalignments appear to be specific to individual hospitals. There was wide variation in the nature of these misalignments across hospitals as they reflect the different routines and norms that the hospitals had acquired over time as a result of varying management and user experience and preferences. There were varied demands for management information across the hospitals with regard to the nature and the extent of details required. Examples include asset depreciation report, long stay report, readmission report, and patient refund. In the area of internal control, examples of misalignments included controls over payment reconciliation, black listing accounts, checks on a variety of treatment and departmental codes associated with billing, authorization for data access and change, waiver of professional fees report, audit trail reports, and so on. Finally, for ease of use, there were requests to search the system by different fields, default entry codes, automating the screen flow and more user-friendly formats for screen displays and reports.

In the negotiations to resolve these types of voluntary structure misalignments, consultants and IS department would try to get users to adopt the package structure, through ‘selling’ and explaining the package processes and information outputs, and proposing various workarounds. For example, users found that the standard reports provided by the package did not meet their requirements. A combination of various workarounds arose in the process of interactions around this set of misalignments. In some instances of misalignment, the consultants taught the users how to “use the query function to create simple reports for themselves”. Some users were ‘sold’ on this approach, since it gave them the flexibility to create their own reports. In other instances, the IS personnel helped to query and create other critical and more complex reports for the users upon request. Users also identified needed data across multiple reports and downloaded them into Excel for further analysis and consolidation. The lack of a unified view of the voluntarily acquired contexts often left users to be persuaded by the project manager and consultants to adapt to the package in the interests of meeting the budget and schedule.

Most of the voluntary structure misalignments resulted in organisational adaptation. However, the courses of action in resolving these misalignments from voluntary acquired context were also different across the hospitals, partly attributable to the different constituents involved in negotiating the resolution. The ERP implementation of Hospital A, for example, was led by the finance group and relative to the other hospitals, we noted a higher proportion of its reporting-related misalignments were customised. One project team member related an incident of ‘who has the loudest voice wins’, i.e. how one finance manager had insisted on having a new report customised to the previous content and presentation format. But with a change of the finance manager, the customised report was soon abandoned and left unused. The new finance manager preferred to rely on SAP standard reports in managing the department. The idiosyncrasy of such user-centric justification made it difficult to sustain the course of resolution as a ‘matter of fact’.

However, there were other voluntary structure misalignments that resulted in package customization because the rhetorical justification was accepted as an organisational ‘matter of fact’. Some of these were related to the particular strategic positioning of the hospitals. For example, Hospital C, with its niche market focus on women and children had evolved structures that were different from the other two more broadly targeted hospitals. The ERP package’s lack of functionality in areas such as billing for nursery and accouchement fees, maternity package pricing, and additional baby information were accepted by the other hospitals, but resulted in package customization in hospital C. In the negotiations, the hospital’s specific context, where it handled a high proportion of maternity cases was used to support the various related RFCs. For example, a Nursing Director noted that the package did not capture the event where a baby was lodged for a few hours in the Special Care Nursery. Since there were over 1000 such cases a year, the misallocated costs and revenue charges were significant, and would adversely affect the reported performance of her department.

Such differences in strategic positioning led the hospitals to make different trade-offs in the resolution of some voluntary misalignments. The ‘bed-planning’ misalignment is one example—Hospital A modified the package to meet its requirements while Hospital C did not. Hospital A’s role as a tertiary care hospital, meant that it had to manage both the broadest range of specialties as well as offer the greatest amount of depth in terms of expertise. Hospital A therefore faced significant challenges in managing their bed resource that was shared across multiple specialties, and had evolved many routines over time to meet this challenge. For example, the hospital had developed a routine called ‘doubledecking’ where two patients were assigned to the same bed, because it was known that one patient would be moving to another ward for other treatment, and would return in a few days time. This maximized the utilization of scarce beds. The hospital had also evolved complex routines for planning bed availability, and this required information from various sources—forecast requirements based on patient stays that had been booked in advance, patient discharge, movement between wards, by bed-class type. Hospital C, while recognizing the misalignment between the package and its previous level of bed-planning functionality, focused its package customization efforts on functionalities specific to its target ‘mother-child’ market, and adapted to the package with regard to bed-planning. One senior user explained the adaptation:

“the system does not reflect the reserved beds in the wards, therefore there can be double booking problems<sup>.</sup>We tell the girls that if there is a bed booked in your area, you should expect the patient to come in by today. If not, call admissions and check if the patient is coming. My clerks have to be alert,<sup>.</sup> we teach them to take the initiative to call admissions to clarify.”

Another set of voluntary structure misalignments that were resolved through package modification related to the various touch points of customer interactions. One cluster reflected routines that the respective hospitals had developed to provide better customer service and patient care. Examples included requests for bill consolidation, decentralized discharge form printing, flagging of VIP patients at point of registration, and quick identification of patient location for physicians. The other cluster related to pricing functionalities, i.e. the varied hospital practices in handling of global price change, package pricing, half-day pricing computation, consultant fees, senior grade fees, and facilities charges. Finally, customizations were also required to map the ERP system to the different hospital management structures that had evolved historically due to specific strategic thrust, varying organisational adoption of DRG (diagnosis-related code) practices in the healthcare industry, and the need for resource sharing across reporting units. Examples of such misalignments include the computation of revenue/cost allocation for support services, reports by operating units, budget reports, account assignment for composite service, and the mapping of service groups to fit ward sharing structure.

## 6. Discussion

Table 6 provides a more generalized summary of the findings in terms of type of structural misalignments (imposed and voluntary) and type of resolution (package customization and organisational adaptation). The table provides a potential framework to account for and understand retrospectively the various outcomes of ERP implementations by examining the contextual differences that contribute to package–organisation misalignments. More proactively, the framework provides cues in surfacing likely dimensions of misalignment during package evaluation, and suggests the type of organisational responses that are likely to be appropriate.

The findings raise several points for discussion regarding what options organisations and package developers can pursue in order to minimize the incidence of misalignments and to mitigate negative outcomes from misalignments. From the organisation’s perspective, it is important to identify all imposed structure misalignments since these tend to require package customization. Significant imposed structure misalignment would greatly increase the cost of package modification as well as project risk. Our study suggests that the ability to identify imposed structure misalignments requires both in-depth understanding of the country and industry context, as well as the structures embedded in the package. The difficulty lies in the fact that such knowledge is distributed among several groups of people. Organisational members may understand their country and industry context well, while consultants are likely to understand the package structures. Such contextual knowledge is often hard to communicate, as its embedded nature gives it a ‘taken-forgranted’ quality. This, in part, explains why significant imposed structure misalignments often go unnoticed until fairly late into the implementation. Steps that organisations can take to minimise this risk include selecting consultants who are knowledgeable about the country and industry, and also training a few organisational members, who have a deep knowledge of the organisation, on the package prior to implementation. Other organisations in the industry that have adopted the package are also a good source of knowledge about country and industry imposed structure misalignments. Finally, recent research suggests that the use of narrative analysis as a means of requirements elicitation should complement existing structured approaches, as it is better at eliciting and capturing the ‘messy’ information about organisational culture, context and politics (Alvarez and Urla, 2002).

These steps will also help the organisation to identify voluntary structure misalignments. However, in the case of voluntary structure misalignments, the organisational response to the misalignment, once identified, is much less clear. The negotiations among the parties involved may sometimes lead to organisational adaptation and at other times to package modification. If we accept that it is usually advisable to keep package modification to a minimum, then the organisation will need to take steps to promote this outcome without compromising organisational performance. Our study found that strategy-related structures resulting in package modification revolved around services developed for niche market segments, competitive pressures for flexible pricing, routines for managing critical resources, and routines for enhanced customer service. The need for customizations of these voluntary structure misalignments supports the view that ERP packages have some limitations with regard to enabling strategic differentiation. Senior management will need to identify the structures that have evolved over time to support the organisation’s strategy, and assess (through a process of negotiation), the extent to which such strategies and structures should be maintained or changed. This is often not done in many ERP implementations where senior management views the project in operational and/or technical terms. Such implementations do not have a good basis for deciding an appropriate response to voluntary structure misalignments. Decisions made during implementation, without strong consensus and understanding of the trade-offs, are likely to result in major user resistance and the implemented system is likely to evolve in use in ways that are very different from that envisioned by the implementation team (Scott and Wagner, 2003).

Voluntary misalignments can also be better managed if organisations have a pro-active change management plan right from the beginning of the project, which educates users on the alternative ways of working that will result from the implementation of the package. Our study found that certain types of the voluntary structure misalignments were amenable to organisational adaptation. These related to different information requirements at the respective hospitals, varied practices among the hospitals in ensuring management control, and user routines in interacting with system interfaces. Consultants played an important role here, by educating users, as well as by suggesting workarounds drawn from their knowledge of implementations in other organisations.

Package developers also have a significant role to play in minimising misalignments. Vendors recognize the problem of different institutional structures when they create country industry versions of their packages. Our experience with the healthcare industry as well as in an ongoing study with the defense industry suggests that early versions of the country and/or industry package usually lack many of the structures belonging to the implementation context. Package development is an incremental process, and ensuing versions include additional features that reflect the learning of vendors from their diverse customers (Pozzebon, 2001). All the three hospitals in our study are in the process of assessing replacements for their ERP package, and recent informal interactions with them suggest that the later versions of the package have incorporated many of the imposed structures that the earlier version installed in 1999 did not. Package developers also need to refine mechanisms for learning about institutional structures specific to countries and industries. While vendors have mechanisms such as industry user groups and employ industry specialists, additional mechanisms appear to be needed for faster learning cycles as vendors move into new countries and industries, as industry and country environments change more rapidly, and as first generation ERP package users become more knowledgeable and demanding of subsequent package versions. Organisations contribute to this package refinement process when they work together within the industry to lobby the vendor to incorporate structures specific to their country and industry (Hislop et al., 2000).

## 7. Conclusion

This study seeks to complement the research on ERP project management by drawing attention to the need for more upfront assessment of package–organisation misalignment. While project management is critical to ERP success, its effectiveness requires a clear understanding of the extent and nature of package–organisation misalignment, so that appropriate resolution approaches can be pursued and needed resources obtained. While ‘appropriate’ resolution may emerge from interaction among the various implementation stakeholders, the nature of the misalignment also exerts and influence on the resolution, via the arguments that stakeholders are able to put forward.

We propose and validate a framework for identifying institutional sources of package– organisation misalignments. The paper also contributes to the body of work on institutional theory by demonstrating that the nature of the institutional structure (in terms of degree of organisational volition) has influence over organisational action in the implementation of package software. In line with more recent research on institutional theory, it recognizes that organisations are not passive responders to institutional pressures, but are capable of a range of differentiated responses. The paper therefore makes a distinction between, and provides empirical evidence of, the aspects of organisations that tend to be the same within the same country and industry, as well as the aspects that differ across countries and industries. The study also suggests that the persistence or strength of voluntary structures varies, with those related to strategic differentiation being most likely to persist even in the face of opposing pressure to adopt different packaged enabled structures.

More intensive research, however, are needed to explore the process in which the users appeal to their institutional contexts (imposed or voluntarily acquired) as rhetorical justification to contest and negotiate the best course of resolving these misalignment. We also recognize the implemented systems are not static once rollouts are completed. There is ample evidence from prior research on technologies in use that structures will continue to emerge as users interact with and appropriate the technology (DeSanctis and Monge, 1999; Orlikowski, 2000; Scott and Wagner, 2003). Future research may also examine the subsequent interactions between system and users, and the extent to which they are shaped by the earlier decisions made during implementation and changes in institutional contexts.
