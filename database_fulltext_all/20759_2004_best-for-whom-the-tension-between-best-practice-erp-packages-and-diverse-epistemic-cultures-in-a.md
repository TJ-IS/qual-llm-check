# ‘Best’ for whom?: the tension between ‘best practice ERP packages and diverse epistemic cultures in a university context

Erica L. Wagner<sup>a,\*</sup>, Sue Newell<sup>b,c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Hotel Administration, Cornell University, 340 Statler Hall, Ithaca, NY, USA <sup>b</sup>Bentley College, Waltham, MA, USA

<sup>c</sup>Royal Holloway College, University of London, London, UK

Available online 28 January 2005

## Abstract

The idea that so-called ‘best’ business practices can be transferred to organizations when they purchase enterprise resource planning (ERP) software packages is a major selling point of these packages. Yet recent research has illustrated a gap between the espoused theory of a best practice solution and the theory-in-use experienced by those who install software with such a design. As researchers begin to examine the difficult process by which organizations recast the best practices model handed down to them by consultancies and software vendors in an effort to make the software ‘work for them’ in practice, it is equally important that we begin to understand the reasons that such a gap exists. To this end, we analyze the strategic partnership between a multinational software vendor and a university who together designed a ‘best practice’ ERP package for the higher education industry. Through the theoretical lens of ‘epistemic cultures’ we argue that in organizational contexts made up of more than one epistemic culture, the use of a best practice model will be problematic because, by definition, the model mandates one epistemological position through the software design. This is counter to a university’s loosely coupled organizational form. 2004 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: ‘Best practice’; ERP software; Epistemic cultures; University; Context; Interpretive research; Longitudinal research

## 1. Introduction

Increasingly, contemporary organizations are choosing to purchase standard software products that are designed based on business practices that have been deemed the most appropriate for achieving organizational goals. This so-called ‘best practice’ model has a long history (Newell et al., 2000) and is currently popularized in the form of enterprise resource planning (ERP) packages, arguably the most popular business software of the last 15 years (Robey et al., 2002). ERP products offer the integration of business processes and functions across the organization based on a way of working deemed ‘the best’ for particular industries by software vendors, management consultants and industry-based experts (Lee and Lee, 2000; Shanks and Seddon, 2000). Organizations adopting ERP software need to configure the software to meet their local needs but are encouraged to adopt the ‘vanilla system’ (that is without modifications) since the ‘best’ industry practices are supposedly embedded in this standard configuration. These ‘best’ practices are typically designed with a software company working in a partnering relationship with a key industry customer to develop a package to meet the unique requirements of a particular industry. In this paper we focus on the university sector.

Despite the popularity of ERP packages, recent research has illustrated a gap between the espoused theory of a best practice solution and the theory-in-use experienced by those who install software with such a design (Shanks and Seddon, 2000; Robey et al., 2002). As researchers begin to examine the difficult process by which organizations recast the best practices model handed down to them by consultancies and software vendors in an effort to make the software ‘work for them’ in practice (Willcocks and Sykes, 2000), it is equally important that we begin to understand the reasons why such a gap exists. In this paper we argue that the reason for this gap is in part due to the best practice design itself which limits different theories of work from flourishing within a single organizational context. We use the concept of epistemic cultures (Knorr-Cetina, 1999) to explore the diversity of practices within a university context. We pose the research question: is it possible to identify a standard ‘best practice’ in a context characterized by users from diverse epistemic cultures? In considering this question, we focus on intra-organizational diversity, rather than inter-organizational diversity that has been the previous focus of ‘best practice critiques (e.g. Clark and Staunton, 1989).

Szulanski (1996) considers the challenges of transferring best practices within organizations from a knowledge perspective. He identifies a number of factors, including the inability of the recipient to absorb the best practice based on their world view, an absence of ‘knowing why’ something is to be done in a particular way, and finally a difficult relationship between the parties involved in the transfer. However, this study does not provide an explanation for why such knowledge-related barriers occur nor does it offer guidelines for handling such impediments. The results from our case analysis highlight how the context of practice influences what is ‘best’ such that a single organizational ‘best practice’, as promoted within ERP packages, is problematic. We conclude that while software vendors recognize the industry context as influencing the appropriate ‘best practice design of an ERP system, they fail to acknowledge how the contextual specificity within an organization makes it difficult if not impossible to meet all users’ needs with a standard organizational solution.

Section 1 has introduced the study and shown its relevance. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 frames our research by introducing the background and objectives of the study. Having presented the landscape inspiring the study’s research questions, Section 3 reviews our research methodology and empirical design. The main body of the paper begins with the case description in Section 4, followed by the analysis of empirical data. The paper concludes with a discussion and suggestions for future research.

## 2. Background

As discussed, the premise behind the design of ERP packages is that it is possible to define an industry ‘best practice’ that can be applied across organizations within that industry, no matter where located, in order to improve the efficiency of processes (O’Leary, 2000). Current ERP software suites are promoted on the premise that industry specific packages can be configured to meet the diversity of organizational arrangements through the selection of a catalogue of available business processes. To this extent, such packages have what Benders and Van Veen (2001) call ‘interpretative viability’, so that they are applicable in a wide variety of circumstances. Nevertheless, organizations are encouraged to adopt the standard configuration, since this, it is argued, captures best industry practices (Davenport, 2000). The university sector is seen as no exception to this rule, which is why several software vendors have developed enterprise systems for this sector.

The university context is a particularly valuable analytical focus for this study because its form is based on enabling coordination without requiring homogeneity of epistemological perspectives (Knorr-Cetina, 1999). As scientific and teaching enterprises become ‘big business’ and universities throughout the world struggle to revamp their identity within an era focused on ‘commercializing higher education’ (Noble, 1998a,b), software vendors are increasingly viewing the education market as a lucrative ‘industry (Winner, 1997), potentially worth several hundred billion dollars in revenue (Lehman Brothers study quoted in Noble, 1998a). The trend toward ERP ‘business solutions’ reflects the ‘marketisation’ (deBoer, 1999) of universities where institutional governance is now the domain of professional managers who aim to mitigate risks while remaining competitive in an increasingly complex global higher education marketplace (Barnett, 2000; Gumport, 2000; Brennan et al., 1999).

Given this increasingly complex context, the university sector represents an industry in which enterprise systems are increasingly being implemented in order to modernize backoffice, administrative functions via an integrated technology platform (Allen and Kern, 2001; Mahrer, 1999; Pollock, 1999; Volkoff, 1999). However, the current generation of ERP systems have evolved from technology designed for the manufacturing industry (Klaus et al., 2000) and still embed ‘reference models that espouse industry best practices<sup>.</sup> at too high a level for an effective assessment of how the ERP system would actually affect the organizational processes’ (Soh et al., 2000; p. 51). Moreover, the integrated, ‘whole-house’ view underpinning ERP technology tries to force all departments into using a standardized template, no matter how decentralized and autonomous are the departments involved (Allen and Kern, 2001; Pollock, 1999). Given this technology design, some authors have pointed to a dichotomy between implementing integrated, standardized ERP technology and the traditional, fiefdom-like structure of universities where information systems develop organically to support the values of academic freedom and ‘scientific separateness’ (Allen and Kern, 2001; Cornford, 2000; Pollock, 1999).

This suggests a tension between the basic assumptions underpinning the design of ERP products and the operation of a university. In order to explore this further we can focus on practice, since ERP packages are designed to support practices that are defined as ‘best’ across a particular industry. We define practice as the ‘organization’s routine use of knowledge<sup>.</sup>often has a tacit component, embedded partly in individual skills and party in collaborative social arrangements’ (Szulanski, 1996; p. 28). We use the lens of epistemic cultures to consider the diverse practices of different ERP user groups in the university setting. Knorr-Cetina (1999) argues that different communities exhibit distinct epistemic cultures; that is different sets of social, material and discursive practices that make up ‘how we know what we know’. In doing this she highlights the heterogeneity of knowledgecreating activity across contexts (Robertson et al., 2003). Epistemic cultures thus guide and constrain practice.

In a university, there will be multiple users of an enterprise system coming from varied backgrounds and with different goals and approaches to practice—different epistemic cultures. In particular, users will be both dispersed faculty members and central administrators. The faculty are those who have won, or are assigned to, a research grant or contract. As principal investigators (PI) they are responsible for the day-to-day spending of the grant funds and must ensure spending in accordance with their original grant proposal or, if they do not, that they agree any changes with the funding agency. They often work with departmental administrators to keep track of their spending. The central administrators, on the other hand, must keep an overall record of grant activity and ensure that spending complies with various regulatory and university audit requirements. The ERP system must support all these groups. Thus, in this paper, we contrast the cultures of administration (both central and departmental) and faculty as they engage with the newly developing ERP system, albeit acknowledging that the faculty researchers in particular are actually more heterogeneous than we are depicting here (Knorr-Cetina, 1999). While diversity is likely to be present in other organizational contexts the heterogeneity is arguably greater in a university setting both in relation to the range of epistemic cultures and to the range of user backgrounds. Thus, we have a diversity of unique research projects, typically requiring fluid and emergent processes and each being run by an ‘amateur’ accountant, as well as a central administration function, focused on real-time financial analysis and involving ‘professional’ accountants.

This study contributes to our understanding of the contextual influences on enterprise system usage within organizations. It does this through an in-depth case study of an Ivy League university (Ivy) that partnered with ‘Vision Corporation’, an international ERP software vendor hoping to break into what they defined as a ‘higher education market’. The project was expected to create an integrated, administrative platform that would later be sold as a template for other higher education institutions. System modernization and business process redesign efforts were conducted within the key functional areas in the University including financial management, human resources and payroll, and grants and contracts administration. In this paper we concentrate on this last aspect of the system. More specifically, given the different goals, priorities and practices across a university, we consider the problems encountered in designing an information system that will support the contextual diversity that is present. We begin this task in Section 3 by introducing the research design that facilitated the study of this process.

## 3. Research method

One of the main purposes of this study is to understand the gap between the espoused theory of software design and its theory-in-use within an organization over time. In order to do so we adopt an interpretive epistemology meaning that we understand ‘reality’ to be socially constructed and seek it’s articulation through human sense making activities on the part of participants and researchers (Walsham, 1993). Access to these interpretations is gained through the collection and analysis of language, symbols and artifacts (Klein and Myers, 1999). Through a qualitative research method involving intensive field research we followed the negotiations that resulted from the design and introduction of the ‘best practice’ software into Ivy University. These negotiations emphasized multiple interpretations of the software and the organizational change that accompanied it. As Walsham (1993) notes, interpretive researchers strive for ‘validity<sup>.</sup>not [from] the representativeness of such cases in a statistical sense, but on the plausibility and cogency of the logical reasoning used in describing the results from the cases and in drawing conclusions from them.’ Our study emphasizes the existence of multiple perspectives and is valuable because it facilitates recognition of different interpretations of the same event—which in turn highlights the existence of multiple epistemic cultures. The official story of this software project is recorded in the ERP ‘best practice’ design and represents the dominant interpretation of the initiative but it does not represent what ‘really happened’ when the software was put to use. This study sheds light on what happens before the espoused theory of the ERP higher education product becomes a ‘black box’ in order obtain alternative versions of the story.

Adopting an interpretive perspective means seeking out the perspectives of those in the field as they speak and act in ways that help them make sense of the world, and persuade others as situations arise (Walsham, 1993). This epistemological stance is particularly valuable for contextual studies of socio-technical information systems where the focus is on understanding the process by which human and non-human components of the information system inform one another. We adopted research methods that would help us analyze how negotiations between people and technology came to be transmitted through the ERP software. As such, data were collected by the first author during four intensive phases of fieldwork centered on the changeover to an ERP-enabled environment between the summers of 1999 and 2000. The timing of the field research towards the latter part of the project rather than when the design was first being discussed and decided upon provides access into the theory of best practices ‘in use’ after said practices were already articulated and designed into software (This is visually represented in Fig. 1). Field data included interviews, official project documentation, public relations material, and notes from meetings. One hundred and twenty-nine interviews were conducted in the narrative style (Wagner, 2003) with 53 interlocutors involved with the project or its outcome (Table 1). Each interview was taped and a verbatim transcript was produced. An incremental transcription process facilitated preliminary analysis and aided the field researcher as she prepared for next round of interviews.

In order to follow multiple agencies of change and order over time the fieldwork involved following action that was illuminated in the interview transcripts through stories of controversy or negotiations. After initial interviews with project team members, the field researcher immediately transcribed the sessions and analyzed their content in terms of actors and issues being discussed by the interviewee, she then scheduled appointments with those actors in order to obtain multiple perspectives of the same event and develop an understanding of the negotiations that were at the forefront of people’s minds. In addition, we were better able to reach actors who might have otherwise remained ‘silent voices (Star, 1991) because we were guided not by recommendations from actors of whom to interview, but rather sought out the allies and controversial actors who arose in the interview transcripts. When reference was made to non-human actors (such as the ERP or a management report) the researcher would interview the human actor who was a delegate for that non-human interest (Pouloudi and Whitley, 2000).

The analysis was conducted by a systematic and careful reading of the field data from which the primary researcher reflected on themes and issues that existed within each narrative and across narratives. These items were organized into lists, discussed during regular meetings with colleagues, and communicated via email with project participants. Large wall charts outlined site visits, the relationships between storylines, and the emergence of analytical themes. ## 4. Case description

In early 1995, management consultants were brought in to advise Ivy on the replacement of the purchasing and accounts payable systems. In this capacity they encouraged Ivy to consider an integrated replacement of all their administrative systems rather than a piece-meal upgrade of each outdated and home-grown system. Management consultants advocated a comprehensive suite of applications and encouraged Ivy to act in a timely manner so as to replace legacy systems that were non-compliant with the year twothousand date format. In considering this, a newly appointed Vice President (VP) for Finance and Administration commissioned a working group to consider the modernization of Ivy’s technical infrastructure, and the professionalization of its administrative practices. Central to the VP’s evaluation of software solutions was the integrity of Ivy’s financial systems which he interpreted as the linchpin of the University’s business backbone. As such, accounting leaders spearheaded the direction of IT-enabled modernization discussions. The VP and a core group of managers became proponents of replacing Ivy’s legacy systems with an integrated enterprise package. In the summer of 1996 Ivy created an alliance with Vision Corporation to develop and implement flagship technology. The choice to work with Vision was two fold. First, Vision’s Government Financials (GF) product was considered very strong by Ivy and was in use within government and public sector institutions as well as several universities. The financially focused agenda of the VP and his entourage meant that the stability of Vision’s package was convincing. The VP explains:

“I heavily leaned in the direction of wanting to go with the strongest financial system. I thought that the largest pay-off from the project, when you really looked at it, ultimately would be in better financial data and the ability to do more interesting things on the clinical and grants management side. And so I really saw that as the real pay-off whereas the other administrative functions like [human resources] seemed to me to be more meat and potatoes.” [Summer 1999]

The VP felt confident that Vision could provide a project-centric financial system that would integrate accounting and budget functionality with all other administrative functions. Second, Vision was interested in entering into a ‘development partnership’ with the University in order to design two grant-focused modules that would integrate with its GF and Human Resource System software to create an ERP package specific to the University context (Ivy official documentation, May, 1996). Beginning in October 1996, system modernization and business process redesign efforts were conducted within the key functional areas of financial management (including general accounting, financial planning and reporting, and purchasing and accounts payable), human resources and payroll, and grants and contracts administration. The project was expected to last two years with the changeover occurring in October 1998. However, the best practice design went live on July 1, 1999—the first day of Ivy’s year 2000 fiscal calendar. In the next sections we explore the different visions and practices of the key stakeholders at Ivy and their reactions to the newly designed ERP system.

## 4.1. The vision and practice of central administrators: time-phased budgeting

Of particular concern to central leadership was the governance of complex research and clinical activities constituting Ivy’s academic programs. Increasingly these activities are funded through external grant awards that must be managed in compliance with thirdparty regulations. Ivy’s 25-year old financial systems could no longer accommodate University needs because the design too rigidly categorized financial data based on an outdated organizational model. This rigidity limited reporting functionality and made the reclassification of data into management information a fragmented process that occurred outside system boundaries, within Departmental business offices.

Academic researchers, their staff, and central administrators all recognized the importance of moving to a more transparent accounting practice in order to manage institutional risk, comply with regulatory bodies, avoid litigious hazards, and act as competent fiduciaries in an increasingly complex operating environment. The institutional risk was considered to be too high to allow an ad hoc review process to continue especially when its practice was contrary to Ivy’s professionalization effort. A member of the management faculty states:

“<sup>.</sup>well from what I understand no-one builds their own systems anymore and so it seems to make sense that they are moving toward an integrated system so that things can’t fall through the cracks.” [Spring 2000]

The Budget Director, in discussion with the VP, decided that the solution to the problem was to encapsulate monthly financial monitoring into a single conceptual idea called time-phased budgeting (TPB). In TPB faculty and staff would budget their total grant funds, which might come from multiple sources, over time—into what were called ‘time-phases’—based on the expected rhythm of grant and contract activity across the fiscal year calendar (e.g. low expenses in summer months, increased gift revenue during a pledge campaign). In other words, time-phased budgeting would take into account the entire grant budget by spreading it over time and manage actual expenses against this plan.

This time-phased approach was coined by the Budget Director based on his experience in the private sector as a business owner. He expected to be able to match central financial systems with what he perceived to be the codifiable nature and cycles of academic work. As such, TPB was designed as a translation point between financial systems and project management. The value of this time-phased approach was seen to be two-fold. First, it was expected to create a bridge between financial accounting practices and grants and contract project management. Second, TPB was seen to be a ‘raising of the intellectual bar’ and shifting of responsibility directly to faculty. As the Budget Director explained:

“I mean let’s give the job directly to the faculty. It doesn’t take a Noble Prize winner to say I’ve got \$50, I spent \$65, I’m in trouble for \$15. That’s really all we’re talking about. And it doesn’t take a Noble Prize winner to do a business plan that’s timed out by months or quarters. So you know, when we talk about somebody who is a secretary who’s put into a position with the ERP that is now totally too complicated for them, that’s really different. I mean if you had somebody with an IQ of 90 and it’s a job for a person with an IQ of 110, there’s a problem. But with the faculty—the fact of the matter is they’re all smart! I mean they may be nutty as hell but they’re smart and there’s nothing here that they can’t figure out<sup>.</sup>I really believe—you put pressure on people to take responsibility for things that they should be responsible for and then you hold them accountable for it”. [Summer 2000]

The Budget Director, supported by the VP and the core group, decided to design TPB as standard practice into the ERP design. This, the core team felt, would provide the ability to track multiple funding sources for a particular project as well as multiple project destinations for single funding sources. This financial management vision was a radical departure from the historic grant and contract accounting practices referred to at Ivy as commitment accounting (CA): a method practiced within many research universities. Faculty were comfortable with, and supportive of, this CA method of calculation and reporting.

## 4.2. The vision and practice of faculty: commitment accounting

Commitment accounting involves reserving, or setting aside funds for future activities and reporting to the Principal Investigator how much money they have left after these commitments are recorded. One Departmental manager explains:

“Its very common for faculty members to come and say ‘my grant started in August and around March of next year I’m going to have a Post Doc starting, the salary’s going to be this, put a commitment in’. Come November, the PI says ‘oops, Post Doc’s not coming until February ‘cause they have to finish some work on their thesis. Change the commitment.’ So its like a—you know—a two minute—not even—a thirty-second transaction—you went in there and you changed it—his grant got updated, and the commitment burdened the account appropriately—and it was done.” [winter 1999–00]

The aim of faculty is to spend all their grant dollars to further their academic research and project an image of productivity and innovation. From the Principal Investigator’s perspective, grant money is a means to achieving a creative and academic end. Obtaining a ‘zero balance’ by the end of their grant is perceived by them as a way to control this end because it speaks to the financial health of their research endeavors.

In summer 1999, when the newly introduced ERP system, based on TPB went live, it no longer provided faculty with the commitment accounting functionality. Their reactions to the new ‘best practice’ were largely negative, as evidenced in the narrative of a young scientist at the Medical School responding to his encounters with the ERP:

“I mean [laugh]—we [the faculty] paid for a good chunk of the Vision system out of our indirect-cost rates—now the reward is we get [Central leadership] telling us how to manage our research with [spoken slowly] time-phased budgeting. It doesn’t work! Our administrators cry over this stuff. So I want to know [pause] who decided that [Central leadership] knew better than me and my department the best way to run my lab and manage my money?” [Spring 2000]

Similarly, another PI from Medicine states that the ERP functionality did not meet faculty needs:

“I don’t understand why they insist that we are asking the wrong question—they say its about managing to a budget plan—asking ‘where am I in relation to where I was three months ago’, but who are they to say what questions we should be asking? I think it’s that they spent almost four times more than they originally planned [pause]. Maybe faculty should tell them they need time-phase budgeting! They would love that! [laughter] So they spent millions and didn’t meet the business needs of the community, that’s hard to take and I think they are having a hard time admitting they were wrong.” [Summer, 2000]

The work practices inscribed within the legacy grant practices versus those underpinning the ERP budget application, point to the distinctly different perspectives of central administrators versus departmental administrators and faculty Principal Investigators. First, commitment accounting is a locally designed system created by departmental management and Principal Investigators to answer the simple, but crucial faculty question: ‘How much money do I have left to spend?’ Second, time-phased budgeting was designed as Ivy’s standard practice and was expected to improve institutional reporting and thereby reduce financial risk, and increase management reporting and analysis capabilities. However, from the faculty perspective, the latter approach reduced the services they were receiving from Ivy administration and changed the way they conducted the business side of their scientific enterprise. As such the ERP design was interpreted by faculty as an attempt to align faculty practice with professional managerial values without an adequate understanding of the nature of grant and contract support:

“I’ve heard it enough times, there would be no need for commitments [in the ERPenabled environment]. That through time-phased budgeting, we were going to be able to do all of our projections. You know—again—having done grant and contract administration, knowing that faculty research buying habits are very different, timephased budgeting to me just doesn’t work in that arena. Their administrators need to be working on what they’ve spent and what they have available to spend. If you wanted to build in some projections—a financial buffer—we were able to do this in very different ways for each faculty member because of the capability of Commitment Accounting. You know, I think that was something that was missed. You move into that new house and you forget that well you had air conditioning in your old house and you’re worried about you know, getting the Jacuzzi tub and the central vacuum and all of a sudden you move in and realize they didn’t put the air conditioning in and there are no windows. I think it’s very common when new systems get implemented that people loose sight of what [worked before]”. [interview with liaison—end-users and system, winter 1999–00]

## 4.3. Departmental administrators: piggy in the middle

Grant finances were controlled by departmental administrators on behalf of faculty whose expertise lay in their ability to straddle two worlds: Ivy’s academic enterprise and central administration:

“It is important to remember that departmental administrators report to Chairmen and Directors not to Auditing or the Financial Controller’s Office. We need to figure out how to help them present the new way of working to their Chairs.” [Direct observation of planning meeting, spring 00]

The departmental management role (and accompanying information systems) grew organically to support the values of academic freedom and ‘scientific separateness’ as distinct from issues of central management and governance. From the perspective or departmental administrators, the new system was more complicated than the old and put on them an increasing burden. This is powerfully expressed by one departmental administrator:

“It takes much longer to do a lot of the things we used to do. You know, we thought when they said modernization, that [the ERP] would be quicker. But I think the question has to be asked, quicker for whom? Because before it used to be, you pulled out a form and you filled it out and you sent it off<sup>.</sup>So what used to be a faculty appointment form<sup>.</sup>became 25 screens of entry [in the ERP]. We don’t have people that just do data entry. They are answering faculty questions. I’ll use the case of the Chairman’s admin associate, she does petty cash, she does faculty appointments, she handles all of the Chairman’s business plus anything else that comes in from the faculty, and so it’s a customer service position it’s not just one where she can shut her door, sit there and take a half-hour or forty-five minutes or however, long it takes to do a faculty appointment. There’s constant interruption, relentless interruption. And that’s very typical<sup>.</sup>I mean we can’t hide behind voicemail when a faculty member wants to see me, they come in.” [interview with user representative for changes to system, spring 00]

One important issue here relates to the skills of these departmental administrators, which were a lot more generalist than those in central administration. One central certified public accountant (CPA) explains:

“People don’t trust the system because they don’t trust their knowledge of why it’s changing. It’s too complicated. and [Ivy]—higher ed—it’s just a different kind of environment. I mean—most big organizations that use these ERP systems—yeah they might have 100 offices worldwide but most people in those offices are accountants and they know what they’re doing—[laugh]—you know? They don’t have to deal with the ‘secretary C [clerical salary grade]’ that doesn’t know a debit from a credit that’s talking to a faculty member whose a Nobel Prize winner whose very angry and is contacting the Provost because the secretary C can’t explain this number on a grant report. It’s a really different environment.” [project leader, summer 2000]

This difference between central and departmental administrative cultures is illustrated even more strongly in the following quotation from a departmental administrator. The quote also illustrates the diversity of organizing research-based practices across the Ivy academic community.

“Each of the central departments has one application to deal with, whether it’s the Purchasing Department or Accounts Payable [the practices are] easily translated to a particular [Vision] module. Human Resources was the HR module, and Payroll was the Payroll module, I compare it to the medical profession<sup>.</sup>[Central] were the specialists whereas the departmental managers were sort of the family physicians out there having to diagnose and figure out what we had to do on all of these different fronts. And for every department it was going to affect them differently because every department is staffed differently<sup>.</sup>So [the ERP design] was going to affect everyone differently. The way my department is fragmented, we had specialists for purchasing but then I saw it was going to affect the person who does my faculty set-ups and she also does petty cash and she also does cash receipts<sup>.</sup>I looked at this and said there isn’t a perfect model in any one department because we, we’re all structured differently because of space, because of size, many different things. I mean we’re not homogeneous, the [academic] departments at [Ivy].” [Winter 1999–00]

## 4.4. Overcoming resistance: the creation of new support structures

In the summer of 1999 a group of science-based departmental administrators joined together to express their concern about the unique challenges associated with the changeover to an integrated environment. They sited a powerful example of recording faculty appointments in the ERP system as illustrative of the added complexity and time that the ERP would require. Appointing faculty is a prerequisite to all academically-based financial transactions such as the payment of faculty salaries and allocation of their grant dollars. One manager explains the Vision business process:

“[Vision] is so time-consuming. To enter faculty human resource details it takes 26 screens—and you had a wait to go between screens, the wait times between screens were like five minutes. You could go out and have a cup of coffee or something because it took so long.” [winter 1999–00]

The inefficiency of this proposed administrative environment coupled with the ‘best practice’ system’s lack of important grant accounting functionality, prompted departmental administrators to forewarn their chairmen that when the new academic year began in September, they should expect lots of administrative difficulties. One Business Manager enrolled her very powerful chairman into helping garner support from the science community. The chairman had a direct and close link with other science chairs and the University Provost. The Business Manager describes her Chair’s attempt to work on her behalf:

“I had a Chairman who, because of my involvement in the project, was actually very instrumental in getting other science chairs to kind of wake up about this [stressful working environment]. Being a senior chair, he was the convener of the Science and Engineers Chairs meetings. And me having his ear<sup>.</sup>he brought the chairs and people from the project to [those meetings] along with representatives from the Provost’s office. I think it was for many of those chairmen, a real wake up call to say—’we don’t know what you’ve heard<sup>.</sup>they’re being handed something that’s going to be very difficult to work with, you’re not going to get the information you’re accustomed to.’ So that to me was a tremendous support.” [winter 1999]

His influence led to the Provost inviting top ERP project leaders to a luncheon in order to speak about the concerns of the science faculty. As a result of this meeting, the Transaction Support Center (TSC) was set up to complete the clerically-based, administrative work of science departments. It was emphasized that the knowledgebased work remained the charge of faculty administrators who did not want to lose any of their authority in the ERP-enabled environment. This center was viewed as temporary and working on behalf of Ivy’s science-based faculty. A different center—the Business Support Center (BSC) was set up for the smaller non-science departments. This was also seen as a temporary center that helped to shift the effort of translation from the personal assistants in the smaller departments to a central administrative unit. Similarly, Ivy’s wealthier professional schools such as Medicine designed their own support mechanisms very similar to the TSC and BSC models.

## 4.5. Outcomes (to date)

TPB was designed as Ivy’s institutional budget system which the core group expected would also satisfy the grant accounting needs previously met by the CA system. Faculty members and their business office staff were frustrated that a business-oriented rationale had been prescribed by the Project team without consulting them about such a significant conceptual shift. They felt insulted that the Commitment Accounting functionality was not considered a priority by the core group and Vision. In the winter 99–00, leaders representing faculty and departmental administrators lobbied for changes to both the system and the support structures. In response to this pressure, the core group agreed three things. First, they agreed to leave the mainframe legacy system running until additional ERP functionality was created. Second, they would meet the faculty functional requirements by designing ERP-based commitments. Third, the BSC and TSC would be left running at least through the end of next fiscal year.

These concessions were made by the core group in an attempt to keep faculty and their support staff enrolled in the ERP ‘best practice’ system in spite of their anxieties related to accounting functionality. The core group reorganized post installation priorities and created ERP-based commitment functionality within thirty working days. This customized application was ‘bolted’ onto the accounting application. The core group sold this as a user-friendly solution with a web-based interface and promptly turned off the Commitment Accounting system. The bolt-on eliminated the need for the legacy system because it converted raw financial data into commitments through particular algorithms but it didn’t keep the managers from using their Excel shadow systems for the creation of PI Reports. Rather, administrators would download from the ERP all commitment data applicable to each faculty member. They would import this data into Excel where they continued to create their reports. This process was more convoluted and time consuming than in the legacy environment but it was successful for the Business Manager who needed to have control over faculty financial commitments in order to answer the all important question ‘how much money do I have left to spend?

In response to an email sent in 2002 to follow-up events that had occurred following completion of the fieldwork, one representative candidly stated:

“The PI’s are receptive to the new reports but this is step 2 in the process, there are web statements available that mimic the old world but departments have not informed the faculty of them because they are afraid of giving faculty all that power. So I show [the faculty] those first and then intro the new reports that they are working on. [The Budget Director and Project Change Manager] tout time-phased budgeting because it is their show but we are trying to make a huge change that isn’t necessary. The faculty are never going to use time-phased budgeting why are we working so hard to change the culture? In fact [a project leader] is going through some angst right now because [he] isn’t sure anymore about the validity of timephased budgeting and grants reporting. [He] says we need to either change the culture to fit the approach or change the approach to fit the culture and now [he] thinks that maybe we shouldn’t really work to change the culture. One way or the other we need to commit to something.”

In 2002 the status of the Ivy’s ERP environment was such that the two ‘interim’ support centers were still in operation, the format and content of faculty reports were still under debate, and the bolt-on system, and Excel shadow systems remained in use.

## 5. Analysis and discussion

It has long been recognized that the diffusion and implementation of new technologies, such as ERP, is not adequately explained by a rational-efficiency model (Abrahamson and Rosenkopf, 1997). Fashion-setting theories in particular, consider how social bandwagon effects are important in explaining why certain solutions become defined as ‘best practices’ and diffuse rapidly (Abrahamson, 1996; Kieser, 1997). So, for example, organizations adopt new managerial ideologies and practices in order to retain legitimacy within their particular field, rather than simply to improve efficiency (Meyer and Rowan,

1997). These authors demonstrate how rhetorics about ‘best practice’ are selectively promoted by particular social groups who have a vested interest in their adoption. Moreover, in order to ensure that these ‘best practices’ have wide appeal they are deliberately promoted in ways that give them interpretative viability such that they are applicable to a variety of contexts and applications and can be opportunistically and selectively interpreted to suit different purposes and agendas (Benders and Van Veen, 2001). While this work on management fashions points to the socio-political and cultural issues influencing the diffusion and adoption of new technologies, it does not address in detail the political character of networking processes that influence their implementation in particular contexts. As Newell et al. (2001) suggest there is a need for more research on the adoption of so-called best practices that addresses in detail the socio-political character of networking processes and the relative and contested nature of networking activity surrounding their adoption. In this paper we explore these processes as they pertain to the implementation of an ERP system in a particular university context. In doing this we demonstrate how a discourse was created around ERP but that because of the diversity of cultures present in this context this was unable to suit the different purposes and agendas of all the different stakeholders. In this section, we use the theoretical lens of ’epistemic cultures’ (Knorr-Cetina, 1999) to explore why finding a single ‘best practice’ was problematic in the case of Ivy.

## 5.1. Co-existing epistemic cultures

Knorr-Cetina (1999) proposed that different communities exhibit distinct cultures that inform how individuals in that community know what they know. These distinctive epistemic cultures guide but also constrain practice. In this section we explore the practices and perceptions of three distinct groups of ERP users—faculty researchers, central administrators and departmental administrators.

## 5.1.1. Faculty researchers

Faculty will obtain a research grant or contract to engage in a set of work practices (a research study) that ultimately aims to lead to the creation and/or legitimation of knowledge. Thus, progress in a given discipline depends on ‘the honest reporting of genuine results’ (Engineering and Physical Science Research Council, 2000) that emerge from adopting methods based on norms of scientific rationality (Hollway, 1989). The actual methods through which knowledge is created and legitimated vary across different scientific and professional communities (Knorr-Cetina, 1999) but the research process is likely to be complex and messy because of the inherently emergent and socially constructed nature of knowledge (Tsoukas, 1996).

In particular, where a research project is multi-disciplinary, which is very often the case as funding bodies often now promote such research (Knights and Willmott, 1997), such messiness and emergence is likely to be particularly apparent because technologies are unclear and participation is fluid and uncertain (Cohen et al., 1972). Individuals from different backgrounds and disciplines will work together and through this process of action and interaction, new ideas are likely to emerge that were not predictable in advance of this interaction (Nonaka, 1994). Thus, knowledge is constructed and legitimated through networks of social relationships (Brown and Duguid, 1991) and this process is not linear, but rather is iterative and difficult to predict. For example, in relation to the biomedical area of research, breakthroughs often occur at the interstices of communities and rely on knowledge (both tacit and explicit) being communicated and integrated across communities (Hage and Hollingsworth, 2000). This may mean embedding new knowledge and work practices but also disembedding the old. Given that knowledge is ‘invested within a given practice’ it will be ‘at stake’ such that the disembedding of this knowledge is a political process (Carlile, 2002). This is why the research process has been described as ‘organized anarchy’ (Newell et al., 2001). This depiction of academic research work is very different to the Budget Director’s view which emphasized the codifiable nature and cycles of academic work.

Given this context, while a grant application will typically propose a project plan this is unlikely to be rigidly adhered to since the process of discovery is typically difficult to predict in advance (Newell and Swan, 2000). Indeed, having to predefine deliverables or outcomes may constrain the research process because of the inherently emergent and fluid nature of research (Newell et al., 2001). This is particularly the case with research that involves multiple investigators since the demands of interactivity will add to the complexity of knowledge creation (Gibbons et al., 1994). A grants and contracts information system in this context will need to support this messy process.

When faculty apply for research funds they will put together a financial proposal that roughly estimates how much they are likely to spend on different activities over the course of the project. There is also likely to be a timetable, depicting periods in which different activities are going to be undertaken. However, both the financial estimates and the timetable are generally considered to be malleable, dependent on the emergent and disordered nature of the research, as discussed above. What the academic researcher therefore wants to know from an information system is what monies they have left under the different headings of the budget originally proposed. Apart from any salary costs (which will be taken from the grant at predetermined and regular intervals) they are not really interested in when money is spent because they recognize that different phases of a project will be more or less intense in relation to expenditure. They want to know what money they have left so that they can make plans to spend it. Most faculty members obtaining grants are also not trained accountants and so do not want complicated accounting statements that they will have difficulty interpreting. While faculty are smart as the Budget Director states, they are not interested in taking responsibility for administrative activities in order to streamline Ivy’s business processes. Their goal is to use the financial resources available creatively over the life of a project to ensure that maximum benefit is derived from the money available in relation to the goals of the research. The legacy systems operating at Ivy, which were based on commitment accounting, provided faculty with both the information they were most interested in— what money they had left—and were simple to use. Commitment accounting was a classification system that worked for Ivy’s academic community precisely because it did not attempt to mandate the analytical process of budgeting and management. Analysis occurred in the minds (not the computers) of faculty and their administrators who then interpreted a single remaining balance for their specific local needs. The new information provided by the ERP, based on TPB, did not provide this information, at least not in simple terms.

## 5.1.2. Central administration

The Central university administrators, however, are working in a very different cultural context. As mentioned in the background section, the administrative side of universities is seeking to become more professional to ensure that they remain competitive in the increasingly complex global higher education marketplace (Barnett, 2000; Gumport, 2000; Brennan et al., 1999). This was certainly central to the agenda of the Director and VP, who saw the implementation of ERP as an opportunity to restructure Ivy’s work activities and increase central control of the financial management of Ivy’s research practice. Their managerialist perspective meant they hoped to fashion Ivy accounting on a corporate business style designed to increase administrative efficiency and make faculty accountable through adherence to a standard.

The Budget Director defined higher education as a network of ‘incredibly complicated businesses even though we’re not-for-profit<sup>.</sup>’. As such, he vehemently argued that University faculty should bring their accounting practices in line with the rest of the University and manage their research projects as professional businesses. The Director and VP were, then, focused on the commercialization of contemporary universities and the importance of professionalizing governance activities in order to stay competitive within an increasingly global marketplace. The perspective of lazy financial management on the part of academics enrolled the Vice President into TPB despite this approach being at odds with Commitment Accounting practices that were standard within multiple research universities. TPB fitted within the powerful rhetoric of the audit society (Power, 1994) and so provided a legitimizing device for its introduction, at least from the perspective of the Director and VP and those in central administration. The audit society relates to revising local cultures in an effort to meet the institutional needs for audit assurance. As Power (1994) writes:

“The audit society is characterized by active processes of making environments auditable, structuring them to conform to the need to be monitored rather than to what may be regarded as their own primary logic.” [p. 300]

This quote alludes to the tension between acquiring institutional order and accountability whilst maintaining the integrity of local epistemological orderings. As fiduciaries of grants and contracts from external funding agencies, Ivy is subject to external audits to ensure regulatory compliance. In addition, these activities are closely scrutinized by legal counsel and Ivy’s Internal Audit department who work in collaboration with external auditors. Therefore, audit compliance was a foremost concern to central leadership when they chose to modernize Ivy’s administrative systems. The narratives of the Budget Director and VP support this argument and illustrate the rhetorical power of audit for justifying shifts to valued legacy accounting practices. As Power (1994) notes, the nature, content, and expectations of audit within organizations often drive actors to problematize work activities and design alternative calculative practices such as timephased budgeting. TPB was thus seen by central administration as providing an independent and standardized way to ensure and evaluate sound financial management. As such, the ERP and time-phased budgeting were regarded by central administration as audit technologies that could be used to reduce institutional risk by ‘inhibiting the deviant actions of agents’ (Power, 1994) through compliance to a standard set of scripts. However, as seen, the faculty had a very different perspective of TPB and simply saw it as eroding the existing financial management system which accommodated their needs.

## 5.1.3. Departmental administration

In between these central administrators and the faculty were the departmental administrators. They were operating in a different cultural context again. The vision of the Budget Director was to decentralize the grants management process by removing this departmental administrative intervention and asking faculty to monitor, review, and report on their grants. This was part of his vision of the ERP as a vehicle for the professionalization and decentralization of administrative activities. The expectation was that administrative personnel acting as intermediaries between central functional departments and the academic community could be removed from the business process and accountability redistributed. However, in reality, faculty continued to rely on departmental administrators and now to an even greater extent because they could not understand the budget statements that were sent to them by the ERP system. However, the departmental administrators also had problems with the system because it was seen as making their work practices more difficult rather than easier.

The problem in this case is that these departmental administrators are generalists, involved in many different practices, including supporting faculty with the control of their grants. The ERP system developed to support this process required a level of specialization that was not possible for the departmental administrators. As Steinbrenner (2001) notes, back-office ERP technology is designed to improve institutional business processes such as accounting and payroll rather than end-user service. As such, the technology assumes high level use by specialists in particular functional areas that may be separated by time and space but share expertise. For this reason we hear of multinationals implementing ERP to integrate worldwide administrative functions. However, within Ivy the reverse is true: while the scale of time-space distanciation is less that that of a global corporation, the intra-organizational gap related to the depth of functional expertise is wide. While functional specialists reside in the central administrative units, and spend their working days concentrating on one area of expertise, many departmental administrators are experts in the program for which they work, but not in particular administrative functions such as accounting.

In other words, the design of the ERP assumed a level of sophistication, and a knowledge base fundamentally at odds with the current arrangement of University work. This is because Ivy, as most other universities, is organized in such a way that accounting responsibilities span levels of organizational expertise. Central financial leaders often hold the professional qualification of CPA. Whereas professional managers and departmental administrators’ grasp of accounting theory is limited to their programmatic agendas. Finally, unionized clerical staff has little or no understanding of accounting as a body of knowledge. Therefore, the design of the ERP shifted the nature of expertise needed within Ivy’s administration. The original vision was simply to remove this group from involvement in the process of grants accounting. However, the reality was that, as the faculty would not take on the responsibilities expected of them because they did not feel the new system accommodated their needs, these departmental administrators were users of the system but were simply over-whelmed by it.

## 5.2. Intra-organizational diversity

The divergence between the different communities in terms of their perceptions of the ERP system generally and the TPB approach specifically is explicable, since as Brunsson (1990) argues, audit is a political technology which can be seen as being less about gaining institutional control and more about making particular groups accountable by allocating responsibility. Nevertheless, those promoting the ERP and TPB approaches used the rhetoric of audit control to try and legitimize the acceptance of what they were presenting as a ‘best practice’. Interestingly, Power (1994) argues that these technologies emphasize the significance of being audited or monitored, over the details involved in operationalizing that process. However, that was not the case at Ivy. While the ERP design did not necessarily preclude the development of an auditable commitment accounting application, the concept, as interpreted by those in central administration, connoted a locally negotiated process of low-level bookkeeping activities that did not fit well with Ivy’s professionalization agenda. Instead the Director and VP chose to create a customized budget application which purposefully excluded the notion of commitment accounting from its design. In this way the time-phased approach was both about assigning responsibility to faculty and standardizing the methods that constitute that practice. We argue that it was the latter form of standardizing the process of grants management that stripped faculty of their autonomy and stalled the naturalization of the best practice design into the University community.

This accords with Doorwewaard and Van Bijsterveld (2001) who argue that in order to successfully disseminate a new management practice and make it widely acceptable, it must be presented as a set of simple, universal and non-controversial ideas. These ideas can then be translated from the perspective of the different actors involved. They argue that given the different actors involved in any context, each with their own assumptions and understandings, the processes of confronting, intermingling and reinterpreting concepts and practices is essential to ensure success. However, in Ivy this was not done and instead a standard method and practice was prescribed by one group of actors. Thus, the opportunity to enroll the different groups, with their different needs and perspectives, was not achieved and instead resistance was encountered from faculty who felt that their needs had been neglected. We see therefore that one must be willing to negotiate on some things in order to keep users enrolled in the system as a whole (Scott and Wagner, 2003). In addition, we argue that for Ivy’s ERP to have ‘interpretive viability’ the role of faculty administrators is essential because they sit at the crossroads between faculty expectations and the best practice design.

More generally, these differing perspectives of what the appropriate epistemic machineries are that should be built in order to construct valid knowledge reveals intraorganizational diversity as limiting the viability of best practice designs. As Knorr-Cetina points out, an intra-organizational focus has much to offer because it challenges the notion that within a particular knowledge institution (in her case science, and in ours ‘the university’) there exists only one kind of knowledge. Instead it encourages the identification of ontological differences and methodological divergence within these institutions (p. 3). She continues:

“Interpretations of science all tend to assume science is a unitary enterprise to which epistemic labels can be applied across the board. The enterprise, however, has a geography of its own. In fact, it is not one enterprise but many, a whole landscape— or market—of independent epistemic monopolies producing vastly different products”. (1999; 4)

This diversity has historically provided the foundation for the production of knowledge in universities. Winner (1997) criticizes higher education for reducing this diversity by adopting corporate concepts in order to assimilate a business-oriented storyline. This leads to the situation where universities emphasize adapting to technology rather than the opposite. The power of best practice software is then to inscribe the values and politics of professional managers over traditional academic standards. This has the potential to reorder the working activities of actors and send a message that there is only one source of valid knowledge within the university. Focusing on these processes of standardization helps us think about the implications of design—of squashing multiple knowledge repositories—when the ultimate goal is to find a way to co-ordinate and govern an organization. In this way perceived success or failure of an ERP design is secondary to understanding the power of technology to act as a delegate for a particular world view, and a silencer of others.

In summary, the coexistence of academic and administrative leadership within Ivy’s organizational hierarchy and of central administrative specialists and departmental generalists resulted in the ERP creating conflict rather than cooperation amongst the enduser community. Faculty preferences for the financial management of their grant awards were designed out of the Ivy-Vision design and as such the faculty no longer acted as-if they trusted administrators to control and account for these activities. The collision of the different epistemological orders was most prevalent when the TPB functionality was introduced to the faculty. The ensuing faculty resistance and opposition to TPB stalled the naturalization of the system and new structures were created as an alternative means through which to communicate with the ERP. These new structures tempered the core group’s rigid standardization because Ivy actors were able to work around the system and maintain their preferred work practices. Pushing the time-phased theory of accounting and budgeting to the margins of the ERP modified the design of the system, and prioritizing neglected aspects of faculty culture had the effect of connecting previously disparate networks thereby igniting the ERP’s stalled naturalization trajectory. This analysis highlights the gap between espoused best practices and their application within an organization. We argue that in the university context it is inappropriate to force homogeneity of work. By definition universities are involved in research, teaching and service activities—an array of interconnected but different epistemological cultures—that thrive with heterogeneity rather than a best practice approach to strategy.

## 6. Conclusion and implications

In contemporary society the traditional academic institution of the university is struggling with its identity (Agre, 2000; Cornford, 2000; Brennan et al., 1999; McNay,

1995). Whilst the joint missions of research and teaching may remain unchanged, the context in which these activities are to be conducted is dramatically changing. Professional managers are running universities in North America, Western Europe and Japan in an effort to compete within a higher education marketplace that has grown increasingly complex and competitive over the past ten years (Barnett, 2000; Gumport, 2000; Brennan et al., 1999; Silver and Silver, 1997).

Information technology is seen as playing a central role in this professionalization agenda. For example, Agre (2000) argues for ‘the potential to create a university which is connected to all other universities around the globe’ by employing technology as an incentive for integration. Agre’s perspective gives voice to the university as part of a collective that must professionalize its practices in a standard way if it hopes to be perpetuated as a bastion of contemporary society. In the following quotation we understand the crux of Agre’s argument related to standardization:

“The key [for universities] is to preserve information by standardizing everything that does not make a difference<sup>.</sup>The great opportunity here lies in the efficiencies that are to be gained by standardizing and networking all of the practices, in accounting systems for example, whose difference make no important difference to the local circumstances of a given campus.” [p. 499]

In this paper we explore the impact of these standardizing practices. Standardizing accounting practice within Ivy involved disrupting many silos of activity that were valued by powerful faculty. While central leadership were informed by a standardization perspective, we saw that shifting the practice of accounting within Ivy was far from unimportant for faculty who relied on flexible reporting practices. In fact this seemingly unimportant change in accounting practice attempted to epistemologically reorder the university work.

Through the theoretical lens of ‘epistemic cultures’ (Knorr-Cetina, 1999) we have argued that in organizational contexts made up of very diverse epistemic cultures, the use of a standardizing best practice model will be problematic because, by definition, the model mandates one epistemological position through the software design. This is counter to the university’s loosely coupled organizational form which is underpinned by semiautonomous reporting relationships. We conclude that, in a context where you have diverse user groups, with different work practices and epistemic cultures (Knorr-Cetina, 1999), and with different levels of background experience, a single industry solution is not going to be ‘best’ from all perspectives.

True, the ERP development alliance between Ivy and Vision resulted in a ‘best practice’ product which is being marketed on the vendor’s international web site as their ‘higher education industry solution’ appropriate for universities across cultural and geographical contexts. However, what Vision’s software product literature doesn’t tell is that Ivy is still radically customizing its business processes, organizational structure, and ERP technology in an effort to create a local information system that meets the needs of both the administrative and academic cultures that coexist within Ivy. We have shown how the problem stemmed from the fact that there were differences between faculty and administrative agendas in relation to their needs for information related to grants and contracts. We have shown that these differences were such that the enterprise system defined and developed to represent ‘best practice’ during this software-university partnership to develop the industry standard, met the needs of only one of the constituent users—the central administrators. Thus, somewhat ironically, even in the site where the so-called ‘best practice’ was developed, the university eventually resorted to modifying the standard in order to ensure that the faculty actually co-operated in the system use. In other words, using the vanilla organization-wide system was not adhered to, even in the site where the ‘best practice’ was initially defined. The likelihood that this ‘best practice will be adopted as standard across other sites is perhaps even less likely.

In conclusion, the main goal of this study has been to challenge the normative definition of software products providing a shortcut to business excellence. We have seen that what is defined as exceptional work practices for one group, flies in the face of another equally important group of interests. Our interpretive research design foregrounds the notion that truth claims are relative and as such, we have sought to foreground this subjectivity by presenting different interpretations of what is best. The official story of a software project which is recorded in organizational documentation and reflected in the ’best practice design, represents the dominant interpretation of the initiative, but it does not represent what ‘really happened’. Unless one believes that power indicates superiority, the notion of ’best practice’ can only ever mean the dominant perspective.
