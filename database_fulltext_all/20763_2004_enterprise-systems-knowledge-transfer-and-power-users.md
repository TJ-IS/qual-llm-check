# Enterprise systems, knowledge transfer and power users

Olga Volkoff<sup>\*</sup>, Michael B. Elmes, Diane M. Strong

Department of Management, Worcester Polytechnic Institute, Worcester, MA 01609, USA

Available online 28 January 2005

## Abstract

There are few studies of knowledge transfer (KT) between groups with dissimilar purposes and dissimilar practices, which is the situation when transferring knowledge from an enterprise systems (ES) developer team to the users of the new ES. To support such KT, organizations identify a group of power users to work with the ES team and then to help other users integrate the new system into their operations. That KT process still encounters barriers. In a comparative case study of four such KT situations at two different companies we identify the critical barriers, and empirically uncover two new KT mechanisms that are effective for addressing the KT barriers in the ES context. q 2004 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Enterprise systems; Implementation; Power users; Knowledge transfer; Case study

## 1. Introduction

A major problem in enterprise system (ES) implementation is overcoming barriers to effective transfer of knowledge from the implementation team to users (Baskerville et al., 2000; Lee and Lee, 2000; Robey et al., 2002). Users must not only learn the software, but also adapt their work processes, both canonical (formally designated or espoused) and non-canonical (pragmatically employed), to fit the business practice and social interaction assumptions embodied in the software (Brown and Duguid, 1991; Lee and Lee, 2000). Since an ES integrates tasks that were once loosely coupled, users must also learn how their actions with the software affect others and are affected by others.

After configuring the software, ES team members are the main repositories of knowledge about it. Since team members learn about existing business processes and specify the new ones, they are also the process experts (Baskerville et al., 2000). They do not, however, perform real business tasks using the software. Compared to software knowledge, ES team members have less understanding and less concern for changes to business processes, organizational infrastructure, and social interactions, which are also harder to articulate.

The team’s knowledge, such as it is, must be transferred to users. While the explicit technical knowledge is fairly easily transferred (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995), users must also learn the associated practices and relationships relevant to their context, which are implicitly embedded in the software and only emerge during use. Users must develop this practice-based knowledge or knowing largely by themselves (Brown and Duguid, 1998; Cook and Brown, 1999; Orlikowski, 2002), but they need a core of knowledge transferred from the ES team to do so.

While various organizational mechanisms and structures can support ES knowledge transfer (KT), few have been effective. Traditional training, delivered by ES team members or consultants, is only partly successful. Ineffective KT can exacerbate the damage from the typical productivity dip after ES implementation (Markus and Tanis, 2000; Ross and Vitale, 2000; Timbrell et al., 2001) and prolong the period before realizing ES benefits. User acceptance of the software can also be harmed.

‘Power users’ now play a key role in ES KT (Baskerville et al., 2000; Volkoff et al., 2002). They are chosen from user groups for their experience and knowledge of existing business processes and are temporarily assigned to the ES team. Based on what they learn from the ES team, they deliver training to other users, and may also be responsible for generating training materials. After implementation, they return to their departments as resident ES experts.

We do not know, however, how best to structure the power user role to overcome critical KT barriers in the ES context. As this ES KT problem emerged during our three-year study of a multi-phase ES implementation in a global manufacturer of specialty equipment, we started to observe and interview power users. To verify and extend our findings from three implementations at this site, we also interviewed power users at a second organization.

Our objective is to study KT in the ES domain and extend existing KT theory. Existing research on KT mechanisms does not address the specific barriers affecting KT from an ES implementation team to an organization’s users. Using a multiple-case design (Yin, 1994), we identify barriers to KT in the ES domain and the KT mechanisms that address these barriers. Through our cross-case analysis, we identify two new KT mechanisms that address barriers in the ES domain.

## 2. Knowledge transfer among communities of practice

## 2.1. Knowledge and knowing

Before discussing KT, we specify our perspective on knowledge itself. Two knowledge conceptualizations dominate the literature (Orlikowski, 2002; Schultze and Leidner, 2002;

Tsoukas, 1996). The first treats knowledge as an object or asset, and constructs knowledge taxonomies. The tacit/explicit knowledge distinction (Nonaka, 1994; Polanyi, 1966), examined at either an individual or collective level (Raelin, 1997; Spender, 1996), is well known. Knowledge has also been categorized as declarative (know-what), procedural (know-how), and causal (know-why) (Brown and Duguid, 1998; Garud, 1997; Ryle, 1949; Sanchez, 2001), as well as embodied, embedded, embrained, encultured and encoded (Blackler, 1995).

The second criticizes this taxonomic approach as ignoring the mutually constituted nature of knowledge components (Tsoukas, 1996). It views knowledge as inseparable from the actions of ‘knowers’ as they engage in daily practices (Orlikowski, 2002). The process of ‘knowing’, not knowledge per se, is the real issue in this practice-based view (Blackler, 1995).

These two views are not, however, mutually exclusive. While knowledge (that which we possess) and knowing (that which we enact through practice) are distinct, both matter and inform each other in a ‘generative dance’ through which knowledge evolves and knowing is enhanced (Cook and Brown, 1999). In this integrated perspective, knowledge on its own, without practice, is incomplete. Similarly, the process of ‘knowing’ requires knowledge, without which practice cannot occur. Furthermore, practice generates new knowledge, which is the foundation for further knowing. We take this integrated perspective, and consider both ‘knowledge’ and ‘knowing’ in our KT study.

## 2.2. Communities of practice

Since knowing is, by definition, specific to a context where knowers engage in practice with each other, an appropriate level of analysis for discussing knowledge and knowing is the ‘communities of practice’ associated with the ES implementation and in which knowing is enacted (Brown and Duguid, 1991). While knowledge is often considered an individual trait (Cook and Brown, 1999), knowing arises through the social interactions of individuals engaged in a shared task, and must be examined in a practice context (Brown and Duguid, 2001).

A ‘community of practice’ (CoP) is a group of people engaged in a specific problem or task domain characterized by (1) mutual engagement—interpersonal interaction related to a shared purpose, (2) joint enterprise—a collectively developed understanding of that shared purpose, and (3) a shared repertoire of communal resources including routines, symbols, stories, language, artifacts, styles, tacit conventions and underlying assumptions (Wenger, 1998; Wenger, 2003). A CoP’s shared purpose and shared practices distinguish it from general aggregations of people. CoP members define their personal identities—an understanding of who they are—by participating in a shared enterprise. Members also participate in negotiating what the shared purpose is and how it is understood, and take responsibility for its accomplishment. Newcomers become CoP members through ‘legitimate peripheral participation’ whereby they engage in the community’s tasks and activities, and both acquire competence and contribute to the shared repertoire of resources (Lave and Wenger, 1991).

One CoP in an ES implementation is the ES team members, whose shared enterprise is to configure and implement the software. Sub-communities focus on different software modules, the database underlying the ES, and the physical infrastructure supporting the ES. Competence in these communities requires understanding the software and its configuration, and familiarity with related technology and technical jargon. The practices engaged in by members include troubleshooting unanticipated transaction outcomes, setting software parameters to ensure that data inputs trigger corresponding database changes, designing data conversion routines, and trying to accommodate user functionality requests within available ES functionality.

The ES supports these tasks, but it should not interfere by requiring users to focus on the software and its transactions. While software use is part of a member’s competence, the communal resources of each user community are focused on business tasks. ## 2.3. Knowledge transfer and its challenges

‘Knowledge transfer’ is a dyadic exchange whereby a recipient is affected by the experience of a source (Argote et al., 2000; Szulanski, 1996). Knowledge is transferred when a knowledge reservoir (embedded in a person, tool, task/technique, or network of these elements) is moved from one group to another, or when an existing reservoir of the recipient is modified (Argote and Ingram 2000). While ‘knowledge’ may be moved, ‘knowing’ must be created by the recipients themselves. KT entails both these aspects since knowledge and knowing are inseparable. In the ES context, the knowledge source is the ES team and the recipients are the future ES users. The KT process utilizes KT mechanisms consisting of structures and activities to transfer the knowledge.

The objective of this paper is to examine the KT process between the ES team and user communities during an ES implementation and, by focusing on the barriers encountered, to identify mechanisms for overcoming them. In the remainder of this section, we discuss KT challenges, barriers, and mechanisms from the literature, and indicate those of special relevance to the ES context.

In general, KT difficulty increases as the differences between the source and recipient increase (Argote and Ingram 2000; Carlile, 2002). Knowledge and knowing are easier to transfer within a CoP than between CoP’s because of their shared purpose and practices (Brown and Duguid, 1998, 2001). When the KT source and recipient are distinct CoP’s, they may have different purposes and practices. With such differences, the knowledge may not be compatible with the recipient CoP, requiring additional effort to adapt the knowledge (Argote and Ingram, 2000). Moving knowledge between CoP’s will involve, at minimum, ‘translating’ it into the recipient’s frame of reference (Newell et al., 2000), or ‘transforming’ it into something new (Carlile, 2002).

## 2.3.1. Knowledge transfer between similar communities of practice

The KT literature has been motivated by the practical difficulty of transferring new practices (e.g., activity based costing, design review processes, sales practices, and total quality management) between similar units in the same organization (e.g., similar subsidiaries in multinational firms, plants in large firms, sales organizations, or software development teams) (Argote and Ingram, 2000; Argote et al., 2000; Szulanski, 1996; Zander and Kogut, 1995). In these situations, the recipient and source CoP’s have similar purposes and similar practices, arguably the easiest KT situation. KT barriers identified relate to characteristics of (1) the knowledge transferred, (2) the knowledge source, (3) the knowledge recipient, and (4) the transfer context (Szulanski, 1996), see Table 1.

The traditional KT literature documents KT mechanisms, although most are not tied to a particular KT barrier. Table 2, adapted from Argote et al. (2000), identifies mechanisms, ranging from training to personnel movement, that support KT, for possessed and practicebased knowledge.

Traditional classroom training, publications and documents are effective mechanisms for transferring explicit knowledge. Other knowledge is better transferred through mechanisms such as personnel transfers, apprenticeship, and stories shared among employees (Argote et al., 2000; Brown and Duguid, 1991). An appropriate mechanism for transferring ‘knowing’ is through legitimate peripheral participation (Lave and Wenger, 1991), by which newcomers engage in a group’s activities.

For knowledge embedded in practice, practice-oriented KT mechanisms are important. Example mechanisms include sending experts from the knowledge source to spend time with the recipient or sending apprentices from the recipient to spend time with the source group—that is, mechanisms that use temporary personnel transfers (Argote et al., 2000). A mechanism used at our case organizations is ‘power users’, individuals from the users CoP sent to work with the ES team and to bring back practice-based knowledge.

## 2.3.2. Knowledge transfer across different communities of practice

Few studies consider KT across distinctly different CoP’s (Bechky, 2003). One research stream that does is motivated by the practical problem of product development (Bechky, 2003; Hansen, 1999), which requires contributions from sales, design engineering, manufacturing engineering, and production (Carlile, 2002). These four CoP’s have a common purpose—successful new or revised products—but different practices. New product development success depends on KT among these CoP’s despite their lack of a common practice base to interpret meaning. Boundary objects, e.g., engineering drawings, are a possible mechanism for addressing KT barriers between CoP’s with different practices but a shared purpose (Carlile, 2002; Carlile and Rebentisch, 2003; Star and Griesemer, 1989). Instead of abstract engineering drawings, Bechky (2003) found that physical objects such as portions of a product being assembled were a better means for developing shared understanding. These studies conclude that KT between different CoP’s cannot involve just transfer; knowledge must be transformed and recreated in the context of the recipient CoP (Bechky, 2003; Carlile, 2002).

Orlikowski (2002) examines globally distributed software development, which requires KT among teams in different locations. Although these globally distributed teams have a common purpose and common practices, maintaining these requires continual transfer and sharing of practice-based knowledge, i.e., knowing, across these similar organizational units. A primary KT mechanism was face-to-face interaction, despite the cost and effort, so that organizational members could gain trust and credibility and build and sustain social networks (Orlikowski, 2002). This study also notes the value of personnel transfers and other people-based approaches as KT mechanisms. The power users at our field sites are examples of people-based KT mechanisms.

As KT mechanisms between different CoP’s, people assume the role of ‘boundary spanner’ (Brown and Duguid, 1998; Friedman and Podolny, 1992; Tushman and Scanlan, 1981). This role takes different forms, the ‘knowledge broker’ being relevant when practice differences are significant (Brown and Duguid, 1998). Knowledge brokers formally participate in both communities. If they are competent in both areas, they reconcile any inconsistencies between the two communities and interpret what they have learned in one context into the language, activity patterns and social interaction networks of the other. A broker, as a participant, can be held partly responsible for the knowledge brought to and knowing enacted in the recipient community, and so suffers the consequences of unpopular resolutions to inconsistencies (Brown and Duguid, 1998). For this reason Friedman and Podolny (1992) propose dividing the broker role into two roles, representatives, who are in charge of what goes out from one community, and gatekeepers, who monitor what comes into the community from outside.

## 2.3.3. Knowledge transfer in the ES context

ES KT differs from the two contexts described above because KT involves CoP’s with dissimilar purposes and dissimilar practices. Furthermore, the ES team and the user communities are not working together to develop a product, the primary context in the literature for KT across communities with dissimilar practices. Given the importance of context to both knowledge and knowing, it is an open question whether the KT barriers and mechanisms discussed in the literature are applicable to ES implementation.

The few KT studies conducted in an ES context underline the complexity of the problem, but do not indicate mechanisms for addressing it. Lee and Lee (2000) note that the canonical processes embedded in the ES software often conflict with the noncanonical processes embedded in the tasks and practices that user CoP’s perform. They suggest a KT perspective, but do not address how knowledge and knowing can be transferred.

Timbrell et al. (2001) used Szulanski (1996) taxonomy of KT barriers (Table 1) to examine inter-firm KT between vendors, consultants, and ES customers. The critical barriers were the recipient’s lack of absorptive capacity, due to ES software complexity, and lack of retentive capacity, seen as an inability to unlearn old routines. The study did not, however, look at potential barriers not on Szulanski’s list, nor examine mechanisms for resolving these problems.

Pan et al. (2001) examine the ES knowledge problem from an integration, rather than a transfer, perspective, and focus on the ES team’s needs to integrate knowledge from across the organization during configuration. They highlight the importance of interpersonal relations between the team and business units so the team can acquire business-related knowledge, and between different business units so they can understand each other’s needs. They note that KT between the ES team and the user community is complex because the user community is actually many communities that become more closely connected after ES implementation. With new integrated business processes, users’ knowledge and knowing must include the networks of interrelated tasks and the people who work together to perform them (Argote and Ingram 2000). Furthermore, these networks change with a new system, and have to be re-established, and then learned and understood.

Robey et al. (2002) studied two KT problems, the challenges ES teams face in configuring the software to meet organizational needs, and the difficulties user communities experience assimilating the new practices. To address configuration, they recommend building a cohesive ES team of knowledgeable business and technical employees, which then becomes a valuable knowledge repository. To address assimilation, they recommend extensive formal training, both conceptual and procedural, and incremental implementation, either by location or by module, to apply lessons learned to the next stage, but they do not address how to stimulate practice-based knowing in user communities. They call for ethnographic studies that examine the problems of situated learning and note the value of mechanisms such as power users. In this study, we respond to Robey et al. (2002) call for research that examines problems related to situated knowledge.

## 3. Research sites and methodology

To understand how KT occurs in the ES context and why it is so challenging, we chose a multiple case study approach. The case study method is particularly appropriate for answering ‘how’ and ‘why’ questions and for describing and explaining the phenomenon in question (Yin, 1994). We compared and contrasted findings from each case, and so extracted more broadly applicable explanations.

We employed intensive methods of prolonged field observation coupled with interviews and document analysis, rather than limiting ourselves to pre-defined theories. Our longitudinal approach, appropriate because the KT process occurs over time, entailed weekly site visits for three years and recurring interviews with power users and other users several times during the ES implementation.

## 3.1. The sites and the cases

Our primary research site was ACRO, a multinational producer of high-precision industrial equipment. The company’s headquarters and twenty operating locations are in the US, with additional plants and service facilities in eleven other countries. In 1998 ACRO initiated a project to implement ES software from SAP, configured as a single instance, throughout the company. We selected ACRO because it had a major ES implementation within which multiple distinct but comprehensive implementation projects were planned.

Our observations at ACRO began in August 2000 at its manufacturing and assembly operations, which were implementing a full set of SAP modules. During these observations, we became aware of the challenges for power users, and decided to focus on their experiences. The three ACRO cases come from the same organization, but represent separate implementations in different locations handled by different ES teams. Case 1 involved the implementation of SAP functionality in one of several maintenance and repair plants. Case 2 examined the implementation of SAP in product development operations. BAC’s European headquarters had decided to implement SAP in all its operations using a common global template. A pilot project in Europe had gone live, and the first major implementation in North America was underway. At the time of our interviews, go-live was imminent.

## 3.2. Data collection

Data was collected through observations, formal interviews, informal conversations, and attendance at meetings and training sessions. Interviews were taped and transcribed. Over the three years of data collection at ACRO, 123 field site trips were made (see Table 3). While at the sites, there were many opportunities for informal discussions with power users. After each trip, we typed our field notes, including reflections on what we observed.

In Case 1, once power users were selected, we shadowed eight of them (a ninth was added later). The only selection criterion for power users was willingness to be shadowed, resulting in the participation of most power users. We observed their integration testing at the ES team location, their delivery of training back at the plant, their project status meetings, and their use of the ES as they returned to work. We conducted formal interviews at three times: before go-live, shortly after go-live, and almost a year later. We also observed, spoke informally with, and interviewed regular users and their managers, as well as members of the ES team. Case 1 observations occurred from October 2000 to November 2001 (go-live was August, 2001) with follow-up interviews in the summer of 2002.

Case 2 employed the same techniques as in Case 1. We followed eight product development power users through testing and training and added three more after go-live. Case 2 was a very large implementation with many power users representing the many user groups. Through attendance at weekly meetings of user managers, we became aware of users who were highly regarded and were becoming power users. We met with these individuals, who all agreed to participate in our study. They introduced us to others who also agreed to participate. Observations and interviews began in December 2001 and continued with follow-up interviews until April 2003 (go-live was October, 2002).

Case 3 presented a slightly different situation. The new contract management group was not only small, with fewer power users, but also spread over ACRO’s many manufacturing facilities. To maintain coordination, these power users, the ES team and the managers met weekly for ten months, and one of the authors attended these meetings. At go-live, the entire contract management group was brought together to verify that data was correctly converted, perform tests, and practice its new business process. This case coincided with Case 2, with observations starting in December 2001 through go-live in October 2002, and follow-up interviews in April 2003. While we only conducted six formal interviews with these users, observations and informal conversations were frequent because of the way they met and worked together.

For Case 4 at BAC, data collection was through formal interviews, as we did not have the opportunity to conduct observations or attend meetings. In the process of scheduling interviews, we had informal discussions with an ES team leader. These interviews occurred in December, 2003.

During our interviews, we asked general questions about organizational changes experienced during and after the ES implementation and questions specifically related to power users’ experiences. In the pre go-live interviews, we asked power users how they had beenselected,andwhattheyweredoingorexpectedtodo.Laterinterviewsincludedquestions about their impressions (both positive and negative) of the power user role, and a request to reflect on what had contributed to or detracted from successful fulfillment of this role.

## 3.3. Data analysis

Data collection and analysis were iterative. As we collected data, we coded it to identify passages related to bridging, boundary spanning, or learning. We then coded these sections further to identify major issues encountered by the participants and their ways of handling them. We also wrote memos and met as a research team to discuss emerging themes. Given our many field site trips and the many opportunities for informal discussions with power users, our field notes were a rich source of data. Based on emerging issues, we revised and refined our interview schedule.

Following accepted practices for multiple case study research (Miles and Huberman, 1994; Yin, 1994), we first did within-case analysis of the four cases, extracting major themes and relating them to the literature. Through this process, we identified the KT barriers encountered and the mechanisms employed, looking for reasons why the power user structure at that time was either effective or ineffective. Table 4 provides a summary of the major outcomes from the analysis of each case presented in a format that facilitates cross-case comparisons.

We then performed a cross-case analysis. Certain themes appeared across the four cases, e.g., without additional KT mechanisms, power users ran into barriers and were ineffective KT mechanisms. By examining the ways these themes manifested themselves, we identified two approaches to structuring KT mechanisms that appear to improve transfer effectiveness. We also examined the variations in how these two approaches were implemented to understand when and how they were effective. The cross-case comparison also provided insights into how the lack of shared purpose and shared practices contributed to other problems sites were experiencing with the new ES.

## 4. Power users as knowledge transfer mechanisms

The designation of employees as ‘power users’ (also known as ‘super users’) is a common mechanism for transferring knowledge from the ES team to user communities, particularly in large-scale implementations. Power users are members of the user community, with on-going business roles, but who spend time working with, or as members of, the ES team. While the role of power users in ES implementations is mentioned in the literature (Baskerville et al., 2000; Davenport, 2000; Volkoff et al., 2002), there are no studies of how they actually effect KT. Power users represent two types of KT mechanisms, namely training and personnel transfer.

Generating training manuals and delivering training are often tasks of the ES team or associated consultants. While these individuals are the most knowledgeable about how the software works, users regularly complain that they still do not know how to do their jobs. This training outcome is not surprising given that the core responsibility of the ES team and consultants is to configure and implement the software, not to perform business tasks. As a CoP, ES team members have a conceptual understanding of canonical business processes, but are not required to execute them. Unfortunately, users cannot create their own knowledge through practice without understanding how to use the software and the canonical processes embedded in it.

Using personnel transfers in the form of power users should improve traditional training as a KT mechanism by bridging the two CoP’s. One typical pattern for power user responsibilities, followed at both ACRO and BAC, is to designate one or two individuals from each affected user department as power users. As the ES team completes software configuration, power users join them for integration testing. Test scripts prepared by the ES team are executed by power users who have received basic navigation training. When they encounter problems, team members show them what to do next or correct the configuration as necessary. When testing is complete, power users are expected to be very familiar with the software.

In theory power users, as broker-style boundary spanners, should be effective in KT from the ES team to users. They are experienced members in their user CoP, and spend time working alongside ES CoP members. The power user structure has the potential to reduce standard KT barriers (Szulanski, 1996), e.g., through their understanding of the software and standard business processes, power users could reduce causal ambiguity by explaining ripple effects arising from the integrated nature of the software. Similarly their presence reduces the distance between the ES team and users by providing a stronger link between them (Hansen, 1999). By returning to their work group, power users should increase absorptive and retentive capacity for the new knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990).

Our observation of several ES implementations suggests that the power user role is extremely challenging, and as structured in some organizations, does not address critical

KT barriers. Our case analyses show how two companies organized their power users, and how their role evolved over time to improve the KT process.

## 5. Case synopses and within-case analysis

## 5.1. Case 1—ACRO maintenance and repair

The maintenance and repair plant selected a dozen power users from around 650 users. The power users represented the various functional areas from materials management and customer service to shipping/receiving, inspection and accounting. Their purpose, and management’s purpose in releasing them to the ES team, was to learn the software as it pertained to their departments.

Their primary task at the ES location, however, was running test scripts developed by the ES team, and indicating problems. Scripts were not matched to user expertise, e.g., a shipping/receiving power user might run inventory planning scripts, nor were they executed in business process order. Since the system was still being changed, scripts might not execute because software functionality had changed. ES team members, busy with development, had little time to work with or train power users, nor to resolve test script problems. Thus, power users spent much time waiting. Occasionally, power users noticed scripts that did not properly support their work processes, but the ES team was not interested in considering changes.

Back in the repair organization, power users complained that they had not learned to apply the software to their department’s business tasks. Nevertheless, they started training with training materials prepared by the ES team, and sometimes not available until the course started. Training did not go well and there were many no-shows in the classes. Power users had no time to review training materials and could not answer questions about how the ES supported users’ tasks. Also, the sample data used in training was not relevant to the users.

Power users as a group decided to create and run scripts that matched the organizations’ tasks and used actual data, to test handling of repair orders from start to finish. At a walkthrough with their managers, each power user handled their department’s portion of an order and passed it to the next power user. This promoted their understanding of how transactions supported the business task, but they continued to train with existing training materials. At go-live, users did not know what they needed to know. Power users, despite working long hours on the implementation, were blamed by users, management, and the ES team for an organization unprepared for the ES.

Eight months after go-live, with the organization still unprepared and struggling, management selected four super power users and relieved them of their normal tasks. This group created new training material and procedure manuals, re-did the training, and worked to resolve the organization’s problems with the ES. These activities prepared the plant, and it began to use the ES effectively.

This case demonstrates several KT barriers and suggests mechanisms for overcoming them. The first barrier was the lack of a common purpose between the ES team and the business units. As demonstrated by power users running test scripts not matched to their jobs, the two groups’ expectations differed, i.e., power users wanted to learn to use the system to perform their jobs, while the ES team needed the power users as software testers. Power users participated in a practice that did not support acquiring knowledge and knowing relevant to their needs. Becoming effective testers was irrelevant to their business tasks. The lack of common purpose also explains why users—whose purpose was doing their job, not implementing the system—often missed scheduled training and did not know what to do at go-live.

A second KT barrier was the lack of common practices. The ES team did not engage in relevant practices that power users could acquire through peripheral participation. The ES team was configuring the system, not using it. They ensured that transactions worked as designed, while power users assumed that transactions worked and wanted to use them for real customers, products and repair orders. The lack of common practices was evident in the ES team’s training material not being grounded in users’ tasks. Executing an abstract transaction with unfamiliar data does not generate context-based knowing.

Another barrier arose from the integrated nature of an ES. After go-live, the ability of any unit to complete its tasks depended on, and was constrained by, what other units did or failed to do. Thus, the practice-based knowing emerging within each community was insufficient; effective practice-based knowing also required an appreciation of the broader picture.

The first mechanism power users devised to address these KT barriers was to develop context-specific scripts for a walkthrough for management. This provided an intermediate form of practice, but it was short term. It did not translate into improved training for users because they continued with existing training materials.

A second mechanism was the super power users. This group provided an ongoing structure whose purpose was to define new practices and establish them in the user community. It also provided the integrating oversight for practices of different user communities. For example, to resolve problems in receiving, super power users developed and delivered focused training to inspectors, who were not correctly completing transactions that sent parts for repair, resulting in a ‘rock pile’ of inventory that receiving could not enter into the system.

Due to the lack of shared purpose and shared practices, the ES team did not respond to power user suggestions for configuration changes. If power users had been real ES team members, these suggestions might have been considered, rather than immediately dismissed. Similarly, if power users had configuration skills and authority, they might have made the changes themselves. The ES team’s response illustrates the lack of shared language, conventions, and assumptions for handling user requests, which can impede and sometimes kill the emergence of knowledge and knowing between the ES team CoP and user CoP’s.

## 5.2. Case 2—ACRO product development

Case 2, the implementation of SAP in ACRO’s product development operations, was a much larger project with 30 power users and thousands of users. Power users ran test scripts related to their jobs and handed scripts to each other as appropriate. ES team members, like in Case 1, were still developing the system and had little time to spend with them. As in Case 1, the ES team did not want to hear about system revisions. One difference is that some power users developed course material from SAP templates, rather than testing. Since these power users did not participate in testing, they did not know the system and could not insert practical knowledge, resulting in course material similar to that of Case 1.

All power users delivered training to the many new users. After delivering a course up to 30 times, they became expert at its content and delivery. Go-live was smoother than in Case 1, but there were user complaints that the software did not provide sufficient flexibility for product development.

When power users returned to their departments, users expected them to know all ES functionality, but power user expertise was specialized and narrow. Because they knew other power users from their shared experiences with the ES team, they could refer users to an appropriate expert.

No new KT barriers arose in Case 2, but new KT mechanisms did. Power users formed a group that simulated business practice as they performed testing, rather than acting as individual testers. They were more confident in delivering training, since they had answers when users asked about how the system supported existing business processes. They overcame the lack of shared practices with the ES team by engaging in activities that looked enough like real practice that appropriate knowledge and knowing emerged.

Back in their business units, no formal structure resembling the Case 1 super power user group emerged. They did, however, use the relationships they formed during testing and training to create a network that performed similar functions, and used it to solve problems related to integrating across user communities. As in Case 1, the ES team dismissed user requests for changes, but power users, with their greater system experience, developed workarounds more quickly.

## 5.3. Case 3—ACRO contract management

The contract management implementation differed from the first two cases because it brought ES functionality to a new group without established business processes. The number of users involved was relatively small, and the organization was highly focused. Before the new business unit was created, 450 individuals managed contracts manually and reported to whichever organizational unit was responsible for the contract. Under the new organization, 150 people oversaw the contracts, and despite working from the old locations, reported to the newly created central department. Under these circumstances, this group started with little shared practice.

At the ES team site, contract management power users ran test scripts related to their jobs, worked alongside the ES team member with corresponding ES expertise, and worked with another power user doing related training material. Power users, ES team members, and the new group’s manager met weekly to resolve issues about the design of new business processes and identify power user tasks for the next week. The ES team was responsive to requested changes, and this ES team’s manager and the user manger resolved issues between them as needed.

Training was tested with the contract management manager and her direct reports, with issues resolved before training others. Training was intensive; each user received more than a week of training. At go-live, all users came to the ES team location to ensure that data for every contract was converted correctly and that each user could manage their contracts using ES transactions.

The KT barriers in Case 3 were slightly different from those in the first two cases. Barriers related to lack of a common purpose and practices still existed, i.e., the ES team focused on configuration and implementation practices, while power users identified new contract management practices. The barrier associated with the need to integrate across business functions did not arise for the group itself, because it had a single if complex function, although their work did need to integrate with the rest of ACRO.

A new KT mechanism in Case 3 was the co-location of power users with their associated ES team member. With this co-location, ES team members felt committed to supporting their power users, and took time from their implementation tasks to do that. This supported better communication about job practices and test script problems.

The central KT mechanism was the weekly meetings chaired by an influential senior manager. Her presence ensured that the ES team and the power users focused on common tasks and that concerns about business practices were discussed and resolutions found. When the ES team fell behind schedule, the managers delayed go-live.

The mechanism of a weekly meeting with a senior manager provided a forum for developing new business practices. Before testing started, meeting agenda items focused on process design, e.g., How would outsourcing of portions of the contract be handled by the ES? How would differences between plan and actual be handled? As testing started, agenda items focused on solving business process issues revealed by testing. With this focus on new practices, power users developed knowledge and knowing about performing their jobs. The ES team verified that the software supported these practices, and through this activity, ES team members experienced business practices vicariously. These meetings evolved into weekly user CoP meetings after go-live. As in Case 2, the relationships developed among power users and ES team members provided an informal network of value to power users back in their jobs.

Similarly, bringing all 150 users together for several weeks to convert and test data helped to develop a network of relationships. Taking all users away from their usual desks for a protracted period is rarely possible. It was only viable because the business unit was new.

## 5.4. Case 4—BAC make-to-order manufacturing

BAC learned to value the role of power users from its experience with a different ES implementation ten years earlier, and its pilot European implementation. BAC carefully selected senior individuals, two from each of five process areas, e.g., order fulfillment and materials management. Some power users were designated ‘process owners’, and they worked with the ES team during configuration, almost a year before go-live, when they could influence system configuration. BAC had, however, adopted a ‘global template’, and few local requests were accommodated. The user community, through the process owners, knew the gaps and planned business processes revisions to address them.

Process owners ran test scripts written by the ES team. As at ACRO, the ES team was busy and had little available time. The process owners were, however, co-located with corresponding ES team members so, as in Case 3, it was easier to resolve issues. Furthermore, the ES team and the process owners had developed shared meaning and language through their joint configuration work. While process owners met weekly with the ES team, they did not consider themselves part of the team. One power user who worked with the ES team from the beginning considered himself fully part of the ES team 50% of his time and fully part of his user community 50% of the time.

All power users did training and were given generic training material to use. They had themselves received training from a consultant using this material. They started training with this material, but most power users decided to customize it. With training already underway, they quickly developed new material including procedure manuals. After receiving training, users were required to spend four hours each week until go-live in an ES practice room. Trainers assisted in the practice rooms and provided users with relevant practice exercises. While this implementation is going well, its success is yet to be determined.

Power users’ experiences were clearly different at BAC compared to ACRO. BAC faced similar KT barriers as at ACRO and employed a few of the same mechanisms, but introduced several new ones. Power user engagement with the ES team at BAC started much earlier than at ACRO. They were part of system blueprinting practices, not just testing practices. At blueprinting, ES practice most resembles business practice, since it focuses on business processes. Power users then became legitimate peripheral participants in the ES community at an appropriate time and in a useful way. They engaged in practices that enabled them to enact knowledge and knowing that had relevance for building future job practices. As in Case 3, they worked directly with designated ES team members, and met weekly to discuss issues.

As at ACRO, BAC power users discovered the importance of building training materials around actual job processes, not transactions, and incorporating real data. This allowed users to simulate practices and to enact knowing prior to go-live, which was further supported at BAC with practice sessions after training. Power users were certain that users could perform their jobs at go-live, if not as quickly, at least with confidence.

Power users formed stronger ties with ES team members than with power users representing other processes. Thus, it was not clear how they would bridge the interconnected practices of an integrated system. They had advantages, however, because they were already operating in an integrated environment with their old ES, and they were organized by business process, which in itself supports integration.

## 6. Cross-case results and discussion

Across the four cases, the primary KT barrier is the large gulf between the ES team and the user communities with respect to both their purpose and their practices. These two factors are related: the purpose of the ES team is to configure and implement the software, while the purpose of the user communities is to perform business tasks, so it is hardly surprising that their practices are distinct.

Overcoming this KT barrier cannot be accomplished simply by assigning power users to the ES team. While the ES team knows how the software functions, the way they enact that knowledge in practice is not helpful to users. Because knowledge and knowing are mutually constitutive (Cook and Brown, 1999), users cannot ‘receive’ this software knowledge from the ES team without engaging in practice themselves. Unfortunately, because the ES team’s core practices are not relevant to the user community, legitimate peripheral participation in the ES community does not provide an opportunity to enact the right kind of knowing.

Across the four cases, we observed two emergent structures, a power user CoP and a bridge between the ES team and the user community. These two structures are organizational forms that support power users as a KT mechanism.

## 6.1. Forming a power user community of practice

As depicted in Fig. 2, one structure to support KT suggested by the cases is an intermediate CoP between the ES team and the business units where power users can simulate business practices. Like the concept of ‘ba’—a shared space that serves as a foundation for knowledge creation (Nonaka and Konno, 1998)—an intermediate CoP provides a community for creating and enacting knowing. We observed three variants of an intermediate CoP: (1) a testing environment that simulates business practice, (2) a walkthrough for the business unit before go-live, and (3) a formal structure or an informal network of users to solve practice-related issues after go-live. Each of these scenarios supports power users’ activities that enact knowing.

In each scenario, power users enacted practices and generated knowing that made sense in the business context, rather than the ES team context. The interplay of knowledge and knowing is what generates both new knowledge and new ways of knowing (Cook and Brown, 1999), but this ‘generative dance’ requires an appropriate venue for enacting knowing. Without an intermediate CoP, knowledge may be transferred, but users must start from scratch in building knowing. Lack of an opportunity to enact practice may explain Timbrell et al.’s (2001) finding of lack of recipient ‘absorptive capacity’.

In the first two scenarios, the opportunity to enact relevant practice occurs before golive. These scenarios suggest that creating a formal power user CoP for conducting walkthroughs and delineating procedures would facilitate KT before go-live. Furthermore, a pre go-live CoP provides an environment for generating training material containing user-relevant business practices.

An intermediate CoP also provides a structure for exploring new practices needed in the new integrated environment, where problems encountered by one software user may have their origin in actions taken in another department. Solving such problems requires knowledge of the interrelated business processes and the relevant software functionality— a breadth of knowledge difficult for one person to grasp. The intermediate CoP provides a venue where members can confer about the broader picture.

The power user CoP addresses the barrier of conflicting purpose between the ES team and user communities by introducing a CoP with its own purpose—to develop the practices and knowing needed by the new ES users. CoP members’ activities, e.g., developing user-relevant training, performing ES-based walkthroughs of user business processes, and solving post go-live problems, help them enact the knowing needed by users.

Using an intermediate CoP as a KT mechanism is not necessary in traditional contexts of transferring practices between similar business units because simple personnel transfers provide relevant knowledge and knowing. Similarly an intermediate CoP does not emerge in the product development context because product development project members meet as a team around a shared purpose, but they never develop the shared practices that define a CoP. They have a common purpose and use boundary objects related to that common purpose to support KT (Bechky, 2003; Carlile, 2002). An intermediate CoP arises in the ES context because there is neither a common purpose nor common practices.

## 6.2. Building a bridge between the ES team and user cop’s

The power user CoP, a KT mechanism that allows the two communities to address their distinct purposes and practices in separate arenas, cannot address all ES KT barriers. Some KT barriers can only be addressed by the two communities together since some tasks are shared. The ES team is delivering, to users, business processes embedded in the ES software around which user practices must be enacted. Thus users are not free to devise whatever practices they choose. The ES team is also not free to embed whatever processes they choose, as the software must serve business needs.

A bridge structure, depicted in Fig. 3, is a KT mechanism for overcoming different purposes and practices related to shared tasks. Such a structure helped the CoP’s focus on the main shared task, namely to delineate appropriate business processes. The barrier this mechanism addresses resembles product development environment barriers where the different CoP’s do not share a repertoire of language, conventions, assumptions, or other communal resources. The ES team has one view of the new business processes, influenced by its knowledge of the software, while users have a different perspective, influenced by their existing practices. Unlike the product development environment, the two communities also do not share a common purpose. The ES team’s purpose is to implement the software quickly with few changes, while the users’ purpose is to perform their business tasks with software that supports them.

A bridge between power users and the ES team overcame this KT barrier by bringing the two CoP’s closer together so they could engage in the joint task of defining business processes. The ES team needed defined business processes so they could configure the system. Users wanted to provide input to ensure that any new processes supported their business tasks. They needed knowledge of the ES-embedded business processes as a foundation for developing the practice-based knowing required to use the ES effectively. The joint task of defining business processes had meaning for each community in terms of their separate purposes.

In Case 3, the bridge structures were the weekly meetings and co-located work. In Case 4, those power users designated as process owners participated in the practice of the ES team during blueprinting, a time when ES practice was meaningful to power users. Through these three bridge structure scenarios—weekly meetings, co-located work, and joint blueprinting—power users were better able to play the broker role. Without such a structure, the ES team expected power users to represent the ES to users, but did not allow them to represent users to the team. The user community expected power users to act as gatekeepers and objected to new business processes that made their job harder. A bridge structure supported the broker role and provided a venue where differences related to lack of shared purpose and practices could be negotiated.

In the product development environment, boundary objects rather than a bridge structure addressed the KT barrier of the lack of shared meaning the groups needed to understand each other. In the ES context, diagrams of the new business processes could serve as boundary objects. At blueprinting time, process diagrams served as boundary objects between the two CoP’s. The ES team used these diagrams at the start of configuration, but they were not updated to reflect the de facto alterations that occurred as the reality of configuration constrained what was possible. Updated process diagrams were not maintained in any of the cases, although in Case 1, power users tried to generate such diagrams as they prepared for the business process walkthrough.

While up-to-date business process diagrams would be boundary objects with meaning in both communities and could be a KT mechanism supporting better training materials and procedure documents, there are two practical problems. The level of process detail needed by the two CoP’s differs, i.e., the ES team worked from higher level diagrams for configuration, while users wanted detailed diagrams with contingencies specified. Also the ES team had little interest and no time to maintain up-to-date diagrams of value to users. For the ES team, the appropriate boundary object is the system itself, which contains the up-to-date processes, but in an embedded form not easily understood by power users.

Without a viable boundary object, a bridge structure is an appropriate mechanism for overcoming KT barriers when there is no common purpose, nor a shared language. A bridge forms when two groups participate in a joint task that has meaning for both, and where the participants have enough authority and credibility to influence how that task unfolds. The ES team uses its technical expertise to provide that authority. In Case 4, process owners had some role-based authority. Often, as in Case 3, the authority comes from top management involvement.

The intermediate CoP and the bridge structure are complementary KT mechanisms for addressing barriers related to lack of common purpose and lack of common practices. An intermediate CoP addresses the differences between the ES team and power users by separating them, and allowing each to pursue its own agenda. It is particularly useful for resolving barriers related to integrating business processes. The bridge structure addresses the need for the two CoP’s to work together on common interests where differences can be negotiated and reconciled. It ensures the two communities stay synchronized for the artifact they share, namely the ES system and its embedded business processes. The hard work of negotiating differences should be reserved for issues that must be resolved. For most of each community’s work, maintaining their separate ‘ba’ is more appropriate.

## 7. Conclusions

When a new ES is brought on-line, users need knowledge about the software and how to use it to perform their jobs. The major source of this knowledge is the ES team, which knows the software and the canonical business processes embedded in it. The main barrier to recreating this knowledge and associated knowing in the user community is the lack of common purpose and common practices between the ES team and users. Since an ES integrates business functions, users in different business units must also enact new practices that account for others practices.

We examined these problems from a knowledge transfer perspective. We contribute to the KT literature by identifying contingencies that frame the choice of KT mechanisms. Which mechanism is appropriate depends on the presence or absence of common purpose or common practices between knowledge sources and recipients. We explain why different mechanisms are effective under different conditions by acknowledging that possessed knowledge is part of a symbiotic relationship with practice-based knowing, and that effective KT requires engaging in practice meaningful to the recipient community,

We also contribute an analysis of KT in the ES implementation context, a previously undiscussed KT scenario where there is neither a common purpose nor common practices. The traditional KT mechanisms of training, personnel transfers or boundary objects are not sufficient in this context. We identified two KT mechanisms—an intermediate community of practice and a bridge structure—that overcome the lack of common purpose and practices. These mechanisms also help resolve KT barriers specific to the ES context, e.g., integrating business practices across functions.

For practitioners, new understanding of what KT means in the ES context explains why some organizations’ efforts to train users are ineffective. It also illuminates the very difficult nature of the power users’ task. The two mechanisms we propose, and the examples in our cases of how to implement them, contribute to ensuring that new ES users know how to execute software transactions and can use them to perform their jobs effectively. The more quickly this occurs, the sooner organizations will realize benefits from their ES investment.

A limitation of our work is that power users may perform a range of roles in ES implementations beyond what we observed. At one extreme they may only get involved when training starts; they are the first trained and then train other users. After go-live, they become the first line of support. Under this scenario, we argue that KT is quite problematic.

At the other extreme, power users become part of the ES team, but return to their business units after go-live. Under this scenario, power users must personally reconcile multiple purposes, those of the ES team and those of the user community. They must also engage in the practices of each community. This reduces the gap between the purpose and practices of the two communities, supporting simpler KT. This structure may work for smaller implementations, but larger organizations retain ES team members for technical support, change requests and deriving new benefits from the system. Similarly, in multiphase implementations, team members move from project to project rather than back to their jobs. People may join an ES team for new challenges and new skills. Furthermore, ES team members often develop a mindset that makes returning to their jobs difficult (Volkoff, 1999).

One inevitable constraint of longitudinal research is that the processes being observed do not end when the research ends, but continue to evolve, with new practices being enacted as organizations respond to new challenges. How the KT mechanisms we observed will change over time, or what long-term impact they have for on-going KT is unknown. Future research should explore those questions. Future research could also explore the effectiveness of the two new KT mechanisms in other ‘no shared purpose-no shared practices’ environments. Similarly there may be other mechanisms appropriate for such environments in addition to the two identified.

KT in the ES context provides continuing challenges for practitioners and many opportunities for researchers. Our intensive longitudinal case-based study addresses the paucity of research on these complex issues.
