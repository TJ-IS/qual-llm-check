# Dominant actors, political agendas, and strategic shifts over time: a critical ethnography of an enterprise systems implementation

Jiunn Chieh Lee<sup>\*,1</sup>, Michael D. Myers

Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, The University of Auckland, Private Bag 92019 Auckland, New Zealand

## Abstract

Much of the literature on enterprise systems implementation suggests that ERP systems should support the strategic objectives of the organization. In fact some ERP vendors tend to assume that implementing their products is a straightforward translation from strategy to IT-enabled business processes. But the strategic management literature reveals that corporate strategy is often contested terrain. Conflict over strategy has the potential to delay or derail ERP implementation efforts, leading to cost overruns, failures, and lack of expected benefits.

This paper seeks to understand and explain how conflicts over strategy can affect the success of ERP implementations. We studied one firm’s attempt to pursue information technology-enabled enterprise integration using critical ethnography. The firm is a small-to-medium sized enterprise within a large conglomerate in the Asia-Pacific region. Our findings show that, as the dominant actors and political agendas changed, so too did the company’s enterprise integration strategy. The ERP system was designed to support a future corporate world and work environment that never arrived.

## 1. Introduction

Much of the literature on ERP implementation suggests that ERP systems should support the strategic objectives of the organization. In fact some ERP vendors tend to assume that implementing their products is a straightforward translation from strategy to IT-enabled business processes. But the political school of thought within strategic management (Mintzberg et al., 1998; Mintzberg and Quinn, 1991) reveals that corporate strategy is often contested terrain. We suggest that conflict over strategy has the potential to delay or derail ERP implementation efforts, leading to cost overruns, failures, and lack of expected benefits.

This paper discusses the strategic context of one organization in relation to the development and use of its enterprise system. We look at the contextual influences, particularly those of a strategic nature, that played a part in this company’s attempt to integrate the organization. The purpose of the paper is to seek to understand and explain how political conflicts over strategy can affect the success of ERP implementations.

The organization was a small-to-medium sized enterprise within a large conglomerate within the Asia-Pacific region, called Stark (all names are pseudonyms in accordance with a confidentiality agreement). Stark was one of many subsidiaries within the Solteria group, one of the global 500 companies with annual revenues of more than US\$4 billion.

One of the authors studied Stark using critical ethnography. The data was obtained over a six-year period in total, from 1996 to 2001, with the most intensive period of fieldwork being from August 1999 to August 2000. Data sources included interviews, informal chats, meetings, observation, the company’s Intranet, and various documents such as e-mails, annual reports, and newspaper articles.

Our findings show that the strategic context that existed at the start of the project changed over time. As the dominant actors and political agendas changed, so too did the company’s enterprise integration strategy. The strategic assumptions that had driven the dream of enterprise integration were no longer valid just a few years later. A key problem here is that implementing an ERP system takes time. By the time the ERP system was implemented, the senior management team within the organization had changed. This meant that the firm now had an ERP system that no longer reflected the strategic objectives of the firm. In effect, the strategic objectives of the earlier managers were locked into the current ERP system. Senior managers were now faced with the additional time and expense of having to re-organize their business and redesign their ERP system.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we briefly review the research literature on enterprise systems and corporate strategy. In Section 3 we discuss the theoretical background for the study. Section 4 explains our research method. In Section 5 we present our data on the history of enterprise integration at Stark. Section 6 analyzes the data. Section 7 is the conclusion.

## 2. Literature review

In this literature review we define enterprise systems and corporate strategy. Then we discuss the relationship between the enterprise systems and corporate strategy.

## 2.1. Defining enterprise systems

ERP systems, or ‘enterprise systems’ for short, have been defined as “comprehensive, packaged software solutions [that] seek to integrate the complete range of a business’ processes and functions in order to present a holistic view of the business from a single information and IT architecture” (Gable, 1998). However, although in theory a single IT architecture is desirable, the way ERP is implemented in practice differs substantially. In their study of several multi-site implementations, Markus et al. (2000) found some variation. This ranged from a company that had different ERP systems configurations in different business units (although the ERP systems were from the same software vendor) to a company that mandated a single ERP system across the whole organization. Bhattacherjee (2000) found one corporation where ERP systems from two different vendors, SAP and BPCS, respectively, had been implemented. The conglomerate we studied, of which Stark was an important part, was different again. Since it was a large, highly diversified international conglomerate with a ranking of around 400th on the US Fortune 500 benchmarks (Fortune 2000), its various subsidiaries had over a dozen different ERP systems from all the major vendors! In this kind of situation, the concept of one tightly integrated package for the entire enterprise is not feasible (although it may be feasible for individual companies within the conglomerate to have one ERP system).

A somewhat broader definition of ERP is offered by Shanks and Seddon (2000). Focusing more on the shared information and data flows that enable integration of enterprise-wide processes, they define ERP as “comprehensive packaged software solutions that integrate organizational processes through shared information and data flows.”

Over time, new modules and technologies have been integrated with the various ERP offerings. Davenport (2000) observes that, as time progresses, enterprise systems vendors tend to incorporate more and more functions into their software to accommodate new business challenges. In fact, ERP vendors like SAP, BaaN or PeopleSoft no longer tout themselves as ERP vendors but describe themselves as providers of ‘collaborative business solutions’ (SAP, 2003), ‘enterprise application’ (BaaN, 2003) or ‘pure Internet platform for the real-time enterprise’ (Peoplesoft, 2003), respectively.

## 2.2. Defining strategy

A general definition of strategy is provided by Andrews (1981). He defines strategy as “the evolving pattern of decisions that both determines and makes clear what a company’s purposes, goals and principal policies have been and now are.” He says that strategy generally defines the range of business the company intends to pursue and excel in while also more broadly reflecting “what kind of business organization the company intends to be for all its constituents—shareholders, employees, customers, and communities” (Andrews, 1981, p. 20).

In the contemporary strategic management literature there are (at least) two broad approaches to research on corporate strategy. The first approach focuses on a company’s strategic position within an industry. This approach is perhaps best exemplified by Porter who defines strategy as “doing things differently from competitors, in a way that delivers a unique type of value to customers (Porter, 2001, p. 70).” The second approach, a broad view of strategy adopted by the political school of thought within strategic management, focuses on the processes of strategy formulation and implementation. This approach is perhaps best exemplified by Mintzberg et al. (1998). Mintzberg identifies five ways in which strategy can be conceptualized: as a plan, as a pattern, as a position, as a perspective, or as a ploy.

In this paper we have adopted the second approach to strategy. In the political school of thought within strategic management, strategy formation and implementation are seen as being shaped by power and politics. Strategies are emergent and involve dominant actors taking positions and creating ploys (Mintzberg et al., 1998; Mintzberg and Quinn, 1991).

## 2.3. Enterprise systems and corporate strategy

Recent IS research on enterprise systems has emphasized the importance of the social, cultural and organizational aspects of ERP implementation (e.g. Krumbholz and Maiden, 2001; Lee et al., 2003; Markus and Tanis, 2000; Newell et al., 2002; Soh et al., 2000). For example, Hanseth and Braa (1999) studied the ability of one organization to change after the ERP had been implemented. They found that the ERP system was considered the most resistant aspect of the organization to change after it had been implemented across many business units. They also found that the dream of standardization was like “hunting for the treasure at the end of the rainbow” (Hanseth and Braa, 1999, p. 121). Other IS researchers have focused specifically on the role of power and politics in ERP implementations (Allen and Kern, 2001; Hislop et al., 2000; Koch, 2001; Larsen and Myers, 1999; Sarker and Lee, 2000).

Much of the literature on ERP implementation suggests that ERP systems are often implemented to support the strategic objectives of the organization (Davenport, 2000; Weill and Broadbent, 1998). For example, Markus and Tanis (2000) reported that some firms implemented enterprise systems to develop new corporate capabilities like a single global view of inventory, whereas other firms implemented enterprise systems as a legacy system replacement.

However, some ERP vendors tend to assume that implementing their products is a relatively straightforward translation from strategy to IT-enabled business processes. For example, SAP assumes a translation from strategy to strategic objectives to key performance indicators to the key processes that are in turn supported by the SAP system (SAP, 2001). This translation is assumed to be one way, from strategy to processes. In SAP’s worldview, the relationship between strategy, critical success factors, general (key) performance indicators, measurable performance indicators, processes and sub-processes is central to the implementation of an enterprise system. This is shown in Fig. 1.

In analyzing the relationship between strategy and business processes, SAP has been influenced by Porter’s value chain (Porter and Millar, 1985). In Fig. 2, for example, SAP promotes the benefits of an enterprise systems implementation by using Porter’s value chain concept.

Thus, we can see that certain strategic paradigms have permeated the way enterprise systems are implemented. One of these is the competition (or positioning) school of thought (cf. McKiernan, 1996), of whom Michael Porter is one of its best known exponents (e.g. Porter and Millar, 1985). Another is the resource-based view of the firm (Kalling, 2003). This means that enterprise systems are often seen as a means of implementing corporate strategy (e.g. Davenport, 2000; Markus and Tanis, 2000; Parr and Shanks, 2000).

However, the strategic management literature reveals that corporate strategy is often contested terrain (Mintzberg et al., 1998; Mintzberg and Quinn, 1991). This means that conflict over strategy has the potential to delay or derail ERP implementation efforts. Not only do enterprise systems take a long time to implement, but the benefits of such systems may take several years after implementation to materialize.

In this paper, we focus on the processes of strategy formulation and implementation. We look at the internal power struggles over strategy insofar as these relate to enterprise integration. One of the primary contributions of this paper is that we bring to light the implications of conflicting organizational strategies for enterprise systems.

## 3. Theoretical background

We analyze the changing strategic context of one organization’s enterprise system using the premises of the political school of thought in strategic management (Mintzberg et al., 1998; Mintzberg and Quinn, 1991). We acknowledge that there are many other important schools of thought in strategic management, such as the positioning school, the learning school and the design school (McKiernan, 1996; Mintzberg et al., 1998), but the political school was the most appropriate for our project.

Fig. 2. SAP’s and Cap Gemini Consulting’s View of how porter’s value chain relates to benefits that can come from an ERP project (adapted from SAP, 2001).

In the political school of thought, strategy formation and implementation are seen as being shaped by power and politics. Strategies are emergent and involve dominant actors taking positions and creating ploys. Seen from within the organization, strategy formulation involves the interplay, through persuasion, bargaining, and sometimes direct confrontation, of various parochial interests and shifting coalitions. Typically, none of these coalitions are dominant for any significant period of time.

Inter-organizationally, an organization promotes its own welfare by controlling, cooperating or competing with other organizations, through the use of strategic maneuvering as well as collective strategies in various kinds of networks and alliances.

Adopting such a perspective highlights the emergent nature of strategy and the centrality of intentions of dominant actors in influencing the shape of the enterprise over the longer term.

In this article, we focus mostly on the formation and implementation of strategy within the organization as it relates to the development and implementation of the company’s

ERP system. A key theoretical concept that we draw upon in our analysis is that of inscription.

Briefly, the idea of inscription is that strategic choices and design decisions become embedded into an information system through the IS development process (Orlikowski, 1992). Of course, this inscription is never perfect: one can never perfectly translate one’s intentions into an information system, and conversely, unexpected ‘emergent structures may result (Orlikowski, 2000).

Nevertheless, in the case of ERP systems, the main idea is that the inscription of the design and intentions of actors into the enterprise systems occurs through the ERP configuration process. A major concern here is that, because ERP systems are so large and complex and take years to implement, the inscription of today’s strategic choices into the enterprise system may significantly constrain future action. By the time the implementation of an ERP system is finished, the strategic context of the firm may have changed.

## 4. Research method

As was mentioned earlier, one of the authors studied Stark—one of many subsidiaries within the Solteria group—using critical ethnography. Ethnographic research has emerged as one important method for studying the social and organizational contexts of IS development and use (Harvey and Myers, 1995; Myers, 1999). Critical ethnography is one particular kind of ethnographic research, emphasizing that the research itself is an emergent process (Myers, 1997). Also, “Critical ethnographers describe, analyze, and open to scrutiny otherwise hidden agendas, power centers, and assumptions that inhibit, repress, and constrain” (Thomas, 1993, p. 2). Given the focus of this research project on the strategic context of enterprise systems within one organization, we believe the choice of critical ethnography was appropriate.

The data was obtained over a six-year period in total, from 1996 to 2001, with the most intensive period of fieldwork being from August 1999 to August 2000. Data sources included interviews, informal chats, meetings, observation, the company’s Intranet, and various documents such as e-mails, annual reports, and newspaper articles. Although the focus of the research was on Stark, we considered it important to understand the broader context within which Stark operated. Hence, data was collected at many levels of larger conglomerate—at the sector, divisional and corporate levels.

One hundred and five formal interviews were conducted (most of which were audio taped) with 69 people, the duration of which ranged from around half an hour to over 2 hours. Many informal discussions with employees also took place. The people interviewed ranged from the truck drivers delivering the product to the chief executives of SEKTOR who managed groups of companies. Horizontally, across the organization, those interviewed included salespersons, IT personnel, accountants, administration workers, customer service representatives, plant managers, logistics personnel, shop floor workers, and customers.

As well as interviews, meetings were attended at various levels and departments. Across the organization, meetings such as sales, finance, production, meetings between functions, meetings between business units, meetings between Information Services

Group members, and SEKTOR group shared services project meetings that included people from various business units, were attended.

Apart from interviews, meetings and documentation, one of the authors was given access to the internal network of Stark and an internal e-mail address for research purposes. This access was given in May 2000 and retained until April 2001.

## 5. Enterprise integration at Stark

Stark is a small-to-medium sized enterprise within a large conglomerate within the Asia-Pacific region. Stark was part of the SEKTOR group of companies with annual revenues of over US\$300 million. SEKTOR was in turn part of the Flavion division (with annual revenues of around US\$1.5 billion). Flavion was one of four divisions of Solteria. Solteria was one of the global 500 companies with annual revenues of more than US\$4 billion. The organization structure of the Solteria empire for the year 2000 is shown in Fig. 3 (with the reporting line for Stark highlighted, and the subsidiaries of other divisions and sectors omitted).

Many of the companies within the Flavion division held dominant market positions in their respective markets with Stark being no exception. The SEKTOR group of companies of which Stark was a part collectively made up an industry supply chain. The organization structure of Stark is shown in Fig. 4.

Stark employs around 700 people at over 68 production sites throughout the country. Stark is now comprised of five businesses: CamCo, MaxCo, HinoCo, DrinCo and ModCo. Before November 1999, Xenon was also part of Stark (as shown by the dotted line in Fig. 4).

Stark was diverse with regards to its operations and information processing requirements. For example, CamCo was a make-to-order business while MaxCo, DrinCo and HinoCo were primarily make-to-stock businesses; Xenon was an engineer-to-order business, while ModCo was a design-to-order business.

We will now provide a historical narrative of the enterprise integration efforts at Stark, beginning with the formation of the SEKTOR group in 1990. The key events are summarized in Table 1.

## 5.1. 1990–1993

In 1990, after a series of mergers and acquisitions, the SEKTOR group (see Fig. 3) was formed with Billy Wilton as the chief executive. However, some of the business units performed poorly in 1991 and 1992. Consequently it was decided that three of the business units within the SEKTOR group—MaxCo, Xenon and CamCo—would be merged to form a new entity: Stark Industries Limited.

The merger brought together three business units, each with their own culture, their own way of doing things, and their own heroes. For example, Wyatt Dunkins, a manager of CamCo, was credited with building CamCo from an insignificant player to become the dominant player in its market (Dunkins subsequently rose to become one of the most powerful figures in Stark, even though he was never appointed as general manager).

## 5.2. 1993—a change of strategy

In February 1993 David Callon was appointed as the first general manager of the newly formed Stark. Given the differences between the business units, he organized Stark along product lines in accordance with the respective product groups that each business unit produced.

A strategic review conducted across the SEKTOR group recognized that Stark had historically been a robust and highly profitable company primarily involved in the marketing, manufacturing and distribution of SEKTOR finished products and components. (SEKTOR group Strategic Plan 1993 to 1995). One of the key strengths of Stark was “its ability to establish and maintain relationships with high volume users of SEKTOR products.”

The SEKTOR group management team recognized that an increase in Stark’s sales revenue would contribute more than any other factor to Stark’s sister companies’ profits in line with the SEKTOR value creation concept. Hence, there needed to be considerable efforts expended to develop Stark’s marketing competencies to increase Stark’s market share and create new markets. In order to accomplish this, the SEKTOR group management team believed that Stark had to transform itself from a traditional productfocused business to a business that would offer:

system solutions rather than [just] products, and will involve greater communication and relationships with key supply chain partners, as well as targeted communication programs to the general consumer (adapted from SEKTOR group Strategic Plan for 1993 to 1995).

Within the context of this strategic direction, Stark’s strategic intent was to topple the use of substitute products of SEKTOR group products by providing solutions in SEKTOR group products.

Over a 15-year period, Stark expected to expand the use of the products produced by the SEKTOR group as a whole:

Our purpose and goal over the next 15 years is to help grow the SEKTOR group from supplying 9.5% of the industry needs to supplying 25% (grow the pie) while increasing overall Stark share from 33 to 40% (earn the right to a bigger slice). (Stark Strategic Review, July 1995)

In doing so, Stark’s size would increase approximately 3.4 times. To achieve this objective, Stark would embark on the following programs:

† Generic marketing to develop the industry.

† Lower the industry cost position. Stark expected to achieve the lowest cost structure in the industry.

† Provision of ‘legendary service’ to differentiate itself from its competitors to increase its market share.

Stark was restructured along regional lines to achieve greater market awareness and customer focus (this was in contrast to the previous emphasis on production). With this reorganization also came the consolidation of administrative centers. An enterprise integration movement now began at Stark.

Stark’s management team believed that Stark’s ability to differentiate itself from its competitors in a traditional commodity market would be crucial for sustaining long term competitive advantage. One of the key areas to be addressed was the provision of better services especially through better on-time delivery. After this strategic review, Stark undertook many projects with the aim of developing a new corporate identity, developing new corporate capabilities, and changing the organization. One of these was a Strategic Information Technology Project (SITP).

## 5.3. 1995—the ERP project

The Strategic Information Technology Project was undertaken from June 1994 to May 1995 and was assisted by a large global consulting company (herein called BigFive Consulting). Stark recognized that the information systems of Stark needed to be linked to its strategic business plan. The SITP was one of the several projects that Stark undertook to realize the vision of the company as a more service-oriented, solutions-focused organization.

The primary objective of the SITP was “to achieve greater integrity, reliability, timeliness and usefulness of information available to Stark management.” The SITP team with the help of BigFive Consulting recommended that “Stark seek an integrated packaged software product to support most if not all of the business functions.” The new system was expected to support a new re-engineered process, namely, the centralized order acceptance and delivery process for Stark. A new order acceptance and delivery centre would be established to centralize order acceptance and delivery across three business units: MaxCo, CamCo and Xenon.

After a long software search period, Stark selected DAREA (a pseudonym for one of the top five ERP vendors in the world). Thus, Project Bridge that sought to implement the recommendations of the SITP team was born. Project Bridge was strongly supported by David Callon, who believed that an ERP system was essential for Stark’s future. Callon decided to hire BigFive Consulting once again, this time to assist with implementing the enterprise systems solution.

The new system was chosen to support a corporate culture and work environment that was yet to be created. This new corporate world would involve the consolidation of the three main businesses of Stark. These sister businesses did not traditionally get along well with each other and viewed themselves as separate companies (and sometimes competed against each other).

Re-engineering was an integral part and parcel of the ERP project where it was explicitly mentioned that:

as systems are implemented in each metropolitan area, the customer service and service delivery functions of Stark’s CamCo, MaxCo and Xenon operations are being merged and the systems will be designed to support these business functions<sup>.</sup> the introduction of new systems is a major catalyst for Stark to redesign its business processes and will enable Stark to achieve its desire to be a world leader in its industry processes and best practice. As such the project is expected to have a significant impact in the way Stark does business. The majority of current processes will require changes—some minor, many major to enable Stark personnel to enhance its service to “legendary service” and thereby maximizing its return on its investment in Information Technology (Project Bridge Management Plan, August 1995)

The new strategy represented a significant shift from what had been accepted before. Traditionally, most of the companies within the Solteria empire were fairly autonomous. As long as these companies remained profitable, the leaders of these companies were allowed to ‘do their own thing’ when it came to business processes and systems.

The Solteria conglomerate as a whole had a cultural norm of commensurate authority, responsibility and accountability at the business unit level.

However, the proposed consolidation and the new emphasis on enterprise integration was diametrically opposed to previous practice and existing norms. The new ERP system would require a certain degree of cooperation and process standardization. The ERP system now started to be implemented across the whole of Stark.

## 5.4. Old-timers versus New-timers

At the highest levels of Stark, there were two groups: the New-timers and the Oldtimers. The New-timers were comprised of the then general manager of Stark, David Callon, and several senior managers. The New-timers believed that the heyday of Stark’s tremendous growth phase was over. Therefore, new technology was needed to improve the business. These included, among other things, new ways of management and new information technologies. One of the managers who belonged to this group, Fred McMorland, believed that technology was an enabler and they did not have the correct people in the company to use that technology. McMorland thought that they should have a training program to get people skilled to use technology but if people were not ‘up to it’, they should have an exit strategy. This was a contentious issue since some of the employees had been in the company for a long time.

The Old-timers believed the business was as simple as it always had been. Therefore, these new initiatives served only to over-complicate the business. In their opinion, there were only a few fundamental things one needed to do to run the business well. The Oldtimers looked back to the days of Billy Wilton, who had oversight of some of the constituent businesses of Stark from 1984 to 1990 (before Stark itself was formed). During these years the various business units grew from next to nothing to a position of dominance in the market.

Wyatt Dunkins, the current leader of the Old-timers, had been part of the team that expanded the business during the 1980s. Legends were told of Wyatt Dunkins driving a truck down to a customer’s site to personally deliver Stark’s products when the customer needed it urgently. Wyatt Dunkins was still considered one of the most powerful figures in Stark. In fact, one manager said that if Wyatt Dunkins were to be involved in a political clash with the general manager, the latter would lose. He was rumored to be extremely close to Billy Wilton, the immediate past chief executive of the SEKTOR group of companies.

## 5.5. 1997–1998: the strategic debates

On 10th October 1997, mid-way through the implementation of the ERP system, a decision was made to replace the current project structure since the budget had been exceeded and the implementation was well behind schedule. The change in project structure appears to have been successful. By 1 July 1998, the ERP system was up and running in the largest commercial centre that Stark operated.

During this period (1997–1998), however, there were strategic debates at senior levels of Solteria and Flavion on the fundamental role that Stark would play within the SEKTOR group. Wyatt Dunkins, one of the Old-timers, lobbied for a change in Stark’s strategic role and enterprise integration strategy with the CEOs of SEKTOR and Flavion. The end result of these debates was a decision to change Stark from a marketing arm of SEKTOR to that of production, i.e. back to what it was before! In late 1998 Gene Romm succeeded David Callon as the new CEO of Stark. Stark’s strategic purpose was changed back to being ‘that of a low-cost product supplier’.

Stark now began to revert from a marketing-oriented organization that was pursuing the differentiation of its products and services to one that competed as the lowest cost producer. Commenting on the last two years of his tenure (1997–1998), Callon comments:

The visionary people became fewer and fewer and the Old-timers who had very product-focused views started to have a louder voice (Interview with David Callon).

## 5.6. 1999: new leader, different vision

As was just mentioned, Gene Romm succeeded David Callon as the new CEO of Stark. Gene Romm had been a business unit general manager within the Solteria empire for 15 years. By this time Stark was no longer seen as the strategic marketing arm of the SEKTOR group.

Gene Romm did not share the same integrated company vision as David Callon. Instead of trying to have a more tightly integrated company, Gene Romm espoused a clearer separation between MaxCo, CamCo and the individual businesses of Stark. He appointed divisional managers for each of Stark’s businesses. This stood in contrast to the previous CEO’s view that Stark’s businesses should be tightly coordinated. The previous enterprise integration strategy for Stark was starting to unravel. Gene Romm comments:

I think Stark’s application of DAREA is wrong<sup>.</sup> They assumed that Xenon, CamCo and MaxCo were all one business, in simple terms, and they’re not. They are three separate businesses. And we may manage them as one, but they are still three separate businesses. And they should be “informationized” as three different businesses not “informationized” as if they are one. What we’re going to be doing with our information system is taking us back to the recognition that we actually have five businesses at Stark<sup>.</sup> And that’s what caused the [fiasco] with DAREA<sup>.</sup> And we have to undo that. (Interview with Gene Romm).

## 5.7. 2000: post-implementation problems

By October 1999, Stark had managed to roll-out DAREA throughout the whole country. One of the project leaders claimed that they had replaced an invalid infrastructure that was badly in need of replacement before Y2K.

However, Stark employees generally disliked DAREA. This was due to a combination of the extra work that was needed to capture the new data required by DAREA, bad publicity resulting from senior managers criticizing it, system downtime, inaccurate data, unmet expectations due to over-selling, and slow system performance. One of the Inventory Controllers comments:

My main issue against DAREA is it’s slow. The more use it got as it [was] rolled out to the different branches, it made it slower, particularly at the month end<sup>.</sup> It becomes a real problem for us as the frontline people, where it can take us up to four minutes if not longer to process a docket for truck drivers standing at the window. When the truck driver got four or five dockets he has got to take, multiplied by four minutes, it takes quite a while and it is a waste of time. Therefore quite often we end up doing manual dockets and we have to go back later and process it. So, that’s creating more work. (Interview with Carole Wyatt, Inventory Controller).

Worse, most senior managers believed that DAREA was a bad investment. This was mostly because the budget was overrun by about 80%. The new CEO, Gene Romm, referred to DAREA as a failure. The new Chief Executive of the SEKTOR group, James Wadsworth, thought that DAREA was inferior to another ERP product used widely throughout the group. Wyatt Dunkins, the manager of CamCo and the leader of the Oldtimers, did not even have a computer on his desk.

The new centralized order acceptance and delivery centre was the epitome of Stark’s efforts to compete through service differentiation that required the creation of a tightly integrated enterprise. However, the centre did not function as expected. Not once did a customer place an order for three different kinds of products from the three business units at the same time (one of the earlier rationales for installing the system). Also, the centre was never able to achieve the goal of on-time predictable delivery (touted earlier as a key differentiator for competitive advantage). From time to time Wyatt Dunkins would force a large order through the already congested product delivery schedule to please a big customer. This was because, in his view, Stark had to take care of its large customers. These customers would call up on one day and make demands that their order be put ahead of others. Wyatt Dunkins did not want to lose these big customers (and he was prepared to disadvantage smaller customers and disrupt the delivery schedule if needed). Given his power within the company, Dunkins was able to succeed in his demands, thereby completely over-riding (and in effect undermining) the system.

In February 2000, the Stark ERP Support team considered various options to upgrade DAREA to a later version. However, given the latest business strategy for Stark, they decided to move towards splitting the ‘sales and distribution’ tables shared by MaxCo and CamCo to enable business-specific process optimization. In hindsight, they considered that these two businesses were too tightly integrated.

In summary, we can see that David Callon, appointed CEO in 1993, tried to change Stark from a company that had positioned itself as a low-cost producer taking advantage of its economies of scale to one that competed on service differentiation. This new marketing-oriented strategy required the creation of a tightly integrated, service-oriented company. DAREA was chosen and implemented to support that strategy. However, David Callon, along with his strategy, left in 1998. Of the 12 managers who signed off the ERP project at the start, only three were left by December 2000. Much of the knowledge of how the ERP system was supposed to add value to the firm had gone.

Now Stark had a new CEO and a new strategic vision that was diametrically opposed to David Callon’s vision, but almost identical to what it had been before he came along. This meant that DAREA was designed for a world that would never exist.

“It didn’t take a 180 degree turn, but it sort of—We were quite expansionist and visionary and we came back to a very conservative operational [focus] and <sup>.</sup> So it wasn’t a dramatic change, but it was more a degree of how expansionist we would have been had we wound the dial back a little bit to be more conservative. But that— that did have an implication on [the] DAREA [project]. And would we have chosen DAREA or a similar ERP system if we had the more conservative vision? Probably not.” (Interview with David Callon).

## 6. Analysis

In this section, we will explain our data from the viewpoint of the political school of thought of strategic management. Through this lens we analyze how dominant coalitions shape a firm’s strategy over time. Then we derive some insights that may help inform current enterprise systems implementation.

The Stark case has demonstrated that strategy formation over time within an enterprise systems context is shaped by power and politics. Strategy formulation within an enterprise systems context involves the interplay between dominant actors taking positions and making ploys to preserve and/or enhance their power bases. Within Stark we saw that there was a conflict of strategic paradigms between two opposing camps: the New-timers and Old-timers. The New-timers believed that Stark should compete on the basis of service differentiation. The Old-timers believed that Stark should complete on a low cost basis where maintaining good relationships with key customers would be a major factor in determining future customer purchases. In other words, favors were exchanged between certain members of Stark’s management and key customers (e.g. by giving them preference in the delivery schedule). Thus, a conflict between competing via differentiation based on on-time delivery versus competing via social capital maintenance and accumulation ensued. Nowhere was this more evident than at the centralized order acceptance and delivery centre.

The new centralized order acceptance and delivery centre was the epitome of Stark’s efforts to compete through service differentiation that required the creation of a tightly integrated enterprise. However, the dream of integration was never achieved at the centre. Not once did a customer place an order for three different kinds of products from the three business units at the same time (one of the earlier rationales for installing the system). Also, the centre was never able to achieve the goal of on-time predictable delivery (touted earlier as a key differentiator for competitive advantage).

Just 2 years after going live with DAREA the reengineered centralized order acceptance and delivery centre was split. This put an end to the dream of ‘mission control that was pursued by David Callon. The Old-timers had won.

As suggested by the political school of thought in strategic management, we can see that strategy formulation involves persuasion of others to do things or to at least approve certain courses of action. In Stark’s case, Dunkins was sufficiently powerful as to be able to over-ride the delivery schedule recommended by the ERP system. He was also able to convince the upper echelons of Solteria that it was in their best interests to revert Stark to a production arm of SEKTOR. He also convinced them that the three constituent businesses of Stark should be managed in a more separate manner. This effort on his part had a dramatic effect on the future strategic direction of the company and on its enterprise integration strategy. It effectively wound back the clock, and re-instated the company’s strategy from approximately 6 years earlier.

It is interesting to note that when David Callon first took power, the New-timers started to become the more dominant coalition. Their power peaked around 1998. But then the Old-timers started to have a louder voice in part due to the problems Stark encountered in the post-implementation stage. From 1999 onwards Stark was seen once again as a production arm of SEKTOR.

However, by the time Stark’s strategic role was changed back to being a low-cost production arm of SEKTOR, the ERP system had already been inscribed with the intentions of the New-timers. As we explained earlier, strategic objectives are inscribed into enterprise systems when the enterprise system is configured and implemented to reflect the strategic intentions of its management team.

In Stark’s case the ERP system tightly integrated three businesses resulting in trade-offs between local business unit process efficiency and supposed benefits from inter-business unit integration. The New-timers had imposed these trade-offs for the sake of integrating Stark as a whole, even though at the process levels of each business there were local business unit inefficiencies. But now, with Stark’s reversion back to a low-cost competitive strategy, the local business unit process efficiencies were an unnecessary burden.

Taking a longer term perspective, we can see that dominant actors with their intentions and power centers supported the development of systems, processes and practices that moved the organization towards their vision of enterprise integration (or disintegration as the case may be). These leaders had a significant influence on the shape of the integrated enterprise. But enterprise integration takes a long time! In Stark’s case, by the time the integration strategies were implemented, there had already been a significant turnover of the dominant actors. By the time DAREA went live, it represented and inscribed the strategic vision of those managers who were no longer present.

In the literature it is usually suggested that the linkages between strategy, strategic objectives, key performance indicators and the capability accorded by the enterprise system are crucial in the realization of benefits in enterprise systems investments (Davenport, 2000; SAP, 2001). However, as we have seen, the translation from strategy through strategic objectives to key processes to be supported by the enterprise systems is by no means straightforward. People change, the organization changes, and strategy of the firm may change as well. The benefits of investing in enterprise integration and ERP may never be realized.

## 7. Conclusion

Much of the literature on ERP implementation suggests that ERP systems should support the strategic objectives of the organization. However, identifying what those strategic objectives are (and keeping them constant) is by no means straightforward. Seen over a sufficiently long time horizon, we have seen that corporate strategy can be contested terrain.

At Stark, enterprise integration was a corporate battleground with dominant actors who had different views as to how to best integrate the organization. As the dominant actors and political agendas changed, so too did the company’s enterprise integration strategy. By the time the system went live, the company’s enterprise integration strategy had changed. The end result was a new ERP system designed to support a future corporate world and work environment that never arrived. We suggest that conflict over strategy has the potential to delay or derail ERP implementation efforts, leading to cost overruns, failures, and lack of expected benefits.

Our research suggests that long term success with enterprise systems projects should take into account inherent and expected shifts in the dominant coalitions and dominant logic of the firm. IT choices can support strategy, but they can also conflict with it, particularly as the firm’s strategy changes. A practical implication of this finding is that IS developers should explicitly consider, and attempt to manage, competing interests in enterprise integration projects. Otherwise there is a danger that the strategic orientation of just one group may end up being inscribed into the ERP system (a situation which may be very expensive and time-consuming to rectify at a later stage). We suggest that, if possible, important strategic debates among the dominant actors in a firm should be resolved (or at least a serious attempt made to do so) before embarking on an enterprise systems project. Another practical implication is that IS academics should teach IS students how to manage competing interests in large, complex projects such those involving enterprise systems.

## Acknowledgements

We acknowledge a limitation of our findings, namely, that we studied enterprise integration in just one firm. However, given the prevalence of political power plays in organizational life (Allen and Kern, 2001; see also Larsen and Myers, 1999; Markus, 1983; Myers and Young, 1997), we suggest our findings may be applicable across a wide range of organizations. We believe this article contributes to a better understanding of the relationship between strategy, politics and enterprise integration.
