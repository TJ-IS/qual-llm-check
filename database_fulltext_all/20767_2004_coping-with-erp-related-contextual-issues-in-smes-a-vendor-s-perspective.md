# Coping with ERP-related contextual issues in SMEs: a vendor’s perspective

Huigang Liang<sup>a,\*</sup>, Yajiong Xue<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Information Technology and Operations Management, Florida Atlantic University, 111 East Las Olas Blvd, Suite 535, Fort Lauderdale, FL, USA <sup>b</sup>College of Business Administration, University of Rhode Island, Kingston, RI, USA Available online 26 January 2005

## Abstract

From an ERP vendor’s perspective, this paper analyzes what can be done to address contextual issues relating to ERP implementation. The case of UFSoft, a Chinese ERP vendor, reveals three strategies that could be useful for achieving fit between ERP systems and adopting organizations. First, ERP systems need to be localized to reflect the local management features. Second, ERP systems should be customizable at a variety of levels. Finally, BPR should be carried out in an incremental manner, taking the dialectic of organizational learning into account. A significan contention is that IS researchers and practitioners should consider companies’ stages of growth as a contextual factor when conducting ERP research or implementing ERP systems. q 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: ERP; Localization; Customization; BPR; UFSoft; Organizational fit; Stage of growth

## 1. Introduction

Enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, which are commercial software packages that enable the integration of transactions-oriented data and business functions throughout an enterprise, hold the potential of greatly enhancing organizational performance and establishing competitive advantage (Davenport, 1998). However, the benefits of ERP cannot be easily acquired and inconsistent ERP outcomes have been reported (Hayes et al., 2001; Hitt et al., 2002; Hunton et al., 2002; Mandal and Gunasekaran, 2002; Palaniswamy and Frank, 2000; Vogt, 2002). Recently, interactions between ERP systems and the context in which they are used emerged as an interesting topic. For example, multi-national corporations’ ERP implementations are complicated by multi-site disparities and suggesting issues related to each site’s institutional context need to be considered (Markus et al., 2000b). Moreover, large ERP vendors such as SAP, Oracle, and PeopleSoft are expanding their market shares worldwide. They also need to resolve conflicts between their ERP systems and various local contexts.

The majority of past research on ERP has focused on critical success factors and implementation processes for ERP projects. Having assumed the legitimacy of ERP systems, these studies have taken it for granted that organizations should carry out organizational change to absorb the ‘best practices’ embedded in ERP. However, ERP may not be a universal solution. Davenport (1998) incisively asserts that companies are not likely to achieve the profits promised by ERP unless a fit is established between the companies and their ERP systems. The misalignment problems between ERP and organizations have become increasingly exposed (Hong and Kim, 2002; Sia and Soh, 2002; Soh et al., 2003), especially in the Asian context (Davison, 2002; Soh et al., 2000). Soh et al. (2000) recommend that ‘there is a need to recognize the unique Asian context when adopting an ERP system, since the embedded business models typically reflect a bias toward Western practices.’ Following Soh et al.’s thought, we contend that the generic solution proposed by ERP systems is unrealistic when it is applied in a fundamentally different context, and not only implementing companies but also ERP vendors should take responsibilities in seeking avenues to achieve the organizational fit of ERP. This paper presents our attempt to explore how a Chinese local ERP vendor has been able to achieve a certain level of success in the small and medium-sized enterprise (SME) segment of China’s ERP market. Our in-depth case analysis shows that successful ERP vendors tend to better accommodate contextual factors such as history, culture, social value, and management style in their ERP systems and implementation strategies.

This article can potentially contribute to the understanding of ERP adoption and implementation because of its novel research perspective. Although much research has been conducted from ERP adopting companies’ standpoint, little research has taken a vendor’s perspective. Our intent is to break the stereotype of taking ERP for granted and study the IT artifact (Orlikowski and Iacono, 2001). Most past researchers have presumed legitimacy and applicability of ERP and attempt to help companies better exploit the generic best practices embedded in ERP without considering if there is an ERP-organization fit. By contrast, we believe that there is no such set of generic best practices and ERP vendors should change their mindsets when deciding their system development and marketing strategies. This understanding of ERP will urge ERP vendors to rethink their products and possibly aid them to increase their market shares in culturally different markets.

## 2. Literature review

Since ERP implementation is such a complex phenomenon involving technical, social, and political issues, both its processes and outcomes are dynamic and difficult to define. It is important to examine the ERP knowledge that has been accumulated in the literature so that our research has a solid starting point. Our research intends to extract lessons learned from a successful ERP vendor, but several critical questions must be answered before this objective can be reasonably attained. For example, how to define success? How does an ERP system interact with its context? How do dialectic forces affect ERP implementations? This section summarizes pertinent past ERP studies to seek answers to these questions. Specifically, issues on ERP success and ERP-organization misfit are reviewed to shed light on the later discussion on ERP contextual issues. In addition, the Chinese ERP market is described in this section to demonstrate the potential of this market, thereby suggesting additional significance of this research.

## 2.1. ERP success

Successful implementation and use of ERP are critical to organizational performance and survival. Yet ERP success is difficult to achieve and failures of ERP projects could lead to serious damage to a company’s health, and even cause bankruptcy (Bulkeley, 1996; Davenport, 1998). Much research has been performed in an attempt to understand ERP success. The bulk of this research body consists of variance research which seeks to identify success factors and utilize these factors to explain variation in ERP implementation outcomes. While this type of research could provide insights into ERP implementation problems, its contribution is considerably limited by its narrow focus on immediate success without considering the dynamic nature of success. By contrast, ERP success is more convincingly described as a moving target (Larsen and Myers, 1999). Recognizing that ERP implementation is an ongoing process during which many conditions could change, some researchers contend that ERP outcomes, including both success and failure, are non-deterministic (Markus et al., 2000a; Robey et al., 2002). ERP success is multi-dimensional and often hard to measure. Judging whether an ERP system is a success or not depends on when it is measured (Markus et al., 2000b) and from which organizational actor’s perspective (Larsen and Myers, 1999; Wilson and Howcroft, 2002). Success at different points in time may only be loosely related to each other (Markus et al., 2000b). An early success can become a later failure and an early failure can turn to a later success. It is found that companies experienced problems at all phases of the ERP system life cycle, and many problems occurring in early phases remained unnoticed or uncorrected and manifested themselves in later phases (Markus et al., 2000b). Consequently, caution must be used when assessing ERP success, especially during the project phase, since serious problems might be concealed underneath the surface of the seeming success. Due to the dynamic nature of ERP success, it is evident that ERP success does not result from a set of success factors in place at the beginning of the ERP project. In the course of the ERP implementation process, the factors might change and leave the final outcome unpredictable or unexplainable. Therefore, in order to understand ERP success one needs to look at what happens in each phase of the ERP system life cycle (Markus et al., 2000b).

ERP success is dynamic in terms of not only time, but also the social context. By taking a social shaping perspective, Wilson and Howcroft (2002) argue that ‘the ascription of failure/success to a given technology was a social accomplishment and depended on the perspective of the subject.’ Different social groups in the organization have interpretive flexibility to perceive ERP outcomes, and organizational politics and power can influence the interpretation of ERP success. As a result, different social actors define ERP success differently. It is important for an ERP study to explicitly indicate the perspective from which ERP success is evaluated. Otherwise, the concept of ERP success becomes illusive and confusing. Our study takes a vendor’s perspective. It should be noted that the understanding gained from this study may not guarantee a successful ERP implementation, given that success is a process changing over time and a success perceived by the vendor is not necessarily considered to be a success by the implementing organization.

## 2.2. Misalignments between ERP and organizations

Misalignment, or misfit, is historically a common problem for packaged software adoption (Lucas et al., 1988). A knowledge-focused perspective (Newell et al., 2000) contends that technology suppliers commodify knowledge and present ‘packaged solutions in complex information systems (e.g. ERP) which create problems for potential users who need to unpack this knowledge and integrate it with existing organizational knowledge. Hence, knowledge transfer is essential between ERP systems and adopting organizations. Lee and Lee (2000) articulate that an ERP implementation entails both explicit and tacit knowledge transfer. The explicit knowledge refers to the standardized processes coded into an ERP system and the tacit knowledge embodies the logic that is carried by the ERP system and imposed on a company’s strategy, organization, and culture (Davenport, 1998). Clashes can happen due to the gap between the values brought in by the best practices built in the ERP system and companies’ existing values (Lee and Lee, 2000). For instance, the high failure rate of ERP implementations has been attributed to the difference in interests between organizations who desire unique business solutions and ERP vendors who supply a generic solution applicable to a broad market (Swan et al., 1999). The disparity of interest leads to the gap between the functionalities offered by the software and the functionalities desired by the user organization. From an organizational learning approach, the misalignment is also seen as between the ‘best practice’ embedded in ERP systems and organizational memories of old business processes (Stijn and Wensley, 2001). ERP success may be jeopardized due to the misalignments (Hong and Kim, 2002).

The dialectic perspective (Van de Van and Poole, 1995) allows IS researchers to conceptualize the underlying sources that give rise to misalignments between ERP and organizations. The dialectic motor of organizational change highlights colliding events, forces, or contradictory values that compete with each other, for domination or control. The opposing forces during the course of IT implementations largely stem from the dualistic nature of technology (Orlikowski, 1992). An IT embeds developer values on the one hand, and the use of IT affects human action on the other hand. Given that IT-based innovation is heavily dependent on the context of application, the notion that it can be a universally applicable best-practice solution is criticized as being flawed (Newell et al., 2000; Swan et al., 1999). Instead, the adoption of ERP can be viewed as a dynamic process of interaction, in which the implementation of ERP shapes the firm’s utilization of ERP and vice versa.

Employing the dialectic perspective facilitates the explanation of inconsistent findings in the ERP literature (Robey et al., 2002). Taking this perspective, Soh et al. (2003) find that misalignments that emerge in ERP implementation can be traced to a few fundamental incompatibilities between the embedded structures of ERP and the implementing organization, and provide suggestions on how to remove the misalignment by using customization, adoption, or workaround resolutions. Consistent with Newell et al. (2000) and Robey et al. (2002) view ERP implementation as a dialectic of learning where the most fundamental dialectic occurs between the old knowledge embedded in business processes and practices associated with legacy systems, and the new business processes and practices that ERP is designed to support. The dialectic perspective helps to identify and address configuration knowledge barriers and assimilation knowledge barriers to implementing ERP systems (Robey et al., 2002).

Aside from the dialectic perspective, ERP-organization misalignments can be explained from a cultural perspective. Culture is conceptualized as shared symbols, norms, and values in a social collectivity such as a country (Walsham, 2002). Given that cultural differences significantly influence IS usage (Lachman et al., 1994; Leidner et al., 1999), misalignments between adopting organizations and ERP systems can be a vital issue for ERP implementations in international contexts in which various cultures coexist. Software designers’ values vary across cultures (Kumar and Bjorn-Anderson, 1990) and their values will be built into the software they designed explicitly or implicitly. As most ERP systems are designed in Europe and the US, these systems reflect European or US industry practices. For example, Carmel (1997) notes that ‘the American culture of packaged software is an intersection of characteristics unique to the (national) American culture and of the subculture of computer programmers and software professionals who share values, interests, and life-styles relating to computers and software (p. 135).’ Employing structuration theory, Walsham (2002) illustrates that conflict, which refers to the actual struggle between actors and groups, may occur in cross-cultural software use if cultural differences affect actors negatively and they are able to act. Thus, when the Western ERP packages are implemented in other countries where different cultures exist, it is likely that the ERP’s underlying culture and business logic conflict with the local culture and business logic, causing misfits which negatively affect the ERP implementation outcomes (Davison, 2002; Soh et al., 2000).

## 2.3. China’s ERP market

China’s ERP market, which started in the late 1980s, has been booming in recent years as more managers started realizing the importance of IT. According to the China Center for Information Industry Development (CCID), a well-known Chinese marketing research firm, the Chinese ERP market has grown from approximately USD \$70 million in 2000 to USD \$106 million in 2001 (CCID, 2002). From 2002 to 2005, the Chinese ERP market is expected to have an annual growth rate of 25% (AsiaPort, 2002). Obviously, the Chinese ERP market as a whole is very attractive for the major ERP vendors. However, since the wave of adoption by Chinese firms, especially SMEs, is in its early stages, little is known about the processes and factors that affect ERP implementation outcomes. Major ERP vendors such as SAP and Oracle came across tremendous difficulties when trailblazing this market. According to a recent ERP market report, China’s ERP market keeps growing and 79% of the market share belongs to Chinese local vendors (SinaCast, 2004). Indeed, several ERP failure stories involving foreign ERP systems have been widely publicized in the Chinese business world and an important reason is that some foreign ERP systems failed to provide what Chinese companies need because of cultural and environmental obstacles (Liang et al., 2004).

SMEs constitute 98% of China’s total industrial sector, and the development of their technological infrastructure is considered to be of high priority to the nation’s economy (World IT Report, 2003). One emerging trend in China’s ERP market is that almost all of the major ERP vendors have taken actions that strategically target the SME segment (Leung, 2003). Hence, research on understanding ERP implementations in Chinese SMEs is likely to have considerable practical significance.

## 3. Methodology

This study employed a case research method following guidelines and suggestions provided by prior researchers (Benbasat et al., 1987; Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 2003). The purpose of this study is to explore how vendor activities can improve ERP implementation success in the context of China’s ERP market. Since ERP implementation is ‘a contemporary phenomenon in its real-life context’ and the boundaries between ERP implementation and its real-life context are not clearly evident due to their interactions, the use of a case research method is justified (Yin, 2003).

Given the importance of China’s ERP market, our research focuses on an ERP vendor who is operating in China. A single case approach was adopted. As Yin (2003, p. 41) stresses, one rationale for using a single case approach is the importance of the case. Our selection of UFSoft, a Chinese domestic ERP vendor, fits this rationale because UFSoft has the largest ERP market share in China. According to CCID’s China IT market report, UFSoft is the leader in China’s ERP market with a 21.6% market share, while SAP takes the second place with a 13.5% market share (CCID, 2003). UFSoft’s uniqueness and the fact, to the authors’ knowledge, that no previous research has studied Chinese ERP vendors make this single case approach suitable.

The authors initiated contact with UFSoft in early 2002 and started collecting data relating to the company since that time. In March 2003, one of the authors went to Beijing to conduct field studies at UFSoft for a week. Face-to-face interviews were made with key respondents including a vice president, a chief training officer, a product manager and a marketing manager of ERP-U8<sup>1</sup> division, and a research fellow at UFSoft. The respondents were chosen because of their knowledge and expertise in the technical properties and market positions of the ERP product. Specifically, the vice president oversees the ERP design and production; the chief training officer supervises the training activities which enable UFSoft employees to effectively participate in ERP implementation processes; the product manager possesses the knowledge of specific ERP-U8 characteristics; the marketing manger has accurate market information of the ERP product; and the research fellow is in charge of all internal research activities and able to provide views from both the technical side and the organizational side. Therefore, we believe that a rather complete picture of ERP-U8 can be drawn by interviewing these respondents.

The face-to-face interviews with some respondents were conducted for more than one round. In addition to the face-to-face interviews, phone interviews were made with five UFSoft’s customer companies to get information about their ERP experiences. An interview could be as short as 30 min or as long as four hours. In order to enhance the reliability of the study, a case study protocol was used which contained interview outlines, procedures, and general rules. The face-to-face interviews were tape-recorded and notes were taken for the phone interviews. The recordings and notes were translated by the authors from Chinese to English. When the two authors’ interpretations for parts of the recordings were in dispute, the pertinent respondents were contacted by email or by phone for clarification. Utilizing the data triangulation tactic (Yin, 2003), additional information was collected from UFSoft’s on-site product demonstration, annual reports, ERP implementation cases, company memos, company news, and Web documents. A case database was created to track the interview transcripts, field notes, and other documentations.

The two authors separately examined the case for evidence relevant to UFSoft’s efforts which contribute to success of its ERP implementations. The evidence was summarized and categorized. The two authors compared their analyses and resolved discrepancies through debate and referencing back to the case database.

## 4. The case study

The UFSoft Co., Ltd, established in 1988, is a Chinese software company dedicated to the development, marketing and service providing of enterprise software products.<sup>2</sup> UFSoft specialized in financial software at its inception stage and soon became the leader in China’s financial software market. In 1999 it developed into the largest independent software supplier in China. UFSoft’s software has been diffused in a variety of industries such as manufacturing, retailing, service, finance, and government. UFSoft currently has more than 300,000 customer organizations all over China (including financial software customers and ERP customers). According to CCID, UFSoft led local Chinese ERP vendors in terms of ERP market share (21.6%) in 2003. UFSoft has three major products: financial software, ERP-U8, and ERP-NC. ERP-U8 is designed for Chinese small and medium enterprises (SMEs) and ERP-NC is a high-end package aiming at large enterprises in seven industries: bank, finance, steel, telecommunications, energy, giant chain stores, and insurance. UFSoft has been mainly focusing on SME customers in the past and just started developing the high-end market recently. Its success is mostly embodied in ERP-U8. Therefore, ERP-U8 was chosen as the focus of this case study.

## 4.1. Localized software

The motto of UFSoft is ‘to be customers’ true friend,’ indicating that UFSoft seeks to satisfy customers’ real business needs. The motto embodies a norm of UFSoft, which posits that in order to survive and thrive, a firm has to help customers solve real world problems. The norm started to form in the early days of UFSoft when Wan Wenjing, the president of UFSoft, who used to work for the Ministry of Finance, designed software packages to facilitate organizational financial management by taking advantage of his knowledge of China’s accounting processes, standards, and national financial regulations and policies. This norm has helped UFSoft develop highly localized financial software and become the financial software market leader in China.

UFSoft’s successful experience in financial software market transfers to its ERP production. In order to become ‘customers’ true friend,’ the design of UFSoft’s ERP-U8 is localized by taking into account Chinese companies’ unique characteristics. Learning from their experience in working with more than 300,000 customers, the development personnel at UFSoft are able to identify the commonality of Chinese companies’ business processes and idiosyncrasy of a specific industry’s or company’s business processes, and utilize appropriate design features to address various business concerns. The research fellow at UFSoft noted:

A major reason UFSoft is successful is that we have a localized strategy. Our customers know that we understand their problems and our ERP systems can help them improve their management levels. They believe that Chinese software packages are the best to solve Chinese companies’ business problems.

An example of localized software feature is ERP-U8’s UFO reporting module which is compliant with the data interface standard required by the Ministry of Finance. The UFO module also allows users to develop a number of user-defined reporting formats. According to the product manager at ERP-U8 division, this localized design is a response to the reality that the customer company may be requested to submit reports to different governmental agencies which require various ‘personalized’ reporting formats. Another example is the good supply practice (GSP) quality management module, which is specifically designed for Chinese pharmaceutical distributors and retailers. The GSP is a regulation enacted by the State Food and Drug Administration (SFDA) to ensure best practices in drug distribution companies. It is highly different from the GSP promulgated in Western countries.

The localized design of UFSoft’s ERP packages has a symbolic effect on Chinese SMEs who tend to perceive domestic ERP products are more suitable for Chinese companies than foreign ERP products. The president of Dongyue Auto, a car manufacturer in Shandong province which has implemented ERP-U8, said:

We concentrated on local vendors when we were looking for ERP systems, because local vendors are familiar with Chinese companies’ management philosophy and management model and their products are more suitable for us.

It is clear that ERP-U8’s success depends on UFSoft’s in-depth understanding of Chinese SMEs. UFSoft had numerous opportunities to work with SMEs when selling and installing financial software and gained valuable knowledge of Chinese SMEs’ management status and business needs. This knowledge-enabled UFSoft to come up with a localized ERP design that literally fits Chinese SMEs’ organizational environment. The ERP literature shows that the fit between ERP and organizations influences not only adoption decisions (Everdingen et al., 2000) but also later implementation outcomes (Hong and Kim, 2002). Therefore, we affirm that the localized design is a key reason to UFSoft’s success.

## 4.2. Customization

UFSoft sells ERP-U8 and services in a bundle, that is, it provides both ERP packages and implementation consulting services, so that customers can get a ‘one stop shopping’ solution. Before helping a company implement ERR-U8, it performs a thorough analysis on the company’s industry type, operation model, and development stage. ERP will be customized according to special requirements relative to these attributes.

ERP-U8 can be customized to fit the business needs of a variety of industries. Nevertheless, an industry-specific ERP can be further customized. For example, UFSoft labels a manufacturer as a ‘dumbbell’ type or an ‘olive’ type according to where the company’s competency locates in its operation. Companies of ‘dumbbell’ type compete on RandD and marketing, whereas companies of ‘olive’ type compete on manufacturing. Pharmaceutical and high-tech companies are examples of ‘dumbbell’ type and textile and furniture companies are examples of ‘olive’ type. UFSoft can customize ERP-U8 to make it appropriate for either operational model.

Based on its understanding of Chinese companies’ development lifecycle, UFSoft categorizes Chinese SMEs into three stages: start-up, development, and expansion. UFSoft observes that the majority of Chinese SMEs are not mature and that companies in different stages have different business imperatives. UFSoft thinks that it is ill-advised to implement an entire set of ERP modules for any company regardless of its growth stage. UFSoft’s philosophy is to provide the core ERP modules that meet customers’ current management needs. As the research fellow at UFSoft explained:

SMEs at the start-up stage are struggling for market entry. Data accuracy and data collection are key management problems for them. For these companies we will concentrate on implementing U8’s data management capability. SMEs at the development stage seek to increase their market shares. Most of these companies’ problems are related to data integration, functional integration, and coordination. For these companies we will focus on implementing U8’s precision management, process standardization, and strategic decision support capabilities. SMEs at the expansion stage are more interested in retaining their competitive advantages through strategic decisionmaking. Accordingly, we will put more effort in implementing U8’s decision support capabilities for these companies.

ERP-U8’s customization on the basis of companies’ industry types, operation models, and development stages is realized by a variety of combinations of different modules. This customization is at a relatively high level, neglecting specific companies’ idiosyncrasies.

If a company has a special process, which cannot be modified to fit any standard U8 module, a low-level customization will be conducted. UFSoft mainly develops add-on modules to meet a company’s special needs and intends to keep source code modification to minimum. For example, a cost control module was customized for Longdan Dairy Technology Co. to accomplish its complicated three-level cost control, and a requirement planning dynamic management module was developed for Dongfeng Yijin Co. to deal with differences between auto parts requirement predictions and orders.

UFSoft makes ERP-U8 relatively flexible to meet various types of SMEs’ business needs. The flexibility has been manifested in ERP-U8’s customizations in three dimensions: industry customization, growth stage customization, and individual customization. The individual customization, which refers to modifications on ERP for the purpose of meeting essential business needs for a particular company, has been noticed in the prior ERP research (Brehm et al., 2001). The industry customization and growth stage customization, especially the latter, seem to draw little attention in the ERP literature. However, they might play critical roles in China’s ERP market. In our case, the industry customization means that relevant modules are put together in response to an industry’s business needs. Companies in different industries might have different operational procedures or customs for the same type of business processes. UFSoft has proposed a list of business solutions for customers’ convenience. Since UFSoft provides both ERP and implementation services, its implementation consulting teams have a good understanding of the software packages and its industry customization is likely to reflect the most optimum business solutions using ERP-U8. The stage of growth customization means that SMEs in different stages of growth have differing business needs and the most salient needs should be highlighted in the ERP system for a specific SME. Therefore, an implementation plan should focus on several key modules rather than an entire set of modules. The marketing manager at UFSoft noticed that some Chinese companies implemented well-known foreign vendors’ ERP systems, but only a few modules were really put into use. As a result, these companies got little back from huge investments. By contrast, UFSoft analyzes a customer company’s stage of growth and implement the most needed modules for the company. Consequently, the company spends less money and is able to take advantage of every module implemented. In addition, since fewer modules are implemented, the project complexity is reduced and the implementation time can be greatly shortened.

## 4.3. Business process improvement, optimization, and reengineering (BPIOR)

According to respondents at UFSoft, U8 is a simplified version of ERP. It can be quickly implemented and customer companies can use it instantly after implementation. At Xi’an Auto Co., the ERP-U8 implementation which constituted deploying more than 10 modules in 27 workshops and 87 inventories for 300 users took only 3 months. A conspicuous voice that UFSoft intends to broadcast is that companies implementing ERP-U8 do not need to carry out business process reengineering (BPR) to fit the ERP package. The vice president of UFSoft said:

Foreign ERP vendors assume business process reengineering to be undertaken with an ERP implementation project. By contrast, our customers do not need to change the way they are doing businesses currently. Our product will adapt to their business processes.

Radical changes associated with BPR are perceived differently in Chinese and Western cultures. In contrast to the Western culture, the Chinese culture is more past-oriented, reactive, and reluctant to conduct organizational transformations (Martinsons and Hempel, 2001). UFSoft informs the market that no BPR is needed if ERP-U8 is implemented. Indeed, the customer companies have to carry out some process changes, more or less, to fit the ERP systems. However, the changes are piecemeal and more acceptable to Chinese companies.

UFSoft’s strategy is to avoid dramatic organizational transformation. Instead, it contends that organizational change should be achieved gradually by utilizing a few milder transforming strategies such as business process improvement, optimization, and reengineering (BPIOR). UFSoft conceives that BPIOR is more appropriate for Chinese SMEs. The research fellow at UFSoft said:

BPR is like an earthquake for Chinese companies. The managers and employees may not be ready for such a massive change. Although the processes work well in Western countries, they can cause damages to Chinese companies rather than raising these companies’ management level.

UFSoft’s BPIOR strategy is consistent with the Chinese culture. The growth stage customization provides support for the BPIOR strategy. Companies in different stages only focus on the key business functions that need to be improved so that they can grow and postpone the changes in less critical business functions. The result of the customization is naturally an incremental approach to reengineering business processes. In addition, the BPIOR strategy is aligned with the characteristics of Chinese SMEs, which typically do not have many resources allocated for IT applications. These companies usually want to take a trial first, see the benefits, and then invest more resources. Persuading them to discard the processes they established and got used to for a long time and adopt a set of foreign, unknown processes would be a huge challenge.

The BPIOR strategy has increased UFSoft’s attractiveness in Chinese companies. A pharmaceutical company, PharmaCo, chose UFSoft as its software provider over a foreign vendor because the foreign vendor required BPR before the software can be deployed, while UFSoft was able to customize its software according to PharmCo’s existing business processes. The general manager of the pharmaceutical company said:

We cannot afford such a BPR. The current business processes are based on many people’s precious experiences. Many processes and rules are designed for specific problems we came across before. I guess it is hard for foreign software companies to understand these solutions. But if these processes and rules are removed by BPR, I am afraid our company will go bankrupt.

UFSoft believes that a company’s business processes should be improved gradually, one functional area at a time. ERP-U8’s localized design reflects the best practices in Chinese industries. UFSoft suggests that a Chinese SME only need to improve its current process to fit the Chinese best practices and no dramatic reengineering is required. When processes in all functional areas are improved and optimized, the entire company’s business processes are reengineered automatically. By fully considering the application context in which ERP systems will be implemented, UFSoft successfully decreased potential user resistance to the IT innovation it tries to sell. While this strategy helped UFSoft quickly increase its market share, it also entailed a software upgrading problem which will be discussed in Section 5.

## 5. Discussion

As mentioned earlier, most ERP researchers have assumed that ERP packages reflect best practices and these should be taken for granted. Consequently, many studies are devoted to investigating ERP adoption and implementation issues from a customer organization’s perspective and few studies have pondered the legitimacy of ERP packages. In agreement with Newell et al. (2000) and Swan et al. (1999), we argue that generic best practices would be rarely the case and the context in which ERP systems are implemented should be considered. Taking a vendor’s perspective, we attempt to examine strategies of a successful local Chinese ERP vendor. We are interested in observing how the broader contextual issues can influence an ERP vendor in shaping products and serving customers. Specifically, we studied ERP-U8, the most popular ERP system in the SME segment of China’s ERP market. The case research findings suggest that attention should be paid to such issues as integrating culture into localized software design, customizing software based on the stage of growth of the customer firm, and using BPIOR to reduce resistance.

The UFSoft case shows that system localization contributes to UFSoft’s leading market position in the SME segment, suggesting that the Chinese context does have a significant impact on ERP adoption and implementations. Although there are many contextual factors in China’s ERP market, we think culture is the most intriguing and an understanding of culture will help explain ERP problems. The discrepancy between the Western and Chinese cultures has been recognized to have profound influences on IS implementations (Martinsons and Westwood, 1997; Ping and Grimshaw, 1992). In general, Chinese societies are based on networks of relationships, and Chinese people are more likely to adapt to the environment rather than to seek a scientific solution. The Chinese business culture is shaped by paternalism, personalism, and high context communications, while the Western culture is centered with individualism, impersonalism, and formal communications (Martinsons and Westwood, 1997). The fundamental differences between the Chinese and Western cultures inevitably influence the use of information technologies developed by Western developers in Chinese organizations. Accordingly, the Chinese culture must be taken into account when investigating ERP implementation issues in China. The implication for international ERP vendors in China’s market is that they should integrate the Chinese culture into their ERP systems. The business models underlying foreign ERP packages reflect European or US industry practices, while procedures in Chinese organizations are likely to be different, having evolved in a different cultural, economic, and regulatory context. In order to resolve the misfits, we recommend Western vendors establishing research centers in different regions of China and hiring Chinese business analysts and software engineers to improve their products.

UFSoft’s stage of growth customization strategy is in accordance with Greiner’s (1998) organizational development model which posits that organizations progress through five phases of smooth evolution and turbulent revolution. In an organization’s life, each evolutionary phase is characterized by the dominant management style used to achieve growth and each revolutionary phase is characterized by the dominant management problem that must be solved before growth can continue (Greiner, 1998). Greiner argues that managerial problems and practices are rooted in time and do not last throughout the life of an organization. Taking the dynamic view of organizational development into consideration, we find that UFSoft’s stage of growth customization is rational. UFSoft proposes ERP implementation plans according to the customer companies’ stages of growth. For a company which is at the start-up stage, for instance, it is unnecessary to implement a sophisticated ERP package since a simplified package is likely to be enough to accommodate the company’s managerial problem at that stage. This strategy is mutually beneficial: the customer company will have a less expensive, quickly implemented ERP system, and UFSoft will have a good chance of winning the loyalty of customers by offering an attractive price and the possibility of upgrading the system as the company grows. This strategy can be viewed as customization at the module level. The vendor flexibly provides a variety of combinations of standard ERP modules to fit the customer’s stage of growth. Given Chinese SMEs’ IT budget and personnel constraints, this strategy is likely to become inevitable for vendors who are interested in the SME segment of China’s ERP market.

With the growth stage customization strategy in mind, a pragmatic question is: how do you determine a company’s stage of growth? Obviously, UFSoft’s growth stage model, which stems from practical experiences, appears primitive. Research is needed to develop a theoretically grounded model of organizational growth which can be used to explain and predict organizational growth in context. Greiner’s model is a good candidate to test in the context of Chinese SMEs. When a company’s stage of growth is determined, a follow-up question is: what ERP modules are adequate to solve the managerial crises impeding the company’s growth? Future research is also called for to answer this question. For both academics and practitioners, the attention to stages of organizational growth has great implications. We contend that research in this direction can offer great insight into the relationship between ERP and organizations. Few researchers have noticed the importance of differentiating organizations with respect to their stages of growth when conducting ERP studies. All organizations have been treated equally as if their stages of growth have no impact on their ERP experiences. One plausible reason is that in the past most ERP adopters are large firms, which tend to be invariably in the mature stage. As to SMEs, which could be in a variety of stages of growth, overlooking organizational growth may seriously constrain our potential to know more about how to implement ERP. Research looking into the relationship between stages of growth and ERP modules selection can offer a richer understanding of ERP implementations.

UFSoft’s BPIOR strategy draws our attention to the debate on approaches to implementing BPR. The clean-slate approach (Hammer and Champy, 1993) entails radical redesign of business processes to achieve dramatic performance improvement and disregards existing business processes. Noticing that constraints imposed by existing business processes have to be considered during implementation of BPR plans, a moderate approach has been advised to plan for radical change, but to implement gradually (Davenport and Stoddard, 1994). From an organizational learning perspective, which posits that organizational memory mediates organizational change, Boudreau and Robey (1996) argue that a dialectic view needs to be taken to study BPR, suggesting that organizational outcomes can be explained as the result of counteracting forces, those promoting the organizational change and those opposing it. Robey et al. (2002) contend that implementing ERP systems can be understood as a dialectic of learning which involves removing organizational memory of existing business processes and legacy systems and integrating new business processes supported by ERP. As Newell et al. (2000) noted, implementing complex information systems is actually unpacking knowledge ‘packaged’ within the systems and it is very difficult to do. Notwithstanding UFSoft’s localized ERP design, some organizational change, more or less, needs to be carried out. UFSoft denies that its ERP should introduce radical change to customers and believes that organizational change should be accomplished gradually by means of ‘improvement’, ‘optimization’, and finally ‘reengineering’. This can be reasonably explained by using the organizational learning theory. According to the theory, organizations cannot unlearn what they already know; therefore, new knowledge is always interpreted or understood in the context of existing knowledge and can only be incorporated as sensible modifications to existing practices (Boudreau and Robey, 1996). The nature of organizational learning determines that organizational change has to be carried out in an incremental manner. As we mentioned earlier, the Chinese culture is more past-oriented, reactive, and reluctant to conduct organizational transformations (Martinsons and Hempel, 2001). In this sense, the understanding of dialectic forces in implementing ERP-enabled organizational change in China becomes more critical. For international ERP vendors attempting to enter the SME segment of China’s ERP market, we suggest a phased implementation approach be employed to reduce the opposing forces in national and organizational dialectic.

Due to the complex interaction between ERP and context, caution ought to be taken when interpreting and applying the findings of this case research. Three cautionary comments are provided here. First, having a localized ERP system, appropriate customization, and a BPIOR strategy may increase the likelihood of achieving ERP success, but it never guarantees ERP success. As discussed earlier, ERP success is dynamic and non-deterministic, depending on when and from whose perspective it is measured (Markus et al., 2000b). The contextual factors we found reflect the starting state of ERP projects. As the ERP implementation progresses, factors keep changing as a result of the interaction between ERP and the context, that is, the context may shape ERP and ERP may redefine the context. Hence, it is hard to determine the relationships between ERP outcomes and specific factors. Highlighting this dynamic nature of ERP success helps to develop a reasonable understanding of ERP implementations.

Second, it should be noted that, from a vendor’s perspective, the implementation does not represent closure of an ERP project. Vendors should consider establishing a longterm relationship with client organizations so that more revenue can be generated from ‘follow on’ work such as system reconfiguration and system upgrading. IT consultants try to build strong ties with clients by seeking to become obligatory passage points in relation to the problems of client organizations (Bloomfield and Danieli, 1995). Thus if clients want to obtain IT-related advices or to implement new systems, it must be seen that the routes to such objectives require the services of consultants. Similarly, ERP vendors should seek to become obligatory passage points with respect to ERP adoption, implementation, and migration. For example, UFSoft symbolizes its ERP packages as being localized for Chinese companies. Many of UFSoft’s customers used its financial software and when they considered installing ERP systems, they regarded UFSoft to be the ‘obligatory passage point.’ It seems that UFSoft managed to establish its identity in the social context of China’s ERP market. It is suggested that ERP vendors need both technical skills and socio-political skills. To have only technical skills is not enough to achieve success.

Finally, we need to point out that UFSoft, like any other profitable company, intends to sell products and strives for commercial success. Its nature determines that it behaves to serve its own self-interest. Therefore, its strategies to cope with contextual issues may increase its market share, yet the ERP system that emerges as a result of these strategies might not be the best option for the user companies. For example, the highly customized ERP modules are able to fit the status quo of the user company, but could cause upgrading problems later. Thus, we need to look at both sides of a coin to develop a balanced understanding.

## 6. Conclusion

From an ERP vendor’s perspective, this paper analyzes what can be done to address issues of the context in which ERP systems are implemented. The case of UFSoft reveals three strategies that could be useful for international ERP vendors intending to enter a market with a different social, economic, and regulatory context. First, ERP systems need to be localized to reflect the local management features. Second, ERP systems should be customizable at different levels. In this regard, we argue that organizational growth should be taken into account so that ERP customization can better serve customers’ managerial imperatives. Finally, it is recommended to carry out BPR in an incremental manner by taking the dialectic of organizational learning into account, especially in a culturally change-resistant context such as China.

We would argue that our study contributes to the IS literature in three ways. First, our research concerned ERP, studies of which are valued in the mainstream IS literature and implementations of which have encountered numerous problems in different contexts. Second, our study dealt with ERP in SMEs in developing countries, which is a much neglected topic in the mainstream IS literature. SMEs differ from large enterprises in terms of their stages of growth. The recognition of this factor would enrich our understanding of ERP implementation issues. Finally, and perhaps of most significance, we feel that our study went beyond traditional concerns of ERP implementations by taking a vendor’s perspective, thereby allowing us to examine how to cope with contextual issues in the design and implementation of ERP systems. We have shown, in our case, the strategies UFSoft employed to achieve success in China’s ERP market. Although such results were derived from our work in China, we believe they have relevance in many other ERP and IS implementation contexts. For international ERP vendors intending to enter foreign markets, our study will be of value in helping the formulation of their strategies.
