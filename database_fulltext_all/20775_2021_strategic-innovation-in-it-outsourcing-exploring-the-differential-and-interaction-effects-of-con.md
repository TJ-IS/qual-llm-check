# Strategic innovation in IT outsourcing: Exploring the differential and interaction effects of contractual and relational governance mechanisms

![](/api/attachments/PGWER4AU/fulltext/images/3a7a076429204520ad516d95ad382a6e1975886b0547c734d0b6537490f64418.jpg)

Johann Kranz

Ludwig-Maximilians-Universitat ¨ Munich, Ludwigstr. 28, 80539 Munich, Germany

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Outsourcing Strategic innovation Governance Contract design Relational exchange theory

## A B S T R A C T

Innovation in IT outsourcing, in which client and vendor aim at co-creating strategic innovation, is becoming a key ingredient of contemporary sourcing arrangements. However, innovation in ITO is regarded as being a paradox as innovation’s dynamic nature conflicts with established guidelines for successful ITO governance. To date, knowledge about how to accommodate innovation in ITO governance is embryonic. Particularly unclear are the differential and inter action effects of contractual and relational governance mechanisms on relationship learning and joint innovation. To investigate these effects, we propose and validate a research model through survey data from 344 senior-level IT employees of firms that collaborate with a vendor on stra tegic innovation. Results indicate that while contractual mechanisms primarily determine joint innovation performance, relational mechanisms are central to relationship learning. Results show an inverted U-shaped relationship between governance mechanisms and joint innovation and an additive complementary relationship between governance mechanisms and relationship learning.

## Introduction

Information technology’s (IT) increasing importance for innovation and many firms’ inadequate in-house IT resources have caused the focus of IT outsourcing (ITO) to shift from the operational level to the strategic level and from saving on costs to co-creating innovation (Aubert et al 2015: Kotlarsky et al 2015: Lacity and Willcocks, 2013: Oshri et al 2018: Oshri et al 2015) Accord: ingly, innovation has become a regular stipulation in clients’ request for proposals as client firms increasingly seek access to vendors innovation capabilities through outsourcing (Lacity and Willcocks, 2017). Thus, most client firms now regard a vendor’s innova tiveness as a main selection criterion (Cullen et al., 2014). and innovation is becoming a key ingredient of ITO arrangements (Oshri et al., 2018; Willcocks and Whitley, 2018, see Appendix A for an example innovation clause).

For instance, Volkswagen recently announced a global, multiyear partnership with Amazon Web Services (AWS) to co-develop a novel cloud-based digital production platform to streamline all of its 122 global manufacturing plants and logistics processes. Core building blocks of this “Industrial Cloud” will be AWS’s storage service S3 and the machine-learning platform SageMaker (Dobush, 2019). Similarly, Toyota Material Handling is developing strategic innovation in a partnership with Infosys; the Indian provider of IT outsourcing services is supporting Toyota by providing customers with a cloud-based Internet of Things service that enables remote monitoring and diagnostic capabilities (Bloomberg, 2019). Other examples of joint innovation in ITO include a data-driven development platform co-developed by BMW and Tencent for providing cars with the data analytics capabilities needed for auton omous driving, an open-source insurance platform and app marketplace jointly implemented by Allianz and Microsoft based on Microsoft’s Azure cloud platform, Infosys’s support of Diageo to establish a global social media marketing platform that streamlines Diageo’s marketing and brand management processes and provides data analytics capabilities, and the implementation of a supply chain platform by IBM-Vodafone that automates the delivery of anti-malaria medication in Africa for Novartis using mobile and internet technology (Kotlarsky et al., 2015; Shirouzu, 2019). These examples illustrate clients’ increased demand for joint innovation in ITO and vendors’ increased ability to support clients’ engagement in new forms of IT-enabled value creation.

Recent literature has recognized that ITO “is increasingly leveraged as a strategic tool for acquiring cutting-edge innovation” (Su et al., 2016, p. 81). However, most established guidelines for successful ITO governance conflict with the uncertain and creative process of innovation co-creation, as traditional contracts limit the “required margin for the supplier to take different paths in order to innovate” (Aubert et al., 2015, p. 2580). Thus, seeking innovation through ITO is described as a paradox, and governance of the client–vendor relationship is regarded as a key issue of innovation through ITO (Aubert et al., 2015; Whitley and Willcocks, 2011; Willcocks and Whitley, 2018).

Because most ITO literature focuses on contracts’ safeguarding function in the tradition of transaction cost economics (Lacity et al., 2016), knowledge is presently embryonic as to how contracts can “specify how risks and rewards will be shared in ways that provide incentives for innovation, collaboration, and high performance to achieve common goals" (Whitley and Willcocks. 2011, p. 98). Prior research has focused rather generally on governance mechanisms (e.g., Oshri et al., 2015) or explicitly on relational mechanisms (Oshri et al., 2018). or has been limited by the use of case-based evidence (e.g., Whitley and Willcocks. 2011) or secondary data (Yan et al., 2016). Thus, present understanding is limited to knowledge that a high-quality client-vendor relationship and certain com mercial forms such as a joint-venture contract are likely to result in improved innovation outcomes (Oshri et al., 2018; Oshri et al., 2015). This emphasis not only leaves unstudied a wide array of governance mechanisms tailored to the specific context of innovation through ITO, but also results in a gap regarding an integrated and systematic investigation of the differential effects of contractual and relational governance mechanisms on two key outcomes of such ITO arrangements: relationship learning and joint innovation.

Recent findings suggest more complex and dynamic interrelationships between relational and contractual governance than the previously assumed dichotomy of complementarity and substitution (Lacity et al., 2017). These studies emphasize the role of relationspecific boundary conditions and find that relational and contractual governance mechanisms can act as substitutes (Rai et al., 2012), simultaneously work as substitutes and complements (Lioliou et al., 2014; Tiwana, 2010), or have an impact that oscillates over time (Huber et al., 2013). We extend this stream of the literature by investigating how alignment or congruence between the two gover nance mechanisms (i.e., both mechanisms are used equally) and misalignment (i.e., incongruence or imbalance in the usage of both mechanisms) influence relationship outcomes.

The effect of alignment between governance modes has received little attention; prior research has mainly addressed how and why client and vendor shift the alignment between governance modes to overcome the weaknesses or use the strengths of a particular mode in response to contextual triggers (Huber et al., 2013; Lioliou et al., 2014). Thus, our study was guided by two main research questions:

(1) What is the differential impact of relational and contractual governance mechanisms on relationship learning and joint inno vation in ITO?

(2) How does the (mis-)alignment of relational and contractual governance mechanisms influence relationship learning and joint innovation in ITO?

To analyze how governance mechanisms can accommodate paradoxical tensions of innovation in ITO, our research model draws upon relational exchange theory (Granovetter, 1985; Macneil, 1980; Williamson, 1996). Our model is validated by a large-scale field survey that comprises 344 senior-level IT employees. Results reveal significant differences in the effect of governance modes on the two outcome variables. While relational governance is crucial for relationship learning, the effect on joint innovation is less pronounced and, at excessive levels, is even harmful. In contrast, contractual governance mechanisms, which limit ex post opportunism and create incentives for vendors' cooperative behavior, are key drivers of joint innovation Further, we reveal that in the case of misalignment joint innovation is greater when misalignment places contractual governance above relational governance and vice versa for relationship learning. Alignment between the two generates an additive complementary relationship for relationship learning and an inverted U-shaped relationship for joint innovation.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. First, we review the literature on ITO governance and innovation. We then develop our hypotheses about the differential and interaction effects of governance mechanisms on relationship learning and joint innovation. After presenting the research methodology and the results, we discuss our findings and acknowledge the study’s impli cations and limitations.

## Conceptual background

## Governing ITO relationships aimed at joint innovation

Innovation through ITO has gained increasing attention in the IS outsourcing literature (Lacity et al., 2016). Our review reveals a shift in the innovation through ITO literature from operational to more strategic levels (see Appendix B). Correspondingly, our study focuses on strategic innovation, defined as an IS innovation that is strategic to a firm's business and is integrated “with core business technology, and typically impacts upon general business administration as well” (Swanson, 1994, p. 1077). Thus, strategic innovation includes process, product, service, and business model innovations that are fundamentally new to client firms or their customers and create additional business value (Weeks and Feeny, 2008). For this reason, we do not consider operational innovations that only concern isolated changes in IT with no impact on business processes and outcomes (Weeks and Feeny, 2008). We refer to strategic innovation that is co-developed by ITO clients and vendors using both parties’ complementary resources and capabilities as joint innovation (Grover and Kohli, 2012; Whitley and Willcocks, 2011).

Research has shown that information and knowledge exchange between clients and vendors is crucial for joint innovation in ITO to occur (Oshri et al., 2018; Su et al., 2016). However, the joint role of relational and contractual governance on relationship learning and joint innovation has gone unexamined. Research on strategic alliances suggests important differences in the superiority of governance modes regarding different outcome dimensions and indicates that a mismatch between governance mechanisms and the assets (both property- and knowledge-based) involved in a relationship has detrimental performance effects (Hoetker and Mellewigt, 2009).

The literature widely agrees that innovation in ITO requires that client and vendor firms switch from a traditional arm's-length relationship to more embedded forms of relationship governance. Further, joint innovation is unlikely to emerge with conventiona contracts that aim at preventing opportunistic and disproportionate gains in the tradition of transaction cost economics (Lacity and Willcocks, 2014b). Innovation through ITO is thus described as being a paradox, since traditional contracts are viewed as too rigid and thwart innovative behavior (Aubert et al., 2015). Although contracting is considered a key issue, our review indicates that most studies have emphasized the role of relational governance to overcome opportunism and agency problems. These studies indicate that informal, employee-based social mechanisms can create strong and intimate client–supplier relationships that benefit joint innovation (Oshri et al., 2018: Oshri et al., 2015). Less is known, however, about how contracts need to be designed to encourage joint innovation but constrain the substantial exchange hazards (Aubert et al., 2015; Whitley and Willcocks, 2011). Only Oshri et al. (2015) provided initial evidence that a joint venture with a profit-sharing type of contract improves clients’ innovation performance. Thus, how clients and vendors can contract on the basis of values and behavior clearly needs investigation (Willcocks and Whitley, 2018)

To address this gap. we build on recent research on innovation-related interfirm exchanges (Lioliou and Willcocks. 2019: Weber et al., 2011) that claims governance must “establish a fair frame of reference” (Poppo and Zhou, 2014, p. 1508). These studies suggest that contracts in the spirit of relational exchange theory are more effective to overcome problems arising from high environmental uncertainty (e.g., development of markets or technologies) and behavioral uncertainty (e.g., shirking, knowledge expropriation). Relational contracts also increase incentives for collaboration because they refrain from costly ex ante specification of unknown inputs and focus on outcomes that are verifiable ex post. Relational exchange theory emphasizes that interorganizational relationships that are long-term, highly customized, and characterized by close cooperation and interdependence are chiefly governed by a set of contractual norms<sup>1</sup> (Macneil, 1978; Macneil, 1980; Milgrom and Roberts, 1992). These norms influence “the behavior that does occur in relations, must occur if relations are to continue, and hence ought to occur so long as their continuance is valued” (Macneil, 1980, p. 64). Hence, the manner in which contracts are applied is determined by the spirit of contracts that specify acceptable behaviors for members of a social group (Lioliou and Willcocks, 2019; Macneil, 1980)

We focus on three key contracting norms and contractual provisions: (1) converging on joint goals and objectives via profit-sharing agreements (Milgrom and Roberts, 1992), (2) intentionally leaving contracting parties room to maneuver via contractual flexibility (Macneil, 1978), and (3) constraining opportunism by emphasizing continuity and long-term benefits via a relationhip’s long-term orientation (Macaulay, 1963; Macneil, 1980).

## Effects of (mis-)alignment of contractual and relational governance on relationship outcomes

Even though governance research has a long tradition in the ITO literature, disagreement reigns as to whether the two governance modes work as substitutes or complements. Substitution effects imply that contractual and relational mechanisms are functiona equivalents that replace each other or dampen each other’s effectiveness (Huber et al., 2013; Uzzi, 1997). Complementarity relates to effects that compensate (e.g., informal communication offsets the limitations of contractual governance) or enable (e.g., well specified and fair formal agreements lead to more trusting relationships) (Huber et al., 2013; Poppo and Zenger, 2002). Advocates of the complementarity view argue that the two mechanisms compensate for each other’s weaknesses (e.g., Goo et al., 2009; Poppo and Zenger. 2002: Tiwana. 2010). whereas adherents of the substitution view claim that contractual and relational governance detrimentally affect each other’s effectiveness (e.g., Gulati and Nickerson, 2008; Rai et al., 2012).

Recent studies have unearthed more complex interrelationships between contractual and relational governance mechanisms and the contingency of their relative power and effectiveness on the specific characteristics of an interfirm exchange (Goo et al., 2009; Huber et al., 2013; Lioliou et al., 2014; Tiwana, 2010). Research has suggested that over-reliance on a single governance mode leads to suboptimal performance (Lioliou and Willcocks, 2019). However, to date no study has analyzed how (mis-)alignment—that is, congruence and incongruence between relational and contractual governance—affects outcomes of ITO relationships. Rather, prior work has focused on how contextual triggers cause changes in the alignment of governance modes and has centered on the limitations and conditions of substitution and complementarity (Huber et al 2013: Lioliou et al 2014). We add a new perspective to this literature by investigating how, complementary or substitutive, (mis-)alignment between governance modes affects relationship outcomes.

## Hypotheses development

In the following, we develop our research model (Fig. 1). First, we argue that relationship learning has a positive effect on joint innovation (H1). Next, we hypothesize direct effects of contractual (H2–H4) and relational (H5–H7) governance mechanisms on relationship learning and joint innovation and compare the differential effects. Finally, we look at how (mis-)alignment of governance mechanisms influences the two outcome variables (H8, H9).

## Joint innovation and relationship learning

Research has widely recognized that innovation is promoted when knowledge and experience from different domains are combined and that diverse perspectives help expand knowledge (Laursen and Salter, 2006; Nielsen and Nielsen, 2009). Thus, learning from partners’ intellectual capital through combination and exchange is vital to achieving innovation in ITO (Su et al., 2016). We define relationship learning as “a joint activity between a supplier and a customer in which the two parties share information. which is then jointly interpreted and integrated” (Selnes and Sallis, 2003, p. 80). Further, relationship learning includes learning about a partner’s needs, routines, and processes as well as learning from a partner’s knowledge and skills—both essential conditions for “bridging knowledge necessary to transfer ideas” (Weeks and Feeny, 2008, p. 135). Correspondingly, knowledge familiarity between clients and vendors (i.e., knowledge that each party possesses of the other party) leads to better innovation outcomes (Oshri et al., 2018). More specifically and contrary to past studies that regarded vendors’ familiarity with a client firm’s technical and business challenges as more important (Whitley and Willcocks, 2011), recent work has shown that for joint innovation, clients’ familiarity with vendors is even more influential than suppliers’ familiarity with clients (Oshri et al., 2018). Findings suggest that as relationship learning lowers information and knowledge asymmetries, chances for opportunistic behaviors decrease and joint innovation tailored to clients innovation agenda is promoted. Thus, relationship learning helps to bridge knowledge gaps and reduce cognitive distance as client and vendor employees absorb new knowledge and reconstruct existing knowledge structures (Huikkola et al., 2013). Greater shared understanding enables more effective joint sensemaking, which fosters the creation of new intellectual capital. essential for the delivery of joint innovation (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998; Vlaar et al., 2008). In addition, innovations based on partners’ combined intellectual capital are more likely to bring forward innovations with greater potential value (Kotlarsky et al., 2016).

H1. Relationship learning has a positive effect on joint innovation.

## Contractual governance mechanisms’ influence on relationship learning and joint innovation

Governance mechanisms take two forms. Contractual governance mechanisms stipulate tasks and responsibilities of the involved parties through legally binding, mutually agreed upon terms to coordinate activities and mitigate risks of opportunistic behavior (Hoetker and Mellewigt, 2009; Vlaar et al., 2007). In contrast, relational governance mechanisms seek to promote appropriate be haviors and to coordinate activities through unwritten, employee-based social mechanisms (Goo et al., 2009; Gopal and Koka, 2012; Inkpen and Tsang, 2005; Uzzi, 1997). The definitions of governance constructs investigated in our study are summarized in Table 1.

Traditionally, ITO contracts have focused on costs and have rarely incentivized vendors to invest in activities beneficial for joint innovation. Rather, they have encouraged vendors to focus on fulfilling operational requirements with minimal effort (Lacity and Willcocks, 2014b). Vendors tend to refrain from client-specific investments with uncertain profitability (Aubert et al., 2015). Accordingly, research has found that to encourage joint innovation, contracts need to include provisions that ensure that vendors attain a fair stake of the jointly generated value (Lacity and Willcocks, 2014a; Oshri et al., 2015).

Case-based evidence shows that sharing risks and gains strengthens cooperative behavior and is thus a crucial element of contracts for innovation through ITO, as these contracts align the two parties’ interests and discourage opportunistic behavior (Chang et al., 2017; Lacity and Willcocks, 2014b; Lioliou and Willcocks, 2019). As co-creation of innovation involves disclosing valuable knowledge and skills, both parties face expropriation risks, and to preclude knowledge spillovers, clients and vendors may be reluctant to grant the other party access to intellectual property. However, when partners’ goals are congruent, the client and vendor employees have a selfinterest in focusing on higher-level obiectives and give less attention to exchange hazards. Both client and vendor will be more willing to allow their proprietary knowledge to be creatively and cooperatively used for their mutual benefit. Moreover, gain-sharing provides incentives for vendors to engage in learning activities, which are important to increase familiarity with the client.

A potential growth in relationship value also increases vendors’ willingness to contribute more and better resources to joint innovation activities, such as employing more experienced staff or cutting-edge technology (Kotlarsky et al., 2016). Moreover, performance-based incentives improve vendors' prioritization of innovation proiects based on client value (Fitoussi and Gurbaxani. 2012). Therefore, greater monetary incentives for vendors should lead to alignment of the two parties’ interests and discourage noncooperative behaviors, motivating the co-creation of joint innovation.

H2a,b. Profit-sharing has a positive effect on (a) relationship learning and (b) joint innovation.

To demonstrate parties’ long-term intent, contracts can include extendability or evergreen clauses that automatically prolong the contract for a pre-specified period unless one party explicitly terminates the contract. Game-theoretic logic posits that a relationship laid out for the future positively influences cooperation in the present (Li. 2014). As both parties anticipate that future benefits from cooperative behavior will be greater than short-term gains from self-interested actions, they realize that neither party gains by defecting from collaborative behavior in the present (Macneil, 1980). The longer this “shadow of the future,” the more likely partners are to act cooperatively and reliably.

The prospect of a profitable long-term relationship also increases partners’ motivation to disclose valuable information (Faems et al., 2007), reducing the need for extensive safeguarding (Susarla et al., 2010). Learning relationships’ effectiveness will further benefit from contractual specifications that curtail a vendor’s employee turnover through a key-people provision or incentives for keeping the workforce stable for the longer term (Zimmermann and Ravishankar, 2013). Therefore, contractually emphasizing re lationships’ long-term orientation will positively affect employees’ willingness and ability to engage in learning activities. Further, the prospects of future increased returns and relationship expansion are likely to encourage vendors to invest more in relationship learning and client-specific assets that promote joint innovation (Li, 2014). In an interorganizational relationship designed for long-term collaboration, hold-up behaviors (e.g., vendors’ underinvestment in a long-term profitable technology) and moral hazard (e.g., abuse of bargaining power owing to economic dependence) are also less likely to occur because defecting from cooperation wil decrease the quantity and quality of joint innovations, thus reducing relationship value.

H3a,b. A client–vendor relationship’s long-term orientation has a positive effect on (a) relationship learning and (b) joint innovation.

Contractual flexibility is a key element of contract design that helps to avoid long and costly renegotiations when uncertainties and contingencies emerge (Goo et al., 2009; Poppo and Zhou, 2014; Rai et al., 2012). We argue that since technologies and market conditions change rapidly, contractual flexibility is vital for joint innovation in ITO relationships owing to the high uncertainty involved in developing innovations. Although precision in specifying contracts for innovation in ITO is not achievable, relationship continuity is important (Macher and Richman, 2008). Thus, contracts should not over-specify mutual obligations but permit straightforward adaptations with marginal friction, freeing up time and personal resources to improve time-to-market (Goo et al., 2009). Flexible contracts may also increase the potential business value of joint innovation because they promote more creative and cooperative behavior (Weber et al., 2011). In contrast, rigid application of contracts leads to strict adherence to contractua specifications, or “work-to-rule behavior” (Macaulay, 1963), which has negative implications for the number and quality of jointly developed innovations (Faems et al., 2008).

H4. Contractual flexibility has a positive effect on joint innovation.

Relational governance mechanisms’ influence on relationship learning and joint innovation

Relying on trust in another’s good intentions, a trustor excludes from consideration certain possibilities and dangers that cannot be removed by contracts (Luhmann, 1979). Our study focuses on clients’ goodwill trust since trusting vendors constitutes a rational choice for clients to deal with the complexity and uncertainty involved in seeking innovation in ITO (Luhmann, 1979). Importantly, trusting in a vendor’s goodwill does not mean naïvely taking for granted that the vendor will abstain from opportunism. Rather, it indicates concern about the considerable opportunism risks involved in innovation through ITO and intentionally creating an atmosphere in which employees of clients and vendors work together on innovation as if they worked for the same firm

Trust is an important lubricant for relationship learning and cooperation, as it contributes to establishing an intimate, open, and reliable relationship that lowers the risk of knowledge expropriation (Lioliou and Willcocks, 2019; Uzzi, 1997). Knowledge expro priation risks present a barrier to openness and transparency and inhibit cooperative behaviors in interfirm collaborations (Kale et al. 2000). Thus, willingness to make important knowledge accessible and disclose sensitive information to the other party is higher in more trusting interorganizational relationships (Muthusamy and White, 2005; Whitley and Willcocks, 2011). This influence is crucia for achieving innovation in ITO because it increases vendors' familiarity with the client and creates more opportunities for exploitation and exploration of knowledge (McEvily et al., 2003; Oshri et al., 2018).

Also, when client employees expect that vendor employees will act in their best interest, they anticipate that the exchanged knowledge is valuable and relevant (McEvily et al., 2003). Thus, they will ascribe greater value to this knowledge, increasing their attention, alertness, and responsiveness toward the conveyed knowledge. Further, trust allows clients to focus more on goals and less on opportunism and agency problems, strengthening collaboration and lowering transaction costs since fewer control mechanisms are required (Humphreys et al., 2004).

H5a,b. Goodwill trust has a positive effect on (a) relationship learning and (b) joint innovation.

Reciprocal norms reduce behavioral uncertainty by turning self-seeking agents “into members of a community with shared in terests, a common identity, and a commitment to the common good” (Adler and Seok-Woo, 2002, p. 25). Thus, since negative in centives for knowledge disclosure are reduced, client and vendor emplovees are more likely to openly and willingly grant access to resources (Muthusamy and White, 2005). Norms of reciprocity establish socially agreed-upon rules that nurture effective learning relationships, and a self-reinforcing sense of mutual indebtedness will lead parties to gradually increase the scope of resources being exchanged in the partnership (Blau, 1964), thereby creating new knowledge and capabilities that increase the likelihood of achieving joint innovation.

Reciprocal norms also benefit the creation of more distinctive value, as through continuing supportive exchanges employees develop a deeper understanding of the other firm’s internal operations and objectives (Uzzi, 1997). Strong reciprocal norms also lessen response times and facilitate impromptu exchanges, reducing time-to-market and increasing the likelihood of more innovative resource combinations (Miranda and Kavan, 2017).

H6a,b. Norms of reciprocity have a positive effect on (a) relationship learning and (b) joint innovation.

Social interaction ties between partners are important for realizing innovation because they facilitate access to a partner’s tacit knowledge and valuable resources that are inaccessible through other strategies (Granovetter, 1985; Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998; Uzzi and Gillespie, 2002). Frequent communication and collaborative problem-solving lead to more in-depth knowledge exchange and improve the quality, relevance, and timeliness of the knowledge exchanged (Adler and Seok-Woo, 2002). Further, through strong social interaction ties employees learn ‘who to talk to’, increasing the likelihood they will actively seek information from the other party’s employees (Lioliou and Zimmermann, 2015).

Strong ties also discourage opportunism by creating common norms and goals (Lioliou and Zimmermann, 2015) and facilitating access to people, knowledge, and resources (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). In addition, interactions among team members are likely to generate unexpected and unforeseen knowledge combinations from which new ideas for joint innovation can emerge (Miranda and Kavan, 2017; Patel et al., 2015).

H7a,b. Social interaction ties have a positive effect on (a) relationship learning and (b) joint innovation

Effects of (mis-)alignment between governance mechanisms on relationship learning and joint innovation

Thus far, we have theorized about the separate effects of contractual and relational governance mechanisms. However, recent ITO research has revealed complex causal relationships between the two governance mechanisms (Huber et al., 2013; Lioliou et al., 2014). We extend this stream of the literature by analyzing how the magnitude of alignment (i.e., high ys. low) and direction of misalignment (i.e., dominance of relational governance vs. dominance of contractual governance) affect relationship learning and joint innovation.

Effects of (mis-)alignment between governance mechanisms on relationship learning

Firm-specific knowledge “can be highly tacit, sticky,' and deeply embedded in the idiosyncratic internal practices" (Tiwana and Bush, 2007, p. 271). Thus, externalizing tacit knowledge is a complex social process that requires intimate collaboration and inter active problem-solving (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995). Relational governance mechanisms facilitate these social processes through “hands-on” experimentation, observation, and demonstration, which are crucial for firms to acquire insights, experiences, and abilities

from partners (McEvily and Marcus, 2005).

In contrast, contractual governance provisions can only specify procedures and structures for relationship learning limited to the transfer of explicit knowledge. Furthermore, social ties stipulated in contracts are less effective than ties that develop on the basis of trustful social interactions, both in breadth as the number of social ties grows larger over time and in depth as relationships develop from purely economic to social contents (Granovetter, 1985; Miranda and Kavan, 2017). An increase in the breadth and depth of the social ties between partners facilitates privileged access to intellectual resources (Granovetter, 1985). It also creates a richer network of boundary-spanners that forms due to a higher number of more intimate informal contacts between employees of the partnering firms. Especially in ITO, boundary-spanners facilitate the sharing and leveraging of expertise across the client–vendor boundary (Gopal and Gosain, 2010; Levina and Vaast, 2005).

At the same time, however, contractual governance mechanisms can create favorable conditions for relationship learning. First, in the process of specifying and adapting contracts parties frequently develop a shared understanding of each others’ capabilities and objectives (Goo et al., 2009; Poppo and Zenger, 2002). Second, contracts that emphasize joint goals and a collaborative spirit enable the formation of relational norms that alleviate fears of opportunistic behaviors (Huber et al., 2013: Krancher et al., 2018). Thus contractually specifying the long-term and win–win intent of a relationship creates fertile ground on which relational norms can unfold their full potential to foster relationship learning. Third, limiting changes in vendors’ workforce helps to retain client-specific knowledge and to maintain effective learning relationships. Workforce changes are identified as a key problem in ITO with detri mental effects on information exchange (Huber et al., 2013). Thus, we argue that relationship learning increases with higher levels of alignment between relational and contractual governance because the two forms of governance have unique strengths that enable each other and compensate for each other’s weaknesses.

H8a. Relationship learning increases with higher levels of alignment between relational and contractual governance.

Conversely, in the case of misalignment between the two governance modes, we expect that relationship learning will be greater if relational governance exceeds contractual governance. An over-reliance on contractual governance creates fewer opportunities for trustful and collaborative exchanges and the emergence of social ties, which are both crucial for relationship learning to occur. Correspondingly, outsourcing partners frequently turn to relational governance to compensate for the limits of contractual governance regarding the sharing and creation of knowledge (Huber et al., 2013). Further, research on strategic alliances has found that where knowledge-based assets are concerned, relational governance mechanisms are superior owing to the inability of contractual gover nance mechanisms to pre-specify exact processes and facilitate the transfer of tacit knowledge (Dyer and Hatch, 2006; Hoetker and Mellewigt, 2009). Contractual governance mechanisms’ formality and codified communication restrict “the pool of resources available for combination” (Miranda and Kavan, 2017, p. 106), and thus novel combinations of intellectual resources. In contrast, strong ties trust, and reciprocal norms enable novel combinations, for example by incorporating knowledge from an earlier project into a new one (Hansen, 1999) or by increasing efforts and willingness on both sides to share valuable intellectual property (Miranda and Kavan 2017).

H8b. Relationship learning will be greater if misalignment is such that relational governance exceeds contractual governance.

Effects of (mis-)alignment between governance mechanisms on joint innovation

Achieving innovation in ITO involves high environmental and behavioral uncertainty, inhibiting ex ante precision in contracting, and increasing the risk of opportunistic ex post behavior. Thus, a key objective of governing such arrangements is to constrain ex post opportunism by creating mutual dependence and interest, which give rise to high levels of commitment on both sides (Lioliou et al., 2014). Crafting contractual provisions on how profits and risks of innovation projects will be shared ties vendor performance to monetary rewards (Tiwana and Bush, 2007) and strengthens vendors’ self-interest in making the project a success. These stipulations also lower the risk of ex post bargaining, which is a key source of ex post transaction costs (Chang et al., 2017). Moreover, negotiating profit-sharing arrangements helps to avoid misunderstandings and enables better communication (Lioliou et al., 2014). Additionally, such agreements reinforce mutual dependence and commitment since they create a shared understanding between clients and vendors regarding their obligations, which in turn creates a psychologically binding sense of commitment and loyalty. Relational governance mechanisms, in contrast, can be unbalanced and one-sided (Lioliou et al., 2014). Thus, relational mechanisms are less reliable in reducing behavioral uncertainty equally on both sides.

Importantly, contracts designed for long-term client–vendor cooperation offer stronger protection than relational governance mechanisms for investments in partner-specific IT and knowledge. Provisions for automatic periodic renewal or penalties for early termination can ensure recovery of partner-specific investments, such as specially trained employees or customized IT solutions, and discourage ex post rent-seeking tactics. Contractual terms that specify retention of key vendor employees can prevent relocation of personnel familiar with the client firm, avoiding delays and inferior innovation performance (Weeks and Thomason, 2011) as well as underinvestment in client-specific knowledge (Benaroch et al., 2016).

Similarly, contractual procedures for flexibly and quickly adapting contracts are more effective coordination and safeguard mechanisms against ex post transaction costs than relational governance. Again, the nonbinding and personal nature of relationa governance mechanisms makes them less dependable in mitigating behavioral uncertainty. Formal procedures, however, give partners “something formalistic so that if something does go wrong you can always accommodate each other by going back to the schedule” (interviewee quote in Lioliou et al., 2014, p. 524). On the other hand, strong relational governance enables parties to resolve upcoming issues in a more informal way by deviating from formal provisions by mutual consent (Huber et al., 2013). Such informal workarounds facilitate experimentation and fast decision-making, which are crucial for innovation projects as they provide both sides with necessary leeway.

However, we anticipate that the effect of relational governance mechanisms on joint innovation has diminishing returns as marginal benefits decrease and new problems emerge. Over-reliance on a good client–vendor relationship risks the neglect and bypassing of contractual governance mechanisms (Huber et al., 2013). This overconfidence might work as long as the initial persons are in charge, but a too-casual way of conducting business does not withstand workforce changes (Huber et al., 2013). Thus, only contracts can ensure that a successful relationship will endure irrespective of workforce changes. An informal working style based on strong relational governance may also lead to omission of necessary contractual amendments to safeguard one’s interests, and partners may neglect to discern “whether their objectives are realistic and in line with business drivers and expectations” (Lioliou et al., 2014, p. 528). Further, relationships that are over-managed by relational governance mechanisms may not produce the desired outcomes, because creativity is an important driver of innovation and requires a certain degree of tension and controversy (Bidault and Castello, 2009; Zahra et al., 2006). Over-reliance on relational governance is likely to lead to overly cozy, accommodating, and unchallenging behaviors that thwart the development of new ideas and creative solutions. Thus, we expect that both low levels and excessive levels of relational governance will have a negative effect on joint innovation. Therefore, with respect to alignment we expect an inverted Ushaped relationship with an initially additive, complementary relationship of relational and contractual governance mechanisms with a downward slope at higher levels of relational governance.

H9a. Relational and contractual governance are curvilinearly (taking an inverted U-shape) related to joint innovation.

We hypothesize that in the event of misalignment between governance modes, joint innovation performance is better when the misalignment is such that contractual governance is higher than relational governance, as contracts are a tangible and enforceable expression of partners’ commitment and loyalty (Woolthuis et al., 2005). In contrast, looser behavioral controls and fewer financial incentives may shift the focus to opportunistic behaviors, as a good relationship alone neither guarantees that clients can harness vendors‘ innovativeness nor ensures that vendors invest enough in client-specific IT and knowledge needed for joint innovation (Yan et al., 2016). Further, an over-emphasis on relational governance results in cognitive biases and avoidance of task-oriented conflicts that promote challenging ideas, critical thinking, and rigorous analysis—breeding grounds of joint innovation.

H9b. Joint innovation will be greater if misalignment is such that contractual governance exceeds relational governance.

## Research methodology

## Sample and data collection procedure

Our data came from a panel hosted by a market research company. The panel consisted of key informants from companies in the U. S., U.K., and Germany that were active in industries other than information and communication technology. The market research company ascertained that all firms in our sample collaborated with an ITO vendor on strategic innovation. To establish a common understanding among respondents, we defined strategic innovation at the beginning of the questionnaire, as outlined in the theory section. The two main inclusion criteria were that respondents worked in the IT department at a senior level and were involved in contracting and collaborating with the vendor. We sent an e-mail containing a link to the online survey instrument to 600 selected panel members who each represented a unique firm. From the 355 completed questionnaires (response rate: 59.2%). we eliminated 11 questionnaires that were incomplete or had implausibly short handling times, leaving a final sample of 344 respondents. Appendix C summarizes the sample’s demographics.

We tested for nonresponse bias. Comparison of construct means showed no significant differences between early and late re spondents. Moreover, no significant difference existed between early and late respondents’ mean ratings for the variable joint inno vation. Therefore, nonresponse bias is unlikely to weaken our results. In addition, we included an item asking respondents about their degree of involvement in contracting and collaborating with the external IT vendor. All respondents rated their involvement as higher than five on a seven-point scale (7 = high involvement), indicating they had adequate knowledge to participate in our study

## Measurement of constructs

We followed standard psychometric scale-development procedures. Table 2 shows all scales used together with descriptive sta tistics and psychometric properties.

We relied on existing validated scales as much as possible and adapted others to our study's context (see Appendix D). We also developed new instruments to measure constructs with no previously validated scales based on related theory and discussions with experts from academia and practice following MacKenzie et al. (2011). To validate these instruments, we conducted a qualitative (n = 12) and a quantitative (n = 30) pilot study with IT professionals and scholars. After we prepared the survey in English, two translators independently translated the questionnaire into German, with a backtranslation into English to confirm that the translation was equivalent.

## Data analysis and results

We first assessed the psychometric properties of the measurement model and subsequently tested the hypotheses with the structura model.

## Assessment of measurement model

We assessed reliability and validity for each reflective measure using the PLS approach (Gefen and Straub, 2005). We calculated the convergent validity of our items using three criteria. First, each item should significantly load on the respective construct (Fornell and Larcker, 1981; Gefen and Straub, 2005). Prior research considers a loading of 0.70 to be the common threshold. Second, the average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct should exceed 0.50. Third, composite reliabilities should exceed the threshold of 0.70. Results show that all items had significant loadings of 0.70 or higher on their respective construct except for the first item of joint innovation (0.63), which we dropped. The AVEs of all constructs largely exceed 0.50 and the composite reliabilities of all constructs exceed 0.70.

We assessed discriminant validity between the reflective measures using the criterion of Fornell and Larcker (1981). For al constructs, the squared correlations between two constructs are lower than the corresponding AVEs. Appendix E shows that the loading of each item on the corresponding construct is above the recommended threshold of 0.60 and that the cross-loadings are acceptable (Gefen and Straub, 2005). We further applied the heterotrait–monotrait ratio (HTMT), which is more sensitive than established methods for detecting problems of discriminant validity (Henseler et al., 2015). The calculated HTMT values are all < 0.90, indicating strong discriminant validity (Henseler et al., 2015). To test for multicollinearity, we computed the variance inflation factor (VIF) values. VIFs are all $< 3 . 3 ,$ indicating that multicollinearity is not an issue (Hair et al., 2009).

## Common method bias assessment

Although using single-respondent surveys as data source is widespread in IS and management research, it risks common method bias, which we evaluated according to Podsakoff et al. (2003). We included several reverse-coded items in our questionnaire, and we assured respondents of anonymity and emphasized that the survey had no right or wrong answers. In addition, we applied the correlational marker technique (Lindell and Whitney, 2001). As the marker variable, we selected job variety because it was theo retically unrelated to the other variables. We then corrected all bivariate correlations between our model variables for the lowest positive correlation with the marker variable (r = 0.03). All significant zero-order correlations between our variables remain signif icant after correcting for this proxy of common method bias. Thus, common method bias is not a significant problem in this study.

## Response surface modeling

The hypothesized effects of (mis-)alignment between governance modes on relationship learning and joint innovation (H8 and H9) were tested with polynomial regression analysis (PRA) and corresponding response surface analysis (RSA). PRA was used because it permits drawing a more accurate picture of the complex relationship between predictor variables and an outcome variable (Edwards 1994, 2002; Edwards and Parry, 1993). In our study, polynomial modeling includes measures of relational governance (X) and contractual governance (Y), their higher order terms $( \dot { \mathrm { X } } ^ { 2 } , \bar { \mathrm { Y } } ^ { 2 } )$ , and their product term (XY) to test their relationship with the dependent variables relationship learning or joint innovation (Z), yielding the following equation:

$$
\mathrm{Z} = \mathrm{b} _ {0} + \mathrm{b} _ {1} \mathrm{X} + \mathrm{b} _ {2} \mathrm{Y} + \mathrm{b} _ {3} \mathrm{X} ^ {2} + \mathrm{b} _ {4} \mathrm{XY} + \mathrm{b} _ {5} \mathrm{Y} ^ {2} + \mathrm{e}\tag{1}
$$

On the basis of the results of Eq. (1), response surface parameters were computed. These parameters were used to formally and visually analyze how (mis-)alignment of relational and contractual governance modes affects relationship learning and joint inno vation using salient features of the surface (see Benlian (2013) for an overview). As PRAs “often yield patterns of coefficients that are difficult to interpret” (Edwards and Parry, 1993, p. 1605), RSAs enable “a rich and deep understanding of the intricacies of polynomia models” (Venkatesh and Goyal, 2010, p. 286). RSAs also help to visualize relationships among variables in three-dimensional space to uncover novel and complex patterns (Benlian, 2013).

Prior to conducting PRA, we calculated the average of scale-centered item measures for relational and contractual governance to avoid multicollinearity and increase interpretability (Shanock et al., 2010). Regression coefficients allow calculation and interpre tation of three main features of the surfaces: stationary point (i.e., the point at which the slope of the surface is zero in all directions), principal axes $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , lines in the X, Y plane perpendicular to one another intersecting at the stationary point), and slopes along the line of congruence or alignment $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ Y = X }$ line, when values of contractual and relational governance are congruent) and the line of incongruence or misalignmen $( \mathrm { i . e . , Y = - X }$ line, when values of contractual and relational governance are opposite).

We calculated these main features of the response surfaces and their respective significance levels as described in Edwards and Parry (1993). To estimate the significance levels for stationary points and principal axes, we used bootstrapping with 10,000 samples and the bias-corrected percentile method (Edwards, 2002). To test the significance of slopes and curvatures along the lines of (mis-) alignment, we used standard regression analyses (Shanock et al., 2010). Appendix F and Appendix G present the results of the polynomial regression and response surface analyses, while Fig. 2a and b show the plots for the two surfaces.

## Discussion

Our study was motivated by the increasing relevance of strategic innovation through ITO and the relatively limited understanding of how these relationships can be governed effectively. Building on relational exchange theory, we developed a research model to investigate direct and interaction effects of contractual and relational governance mechanisms on two key outcomes, relationship learning and joint innovation. We posed two questions that are important to reconsider.

The first question was whether relational and contractual governance mechanisms differ in their effect on relationship learning and joint innovation. Our results show that they do. Social structures more effectively promote relationship learning while contractual governance mechanisms that focus on establishing a mutually beneficial long-term relationship and limiting ex post opportunism are vital for joint innovation. Hence, our study demonstrates that to overcome the innovation-in-ITO paradox, contracting parties need to forgo traditional cost- and control-focused contracting guidelines to enable joint innovation.

The second question we sought to answer is how (mis-)alignment between relational governance and contractual governance influences relationship learning and joint innovation. Our results suggest that with respect to alignment, relationship learning benefits from higher levels of both relational and contractual governance, indicating a complimentary relationship. For joint innovation, however. moderate levels of relational governance and high levels of contractual governance are more advantageous. In the case of misalignment between governance modes, relationship learning is greater when the relationship is managed predominantly by relational governance, whereas the opposite holds for joint innovation.

## Theoretical implications

Our study has three major implications for theory and research on governing ITO relationships aiming at joint innovation and also for the broader literature on ITO governance, as summarized in Table 4.

## Differential effects of relational and contractual governance mechanisms

Individuals in our client firms appear to have used their connections and relationships to the vendor’s co-workers primarily to get access to knowledge and skills and learn about the vendor’s technological solutions and business insights. This finding shows that relational learning occurs quite naturally in a good client–vendor relationship (Inkpen and Tsang, 2005; Loebbecke et al., 2016). In contrast, the impact of contractual specifications on relationship learning is marginal. Only a relationship’s long-term orientation promotes more effective learning. This finding highlights that the longer a relationship’s orientation, the more employees perceive their counterparts as trusted partners rather than opportunistic agents (Willcocks et al., 2004). Further, less workforce change helps retain client-specific knowledge and stable working relations (Zimmermann and Ravishankar, 2013). Thus, our results indicate that contractual specifications such as evergreen clauses or key employee provisions are important complements of relational governance in enabling successful learning relationships. In contrast to Weber et al. (2011), we find no support for relational governance mechanisms substituting for a contractually specified long-term orientation.

For achieving innovation through ITO, prior literature has chiefly pointed to the importance of relational governance (e.g., Oshr et al.. 2018: Willcocks and Whitley. 2018). Our study shows that relational governance is indeed important for joint innovation. but only through its indirect effect on relationship learning. For the actual transformation of partners’ resources and capabilities into joint innovation, contractual governance mechanisms have a greater impact. As the power relationship tends to favor vendors once the contract is signed (Cullen et al., 2014), contractual governance mechanisms are key to overcoming hold-up and moral hazard problems through formally specified incentives that offer vendors opportunities to increase relationship value after the contract has been signed. Our findings show that a high-quality relationship alone, in contrast, does not create enough incentives for vendors to invest in ac tivities and resources vital for joint innovation (Yan et al., 2016). Thus, our study draws a clearer picture of the precise impact of both governance modes. While prior hypothetico-deductive studies have suggested a direct effect of relationship learning on joint inno vation (Oshri et al., 2018; Oshri et al., 2015), our study shows that when innovation-tailored contractual governance mechanisms are considered, relational governance mechanisms influence joint innovation only indirectly, through fostering relationship learning.

## Interaction effects of relational and contractual governance mechanisms

A second implication touches on the interaction effects of (mis-)alignment between relational and contractual governance mechanisms on relationship outcomes. Recent studies have suggested that the two governance modes are entangled in such a way that they can simultaneously work as complements and substitutes and that contextual triggers and an over-reliance on one mode lead to changes in how relationships are governed (Huber et al., 2013; Lioliou et al., 2014). We extend these observations by investigating the outcome effects of (mis-)alignment between the two governance modes. Results indicate that using relational and contractual governance in a complementary way generally fosters better outsourcing outcomes than using them as substitutes. Thus, our findings are more in line with the complementary view of governance (e.g., Goo et al., 2009; Poppo and Zenger, 2002; Tiwana, 2010). However, we observed that while relationship learning profited from higher levels of alignment between the two governance modes, joint innovation is highest when contractual governance is high and relational governance is at a medium level. This finding indicates that contrary to the findings of prior research (e.g., Oshri et al., 2015; Willcocks and Whitley, 2018), relying too heavily on a good rela tionship can harm joint innovation in ITO.

We found that misalignment of governance modes leads to inferior outcomes. However, in the event that governance modes are misaligned, one governance mode can compensate for the weakness of the other to some extent (Goo et al., 2009; Huber et al., 2013; Lioliou et al., 2014). In the case of relationship learning, partners can overcome low levels of contractual governance by employing high levels of relational governance. In the case of joint innovation, partners can overcome low levels of relational governance by employing high levels of contractual governance.

## Designing contracts to accommodate innovation in ITO

Emerging literature on strategic alliances shows that contracts are important framing devices that can stimulate certain behaviors and expectations that affect an ongoing relationship and its success (Schilke and Lumineau, 2018). Many case studies in the ITO literature note that contracts do more than specify in words the conditions of a relationship (Lioliou and Willcocks, 2019; Lioliou et al., 2014). Contracts also define the spirit of the relationship. Lioliou and Willcocks (2019. p. 235) observe that “following the spirit' of the contract means achieving by other means what the contract in itself as a disciplinary mechanism is unable to enforce.” However, as the spirit of a contract is represented in unwritten, tacitly held expectations (Harmon et al., 2015), the emerging literature on contract framing provides a more practical perspective (Schilke and Lumineau, 2018; Weber et al., 2011). These studies suggest that if longterm embedded relationships are desired, the use of coordination-focused contract clauses supports collaboration “by setting posi tive expectations of creativity and flexibility between the partners” (Weber et al., 2011, p. 199). Additionally, these studies show that detailed investigation of the framing of contractual provisions is important to discern their impact on relationship performance. Our study adds to this literature as we found that positively framed contracting mechanisms that emphasize parties’ long-term intent, contractual flexibility, and sharing of co-created value over short-term gains, self-interest, and rigidity have a positive effect on joint innovation.

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to provide initial survey-based support for the notion that contract framing plays a key role in ITO innovation partnerships (Aubert et al., 2015; Whitley and Willcocks, 2011). Our findings underscore the multidi mensional effects of contract design and the contract’s coordinating function, indicating that contracts can be effectively used to establish a symbiotic relationship based on fairness that is both distributive (e.g., profit-sharing) and procedural (e.g., contractua flexibility) (Goo et al., 2009; Poppo and Zhou, 2014; Weber et al., 2011).

To date, researchers have not extensively studied contract framing as an alternative instrument for coping with exchange hazards and managing innovation-oriented ITO relationships We argue that the more uncertainty reigns regarding the “what” and “how” of innovation in ITO, the more important it is to have a cooperative framing of contracts and an emphasis on outcomes that can be verified ex post. Notably, however, we do not suggest replacing control-focused contracts for more distant arm’s-length relationships with coordination-focused contracts. Depending on the type of ITO relationship (i.e., arm’s-length vs. embedded), each contract type has its own raison d’ˆetre, and using both contract types can have complementary effects (Schilke and Lumineau, 2018).

## Managerial implications

Many CIOs state that the failure of innovation in outsourcing frequently results from the difficulty of designing a contract that facilitates joint innovation and mitigates the client’s exposure to opportunistic exploitation by the vendor (Oshri and Kotlarsky, 2011). Our study provides some suggestions for how managers can overcome these problems to leverage the technology and business knowhow of ITO vendors for joint innovation.

Vendors often suffer from the “winner's curse" that occurs because vendors often make economically unviable bids to win a contract (Kern et al., 2002). Thus, vendors are keen to sell additional services to clients. They need meaningful incentives to invest in activities that promote joint innovation. In practice, clients respond to the volatility and ambiguity surrounding innovation with more detailed and complex contracts (Carson et al., 2006)—but these contracts inhibit innovation (Van der Linden, 2011). Our study shows that managers should reconsider this tendency and design contracts that establish distributive and procedural fairness to promote cooperative behavior. Instead of pursuing prohibitively costly ex ante specification of uncertain outcomes, contracting parties should focus on outcomes that are verifiable ex post to diminish exchange hazards and acknowledge the dynamic and uncertain nature of innovation. Contracts that limit ex post opportunism alleviate both vendors’ fears of an inadequate return on their investment and clients’ concerns regarding ex post hazard costs (Benaroch et al., 2016). To mitigate ex post opportunism, the contractual mechanisms we examined provide effective safeguards unattainable by ex ante incentives (Chang et al., 2017). Managers should also particularly focus on signaling their long-term commitment to the vendor.

On the basis of our findings, we suggest that managers should nurture a “trust but verify” culture (Zahra et al., 2006, p. 553) that allows reaping the learning benefits of close social relationships, but limits the negative side effects on joint innovation. For instance, gradually changing teams and injecting “outsiders” into the relationship ensures that the injection of new ideas and views and avoids the groupthink and cognitive biases of established teams. Additionally, our study can help managers to find a performance-maximizing alignment between relational and contractual governance modes that ensures optimal compensation of their weaknesses and ena blement of their strengths.

## Limitations and future research

Our study has some limitations that future research should address. As our cross-sectional research design offers only a snapshot, longitudinal research is required to account for temporal cause-and-effect relationships, particularly regarding changes in the align ment of governance modes. Longitudinal designs would also allow the study of how and why endogenous triggers cause shifts in the dominance of governance modes over time (Huber et al., 2013; Lioliou et al., 2014). Additional research is also needed to explore how contracts for innovation in ITO are negotiated, as the process and outcome of contracting influences success but has received little attention. Another limitation is our focus on the client side of a dyadic relationship. Thus, future research should follow a matched-pair design integrating client and vendor perspectives. As our results show differences across countries, further analysis of the effects of cultural differences between client and vendor firms in the context of innovation in ITO is needed. Another question arising from the increased popularity of multi-sourcing in ITO is how innovation through ITO can be accommodated in multi-stakeholder environments.

In conclusion. we believe that our empirical findings, which disentangle the differential and interaction effects of governance modes, will stimulate researchers to further investigate the question of how to accommodate innovation in ITO. Beyond addressing the limitations of our study, future research ought to advance our study vis-a-vis \` the governance of ITO relationships that include both predictable exploitative activities and goals (related to efficiency, refinement, execution, selection) and unpredictable explorative activities and goals (related to innovation, creativity, search, discovery) (March 1991). As partners in innovation-oriented ITO re lationships typically collaborate simultaneously on exploitative and explorative activities, we believe that it is important to understand the relationship between control- and coordination-focused contractual clauses and its effect on governance and relationship out comes. A mismatch between activities, goals and contractual clauses can have detrimental effects on the ongoing relationship (Schilke and Lumineau, 2018; Weber et al., 2011). Thus, we need to better understand the interactions between control- and coordinationfocused provisions to comprehend how contracts can enable an ambidextrous governance approach that balances exploitative and explorative activities and short- and long-term objectives.

## Appendix A. Example of an innovation clause

The Supplier shall regularly explore and identify service improvements, cost reduction, and innovation opportunities, including those on operational, technical, or strategic levels.

For this purpose, the Supplier shall monitor developments in the industry and in the technology and business environment of the Client on an ongoing basis. On a quarterly or more frequent basis, the Supplier shall provide briefing sessions addressing relevant and significant (in the Supplier’s opinion) developments in the industry and in the technology and business environment which have the potential to affect the profitability and competitive environment of the Supplier. The performance of the Supplier will be assessed by the frequency of briefings (at least quarterly) and the overall satisfactory rating by participants of the briefings.

The Supplier shall provide a business case for the Client outlining the costs and benefits of progressing with identified opportunities to use technologies in new ways for product, process, or service innovations. Where the opportunities identified are outside the contemplation of the parties at the commencement date and the financial benefit is material, the parties shall discuss the same and agree on the share of any net benefit which will be attributed to each party
