# The impact of structure and flux of corporate boards on organizational performance: A perspective from the information technology industry

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Board flux Board age Board tenure Gender diversity IT experience

## A B S T R A C T

Corporate governance mechanisms in the Information Technology (IT) industry are viewed as pivotal for the success of IT organizations. While corporate governance, particularly in terms of board characteristics, has been extensively studied in other disciplines, it has not received as much attention in the Information Systems discipline. This study investigates the effects of board structure and its changes (i.e., board flux) on organizational performance. Results show that board size, gender diversity, and board age have curvilinear effects while board independence has a positive effect and board flux has a negative impact on performance. However, the negative relationship between board flux and performance was weaker for firms with a higher, as compared with those with fewer, number of independent directors. In addition to making a useful contribution to the sparse literature on the role of corporate governance in IT organizations, our study has strong implications for research and practice.

## Introduction

It is only in recent times that governance has gained prominence in information systems (IS) research. The publication of Peter Weill’s and Jeanne Ross’s celebrated book on Information Technology (IT) Governance (Weill and Ross, 2004) provided organizations with practical insights into how emplovees could use IS/IT in desirable ways that would add value to the firm. In contrast. there is a long-standing, rich tradition of research on corporate governance, the role of the board, its composition and size, and their re lationships to organizational performance. Much of what we know about the impact of board size, its diversity, and other charac teristics on corporate performance appears in the areas of management, economics, finance and accounting, with a few notable exceptions in IT (e.g., Pan et al., 2018). The question that immediately presents itself is: How do these relationships stand up to empirical scrutiny in the IT industry?

It has long been argued that organizations in the IT industry are subiect to unique pressures and tensions that may be different from those in other industries, In general, rapid change, shrinking product life cycles complex technologies, less tangible assets, and higher levels of uncertainty characterize the IT industry (Pan et al., 2018; Schmidl, 2012). The IT industry continuously faces high compe tition, rapid technological changes, and constant threats of new entrants (Pan et al., 2018). Given the turbulent business environment in which they operate, organizations in the IT industry have to be extremely proactive, continually adapt and learn, and develop the capability to sense and respond with swiftness to changing customer demands (Pan et al., 2018). In this context, “the boards of di rectors play a key role in putting forward valuable suggestions aimed at running the business, determining strategy, or interpreting business opportunities (Adams and Ferreira, 2007; Coles et al., 2008)” (de Andr´es and Rodríguez, 2011, p. 71), the board’s monitoring and oversight functions become more complex (Raheja, 2005) and its advisory role becomes critical (de Andr´es and Rodríguez, 2011). Given these imperatives, how would board characteristics impact organizational performance? Specifically, how would the size of the board, the experience of board members, their average age, the board’s gender composition, the independence of the board, and the rate at which its members enter or exit the board affect performance of organizations in the IT industry? These considerations provide the motivation for our study.

Prior literature has examined the relationship between board characteristics (e.g., board size, gender diversity) and organizationa performance typically in static environments where the composition of the board is implicitly assumed to be unchanged (Shrader et al., 1997; Wang et al., 2017). However, the board is often in a state of flux, with one or more members either joining or leaving the board in a given time period. This is particularly relevant to fast-moving industries such as IT. We contend that flux enables successful IT organizations to cope with the new entrants and rapidly changing market conditions that characterize the IT industry. We introduce the notion of board flux to expressly address a dynamic that has not been investigated in the past. Specifically, we evolve a measure of flux that captures the degree to which the composition of the board changes. and subsequently assess its impact on organizational performance. We tested our model using corporate board data of firms in the IT industry. Consistent with the literature on corporate boards in other disciplines (Wang et al., 2017), our results showed that board characteristics have significant impact on organizational performance. Particularly, we found that: board size, gender diversity, and age have an inverted U-shaped relationship with orga nizational performance (measured as return on assets (ROA) (Pan et al., 2018)); the changes in board characteristics have significant lag effects on organizational performance; and the presence of more independent members on the board mitigates the negative impact of flux and enables an organization to perform better.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The next section reviews the literature related to board structure, including size, gender diversity, board tenure, board age, and member changes. It is followed by a description of our research model and the hypotheses. Subsequently, we describe our data and measures, and then present our findings. We conclude with a discussion of the study’s theoretical and managerial implications.

## Corporate boards

Prior literature on corporate boards and their impact on organizational performance has drawn from multiple theoretical per spectives. The most common theories used to understand the role of boards have been agency theory and resource dependency theory (RDT). Our study is anchored in the conceptual foundations of RDT, primarily because our focus is on the advice and counsel as well as resource provisioning roles of the board. The usefulness of RDT for providing important insights into the functioning of boards is wel established in the literature, which led Hillman et al. (2009, p. 1408) to comment that “empirical evidence to date suggests that it is a more successful lens for understanding boards.”

According to RDT, the corporate board of directors is a governance structure of organizations that serves the dual purpose of reducing uncertainties arising from environmental dependence and providing the critical resources that a firm might need (Pfeffer and Salancik 1978; Hillman et al. 2009). The board wields considerable power over senior management functions (Baysinger and Butler, 1985), has significant decision-making latitude, and handles complex tasks (Piekkari et al., 2015). The board of directors may be viewed as the ultimate decision-making authority that strives to safeguard the interests of shareholders (Haynes and Hillman, 2010) while providing external linkage and maintaining internal control (Cannella et al., 2008). The board is generally expected to provide expertise, advice and counsel, and strive to enhance the organization's legitimacy and reputation (Jermias and Gani 2014: Pfeffer and Salancik 1978). There is a general recognition that the effectiveness of boards may be attributed to its structure and configuration (Mehrotra, 2016; Masli et al., 2018).

Boards perform executive, monitoring, and instrumental functions (Baysinger and Butler, 1985), in which the executive function is aimed at assisting organizational decision-makers in conceiving and executing strategies: the monitoring function is responsible for evaluating and ensuring efficient and effective performance; and the instrumental function is primed to offer specialized expertise that can benefit organizational decision-makers. The directors may assume different roles, such as insiders, business experts, support specialists, and community influentials (Hillman et al., 2000). According to Hillman et al. (2000, p. 240), insiders may provide expertise on the organization as well as its direction and strategies: business experts provide knowledge of the competition, offer alternative viewpoints and communication channels to external organizations, and bring legitimacy; support specialists provide domain expertise in areas such as law, public relations, and government: and community influentials provide non-business perspectives and exercise influence with powerful groups within the community. Boards typically comprise both inside (i.e., executive) and independent (i.e., non-executive) directors and strive to make effective contributions toward organizational performance (Hillman et al., 2000; Petrovic, 2008). The board of directors has also been viewed as a small group that can be subject to task and relationship conflict; lack of cohesiveness, communication, and coordination; and groupthink, all of which can adversely affect performance (Petrovic, 2008).

Prior research has lavished attention on many aspects of corporate boards. Studies have examined board structure using different parameters including size, composition, diversity, age, and tenure (Dalton et al., 1998; Muth and Donaldson, 1998; Ujunwa, 2012). They have also examined specific characteristics of board members such as expertise, competence (including leadership ability and vision), and compensation (Masli et al., 2018; Young et al., 2003). Empirical evidence exists for the impact of boards on various aspects of organizations such as organizational performance, shareholder wealth, and corporate decisions (e.g., Deutsch and Ross, 2003; Fisman et al., 2013; Hendry and Kiel, 2004).

In the context of IT, prior studies have generally focused on IT management or IT governance (Dawson et al. 2016). However, there are a few exceptions that have investigated the effect of board structure on firm performance in the IT industry. For example, Pan et al. (2018) examined the effect of board independence on firm financial performance (e.g., ROA and ROE) and found that organizations facing “new entry threats” that had boards comprising a higher proportion of independent directors outperformed those with boards with a lower proportion of independent directors. These results are applicable to the IT industry, as it is characterized by an envi ronment in which rapid technological changes, and boards may engage in exploratory organizational learning, effective monitoring, and strategic decision-making (Pan et al. 2018). In other research on the role of boards in high-tech firms, de Andr´es and Rodríguez (2011) found that the advisory role of the board is important for high-tech industries such as IT, which demands high levels of technica knowledge and information processing. Wiley and Monllor-Tormos (2018) investigated the link between board gender diversity and firm performance in the Science, Technology, Engineering, Mathematics, and Finance (STEM&F) sector; they found evidence for a curvilinear relationship between gender diversity and firm performance, and argued that gender diversity facilitates effective monitoring, better resource provisions, and creative thinking. Mukarram et al. (2018) investigated the impact of the presence of women directors on firm’s risk-taking behavior in R&D intensive technology firms; female directors were shown as risk-takers, challenging the belief that female directors are reluctant to take risks.

However, the effect of changes in board structure on organizational performance has not been empirically examined. This was assessed by examining the abstracts<sup>1</sup> of articles<sup>2</sup> on corporate boards published until 2016 using VOSViewer (Van Eck and Waltman, 2011) and MALLET (which stands for Machine Learning for Language Toolkit – see McCallum 2002). While a few recommendations for board turnover have been prescribed to counter environmental threats or uncertainties and to integrate new ideas into strategic de liberations (Liu et al., 2013), the effects of changes to the board of directors remain empirically untested. The impact of changes inboard structure on organizational performance could be intuitively understood and expected but empirical support would provide greater credence. To address this gap in the extant research, this study proposes the “board flux” construct, which represents the extent of changes in the board structure.

## Research model

## Board structure

Board size refers to the number of individuals serving on the corporate board of an organization (Goodstein et al., 1994). The greater the number of members on a board, the greater the resources available for the organization (Siciliano, 1996) and the broader the range of perspectives that the organization may be able to exploit (Ning et al., 2010). Larger boards are expected to pool greater expertise (e.g., high diversity in education and experience), provide access to greater resources (e.g., new/better technologies, mar kets) (Ning et al., 2010), have more visibility and greater ability to create external links, and receive greater support from the com munity (Provan, 1980). From the perspective of RDT, larger boards provide the variety required to mitigate uncertainties and environmental dependency, provision resources, provide valuable information about the external environment, and lend legitimacy to the firm (Hillman et al., 2009). Consistent with this reasoning, Dalton et al., (1998) found support for a positive relationship between board size and organizational performance based on a meta-analysis of prior studies.

On the other hand, larger boards are more prone to internal conflicts, free-riding, and coordination and communication difficulties, and are therefore likely to be more expensive (Ning et al., 2010). Larger boards have also been shown to have a greater propensity “to accumulate assets beyond the firm’s optimal scale” (Nakano and Nguyen, 2013). Thus, board size can be expected to have a negative relationship with organizational performance, as amply demonstrated by research conducted using corporate board data for firms in the United States (Yermack, 1996), Finland (Eisenberg et al., 1998), Singapore and Malaysia (Mak and Kusnadi, 2005), the United Kingdom (Guest, 2009), Australia (Nguyen et al., 2016), and Japan (Nakano and Nguyen, 2013).

As the board gets larger, the communication and coordination among board members become difficult due to a larger number of potential interactions (Gladstein. 1984). Further. the variety of perspectives possessed by the board members can preclude the board from reaching a consensus because of potential factions and conflicts (Goodstein et al., 1994). Larger boards are likely to be less effective in decision making processes, especially in turbulent environments (Goodstein et al., 1994). Smaller boards may be able to overcome the challenges in communication and coordination; however, they may be constrained by a limited range of perspectives and experience that may make decision-making difficult (Wang et al., 2017). This research suggests that the performance of boards that are too small and boards that are too large is likely to be adversely impacted. Therefore, we argue for a curvilinear relationship for the effect of board size on organizational performance.

H1: Board size will have an inverted U-shaped relationship with organizational performance.

Gender diversity refers to the distribution of male and female members on a board of directors (Olofsson and Larsson, 2017). It is commonly measured by taking the ratio or percentage of female members on the board (Ujunwa, 2012). Boards have generally been dominated by male directors rather than female directors (Ujunwa, 2012) although a greater emphasis on equality of representation on boards has gained momentum in recent years in at least the more-economically developed regions of the world (Hillman et al., 2007). Shrader et al. (1997) viewed women as an underutilized human resource whereas Hillman et al. (2007) explained the benefits of having greater representation of women on corporate boards. Women can bring different perspectives to the firm, facilitate creativity (Hillman et al., 2007), successfully develop interpersonal relations (Shrader et al., 1997), exercise greater independence (Ujunwa, 2012), and provide a competitive advantage.

Prior studies on the relationship between gender diversity and organizational performance are largely inconclusive. Some studies (Carter et al., 2003; Erhardt et al., 2003; Reguera-Alvarado et al., 2017) showed that the proportion of women on the board has a positive relationship with firm value while others (Ujunwa, 2012) found empirical evidence to the contrary. Gender diversity may be a mechanism to reduce costs associated with agency problems (Reguera-Alvarado et al., 2017) through increasing board independence. Resource dependency theory suggests gender diversity enhances critical resourcing (e.g., ability to make external connections), which in turn improves performance (Reguera-Alvarado et al., 2017). Engaging women on corporate boards and other top management positions may be viewed as signs of the firm’s involvement in corporate social responsibility (Reguera-Alvarado et al., 2017).

Male and female leaders are known to lead in different ways; for example, males tend to adopt a command-and-control style whereas females tend to share power and information and lean towards a participatory style (Rosener, 1990). Furthermore, males may be task-focused while females may strive for consensus. These contrasting styles between males and females could impact perfor mance. These suggest that low diversity (i.e., all males or all females) may adversely affect organizational performance since a maledominated board could be highly authoritarian with swift decisions and mandates that may not generate enthusiastic cooperation from management stakeholders whereas a female-dominated board could strive for cooperation and consensus with delayed decisions (Pletzer et al., 2015). Thus, it seems that a board with a balanced representation of males and female members may engender higher performance. Hence, we hypothesize a curvilinear relationship between gender diversity and performance.

H2: Gender diversity will have an inverted U-shaped relationship with organizational performance.

Board tenure describes the duration for which a board has existed without changes in its membership. It may be quantified as the average measure of the number of years served on the board by each board member (Anderson et al., 2004). Since board members may or may not have term limits and may or may not choose to serve their full terms, board tenure can be somewhat unpredictable. Prior studies have found both positive and negative effects of board tenure on organizational performance. Long-term directors bring greater experience, competence, and willingness to exert more effort towards firm goals (Vafeas, 2003). Longer tenure enhances the ability to arrive at a consensus quickly and make decisions in a timely manner and develops more loyalty and commitment (Muth and Donaldson, 1998). These could indicate a positive effect on performance.

However. longer tenure could be detrimental to board independence as board members may become more familiar with the firm's upper management (Huang, 2013). Since members gradually become familiar with each other’s capabilities, interests, and contri butions, they may have developed strong ties with others (West and Anderson, 1996). Longer tenure may also result in less monitoring by directors and may reduce intra-group communications among board members (Vafeas, 2003; Katz, 1982). Board tenure also in creases group homogeneity and reduces the willingness of directors to strive for new knowledge and ideas (Katz, 1982) leading to a decline in learning effort (Vafeas, 2003) and resulting in homogenous responses and behaviors. Such boards do not benefit from nove information that may be available through new members who have weak ties with other members of the board. Board members may also have become inertial and resistant to change and may not have the incentive and drive to alter their behaviors (Kor, 2006). In an industry such as IT, where changes occur at a rapid pace, these factors could negatively impact organizational performance. Therefore

H3: Board tenure will have a negative relationship with organizational performance.

Tenure diversity refers to the variations in the tenures of board members, which may be interpreted as the mix of long-serving and recently-appointed members. A lower level of tenure diversity indicates the board has a large proportion of either long-serving or recently-appointed members. As discussed before, boards that comprise predominantly long-serving members may be resistant to change, not be independent of top management, and aim to preserve the status quo. Such members are likely to have developed strong ties over time and exhibit homogeneity of actions and behaviors such that they fall back on traditional responses and are not nimble to rapid environmental changes. Boards with predominantly recently-appointed members may be open to change, independent of upper management, and willing to experiment with new ideas, Such members are likely to possess weak ties, experience a significant learning curve in understanding the business, be unshackled by existing practices, and are still in the early stages of team building (i.e., process of forming, storming, and norming) and not yet in the performing stages (Tuckman and Jensen, 1977). Performance can therefore be adversely affected by low tenure diversity.

A higher level of tenure diversity indicates that the board membership is more evenly distributed between long-serving and recently-appointed members. Boards with higher tenure diversity include newer members who may not be as influenced by the management and might express their opinions more freely (Li and Wahid, 2018). Such boards also benefit from fresh perspectives brought in by new members and the diversity of ideas generally associated with weak ties between the board members (Granovetter,

1983). As long-serving members bring firm-specific knowledge and understanding (Li and Wahid, 2018), members of tenure diverse boards may be able to experiment with newer initiatives while not completely yielding their existing practices. Higher tenure diversit can improve board performance through enhancing board independence and learning (Huang, 2013).

H4: Tenure diversity will have a positive relationship with organizational performance.

Board age refers to the average of the ages of all board members. A higher value of board age indicates that the board comprises relatively older and more experienced members, whereas a lower value of board age suggests that the board houses relatively younger and less experienced members.

Older directors may possess considerable experience and expertise in directing organizational strategies and likely to have access to greater social capital, but may not be as energetic, familiar with new technologies, creative, or have the incentive to engage in improving performance especially when they are close to retirement (Ali and Ng, 2013; Muth and Donaldson, 1998). They are likely to be more conservative, think independently, and make better judgments (Muth and Donaldson, 1998). But some cognitive abilities such as learning, reasoning, and memory seem to diminish with age and this could be an impediment for older directors (Muth and Donaldson, 1998). Younger directors may be more energetic, well-educated, creative, flexible, familiar with new technologies, and have high cognitive abilities (Ali and Ng, 2013; Muth and Donaldson, 1998). They may possess the necessary skills to effectively analyze risks and take decisions that could result in high returns to the shareholders (Muth and Donaldson, 1998). Younger directors may possess the drive to perform well and establish a good track record but may not be as experienced in directing the strategies for organizations, and they are more likely to take risks at the expense of shareholders (Muth and Donaldson, 1998). Stated differently, organizational performance could be adversely affected when the boards are either very old or very young. We propose that an optima board age is required for organizational performance. Therefore,

H5: Board age will have an inverted U-shaped relationship with organizational performance.

Board IT experience refers to the extent to which the board members had prior experience serving on boards of other IT companies. The IT industry is widely recognized to be a dynamic industry (Applegate et al., 2006). It is also increasingly apparent that a robust IT architecture, a secure IT platform, and appropriate IT governance are critical to every industry (Ross et al., 2006). Members who have served on the boards of IT companies have greater experience understanding the dynamics of the IT industry and can therefore quickly respond to market changes, institutional norms, or governmental regulations relating to IT. Specifically, the peculiarities of the IT industry may pose significant problems for board members with little or no experience in working with IT firms. These notions are consistent with the idea that the directors have to understand the peculiarities and challenges endemic to the industry in which the organization competes (Porter, 1996). Thus, we argue that boards with greater IT experience may influence organizational performance.

H6: Board IT experience will have a positive relationship with organizational performance.

## Board flux

Board Flux refers to the changes in the structure of a board due to attrition (i.e., separation, resignation, retirement, term completion, death) or recruitment (i.e., appointment, on-boarding, election) of members (e.g., Fox and Opong (1999)). Such changes may be triggered by various factors; they may be instituted to check poor organizational performance, counter environmental shifts increase organizational efficiency and effectiveness, access external resources and expertise, and gain legitimacy (Hillman et al. 2000).

When a member leaves or joins the board, all the typical characteristics of the board are likely to change, including the board size, board age, gender diversity, board tenure, and tenure diversity. Any such change in membership is likely to set back the board, even temporarily. for various reasons. The board may lose expertise, access to external connections and resources, and decision-making capabilities when a member leaves the board (Hillman et al., 2000) which may reduce its effectiveness and efficiency. When new members join the board, it may need to progress through the various stages of group cohesion such as forming, storming, norming, and performing (Egolf and Chester, 2013). The board may need to identify and institute new mechanisms of communication and coor dination for effective operations but may not be able to fully resolve such issues due to the low frequency of meetings (Piekkari et al., 2015). The executive, monitoring, and instrumental components of the board may be altered (Baysinger and Butler, 1985) and it may need to organize new divisions of labor for effective management. Due to such dynamics, organizational performance is likely to be adversely affected when the board is in flux.

H7a: Board flux will have a negative relationship with organizational performance.

Board independence – the proportion directors who are not affiliated with the organization – may be of considerable significance to the IT industry (Pan et al., 2018). Specifically, the independent directors of IT firms in turbulent environments may offer advantages such as strengthening the monitoring and oversight functions as well as bringing critical external resources that may help with strategic decision making (Pan et al., 2018). According to RDT, independent directors provide more linkages to the external environment, facilitate access to more crucial resources, and bring diverse experience. skills and expertise. Moreover, independent directors. as compared with non-independent ones, contribute to explorative organizational learning and initiation of strategic changes to a greater extent (Pan et al., 2018). Therefore, board independence mitigates and/or absorbs the negative shocks arising from board flux.

H7b: Board independence lessens the negative effect of board flux on organizational performance.

Moreover, the coefficients of all the hypothesized variables in Model 4 are lower than those of the corresponding variables that were significant in Model 3. Certain hy pothesized variables (e.g., board size squared and board flux) have even become non-significant in Model 4. indicating diminishing effects over time. For example, board flux had a significant negative effect on 2015 performance, a marginal negative effect on 2016 performance, and a non-significant effect on 2017 performance

From a theoretical perspective, the decrease in the adverse effect of board flux on organizational performance over time resonates with the idea that perturbations injected into an organization could result in losses in the short term (Brunner et al., 2010). Pertur bations are “novel stimuli that disrupt the execution of specialized routines” (Brunner et al., 2010, p. 1) that could originate within or outside the organization and have the potential to create a more adaptive and exploratory organization in the long term, although they may result in inefficiencies in the short term. It is, therefore, not surprising that our conceptualization of flux, which is akin to per turbations, is detrimental to performance in the short term (i.e., a lag of two years in our model) but is subsequently non-significant.

The three variables for board flux were also used in separate regression models to draw additional insights. As shown in Table 3, Models 5A, 5B, and 5C included age flux, tenure flux, and gender flux respectively. Results showed that age flux and tenure flux had significant effects on performance whereas gender flux was non-significant. A likely reason for the lack of support for gender flux is that it occurred to a lesser extent in our data sample. The other hypothesized effects remained consistent with the main results shown in Table 2.

Our preceding analysis shows that board flux adversly affects organizational performance. ## Discussion

## Findings

This study investigated the effects of board structure and board flux on organizational performance. Results showed that board size, gender diversity, and board age exerted curvilinear effects on performance, board flux had a negative effect on performance, and board independence mitigated the effect of board flux on performance. Board tenure (H3), tenure diversity (H4), and board IT experience (H6) did not have significant effects on performance.

Board size had a curvilinear effect on organizational performance (H1). This finding implies that organizational performance is adversely affected with small or large boards. RDT suggests that boards play a critical role in provisioning resources (e.g., information, skills, finance, and human/social capital) that enable firms to become more independent and responsive to uncertain environments (Hillman et al., 2009). Small boards may not be as effective because they do not have adequate linkages to provide the resources that a firm might need. On the other hand, a large board is likely to bring more connections, information, and expertise that can reduce an organization’s reliance on the environment for scarce resources (Knockaert and Ucbasaran, 2013). At the same time, a large board may experience significant communication and coordination issues that may hinder the performance of a company. Given the positive coefficient for the main effect of board size and the negative coefficient for the squared term of board size, it can be concluded that the effect of board size on performance decreases with an increase in board size. The scatterplot on Fig. 1 shows the relationships between board size and performance along with a fitted curve. The plot shows that performance is low for small board sizes, then increases at a decreasing rate as the size increases, and drops for higher values of board size.

Gender diversity exerted a curvilinear effect on organizational performance (H2). This suggests that organizations with genderdiverse boards perform better than those with gender-uniformity (i.e., all-female or all-male) boards. When examined through the lens of RDT, which is particularly useful for understanding the impact of board diversity on financial performance (Carter et al., 2010), gender-diverse boards could not only increase board independence but also reduce an organization’s dependence on environmental resources by providing broader perspectives, varied levels of risk aversion, heterogeneity of opinion, and information that can enhance decision-making (e.g., Mateos de Cabo et al., 2012). However, diversity can generate conflict that can adversely impact performance. Results show that the marginal benefit of gender diversity on organizational performance decreases as the ratio of female board members increases. Our sample did not have any female-dominated boards and hence the findings of our study are empirically limited. Fig. 2 depicts a scatterplot along with a fitted curve for the relationship between gender diversity and performance. The plot shows that performance drops when diversity is either low or high while performance improves with non-extreme values of gender diversity.

Board age showed a curvilinear effect on organizational performance (H5). This finding suggests that both younger boards and older boards may be detrimental to performance. Younger boards may comprise directors who are highly educated and familiar with new technologies (Hatfield 2002; Jhunjhunwala and Mishra, 2012) but willing to take risks and experiment with changes, whereas older boards may comprise directors who have considerable experience accumulated from the industry (Li et al. 2011; Jhunjhunwala and Mishra, 2012) but may be inertial and unwilling to take risks. These notions are consistent with RDT, which suggests that an organization can use the combined expertise of younger and older board members. Given the positive coefficient for the main effect of board age and the negative coefficient for the squared term of board age, it can be concluded that the effect of board age on per formance decreases with an increase in board age. Fig. 3 depicts a scatterplot of board age and performance along with a fitted curve The plot shows that performance weakens when the board age is extremely low or high but strengthens otherwise.

Board flux had a negative direct effect and positive indirect (through its interaction with board independence) on organizationa performance (H7a and H7b). In other words, changes in board structure seem to be detrimental to performance, specifically when boards consist of a higher number of internal directors. Although changes in board structure may be inevitable as organizations adapt to environmental changes, strive for external resources, or alter strategic directions, organizations may find it helpful to minimize the extent or the effect of flux. A higher percentage of independent directors is valuable in industries such as IT where frequent changes are inevitable. The RDT logic, which has proven to be useful in explaining the performance of entrepreneurial firms (Daily et al., 2002). would argue that such independent directors can provide crucial resources that the organization might need to cope with the externa environment (Hillman et al., 2009).

There are several plausible reasons for the lack of support for H3 (i.e., negative relationship between board tenure and perfor mance), H4 (i.e., positive association between board tenure diversity and performance), and H6 (i.e., positive association between board IT experience and performance). It is conceivable that some of the benefits of having board members with longer tenure might have blunted the negative impact that tenure was expected to have on performance. For example, prior studies have argued and/or demonstrated that members with longer tenure have a deeper understanding of the firm’s operations and are likely to have earned the trust of the managers, as a consequence of which the latter are less inclined to act in their own interests or place undue stress on the board members (e.g., Schnake et al., 2005; Rutherford and Buchholtz, 2007; Dou et al., 2015; Livnat et al., 2019). Livnat et al. (2019, p. 98) also point to an “inherent endogeneity of board selection” resulting from either the possible tendency of board members to extend their tenure in firms that are doing well or the reluctance of shareholders to alter a board that is doing well.

The long-standing research on small groups has consistently maintained that group processes, such as cohesion, social integration, communication and coordination, are critical to team outcomes (e.g., O’Reilly et al., 1989; Shaw and Barrett-Power, 1998). While diversity can promote innovative thinking and help with performance, there is likely to be a cost in terms of its potential for adversely affecting group processes, which could possibly make teams dysfunctional (e.g., Williams and O’Reilly, 1998). This seems to be a reasonable explanation for the lack of support for the hypothesis that tenure diversity will be positively associated with performance.

As mentioned earlier, the IT experience of board members had a positive but not significant relationship with performance. A plausible reason for this could be that rapid and revolutionary changes in the technological landscape as well as in the business environment may dampen the advantages that experience with IT might provide. For example, extensive IT experience in older technologies such as traditional software development (vis-a-vis \` agile) may impede rather than enhance one’s ability to perform wel in settings where agility of response and customer engagement are paramount.

Collectively, our findings provide considerable support for the fundamental thesis of RDT that organizations are dependent on external resources for survival. Since corporate boards are a mechanism by which organizations can access and manage external resources, attention to flux in corporate boards is of considerable importance for organizations. Our findings show that board flux can adversely affect organizational performance in the short-term but its impact fades away over the long-term as organizations adapt to and manage flux.

## Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of its limitations. First, this research was based on data gathered from archival sources. Therefore, this study assumes that the quality of the data sources is appropriate for the purposes of this research. Second, data gathering and analysis was restricted to specific years (such as 2013 and 2014) and this study assumes that there were no idiosyncrasies in the data during those years that could potentially call into question the validity of the findings. However, we included three years of performance data (i.e., 2015, 2016, and 2017) to partially mitigate such issues. Third, this research was restricted to a single industry (i.e., IT). Although the research focus is on corporate boards that are a common feature across most organizations, the findings could potentially be biased due to the focus on the IT industry. Fourth, organizational performance was measured using a single variable (i.e., ROA). Despite the common use of ROA as an indicator of performance, it may not completely capture all facets of organizational performance and may provide only a partial view of the relationships examined in the study. Finally, board charac teristics (including structure and flux) were represented using archival and computed data that may be somewhat limited.

## Implications for research

This study has several implications for research. First, this study examined the relationship between board characteristics (structure and flux) and organizational performance in the context of the IT industry. Based on the assertion that RDT provides a useful lens for studying boards (Hillman et al. 2009), our study relied predominantly on RDT logic to formulate the hypotheses. While a significant body of research on corporate governance, board interlocks<sup>3</sup>, and board structure exists in disciplines such as management, ac counting, and finance, such studies in the IS discipline are rare. The preliminary evidence offered by this study can generate further interest within the IS profession to spawn more research to expand our understanding of the role of boards in rapidly changing in dustries such as IT. At a broader level, our study is a valuable addition to the growing body of empirical papers that are anchored in RDT.

Second, we contribute to the literature on corporate governance by conceptualizing and empirically testing board flux as a variable that can have a significant adverse effect on organizational performance. Despite attention to notions such as board turnover, effec tiveness, and configuration, prior research has generally not examined the impacts on organizational performance when the board is in flux. Board flux is likely inevitable in industries such as IT that operate in turbulent environments (i.e., fast clock-speed, high competition, and rapid-technological changes). In such contexts, boards continually respond to environmental changes by altering their board structure and composition. For example, bringing in an external member with knowledge of a specific technology is one way of quickly adjusting to a rapid technological change. In addition, the study also contributes to the literature on board indepen dence by clarifving its moderating role in mitigating the negative impact of flux on performance.

Third, our articulation of flux and its effect on organizational performance presents scholars in IS and other disciplines with the opportunity to revisit the theoretical underpinnings of organizational transformation through “the dissolution and creation of order” (Nonaka 1988, p. 58). Inspired by the works of Ilya Prigogine<sup>4</sup> on dissipative structures, management scholars such as Gemmill and Smith (1985). Nonaka (1988), and Leifer (1989) have advanced a view that is counter to entrenched theories of management, which strive for organizational order and equilibrium through established and stable routines aimed at controlling uncertainties in the environment. Rather, they endorse the notion that perturbations induced internally or externally can move an organization away from its equilibrium and serve as a catalyst for a new order that disrupts rigid routines and makes the organization more explorative (also see Brunner et al., 2010). In other words, the state of organizational non-equilibrium engendered by new stimuli – such as the concept of flux we have introduced – can bring about “order through fluctuation” (Nonaka 1988, p. 59) that can help an organization become more adaptive and responsive to changes in the environment. Given this backdrop, it is conceivable that flux, as conceptualized and operationalized by us. may adversely affect performance in the short-term but may actually help an organization re-orient itself and perform better in the long-term. Of course, it all depends on the reasons for the flux: Was flux a consequence of deliberate action to cause perturbations in the organization (e.g., to bring in external members who add diversity and creativity to the board)? Or, did the flux occur because of financial distress? Thus, our study sets the platform for exploring alternative – and non-rationalistic – man agement theories to understand the dynamics of organizational self-renewal and transformation in the face of uncertainties in a turbulent business environment (Nonaka. 1988: Leifer. 1989: Brunner et al., 2010)

Finally, this study also examined the effects of board structure on performance using a variety of measures such as size, gender, age, tenure, and IT experience. These findings may be used to compare and contrast the extent to which the dynamics within the IT industry compares with other industries.

## Implications for practice

This research offers several implications for practice. First, this study found a curvilinear relationship between board size and organizational performance at least in the IT industry. Organizations may find it helpful to set up corporate boards that are not so small as to result in a paucity or resources (e.g.. IT expertise/experience) and not so large as to become dysfunctional in terms of coordination

and communication.

Second, this study found that both younger and older boards may negatively influence organizational performance at least in the IT industry. Boards with older members may prefer to maintain status quo when change is required, whereas boards with younger members may lack the necessary experience to navigate rapid change and turbulent environments. It may be helpful for organizations to set up corporate boards with a mix of younger and older directors such that the older directors can provide experience and stabilit while the younger directors can take risks and break the status quo as needed

Third, this study found that gender-diverse boards perform better than gender-uniform boards. Organizations may benefit from creating corporate boards that have a mix of both female and male members. A mix of male and female members on the board may enable organizations to achieve balance between contrasting styles of management and engagement with stakeholders.

Finally, this study found that changes to the board can hurt organizational performance, especially when the board comprises a higher proportion of internal directors. A plausible reason for this is that internal directors are unlikely to provide the requisite variety and resources that can alleviate the adverse effect of flux on performance. While flux cannot be completely avoided because corporate boards are likely to change in response to environmental conditions, it could be helpful to minimize flux to the extent possible, or increase representation of independent directors to mitigate the negative impact of flux. In addition, when a board appoints a new member, the negative impact of flux may be mitigated by not only selecting a member wisely based on his/ her expertise but also by considering the current composition of the board. For example, our results show the importance of balancing the board composition in terms of young/old directors and female/male directors.

As discussed earlier. the possibility that flux may enhance long-term adaptiveness of a firm while adversely affecting short-term performance is consistent with non-rationalistic strategies put forth by management scholars (Nonaka, 1988; Leifer, 1989; Brunner et al., 2010). Indeed, our finding that the negative effect of flux diminishes after a couple of years appears to be in line with this reasoning. The lessons from Japanese firms (see Nonaka, 1988; Brunner et al., 2010) coupled with our findings suggest that organi zations should strategically induce flux or perturbations while instituting measures to dampen their adverse effect in the short-term

## Conclusion

With a goal to understand the roles of governance mechanisms in the IT industry, this study investigated the effects of board structure and board flux on organizational performance in the IT industry. Results showed that both structure and flux impact per formance in different ways. Based on the findings of this study, organizations can institute corporate boards with specific charac teristics such as optimal number of directors, a mix of younger and older directors, a mix of female and male directors, and a higher proportion of independent directors. This study thus contributes to the IS literature on corporate governance.

This paper also provides some directions for future research in the area of governance. First, we conceptualized and operationalized a new construct called board flux and investigated its impact on the performance of IT firms. Board flux can be examined more closely and extended to other contexts such as not-for-profit organizations, family-owned businesses, and other industry sectors. Second, our study examined the effect of board flux on only the financial performance of an organization. Researchers may focus on how board flux impacts other key performance measures such as firm innovativeness. Third, the study showed that external directors mitigate the negative effect of flux on performance. Future research should investigate other board characteristics/factors that could moderate the effect of flux. Fourth, changes are inevitable at any level in organizations from top management to operational teams. We oper ationalized flux as a board level construct, but it could also be conceptualized as a multi-level construct that can be applied at any leve within the organization—from top management teams to operational teams. Additional insights can perhaps be gained by investi gating both the short- and long-term effects of flux. Finally, although board flux is a multifaceted attribute, we examined only three types of flux, namely, age flux, tenure flux, and gender flux. It is conceivable that an expanded view of flux will build on our work and yield deeper insights.
