# Sustaining collaborative software development through strategic consortium

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Strategic consortium Exploration and exploitation Kuali Open source Collaboration Open source software developmen

## A B S T R A C T

In recent years, consortium-based open source software (COSS) development has emerged as a strategic alternative to resource-intensive in-house development and misaligned commercial software acquisition. Yet consortia undertaking such collaborative efforts struggle to balance the oft-competing demands of innovation and sustainability. Drawing upon a longitudinal case study of the Kuali initiative, this analysis examines the mechanisms of exploration and exploitation involved in developing and sustaining a COSS approach. Based on our findings in the Kuali case, we develop a conceptual framework to discern whether and how COSS development initiatives can achieve contextual ambidexterity, balancing the strategic goals of innovation and sustain ability over time. While exploration- and exploitation-oriented efforts compete for resources, our analysis reveals that they may also complement one another across distinct layers of institutional activity. The contextual ambidexterity is achieved through multi-level collaboration enabled by a modular technical and organizational design.

## Introduction

Strategic IT investments are some of the most significant decisions for contemporary managers. Organizations often struggle to find commercial application software that aligns with their specific needs and processes. At the same time, the cost of in-house software development significantly outstrips that of acquiring commercial software (Marshall & McKay, 2004). Furthermore, many organi zations simply do not have all the necessary competencies to build software on their own (Mithas & Krishnan. 2008). One emergent approach to solving this conundrum is that of consortium-based open source software (hereafter, labeled simply “COSS”) development.

COSS development is defined as the development of open source software through a united, cooperative effort executed by multiple independent entities (Hauge et al., 2010; Yamakami, 2010). Specifically, COSS development as it is characterized in this study is a relatively new approach to software development (Liu et al., 2014) in which collaborative entities pool institutional resources to develop an open source platform. This approach provides an attractive alternative to the “make vs. buy” dilemma, because it focuses on the specific needs of collaborators while dramatically reducing the costs for any single institution. It also enables institutions to address both individual and shared requirements because of the open source nature of the platform code (Liu et al., 2012).

Despite its strategic potential, ongoing challenges suggest critical questions regarding the viability of this emergent approach: How can the openness and innovation of COSS development be maintained as the community grows? Are there mechanisms that can provide the collaborative scaffolding to enable the creation and growth of strong and vibrant COSS development communities? How do COSS development projects achieve a balance between structural control and openness in the pursuit of long-term sustainability? Thus, there is a clear need for a theoretically rigorous and practically relevant framework for understanding organizational mecha nisms in COSS development projects.

In the present study, we leverage March’s (1991) theory of exploration and exploitation to analyze the phenomenon of COSS development. Scholars have increasingly asked how organizations can avoid competency traps by learning to tack between the exploration of new possibilities and exploitation of existing certainties, a capability referred to as “institutional ambidexterity” (Hahn et al., 2016; Ramesh et al., 2012; Tiwana, 2010). To do so, organizations must allocate resources dynamically through continua acquisition, integration, re-combination, and removal of capabilities (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000; Karim & Kaul, 2014). These reallocations shift the organization’s learning focus through punctuated moves between exploration and exploitation (Garud et al., 2011; Teece et al., 1997; Vidgen & Wang, 2006). For the domain of IS design, developers must achieve a balance between exploration and exploitation to engender solutions that are both responsive to changing needs and sustainable in the long run.

We apply exploration and exploitation theory in a longitudinal case study of a COSS development project. Specifically, we analyze the experiences of the Kuali Initiative, a COSS development project in the field of higher education, to better understand the mech anisms of openness, collaboration, and process evolution involved in developing and sustaining a community as it grows.<sup>2</sup> The research questions that guide our study include:

• What mechanisms of exploration and exploitation are observable in the Kuali community?

• To what degree have these competing forces been balanced within the community?

• What implications for the sustainability of the strategic consortium approach can be drawn from the Kuali experience?

Our analysis of the Kuali community offers a number of critical contributions with respect to both the theoretical foundations of exploration and exploitation in organizational learning and the practical exercise of software development in interorganizationa strategic alliances. First, we extend the theory of exploration and exploitation (as well as related theories of organizational ambi: dexterity) by challenging the traditional framing of the two logics as inherently inconsistent and adversarial. Specifically, we identify mechanisms through which the Kuali community simultaneously fosters both exploration and exploitation, across multiple layers of institutional action. Second, our study reveals how community sustainability is supported through the modularization of both tech nical and organizational facets of a COSS development project with multi-level collaborative processes. Third, we reveal the ways in which concern for balancing the forces of exploration and exploitation extends beyond the realm of a single enterprise to impact organizational learning and sustainability of more diverse, multi-party communities. These insights extend to a range of implications for sustainability in the creation and maintenance of a strategic consortium.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: We first lay out the theoretical foundations that emerged in our data analysis. Next, we provide an introduction to the phenomenon of COSS development and its comparison to other software development approaches. We then describe the research methods, including a thorough description of the Kuali consortium and its evolution over a decade of activity. Following the discussion of our research methods, we move to the presentation of our findings before turning to a detailed discussion of their theoretical and practical implications for the phenomenon of COSS development. Finally, we close with future research directions and the limitations of the study.

## Theoretical foundations

As noted above, the theoretical foundations employed in this study were not adopted a priori by the research team. Rather, these principles emerged as relevant and insightful perspectives during the process of data analysis, as the research team sought to un derstand the experiences of the Kuali community. The relevant theoretical lenses reflected in the present analysis include (1) the concept of exploration and exploitation as distinct modes of organizational learning and (2) the structure-behavior-function frame work for the analysis of complex systems. While both perspectives emerged during data analysis, these foundational principles are highlighted here simply to support the conceptual clarity and narrative flow of the study

## Exploration and exploitation

In his seminal article, March (1991) introduces exploration and exploitation as competing archetypes or logics of organizational learning and change. These archetypes provide distinct logics for how organizations learn and how they organize, strategize, and execute. Both perspectives provide value to an organization, but they also come with distinctive downsides. Ultimately, for March (1991), both exploration and exploitation are necessary for the long-term success of an organization, but because they compete for resources achieving a balance between the two logics can be challenging.

Exploration is about discovering new opportunities by searching, identifying, and garnering competencies through critica reflection, or double-loop learning (Baskerville et al., 2014; Phene et al., 2012). Exploration-oriented behaviors are associated with a wide range of terms, including search, discovery, experimentation, risk-taking, and radical innovation. Exploration is supported by organic structures, loose coupling, improvisation, chaos, and emergence. Returns from exploration include strategic flexibility, the potential for adaptation, and the pursuit of new opportunities, but these returns are temporally distant and highly variable (Levintha & March, 1993; March, 1991). On the downside, exploration reduces the speed with which existing competencies are utilized.

In contrast, exploitation extends and refines an organization’s existing competencies through repeated actions that follow trial-anderror patterns, or single-loop learning (Raisch et al., 2009). Exploitation is about harnessing “old certainties,” and it reflects behaviors that can be characterized by labels such as refinement, implementation, efficiency, production, selection, and incremental change. Exploitation draws upon mechanistic structures, tight coupling, routinization, bureaucracy, and stable environments. The returns from exploitation include operational efficiency and effective resource allocation. In contrast to the variable and temporally distant returns of exploration, the returns from exploitation are near term in nature, with higher certainty and lower variance (Levinthal & March, 1993; March, 1991). The downsides of exploitation are a tendency toward of inertia and a reduced capacity to adapt to new opportunities.

Thus, as Levinthal and March (1993) note, exploration involves “a pursuit of new knowledge,” whereas exploitation involves “the use and development of things already known.” While both logics are valuable (indeed, essential), exploration and exploitation function as competing forces (Brown & Eisenhardt, 1997; Tushman & Anderson, 1986). They require contradictory structures, pro cesses, strategies, capabilities, and culture. Due to these countervailing tendencies (Choi et al., 2016), allocating resources between exploration and exploitation poses an essential challenge for managers (Andriopoulos & Lewis, 2009; Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; Hahn et al., 2016). Inattention to the tensions between the two logics causes dysfunctional learning when either force is over whelmingly adopted (Levinthal & March, 1993; March, 1991). The emphasis on specialization and incremental refinement in exploitation biases management to focus solely on current capabilities, engendering core “rigidities". Search for efficiency leads to myopic learning and competency traps (Levitt & March, 1988). In contrast, when organizations engage in excessive exploration, organizational learning becomes random and chaotic, with managers failing to leverage existing competencies (Levinthal & March, 1993).

One of the most substantial outgrowths of March's observations regarding the countervailing dynamics of exploration and exploitation is an extensive body of research on organizational ambidexterity (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; Raisch et al., 2009; Tushman & O’Reilly, 1996). Organizational ambidexterity refers to an organization’s ability to address the demands of both exploration and exploitation simultaneously – “to compete in mature technologies and markets where efficiency, control and incrementa improvement are prized and to also compete in new technologies and markets where flexibility, autonomy, and experimentation are needed” (O’Reilly & Tushman, 2013: p. 324). Framed as a dynamic capability with diverse antecedents, organizational ambidexterity has been repeatedly tied to enhancements in firm performance on a wide range of measures, including sales, innovation, market valuation, and firm survival (O’Reilly & Tushman, 2013). While several studies of ambidexterity highlight organizational efforts to maintain structural or temporal separation as a means of managing the inherent tensions between exploration and exploitation, others explore how the forces can be balanced based on the contextual characteristics of an organization (i.e., contextual ambidexterity) (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; Lavie et al., 2010).

In both the initial formulation and in the subsequent ambidexterity literature, the exploration and exploitation perspective on organizational learning has been widely applied in the study of software development environments (Hansen et al., 2009). Several studies focus on the exploratory potential of developers in a software development process, with an emphasis on the tradeoffs between specialization and exposure to multiple domains (Boh et al., 2007; Gregory & Keil, 2014; Napier et al., 2011; Vidgen & Wang, 2006) Another prominent area of application is in the study of agile development methods, where researchers highlight the benefits of agile methods for enabling exploration (Ramesh et al., 2012; Vidgen & Wang, 2009; Vinekar et al., 2006). March’s framing of organizationa learning has also been widely applied to open source software development effort, with a particular focus on the exploratory benefits that open source software development engenders (Garzarelli & Fontanella, 2011; Haefliger et al., 2008; Henningsson, 2015; Lich tenthaler, 2011). Finally, ambidexterity theory has been widely applied to the study of software development environments, including in the assessment of distinct development methodologies (Ramesh et al., 2012). sourcing arrangements (Aubert et al., 2015). control processes (Tiwana, 2010; Tiwana & Keil, 2009), project management styles (Gregory & Keil, 2014), and organizational application management strategies (Queiroz et al., 2018).

## Structure-behavior-function framework

COSS development (and indeed any interorganizational collaboration) is indicative of a complex system, marked by the interaction of heterogeneous parts, displaying emergent properties that arise from those interactions, and directed toward one or more goals or functions (Corrado. 2019). In recent vears, the structure-behavior-function framework (Hmelo-Silver & Pfeffer. 2004) has emerged as a valuable perspective in the study of complex systems. because it highlights the potential for interactions between system elements at multiple levels of abstraction (Goel et al., 2009). In this analytical framework, the dynamics of a given complex system can be assessed with respect to three fundamental facets – namely, the system's structural, behavioral, and functional characteristics, Structure refers to the parts that comprise the system and the ways in which they are connected or configured (Lin & Zhang, 2004). In the case of an interorganizational collaboration such as Kuali, this would include the individual and organizational members of the consortium and the channels by which they are connected. Behavior refers to the ways in which the parts of the system interact to achieve the ends for which the system exists (Hmelo-Silver & Pfeffer. 2004). For Kuali. this would mean the various practices undertaken by the consortium, including the diverse activities involved in software development and implementation, cross-project coordination, and community planning. Finally, function represents the purpose(s) for which the system exists (Lin & Zhang, 2004). In the Kuali initiative, the functional focus of the system centers on the creation and maintenance of cost-effective software that is tailored to the needs of member institutions. Thus, the structure-behavior-function framework captures the what (structure), how (behavior), and why (function) of complex systems.

## COSS development: straddling the commercial-open source divide

The COSS development approach incorporates elements of both the commercial and free and open source software $\left( \mathrm { F O S S } \right) ^ { 3 }$ models of development (Liu et al., 2014). Commonalities with commercial development include institutional control of development activ ities. the creation of formal partnerships between participating institutions/developers, and competition for access to development skills (Liu et al., 2014). Parallels with FOSS include a commitment to open source code, the integrative role of shared values, wide spread distribution of knowledge and expertise, and openness in both the membership and processes of the community (Lee & Cole, 2003; Liu et al., 2014; Shaikh & Henfridsson, 2017; von Krogh, 2012). Consortium-based communities require members to limit their autonomy in the interest of directed action toward commonly-held objectives (Wheeler & Hilton, 2012). Accordingly, the COSS development model is purported to leverage certain strengths of both FOSS development and commercial development (Liu et al., 2014). As in FOSS development, COSS development projects enable openness and process innovation as well as adaptation to changing market needs through the dynamic configuration of individuals and organizations. At the same time, as in commercial development, COSS development projects maintain some institutional control over the community’s work, reinforcing a shared vision and plan. While this is also true of FOSS projects sponsored or funded by organizations (Schaarschmidt & Stol, 2018; Jullien et al., 2019), the COSS approach is differentiated by the central role of a collective of member companies jointly deciding on the course of the project and sharing the cost and risk of implementation.

Table 1 provides a comparison and contrast of the three development models (i.e., COSS development, FOSS development, and commercial software development) along several significant dimensions.

These diverse approaches present several significant considerations from the perspective of exploration and exploitation. Com mercial development can be seen as heavily exploitation-oriented. since the software is developed in a closed setting with a formal organizational structure. Because of strict control of proprietary platforms and exclusive reliance upon organizational employees, commercial development firms have struggled to innovate and address the changing needs of clients (Alexy et al., 2013; Gassmann et al., 2010; West & Gallagher, 2006). Indeed, the desire for greater innovation has led many firms to engage with and sponsor FOSS development communities (Agerfalk & Fitzgerald, 2008: Jullien et al., 2019: Temizkan & Kumar, 2015). However, FOSS development engagement remains challenging for proprietary firms, with concerns regarding limited control over community activities (Hauge et al., 2010; Von Krogh et al., 2012), the potential undermining of organizational strategies (Dahlander & Magnusson, 2008), and difficulties maintaining open source software development community interest in firm-specific functionality (Ven & Mannaert, 2008).

At the other extreme, the openness of FOSS development communities has led to significant innovation. Such openness provides opportunities to reduce the development cost and development time (Munir et al., 2018). However. FOSS development proiects face challenges with respect to long-term sustainability (Amrit & van Hillegersberg, 2010; Crowston et al., 2013). In the context of FOSS development (and indeed in the present study). sustainability means the continued existence and long-term viability of a development project (Chengalur-Smith et al., 2010; Cruz et al., 2006). While high-profile FOSS development projects (e.g., Linux OS, Apache web server) receive significant attention in discussions of the open source movement. a majority of FOSS development projects fail to progress beyond the alpha development phase (Lee et al., 2009; Midha & Palvia, 2012). Indeed, most FOSS development projects cease to show substantive activity beyond the first year from their inception (Chengalur-Smith et al., 2010; Stewart et al., 2006). Even those FOSS development projects that progress to a mature status continue to encounter the threat of “forking” – the splintering of devel opment efforts through the creation of separate versions by distinct community factions (Nevo & Chengular-Smith, 2017; Setia et al., 2020; von Krogh & von Hippel, 2006). Thus, sustainability of development projects and the communities through which they are executed is a critical challenge in FOSS development environments.

COSS development has the potential to balance the strengths and weaknesses of the commercial and FOSS development ap proaches. By developing systems in an open collaborative community that adheres to a formal organizational structure, COSS development may enable collaborating entities to enjoy the benefits of openness and experimentation (e.g., tailored applications, significant cost reductions, ongoing community-based support) while still maintaining the institutional scaffolding that engenders community sustainability. The question of whether and how these ends can be achieved is a core concern of this research.

## Research methods

In this research. we conduct a longitudinal case study of the Kuali initiative. This methodological approach enables us to investigate a contemporary phenomenon within its natural setting (Lee, 1989; Yin, 2009). The case study method is especially appropriate for novel research domains, such as COSS development projects, where theory and research are still in their formative stages (Eisenhardt, 1989). Furthermore, the approach is beneficial for exploring “how”, “why”, and “what” questions (Dub´e & Par´e, 2003; Par´e, 2004), to foster a deeper contextual understanding of the domain (Yin, 2009). Finally, the longitudinal nature of the study allows us to observe project developments as events unfold, instead of relying on retrospective accounts (Dub´e & Par´e, 2003).

Our case inquiries were conducted in accordance with prevailing case study field procedures, including the development of a case study protocol prior to data collection, triangulation using multiple sources of evidence, and the maintenance of a chain of evidence (Par´e, 2004; Yin, 2009). In addition to the methodological fit with the qualitative nature of the Kuali case data, a grounded theory approach was adopted because the method is especially valuable for the analysis of emergent phenomena, such as COSS development. on which limited research has been conducted and for which few theoretical perspectives exist (Glaser, 1998; Hutchinson, 1986). Furthermore, grounded theory is well-suited to the exploration of socio-technical conditions that evolve over an extended period of time, as observed in the Kuali case (Vaast & Walsham, 2013). In the current analysis, we specifically employ what is generally referred to as a Straussian approach to grounded theory methodology (Seidel and Urquhart, 2013; Urquhart and Fernandez, ´ 2013). Interview transcripts, observational field notes, and documentary evidence were coded using NVivo (a qualitative data analysis tool).

## The evolution of Kuali: The longitudinal case study setting

Kuali was introduced in August 2004 as a COSS development initiative with the purpose of building a financial accounting system “for higher education institutions, by higher education institutions.” When Indiana University (IU) considered changing their financia system, a \$20 million price tag for purchasing a proprietary ERP system posed a significant concern. The lack of control over main tenance costs, the inflexibility of features for higher education, and the perceived increase in workloads made IU turn away from traditional options. With support from the Andrew W. Mellon Foundation, IU started exploring collaboration with other universities to create a financial platform targeting the needs of higher education institutions, thereby beginning the Kuali initiative. This crossinstitutional strategic partnership built upon lessons learned in an earlier COSS development effort called Sakai. The Sakai project was a consortium-based development effort to design, build, and deploy a new collaboration and learning environment (CLE) for higher education (www.sakaiproject.org). IU and other founding members of Kuali had participated in Sakai and sought to improve upon that experience in the creation of Kuali.

Since its inception, Kuali has grown dramatically. In 2004, Kuali had five development partners, one software system (Kual Financial System (KES)), and one commercial affiliate. Commercial affiliates are for-profit professional services firms that have aligned themselves with the community. From this start, the Kuali community has swelled to more than 100 development partners, eight software systems, and 12 commercial affiliates. More than 50 universities have implemented Kuali systems and more than \$100 million has been invested in Kuali development. The net assets of the Kuali Foundation total \$30 million. From its initial focus on financial management software, the Kuali mission has expanded to include modules for research administration (Kuali Coeus), student information management (Kuali Student), library management (Kuali Open Library Environment; OLE), business continuity (Kual Ready), human capital management (Kuali People Management for the Enterprise; KPME), mobile device integration (Kuali Mobility), and middleware integration (Kuali Rice).

The Kuali participants are united by a system of shared values, including the importance of collaboration and sharing, a commitment to open source licensing, transparency, modularity and re-usability, scalability, accessibility, and projects that are driven by functional stakeholders (www. kuali.org). Adherence to these values is the glue that binds the Kuali partners together. To help one better understand the Kuali initiative, the experience of one Kuali member university is elaborated in Appendix 1.

Although all Kuali software is open source, the project does not rely upon volunteer effort at the individual developer level. The distributed development teams operate under a formal organizational structure and the collaborating development partners work in a project team. The Kuali Board, an oversight body for the entire consortium, is the final decision-making entity for all Kuali devel opment. A Kuali Functional Council maintains oversight of the desired functionality and requirements determination for the various Kuali systems. A separate Kuali Technical Council addresses issues around technical architectures and other technical questions raised within the consortium. For each individual Kuali systems development project (i.e., creation of new Kuali modules, rather than implementation efforts at a member institution), a project manager supervises all project staff and development staff attached to the project. As such, the project manager is managing an organization that is inherently virtual, cutting across multiple distinct organi zations. The task of the project manager is to complete a project of size and complexity on time and on budget. Despite the centra oversight provided by the Functional and Technical Councils, each project maintains a degree of independence, with developers drawn from different sets of member institutions. This relative independence represents a critical point of distinction from traditional intra organizational coordination. The Kuali Functional Council, Technical Council, and Project Managers all report to the Kuali Board. This governance structure has evolved as the consortium has wrestled with the opportunities and challenges of community growth. We identify five critical phases in this evolution (Table 2).

While Kuali received a start-up grant (\$2.5 million) from the Andrew W. Mellon Foundation, the Kuali project is currently funded by partner institutions, which raises the question of what the main motivations are for institutions to join the Kuali community. The primary drivers for participation in the consortium are significant reduction in costs through resource sharing, access to software that is well tailored to needs of universities, and greater ongoing support through access to the Kuali community and the knowledge developed on projects. As noted at the opening of the paper, the ability to dramatically reduce costs vis-a-vis \` commercial enterprise systems is a crucial starting point for consideration of Kuali participation. Through the sharing of resources, the cost of both devel opment and deployment is significantly reduced in the consortium-based approach. In an exemplary case, a large public university’s Oracle deployment effort is estimated to cost \$15 million. Comparable Kuali deployments are generally less than half of that cost, even for development partners who contribute personnel to the development effort. The key cost savings derive from the elimination of software licensing and maintenance costs as well as reduction in the implementation costs due to a reduced need for customization. The lower customization is related to the second core benefit of Kuali participation – access to systems that are more tailored to the demands of the adopting institutions. As a suite of tools developed “by higher educational institutions, for higher educational in stitutions,” the Kuali systems are perceived to be a better fit with university needs. Finally, Kuali participation provides universities with access to robust ongoing support through two mechanisms. For development partners. active participation in the development effort fosters much deeper knowledge of the systems among the partners’ information systems personnel than is generally achieved with proprietary commercial applications. In addition, all partner organizations within the consortium have access to one another and the knowledge base that has evolved around effective approaches to customization or deployment of Kuali modules.

## Data collection

Over ten years, one of the authors observed the Kuali community through participating in Kuali annual conferences, talking to Kual management teams, interviewing with Kuali members and other observers (e.g., those contemplating Kuali adoption), and subscribing to the community mailing list. This observation derives insight from reflection on embedded, longitudinal, and lived experience (Lewis, 1985: Mvers, 1999: Mvers &. Avison, 1997) The data collection effort included an archive of discovery, semi-structured in: terviews with Kuali participants, direct observation of project events, milestone review presentations and minutes of community meetings, and formal project documentation. Throughout this period, one of the authors maintained field notes and produced periodic memos for discussion, as recommended by Myers (1999), with the key principle that memos capture understandings that were sur prising. These notes were frequently discussed with the other authors (Walsham &. Sahay. 1999). The evolving understandings were “disciplined” toward plausibility and interestingness (Weick, 1989) through interaction with the Kuali management team and through presentations and discussions at academic conferences.

Our examination of the Kuali initiative began in 2005. We have conducted four rounds of formal interviews since then, resulting in a total of 83 interviews with 39 individuals actively involved in the Kuali community. Interviews were conducted with individuals from all key community stakeholder groups, including Kuali Foundation members (24). founding partner institutions (14). development partner institutions (20), deployment partner institutions (14), commercial affiliates (7), and even non-affiliated observers contem plating adoption of Kuali systems (4). Most of the respondents had extensive involvement in the development of the COSS development project. Interviews varied in length from 30 to 90 min, with most interviews lasting approximately 60 min. Interview notes were consolidated and typed within 24 h after the interview. Field notes were also taken to record observations (Paré. 2004).

In all interviews, respondents were encouraged to discuss their Kuali participation in an open-ended fashion. We asked respondents to share their specific experiences in the project, reflecting upon their activities, problems encountered, lessons learned, how they interacted with others, steps taken to resolve problems, general perceptions of the benefits and challenges of the COSS development experience, and their perspectives on the future direction of COSS development. We further asked for respondents’ perceptions of project success and project control. Most of the questions were open-ended and non-directive. In each of the interview sessions, the researchers allowed flexibility to focus on particular questions, factual information, and evaluative comments, and to skip others based on a respondent’s personal experience and expertise.

Respondents were asked about their role in the project, the tasks in which they were involved, and the deliverables for which they were responsible. ## Data analysis

Our data analysis consisted of multiple phases of coding that were recursively executed. The first phase of coding involved thematic analysis (Boyatzis, 1998) of the data using the open coding approach of the grounded theory method (Corbin & Strauss, 1990). As noted above, the analysis specifically reflects Straussian grounded theory procedures (Seidel and Urquhart, 2013; Urquhart and Fern´andez, 2013), including open, axial, and selective phases of coding, as well as consideration of existing theoretical frameworks during the analysis.<sup>4</sup> In the open coding phase, we became immersed in the data and employed constant comparison to identify persistent patterns in the experiences and perceptions of the respondents. Key descriptive areas of focus during this initial phase of coding included institutional roles within the community, the nature of inter-organizational collaboration, resources sharing efforts, community governance structures and practices, and the relevance of community values in supporting coordination. This phase also revealed analytical themes concerning the drivers for community participation, including the desire for efficiency and cost savings, more tailored solutions, empowering individual and organizational engagement, and the pursuit of innovation in generating solutions to shared concerns.

The second phase of analysis focused on an effort to discern core themes around the topics of Kuali participant interactions, community growth and innovation, challenges encountered, project control mechanisms, and changes over time in the Kuali expe rience. In keeping with the principle of theoretical sensitivity, we were careful not to force any theoretical constructs onto the data set. Rather, based on the theoretical knowledge of the research team, we considered the applicability of concepts from a wide range of theoretical perspectives to the experiences of the Kuali community members. Accordingly, it was during the axial coding process that the theoretical lens of exploration and exploitation (and the associated concept of ambidexterity) emerged as a valuable perspective for making sense of the reflections of our respondents. Specifically, our analysis revealed critical parallels between themes identified in open coding and a range of indicators articulated in March’s (1991) theory (e.g., exploration—“search, variation, risk taking, exper imentation, flexibility, discovery, innovation”; exploitation—“refinement, choice, production, efficiency, selection, implementation, execution”).

The third phase of coding focused on modes of change within the Kuali community, elements of institutional learning observed, and comparisons with other development environments. This coding involved categorization of various facets of the Kuali experience based on the relative prominence of exploratory and exploitative impact. Importantly, in this analysis process, we did not approach the designations of exploration and exploitation as dichotomous modes of organizational learning. Rather, in keeping with the arguments of Lavie et al. (2010), our analysis assumed a continuum of activity along the exploration–exploitation axis. That is, for all factors identified. we assessed the degree to which they reflect principles of both exploration and exploitation. Through this analytical review various facets of the Kuali experience were determined to be primarily exploration- or exploitation-oriented in effect based on pre dominance of alignment with the aforementioned indicators highlighted in the theory. For example, if a given practice employed by the Kuali community was consistently coded with the principle of efficiency or execution, we deemed that practice to be primarily aligned with an exploitation-based approach to organizational learning. Similarly, if another practice was consistently coded with the principle of innovation or experimentation, the practice was labeled as predominantly exploration oriented. In this coding process, a small number of factors defied straightforward categorization as either predominantly exploration- or exploitation-oriented, because of significant evidence of linkage to both sets of theoretical indicators. This resulted in a third category of factors which is elaborated in the Findings section below.

A final phase of coding was pursued to apply greater structure in understanding the diverse factors identified in the maintenance of the Kuali community. Specifically, in assessing the variety of exploration- and exploitation-oriented facets of the Kuali experience, the research team discerned that these factors were differentiated with respect to their focus on (1) the ultimate obiectives of Kuali initiative and its member organizations, (2) the formal structures of the consortium, and (3) the practices collectively employed by participating members. In short, the various themes identified centered on the questions of why, what, and how underlying the Kual experience. Based on these analyses, we identified critical parallels in the structure-behavior-function framework from the study of complex systems (Goel et al., 2009; Hmelo-Silver & Pfeffer, 2004). As with the application of the exploration–exploitation perspective, the structure-behavior-function framework was considered based on comparisons with the earlier analytical findings rather than in an a priori fashion. In the final phase of analytical coding, the framework was applied to the findings from the previous phases of analysis to arrive at the final structure outlined in our findings section. Table 4 summarizes four coding phases in data analysis.

As this description suggests, data collection and data analysis activities were iterative and interspersed. Open and axial coding was conducted following each of the first two data collection periods. The coding structure developed following the first round of data collection was used as a foundational framework for analyzing the second data set. In line with the principle of constant comparison, we modified the coding structure in light of the new responses. Data analysis following the third and fourth round of data collection involved only axial (i.e., building upon the earlier analyses) and selective coding. Open and axial coding of interview transcripts and field notes were collaboratively executed by two of the three researchers. Subsequent selective coding, including the superposition of the structure-behavior-function framework, was conducted by two of the three researchers, with the resulting code structure and coded segments reviewed amongst all members of the research team.

## Findings

The findings from our analysis of the Kuali case center on two sets of observations. First, we highlight the key exploration- and exploitation-oriented factors observed in the Kuali community as well as an additional set of factors, which we have dubbed ambi dextrous enablers, which reflect the simultaneous support for both logics. We then explore the dynamics of contextual ambidexterity in the experiences of the Kuali community.

## Factors observed in Kuali

The analysis of the Kuali community reveals a variety of factors oriented predominantly toward the exploration of new oppor tunities or the exploitation of existing competencies. As noted above, because exploration and exploitation represent a continuum of organizational learning phenomena rather than dichotomous options (Lavie et al., 2010), it is inappropriate to view most such factors as “purely” exploratory or exploitative in nature. Nevertheless, critical facets of the community’s culture, structure, and practices reflect a clear orientation toward one side of this important continuum. In looking at the evolution of the COSS development effort (and in keeping with March’s original theoretical argument), we see the tendency toward increasing exploitation over time. However, several factors favoring exploration have been maintained as the community has grown. We highlight the key exploration- and exploitation-oriented factors observed within the community and discuss the balance between exploration and exploitation achieved in the community’s development.

## Exploration-oriented factors

With its foundation in the broader movement of FOSS development, the Kuali effort is firmly grounded in an ethos of openness – enabling individual member institutions to identify and pursue new solutions that address shared needs. Specific exploration-oriented factors identified in the case include the persistent focus on open source development, the influx of new members contributing to a broad diversity of institutions, openness around approaches to system implementation and module configuration, and explicit efforts to foster exchange of ideas across the Kuali community. At a functional level (i.e., relating to the strategic purpose/objectives of the community), the Kuali community is marked by an explicit emphasis on innovation within its ranks. Partner institutions are encouraged to explore adaptions or initiate changes based on their own institutional needs, and the community’s leadership regularly invokes the importance of enabling a variety of approaches to emerge in response to any given challenge. Structurally (i.e., relating to the essential elements or components of the community), the community has seen continued community growth with the regular incorporation of new institutional members since its inception, mirroring one of the central mechanisms of exploration highlighted by March (1991). ## Exploitation-oriented factors

While Kuali maintains several processes and structures that foster exploration, the community has moved toward exploitation of established modes of practice in several ways over its decade of existence. Community Values. As noted earlier, the Kuali community is bound together largely through adherence to a set of shared values (e. g., collaboration and sharing, a commitment to open source licensing, transparency, modularity and re-usability). From the perspective of March’s (1991) framework, a focus on shared values implies a force of exploitation, because such values represent normative foundations for selection and choice in organizational decision-making. However, in the case of Kuali, several of the community’s values are explicitly exploration-oriented – e.g., the emphasis on collaboration and open exchange, commitment to open source licensing, and transparency. As a result. the shared values of Kuali have the effect of simultaneously reinforcing both exploration and exploitation. Considering just the emphasis on open exchange, knowledge sharing is viewed as a principle source of organizational rent and sustainable competitive advantage, but it also enhances the capacity for new learning and exchange across organizational boundaries (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Østerlund & Carlile, 2005). From an exploitation perspective, the Kuali values are commonly seen by members as a key source of the familial feeling within the community (“I feel like we are very close like a family”). This family atmosphere engenders close social ties and a sense of interdependence between members. At the same time, the family environment includes an expectation of “contributing back" to the community. As member institutions glean new insights or technical solutions in their separate efforts, they are expected to offer those lessons to the broader community – a key source of exploratory learning.

Modular Design. An emphasis on modular design guides both the technical development of the platforms and the nature of the social interactions. All software developed in Kuali is created with an eye to reuse. This focus on reuse through modularity implies a clear exploitation strategy by enabling the community to leverage past efforts. However, modular design also fosters exploration by creating the potential for dynamic reconfiguration within the community (Karim. 2006). Indeed, Kuali allows for dynamic reconfiguration of elements at multiple levels. At the software level, member institutions are able to modify the Kuali platforms in ways that enhance the fit with their unique needs, creating a virtually unlimited set of possible solutions. At the organizational level, modularity is also reflected in the ways in which member institutions can dynamically reconfigure their institutional interactions – partnering in different ways across multiple projects and development tasks.

Multi-Level Collaboration. An outgrowth of the modular focus is the way in which Kuali’s systems and governance structures enable interactions that cut across the various levels of community structure. Indeed, we see evidence of collaborations emerging at multiple layers within the community, including the levels of individual developers, institutions, projects, and the broader community. In addition, the layering of the community’s action reflects the interplay of multiple distinct types of organizations, including uni versities, higher education administration, the Kuali Foundation members, commercial companies, and competitors. In keeping with the concept of a layered modular architecture (Yoo et al., 2010), this multi-level collaboration means that refinement (exploitationoriented) of practices at one level can engender enhanced diversity or novelty (exploration-oriented) at another layer. For example, the creation of refined governance structures at the level of the entire Kuali community observed in Phases 3 and 4 of the consortium’s evolution has created the potential for organizational learning and the identification of diverse solution sets across the distinct Kuali projects. In this way, the layering of activities and processes within Kuali may drive both exploitation of existing practices and the exploratory potential of the community simultaneously.

## Striking the balance: Attaining contextual ambidexterity

As we have noted, the principle that there are factors that may simultaneously enhance both exploration and exploitation runs counter to the traditional framing of the logics of organizational learning as being adversarial in nature. March (1991) and much of the related research on organizational ambidexterity (Jansen et al., 2009: Raisch et al., 2009: Ramesh et al., 2012) focus on the inherent inconsistency and conflict between the demands of exploration and exploitation. As Lavie et al. (2010) note, “if exploration and exploitation compete for the organization’s scarce resources and entail distinctive sets of skills and capabilities, exploration must be inversely related to exploitation at any given time” (p. 116). Indeed, this inherent tension between exploration and exploitation is apparent in the reflections of Kuali stakeholders, particularly around the competing demands of stability (exploitation) and innovation (exploration)

Despite this inherent tension, our observation of ambidextrous enablers in the COSS development context reveal factors that can bring these ostensibly competing logics into alignment, creating contextual ambidexterity – an organization’s ability to support exploration and exploitation simultaneously as a result of the organizational context (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). Through a consideration of the three ambidextrous enablers identified in the case, we propose a conceptual framework by which contextua ambidexterity may be achieved. Specifically, we propose a causal flow in the functional, structural, and behavioral enablers observed in the case. Fig. 1 provides a graphical rendering of the proposed framework.

In the Kuali case, we observe that the community values of the consortium established the loose coupling of participating in dividuals and organizations. The community values establish the norms for engagement with the community. While member orga nizations vary widely in terms of university size, research and teaching focus, national context, and availability of resources, diverse modes of participation in the consortium are available conditional only upon adherence to the shared values espoused by the com munity. As a large body of research on modular systems highlights (Langlois, 2002; Orton & Weick, 1990; Schilling, 2000), loose coupling in turn creates a foundation for modular design. In the case of Kuali, the loose coupling created by the community values engenders modularity in both organizational and technical design (indeed, ‘modularity’ is one of the key values that guides the community). Specifically, since participation in Kuali is conditioned only on recognition of, and adherence to, the norms embodied in the community values, various components of the community (e.g., member institutions, individual development teams, commercial affiliates) can be reconfigured in diverse ways to address emergent member needs. This modular structure, in turn, fosters the emergence of collaboration across various strata of organizational activity (e.g., community governance, projects, and individua development teams). Such multi-level collaboration enables common factors to foster both exploration and exploitation across distinct layers of the community (Bogers, 2011). The proposed framework reflects an organizational corollary for the concept of layered modular architecture in the design of products and services, enabling recombination and complex patterns of organizational inter action (Yoo et al., 2010).

This general model for the simultaneous enabling of both exploitation and exploration (i.e., contextual ambidexterity) across layers of organizational activity can be usefully illustrated with examples from the Kuali case. As outlined in Table 5. open source devel: opment is a primarily exploration-oriented factor within the community, because it fosters experimentation and novel recombination of technical modules, particularly at the level of an individual developer or the development team. However, because of the ambi dextrous enablers of modular design and multi-level collaboration of the community, the same factor may foster distinct exploitation oriented factors at other layers of organizational effort. Specifically, by promoting the reuse of solution patterns and code developed in earlier waves of development, the exploration-oriented emphasis on open source development by development teams may support an exploitation-oriented factor, such as best practice identification, at the projiect level. Similarly. the refinement of the community's governance structure that has emerged over time is an exploitation-oriented factor, because of its focus on refinement and iterative modification of existing structures. Indeed, this refinement has had a significant exploitation effect at the community level by clari fying roles and processes, dramatically enhancing the consortium’s ability to spin up new projects that replicate successful approaches of previous efforts. At the same time, by clarifying the options for integrating diverse new member organizations into the consortium, the refinement of governance structures may foster the exploration-oriented factor of membership growth. Universities of all shapes and sizes can identify clear modes of engagement with individual Kuali projects. As March (1991) notes, integration of new members is a central form of exploration, bringing new knowledge, perspectives, and needs to an organization. Thus, because of the ambidextrous enablers of modular design and multi-level collaboration, the refinement of the governance structure at the community leve (exploitation) enables membership growth (exploration) at the project level. As March (1991) contends, both exploration and exploitation are essential for organizations to remain viable in the long term. For long-term sustainability, organizations must maintain a balance between the efficiency and performance advantages of exploitation and the innovation and adaptiveness of exploration. Through the suggested mechanism, such a balance can be achieved in the Kuali consortium’s shared software development efforts. This mechanism provides valuable insights for both our understanding of the paradoxical relationship between these two essential logics and the strategic avenues open to those pursuing the COSS development model.

## Discussion

Our analysis of the Kuali case offers insights at both theoretical and practical levels. At the theoretical level, the experiences of the Kuali community and its evolution over time present significant implications for our understanding of the competing forces of exploration and exploitation and the degree to which these forces can be effectively balanced to support both openness and long-term viability. At the practical level, we see evidence of ways that exploratory potential can be maintained even in the face of pressures for greater exploitation of existing competencies, which is central to the lessons that COSS development has to offer to other approaches of software development.

From a theoretical perspective, our analysis of the Kuali experience offers an insightful extension to the exploration–exploitation dichotomy by revealing the degree to which exploration and exploitation occur at multiple levels of organizational activity. March (1991) argues that the logics of exploration and exploitation “compete for scarce resources” requiring organizations to “make explicit and implicit choices between the two” (p. 71). Much of the subsequent application of the theory, including the derived study of organizational ambidexterity (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; O’Reilly & Tushman, 2013; Tiwana, 2008), has maintained this focus, assuming an adversarial dynamic between the two modes of organizational learning even while acknowledging the potential for a balance between them. However, our analysis and the observation of ambidextrous enablers suggest the potential for critical com plementarities between the two logics. That is, through the confluence of a modular organizational design and multi-level collabo ration, an organization or inter-organizational consortium may achieve a state of contextual ambidexterity in which forces of exploration and exploitation are mutually reinforcing. Exploitation at one level of institutional action enables exploration at another level and vice versa. For example, the refinement of shared governance structures (exploitation) enables exploration at the level of a software development project, where multiple options for identifying and integrating individual and collective participants are pur sued. Working in the opposite direction, a consistent focus on the exploratory potential of open source development enables different individual projects to identify and apply solutions generated and tested elsewhere within a community (exploitation). Thus, our findings suggest that a dichotomous understanding of exploration and exploitation as opposing forces is an oversimplification of the dynamics at play. While exploration- and exploitation-oriented efforts may compete for resources, they also complement one another across layers of organizational action.

With respect to the theory of organizational ambidexterity (and the principle of contextual ambidexterity, in particular), this research offers a fruitful extension. As several observers have noted, the ambidexterity literature is split between approaches emphasizing either differentiation (i.e., promotion of separate and distinct bases of competence in exploitation and exploration) or integration (i.e., mechanisms for combining the two forces) as the main routes to the attainment of ambidexterity (Koryak et al., 2018; Raisch et al., 2009). Much of the initial theorizing adopted a differentiation focus, suggesting ways in which exploitative and exploratory capabilities could be separately supported through sequential temporal patterns (Boumgarden et al., 2012; O’Reilly & Tushman, 2013) or distinct structural units (Andriopoulos & Lewis, 2009; O’Reilly & Tushman, 2008). With the exposition of contextual ambidexterity, Gibson and Birkinshaw (2004) introduced the integrative perspective, whereby exploration and exploitation could be achieved within a single business unit if organizational actors are given the freedom to choose between the two based on contextual demands. However, in that research, the principle functioned purely at the individual level. Our study thus extends this understanding of contextual ambidexterity by proposing a mechanism for its attainment based not on individual choice but on emergent properties across layers of institutional action.

The centrality of modular design in enabling contextual ambidexterity to emerge is another critical contribution of the study. To a significant extent, Kuali evolved over time toward greater exploitation of known certainties through the progressive refinement and standardization of processes. However, modularity in the design of both the consortium and its technical platforms provided a valuable mechanism for the maintenance of exploration. Indeed, much of the refinement pursued by Kuali focused on creating governance structures that fostered a modular structure for community interaction and execution of development efforts. As a result of such a modular structure, elements of an organization – including individual members, business units, or development teams – can be reconfigured in dynamic ways to address emergent requirements. Since the Kuali initiative’s origins, each new module added to the suite has reflected new configurations of organizations and teams, generally driven by the perceived need for functionality in a new facet of higher education administration. Such re-configurability means that, even if an organization’s membership were to remain static, the number of possible configurations of distinct elements would still be essentially limitless. Thus, the potential for ongoing exploration of new competencies is maintained through the modular structuring of the organization.

Importantly, modular organizational design may mirror the modularity maintained in a community’s technical systems. With the loose coupling of platform modules, each module provides value independent of the other modules, while still enabling strong inte gration and dynamic combinations of functionality that will meet the unique needs of organizational members. This technica modularity also fosters significant reuse of technical elements across the various modules. A parallel emphasis on organizational and technical modularity provides a critical basis for balancing the demands of exploitation and exploration in the wake of organizationa growth.

Finally, our findings suggest that the exploration–exploitation model can be fruitfully applied to systems of collective action beyond the traditional bounds of a given organization. While March (1991) and the literature on organizational ambidexterity focus on the ways in which these forces play out within a given organization,<sup>5</sup> our case analysis reveals that the balance between exploration and exploitation is an equally valid consideration for the survival of broader open collaborations and inter-organizational alliances. In the absence of shared functional, structural, and behavioral characteristics, the predominance of exploration-oriented forces undermines the attainment of desired collaborative obiectives. As March (1991) notes, such efforts “exhibit too many undeveloped new ideas and too little distinctive competence” (p. 71). Conversely, once inter-organizational collaborations achieve a foundation of shared governance structures and proven practices, exploration-oriented mechanisms must be maintained to ensure that the collaborative endeavor continues to learn and adapt to new market conditions and the shifting needs of its member organizations. This application of the theory in the context of inter-organizational collaboration addresses the call from O’Reilly and Tushman (2013) for ambidexterity research to move beyond a strict focus within firms to larger ecosystems of institutional action.

Turning from theoretical to practical insights, we contend that COSS development provides a rich environment for understanding the balancing of exploratory and exploitative forces in collaborative action. By embracing the openness and variability of FOSS development and the potential for dynamic reconfiguration, the Kuali community effectively responded to changing demands. At the same time, the community has developed stable governance structures and shared practices, enhancing the exploitation-oriented efficiency of the collaboration. What insights then can we draw from the Kuali experience to inform other software development environments? In particular, as proprietary software firms continue to explore the value of open innovation, are there lessons that can inform their strategic approach to such initiatives? Our analysis reveals dynamic factors that foster balance between exploration and exploitation at functional, structural, and behavioral levels.

At the functional level of broad system objectives, the articulation and continued emphasis on shared values that guide the practices of collaboration and development creates a form of clan control and loose coupling amongst development collaborators (Liu et al., 2014). In contrast to the formal structures of bureaucratic control and the price mechanisms of market control. clan control aligns the goals and activities of individuals through socialization and shared belief systems (Ouchi, 1980). Furthermore, when such shared beliefs include commitments to exploratory principles such as openness, mutuality of contributions, and trial-and-error learning, those values serve to inculcate experimentation within a predictable system that leverages existing certainties (Khazanchi et al., 2007; Linåker et al., 2018). Specific to the practices of FOSS development, these shared values can help to foster an “open source way of being” (Knorr, 2014) within an enterprise – a commitment to transparency and open innovation directed toward collective goals.

The loose coupling engendered by shared values at the functional level enables the adoption of a modular organizational design at the structural level. Based on our findings, we assert that this modular structure is central to the strategic balance of exploration and exploitation across lavers of institutional activity. A stable governance structure at the highest level of a community or organization can establish the objectives for platform evolution. Similarly, the creation of consistent structures at the levels of individual projects, project modules, and development teams establishes a common basis of predictability, comparison, and exchange. Provided that these structures are modular and layered, a community or organization maintains the capacity for dynamic reconfiguration of individuals and units, engendering multi-level collaboration at the behavioral level. This creates the potential for significant innovation and the pursuit of new approaches within a stable structure. Organizations pursuing a better balance between exploration and exploitation in their software development can adopt a similar layered modular structure, enabling development teams to recombine dynamically in response to new consumer demands while drawing upon consistent governance practices. Importantly, this insight is not unique to the context of COSS development; rather, it could be explored in any setting with diverse teams and rapidly changing market demands

## Future research directions and limitations

The COSS development approach represents a rich field of inquiry for IS research. This research team will continue to study the Kuali community as it transitions through the current phase of its evolution to evaluate the implications of its structural changes for the long-term sustainability of the movement. Aside from the idiosyncratic experiences of Kuali, the COSS development approach poses several fruitful avenues for additional inquiry. Specific areas of investigation that we propose include the discernment of operation alized factors that influence the balance of exploration and exploitation in software development efforts; the evaluation of sustain ability for COSS development vis-a-vis \` commercial development, FOSS development, and hybrid forms; and the application of alternative theoretical perspectives to the processes of consortium-based development. Future efforts could also include testing and improving the model we have developed here, in the context of consortium based open source specifically and more generally in the contexts of strategic alliances.

While we believe the promise of further research around the model of COSS development to be significant, we certainly acknowledge a number of limitations in the present research. Most fundamentally, the focus on a single case limits the generalizability of our findings; they may not be applicable to all contexts of consortium-based collaboration or to other contexts of open source software development. Furthermore, we acknowledge that the experiences of the Kuali community remain relatively unique in terms of the number of organizational participants, their institutional diversity, and the duration of active collaboration, suggesting addi tional points for consideration in generalizing the findings to other organizational settings. Future efforts could study consortiumbased projects outside of higher education. It would also be interesting to compare consortia in non-profit and for-profit environ ments. Nevertheless, we believe the richness of Kuali case presents a variety of valuable insights to researchers interested in the phenomenon of COSS development as well as to individuals and organizations considering engagement with such an endeavor.

## Conclusion

Through a longitudinal analysis of the experiences of the Kuali initiative, we studied the dynamics of exploration and exploitation in the creation and evolution of a COSS development community. Specifically, we assessed the ways in which this community balances the need for continued innovation and openness to new opportunities (exploration) with the demands of cost efficiency, manageable community growth, and long-term sustainability (exploitation). Based on our case study findings, we have proposed a conceptua framework for the interplay of functional, structural, and behavioral factors that have enabled Kuali to achieve contextual ambi dexterity, balancing the strategic goals of openness and sustainability over time. Leveraging this model, we argue that an adversaria framing of exploration and exploitation as distinct and competing modes of organizational learning fails to appreciate the potential for these logics to complement one another across multiple layers of institutional activity.

## Appendix 1. One University’s experience

[Data drawn from interviews and Kuali Foundation sources (http://www.kuali.org/join/stories/colostate)]

Colorado State University (CSU) was an early adopter of the Kuali Initiative’s first enterprise module, the Kuali Financial System (KFS). The story of CSU’s Kuali experience started in 2006, when the university’s Board of Governors acknowledged that CSU needed a new, integrated system for financial management. Early in 2007, the university began to consider its options

Surveying the marketplace, the CSU planners anticipated that the new system was likely to come with a substantial price tag – the starting price to acquire a commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) enterprise platform was estimated at between \$5 Million to \$7 Million. Perhaps more importantly, several of the systems on the market lacked the full functionality sought by CSU. Ultimately, the team identified Kuali as an opportunity to create a tailored platform at a substantially reduced price:

“In January 2007, our VP asked for a new financial system to be implemented within two years. By late 2007, we had looked at all the options and decided to deploy Kuali. We got the approval from the CIO without the need to get approval from VP of the University.” – CSU Kuali Deployment Team Leader

CSU invested around \$1 million, including cash and human resource commitments, to join Kuali as a development partner of KFS in 2007:

“CSU joined KFS development after they made the decision for implementation. They joined in the last stage of the development. The formal organization structure helped them to get into the team quickly and contribute to the development.” – KFS Functional Council Leader

With a team of approximately 100 members, the KFS development and implementation at CSU was completed in less than two years. During this process, the CSU team worked closely with their Kuali partner institutions to develop functionality to address the shared needs of the Kuali community. At the same time, the team collaborated with their own campus users to ensure that the KFS system was tailored to the specific requirements of the CSU’s environment.

“I cannot say enough about Kuali. We used to have problem in implementation. We called Kuali. Kuali sent the solution back in three hours and fixed the documents in two hours.”– CSU Kuali Deployment Team Leader

By the time the system was implemented in 2009, CSU stakeholders were overwhelmed by the outcome. They had developed a system which exceeded their functional requirements, despite a much lower cost than initially expected. The final cost of the system for CSU was under \$2 Million, less than half of the anticipated expense for a low-end COTS platform:

“We were able to implement a state-of-the-art infrastructure for a fraction of the cost of our peers through a partnership with the Kuali Foundation … Kuali is the wave of the future. Basically, we keep 50 sets of books. Now we can keep them all on one database.” CSU Vice President of Finance (http://www.kuali.org/join/stories/colostate)

“For the campus, the benefit of the new system is clear. It is web based, allowing all authorized users easy, online access. Documents in the system can be routed electronically for approvals - and then electronically stored, saving paper and staff time. Purchasing is integrated as well. It also supports multi-year budgeting - important for research contracts. All in all, it will make management of CSU’s finances easier, better and more secure.” CSU Vice President for Information Technology (http://www.kuali.org/join/stories colostate).
