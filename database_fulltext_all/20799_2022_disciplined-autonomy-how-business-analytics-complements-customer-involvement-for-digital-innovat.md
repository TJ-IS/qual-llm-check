## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: BA value realization Complementarity Business analytics culture Business analytics skills Customer involvement Digital innovation

## A B S T R A C T

The rise of big data and the fluid boundaries of digital products are driving companies to use business analytics (BA) to power their customer involvement. The complementarity view offers unique competence to generate value from BA because capability complementarity is less likely to be replicated or imitated. Unlike prior studies on BA-enabled value realization, our research in: vestigates the interactions of BA and customer involvement capabilities using the complemen tarity view. We tested our model using data collected from 317 IT companies in China. Our results suggest that BA value realization requires both a top-down mechanism in which BA skills provide global guidance for alignment with a company’s goals and a bottom-up mechanism in which BA culture empowers local autonomy for adaptation to ever-changing needs. Our BA-complemented mechanisms provide research and practice with a way to concurrently use BA and customer involvement capabilities to address the duality of digital innovation. We further suggest that BA skills are necessary but insufficient for digital innovation because BA culture demonstrates a stronger effect in complementing organizations’ existing capabilities than BA skills do.

## Introduction

Big data has disrupted the “traditional information value chain” (Abbasi et al., 2016, p. ii), offering companies opportunities to use it to create product and service innovations (Boˇziˇc and Dimovski, 2019; Sharma et al., 2014). Data-driven innovation requires building technical competence and changing organizational culture (Grover et al., 2018; Vidgen et al., 2017). These technical and cultura requirements are challenging as 95% of analytics-driven innovation projects fail (Andriole, 2021). Given the opportunities and challenges, companies must understand how to realize value from business analytics capabilities. i.e., the abilities to transform data into actional insights for decision making (Seddon et al., 2017; Sharma et al., 2014).

Prior research focuses on the role of business analytics (BA) capabilities in enabling organizational capabilities for competitive advantage or company performance (Mikalef et al., 2020b; Torres et al., 2018). Different from company performance in general, innovation requires cross-functional capabilities to interact “concurrently, rather than sequentially” (Teece, 2010, p. 704). Therefore, instead of focusing on the enabling role of BA capabilities, we should explore how BA capabilities concurrently interact with other functional capabilities for innovation. The interactive relationships between capabilities are underpinned by the notion of comple mentarity in dynamic capabilities theory, the core of profiting from innovation (Teece. 2018). Specifically, complementarity means that the value of one capability is enhanced by the presence of another capability (Teece, 2007; Zhu, 2004). Existing research, however, offers limited insights into how BA capabilities complement other functional capabilities for innovation, let alone digital innovation.

Compared to the fixed boundaries of traditional innovation, digital innovation has fluid boundaries with highly changeable user requirements and frequent product enhancement (Henfridsson et al., 2018; Vega and Chiasson, 2019). For example, a digital product allows its users to develop interpretations in a specific context, which generates contextualized requirements (Arvidsson and Mønsted, 2018). Digital technology can also be reprogrammed cheaply and quickly, which triggers fierce competition in the marketplace (Huang et al., 2017). To outperform the competition, companies should use their customer involvement capabilities to explore and reconfigure customers’ feedback and knowledge to enhance innovation (Nambisan et al., 2017). By definition, customer involvement capabilities refer to a company’s abilities to interact with customers to incorporate their requirements and knowledge for product innovation (Carbonell et al., 2009; Saldanha et al., 2017).

Effective customer involvement needs to ensure that digital innovation achieves both autonomy and discipline, a phenomenon known as disciplined autonomy (Saldanha et al., 2017). From the perspective of autonomy, digital innovation adapts to changing customer needs and technology. From the perspective of discipline, digital innovation follows companies' product development roadmap. Disciplined autonomy represents the duality of growing innovation, which means that dichotomized yet interrelated viewpoints coexist in an organization (Smith and Lewis, 2011; Vessey and Ward, 2013). Duality is vital in shaping innovation and the effective management of the tension in duality leads to sustainable innovation (O'Reilly and Tushman. 2008). To achieve sustainable performance, companies develop a “both/and approach” by simultaneously using complementary capabilities to address dichotomized views of the duality (Oueiroz et al.. 2020: Yeow et al.. 2018).

Our research aims to explicate the mechanisms through which BA complements customer involvement to achieve disciplined autonomy for innovation. Specifically, we address the research question: how and why do BA capabilities complement customer involvement capabilities for the high-level market performance of digital innovation? Drawing on the theoretical viewpoint of comple mentarity (Teece, 2010; Teece et al., 1997), we develop a model of BA-complemented customer involvement for digital innovation Because shaping digital innovation requires technical skills and data-driven culture (Chiang et al., 2018; Duan et al., 2020), we examine the complementary role of technical and cultural BA capabilities in customer involvement.

We tested our model using data collected from 317 companies in China that produce digital innovation. Our findings support our hypothesized moderating relationships. We theorize two mechanisms that underpin these relationships: first, a technology complemented mechanism, a top-down process in which BA skills guide customer involvement for digital innovation aligned with the company’s vision (Boˇziˇc and Dimovski, 2019; Saldanha et al., 2017); and second, a culture-complemented mechanism, a bottomup process in which BA culture empowers the decision-making autonomy of customer involvement for innovation (Kiron et al., 2014; Watson, 2016). Our findings further suggest that BA skills are necessary but insufficient for value realization because BA culture presents stronger complementing effects when interacting with customer involvement capabilities.

Our research offers three important contributions. First, it theorizes two BA-complemented mechanisms for business value reali zation. Although complementarity is the nature of innovating firms, few studies have used it to examine the process of realizing value from BA (Grover et al., 2018; Teece, 2018). Our findings demonstrate that technical and cultural BA capabilities complement orga nizations' existing capabilities via different mechanisms, i.e., top-down ys bottom-up. The bottom-up mechanism plays a stronger role than the top-down mechanism does. Second, our research explains the contextual conditions that augment companies’ customer involvement (Saldanha et al., 2017). Prior research shows mixed results of engaging with customers (Cui and Wu, 2016; Ittner and Larcker, 1997). We suggest that companies need to promote autonomy most of the time and use global insights to inform local in teractions when front-line employees need guidance. Third, our research addresses how to shape the duality of digital product innovation by using capability complementarity (Nambisan et al., 2017; Yeow et al., 2018). The complementarity of BA and customer involvement enables companies to align their innovation with companies’ goals as well as to adapt to external changes in the marketplace.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. First, we present the theoretical background of our research, followed by the hypotheses we develop based on our research model. We then present the research method, data analysis, and results. Lastly, we discuss our results and our contributions to theory and implications for practice, followed by opportunities for future research.

## Theoretical background

In this section, we explain dynamic capabilities theory by focusing on its complementarity viewpoint (Teece, 2007; 2010; Teece et al., 1997), and justify the suitability of this viewpoint as our theoretical foundation. We then review the literature stream of BA business value and customer involvement, and point out the issues that our research aims to address.

## Complementarity viewpoint in dynamic capabilities theory

Dynamic capabilities refer to organizational abilities that “integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external competencies to address rapidly changing environments” (Teece et al., 1997, p. 516). For business environments with volatile customer needs and emerging technology, dynamic capabilities enable companies to continuously shape and adapt to environmental changes (Li and Chan, 2019). To renew the competitive advantage in ever-changing environments, companies develop “difficult-to-imitate" capabilities to sense and address opportunities and challenges (Teece, 2007).

Developing distinctive, difficult-to-replicate capabilities draws on the concept of complementarity, which has been referred to as “the nature of innovating firms” (Teece, 2010, p. 722). Complementarity exists when the value of a capability is augmented by the concurrent use of another capability (Queiroz et al., 2020; Zhu, 2004). The concurrent deployment of capabilities has non-linear effects that are path-dependent and causal ambiguous (Lim et al., 2011). Therefore, capability complementarity equips companies with unique competence that cannot be easily replicated by competitors or be traded in the marketplace like other commoditized know-how (Teece, 2007).

Despite the potential of the complementarity viewpoint on business performance, “how” and “why” information systems (IS) capabilities interact with complementary organizational capabilities to create value is under-researched. For example, a review of IS business value suggests that “the levels of IS assets and complementarities are fictional and would need to be empirically analysed in future research” (Schryen, 2013, p. 158). A few studies have examined the complementarity of IS and organizational capabilities (e.g., marketing, e-commerce, supply chain capabilities) for financial performance (Song et al., 2005; Zhu, 2004) and manufacturing per formance (Bharadwai et al., 2007). These studies, however, focus on traditional IS capabilities and performance in general, with limited insights on how BA capabilities are used for digital innovation.

## Business value of business analytics

As the demand for generating business insights from big data grows, research increasingly focuses on creating business value through BA capabilities, which is evidenced by the conceptual discussion in the editorials of IS journals (e.g., Abbasi et al., 2016; Chiang et al., 2018; Mikalef et al., 2020a; Sharma et al., 2010) as well as empirical investigation (e.g., Boˇziˇc and Dimovski, 2019; Conboy et al., 2020; Mikalef et al., 2020b; Torres et al., 2018). We now summarize the key insights from existing literature and identify the aspects that require a deeper understanding to advance this research field.

First, most of the extant research adopts an enabling view to examine BA value realization. Enabling-based studies assume that BA capabilities are the antecedents that alter companies’ existing capabilities, which then improves their performance (Mikalef et al., 2020a). For example. business intelligence and analytics capabilities enable the capabilities of sensing and seizing that improve firm performance (Torres et al., 2018). BA capabilities lead to dynamic capabilities and marketing capabilities that provide competitive advantage (Mikalef et al., 2020b). Furthermore, the capabilities of using business intelligence and analytics enable the absorptive capacity to achieve innovation ambidexterity (Boˇziˇc and Dimovski, 2019).

The enabling view of BA capabilities is insufficient to capture the variation of innovation performance for two reasons. First. “if firms are to cut time-to-market for new products and processes, cross-functional interaction must take place concurrently, rather than sequentially” (Teece, 2010, p. 704). For example, instead of letting functional teams deliver tasks sequentially, companies can organize cross-functional teams that work together to launch new products. Second, the individual capability enhancement offered by the enabling view cannot address the duality of innovation. Innovation duality requires companies to use co-specialized capabilities to balance the dichotomized needs for innovative products (O’Reilly and Tushman, 2008; Yeow et al., 2018). Therefore, we need the complementarity viewpoint to research BA value realization for innovation.

Second, BA value realization is a socio-technical phenomenon where companies require technical and socio-organizational BA capabilities (Conboy et al., 2020; Vidgen et al., 2017). Compared to technical capabilities such as BA infrastructure (Torres et al., 2018) and BA tools (Božič and Dimoyski. 2019). extant research lacks insights into how the socio-organizational aspect of BA capabilities is used to realize business value. For example, researchers suggest that “the research conducted to date has tended to largely focus on analytics tools and not enough on the people and their institutional and social environments” (Conboy et al., 2020, p. 1).

Organizational environments are crucial for BA capabilities because companies must foster BA-focused activities and support datadriven insights to generate value from BA (Grover et al., 2018; Vidgen et al., 2017). To release the potential of BA capabilities for business value, companies need to move beyond the traditional decision making that is driven by the experiences or intuition of top management, and develop a data-driven culture that values and promotes fact-based decision making. BA culture has been referred to as the “lynchpin” that sets a company apart from its competitors especially when other capabilities are similar (Kiron et al., 2014). Therefore, in addition to technical BA capabilities, we examine how to realize value from BA culture.

## Customer involvement capabilities

Customer involvement capabilities enable companies to identify customers’ requirements and incorporate customer knowledge into product innovation (Carbonell et al., 2009; Saldanha et al., 2017). Reconfiguring customer knowledge is particularly important for digital innovation due to the unique features of digital technology such as interpretation flexibility and reprogrammability. Inter: pretive flexibility enables users to develop situated interpretations of digital products, which generates contextualized requirements (Arvidsson and Mønsted, 2018). Reprogrammability means that digital products can be reprogrammed at a low marginal cost, which shortens the product development cycle and triggers intense competition between a company and its competitors (Huang et al.. 2017). As a result, digital innovation requires companies to continuously recombine customer knowledge to address changing needs and emerging technology (Henfridsson et al.. 2018: Nambisan et al.. 2017)

Despite its importance, customer involvement is not always beneficial. Companies that immerse themselves in customer in teractions or overreact to customer requirements may lose the motivation to innovate or the control of their product roadmap (Gruner et al., 2014; Hoyer et al., 2010). These risks highlight the need to understand contingent conditions where companies effectively engage with customers for innovation (Gemser and Perks, 2015). Prior research suggests that technical capabilities moderate the relationship between customer involvement and innovation (Cui and Wu, 2016; Mahr et al., 2014). These studies, however, focus on how to better absorb customer knowledge for new products and offer limited insights into aligning customer involvement with business goals.

IS researchers have started to acknowledge the role of BA in shaping customer involvement. A few studies examine how BA helps companies better engage with customers. For example, researchers suggest that analytic tools complement companies’ IT infra structure to improve their capabilities of sensing customer needs (Roberts and Grover, 2012). Analytic information processing capability also complements customer involvement to enhance innovation (Saldanha et al., 2017). Both studies suggest comple mentary effects occur because companies use BA tools to synthesize internal and external information, which helps innovators use customer feedback to align innovation with the product development strategy. Similar to the BA value realization literature in general, the cultural aspect of BA that facilitates customer involvement receives little attention.

## Hypotheses development

Drawing on complementarity, our research assumes that BA capabilities enhance the effects of customer involvement capabilities on the market performance of digital innovation. In acknowledging the socio-technical nature of BA value realization, we examine the technical aspect of BA capabilities in BA skills and the socio-organizational aspect of BA capabilities in BA culture. ## BA skills complement customer involvement for digital innovation

Recall that customer involvement capabilities refer to a company’s abilities to interact with customers to incorporate their re quirements and knowledge for product innovation (Carbonell et al., 2009; Saldanha et al., 2017). Using customer involvement ca pabilities, companies consult with customers about their feedback and requirements, implement online tools to facilitate interactions, and engage with customers in product development meetings (Foss et al., 2011). Effective customer involvement improves perfor mance such as sales, profitability, and customer satisfaction (Lau et al., 2011; Story et al., 2015).

Interactions with customers generate a large amount of information and employees can be overwhelmed by responding to externa needs (Roberts and Grover, 2012). BA skills complement customer involvement capabilities by offering high-level guidance in making decisions that prioritize customer needs, resolve conflicts, and make sense of business opportunities and challenges for shaping digita innovation. Specifically, BA skills refer to a company’s abilities to apply data analytics techniques (e.g., social media analytics, text analytics, real-time analytics) to generate insights for their decision making (Kiron et al., 2014). Applying these insights, companies integrate the information from functional domains and external sources to develop a holistic and synthesized view to guide customer involvement in three ways (Boˇziˇc and Dimovski, 2019).

First, BA skills complement customer involvement by prioritizing tasks. Because customer involvement generates many re quirements, companies have the risk of passively reacting to various needs and losing control of their innovation development (Hoyer et al., 2010). When equipped with high-level BA skills, companies generate insights by identifying the patterns among different customer requests and analyzing the importance of customer needs against business vision and technology trends (Kitchens et al., 2018). Front-line employees use these insights to prioritize innovation opportunities. For example, customer involvement staff and business analysts set up metrics to evaluate customer requirements for enhancing products (Duncan et al., 2016). By doing so, a company not only efficiently addresses customer needs but also strategically manages the growth of digital innovation in alignment with the company goals.

Second. BA skills complement customer involvement by reconciling conflicting tasks. Due to its interpretive flexibility. digital innovation promotes contextualized requirements when the product is introduced to customers (Arvidsson and Mønsted, 2018). Contextualized requirements are not always consistent as users make specific requests for their individual needs. When requirements conflict, employees need guidance to reconcile the inconsistency (Svahn et al., 2017). Prior research has suggested that the analytics team and other business teams collaborate to deliver business goals (Krishnamoorthi and Mathew, 2018). These goals are guiding principles for employees to decide whether certain customer needs fit their innovation vision or not. For example, companies often organize cross-functional teams, including analyst and customer involvement staff, to collectively agree on the tasks to be completed to enhance products and the tasks to be dropped because of their misalignment with business vision (Kowalczyk and Buxmann, 2015).

Third, BA skills complement customer involvement by making sense of opportunities and challenges that emerge from the marketplace. The IT industry has high clock speed driven by changing customer requirements, emerging technology, and intense competition, which challenges customer involvement to address ever-changing needs (Mendelson and Pillai, 1999). Companies with high-level BA skills use analytics techniques to obtain insights from data in the marketplace, which augments their capabilities to engage with customers on innovation. For example, using predictive analytics, companies forecast trends in technology advances and customer preferences. These insights give employees the capabilities to proactively configure customer requests into the company’s product development roadmap (Someh et al., 2019). Furthermore, using real-time analytics, employees can better address customers’ on-demand requests to improve innovation in a timely manner (Naseer et al., 2021). Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. BA skills positively moderate the relationship between customer involvement capabilities and digital innovation performance

BA culture complements customer involvement for digital innovation

Organizational culture, in general, refers to a company’s ability to develop shared beliefs and values that manifest in organizational members’ behaviors (Leidner and Kayworth, 2006). BA culture specifically refers to a company’s abilities to value and promote analytical activities and data-driven decision making (Kiron et al., 2014). Data-driven culture has been referred to as “the culture that is needed to derive the full value from analytics” (Watson, 2016, p. 5). Enabling and improving digital innovation requires formulating opportunities to innovate in “an incremental and inductive manner” (Nambisan et al., 2017, p. 229). To foster the inductive process for innovation to emerge, companies need to develop an organizational culture that supports collaborative knowledge sharing and fact based decision making (Duan et al., 2020).

BA culture strengthens the effects of customer involvement capabilities on digital innovation performance for three reasons. First, BA culture complements customer involvement by promoting knowledge sharing among organizational members. Culture implies that employees hold and share value assumptions (Schein, 1996). BA culture specifically indicates the value of the collaborative use of data for decision making, which promotes collaboration across a company’s functional domains (Storm and Borgman, 2020). Compared with fragmented knowledge from functional silos, collective insights generated by analytics are more beneficial for interacting with customers because a shared understanding means that employees more sensibly address innovation opportunities (Grover et al., 2018). For example, facilitated by BA culture initiatives, healthcare company WellPoint built up its analytics team between IT and other business functions to deliver a new healthcare provider payment system (Kiron et al., 2014).

Second, BA culture complements customer involvement by shaping employees’ beliefs in using analytics insights for decision making. Traditional companies follow a top-down process where decisions are based on management’s intuition or experiences, which may limit employees’ abilities to interact with customers (Abbasi et al., 2016). On the contrary, data-driven insights offer a deeper and more comprehensive understanding of customers as well as more opportunities for innovation (Kitchens et al., 2018). In companies that establish a BA culture, employees support analytics activities and value data-driven insights (Krishnamoorthi and Mathew, 2018). Therefore, employees are more willing to use insights such as patterns of customer preferences and technological advances to make decisions, which not only meet customer requirements in the short term but also proactively influence customer preferences in the long term (Grover et al., 2018).

Third, BA culture complements customer involvement by giving front-line employees the autonomy to adapt to customer needs. Encouraged by a strong BA culture, employees have high-level freedom to make decisions based on the evidence they collect instead of being directed by top management (Watson, 2016). Furthermore, when the interactions are driven by the differences of knowledge rather than hierarchical rules, customers are more willing to share their experiences and ideas for improving digital innovation (Harmeling et al., 2017). For example, when there is a strong BA culture, customer involvement staff have more autonomy to respond to customers’ feedback or requirements because they are empowered to make decisions based on the facts they collect instead of going through hierarchical reporting in a formal organizational system (Cosic et al., 2015). Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2. BA culture positively moderates the relationship between customer involvement capabilities and digital innovation performance.

## Research method

We used a field survey design to test our hypotheses. In this section, we explain the measures for our constructs and validate our survey instrument. We then present our data collection procedures.

## Measurement development and conceptual validation

We developed our measurement items based on existing literature and used a 7-point Likert scale in our survey. We developed our instrument in English and then translated it into Chinese. We used back-translation to ensure conceptual equivalence between the English and Chinese versions (Hoskisson et al., 2000). Following suggestions from the academics who conducted the back-translation. we modified the wording of items in both the English and Chinese surveys. We pilot tested our survey before formal data collection (MacKenzie et al., 2011). Appendix A presents the definition and the operationalization of our constructs.

We used a two-round Q-sort method to verify the content validity of our measurements in both the English and Chinese versions (Moore and Benbasat, 1991: Petter et al., 2007). For the survey in English, we invited five PhD students and five faculty members to participate in the Q-sort task in each round. In the first round, we did not provide a label and a definition for a “target” construct. We asked each participant to sort 17 items into any number of categories and provide a label for a category using their own words. Al participants came up with four categories. The average placement ratio was 93% and the average Kappa score was 0.77, demonstrating adequate content validity based on recommended thresholds (Moore and Benbasat, 1991).

Based on the Q-sort results and participants’ comments, we revised the wording in two items for the construct BA culture and the wording in one item for the construct digital innovation performance. In the second round, we invited another 10 participants to sort the revised items. In this round, we specified the label and the definition of each of our four constructs. The average placement ratio was 95% and the average Kappa score was 0.79.

We also examined the content validity of the survey in Chinese using the same procedure as for the English survey. For the un labeled Q-sort, the placement ratio scores averaged 91% and Kappa scores averaged 0.80. For the labeled Q-sort, the placement ratio scores averaged 96% and Kappa scores averaged 0.83.

## Data collection

We distributed our survey to 850 companies randomly selected from the IT industry in China. We dropped 22 of the 339 complete questionairres due to missing data. As a result, we used 317 questionnaires for our data analysis, with a response rate of 37.3%. Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the sample companies. To assess non-response bias, we compared the first 80 respondents (25%) with the last 80 respondents (25%) on their company characteristics and on their responses to the survey questions. There were no statistical differences between these two groups. Non-response bias, therefore, is not a concern in our study (Armstrong and Overton, 1977).

Given that the organizational structure of our participating companies varies, our informants are a diverse group of professionals including CEOs, CTOs, IT managers, IT product managers, BA managers, and BA analysts. To be specific, some start-up companies have an informal organizational structure consisting of a few co-founders, so their executive leaders are the informants of our survey. Some companies do not have an independent BA department, so IT or IT product managers are identified as the suitable informant to participate in our survey. Despite the difference in job titles, in the cover letter of our survey, we required an informant to be knowledgeable of the analytics and the innovation at their company. On average, our informants have worked at the same company for 8.6 years. Because using a single respondent from a company may lead to the issue of common method variance (CMV), we assess the potential for CMV in Appendix B. Our assessment results show that CMV does not affect our results. Furthermore, our findings are less likely to be threatened by CMV because the purpose of our research is to examine interaction effects (Siemsen et al., 2010).

## Complementarity analysis

We examine the complementing effects of BA skills and BA culture on customer involvement using complementarity analysis. Complementarity exists when increasing one variable from its lower level to a higher level is more beneficial when the other variable is at the higher level than its lower level (Porter and Siggelkow, 2008; Queiroz et al., 2020). Figs. 2 and 3 present our complementarity analysis results. Fig. 2 shows that when increasing the variable customer involvement capabilities from low to high level, the increase of digital innovation performance (DIP) is larger when BA skills are at the high level (increase in DIP: 35.2%) than when BA skills are at the low level (increase in DIP: 23.4%), thereby supporting the complementarity of BA skills and customer involvement capabilities.

Similarly, Fig. 3 shows that when customer involvement capabilities increase, the increase of innovation performance is larger when BA culture is at the high level (increase in DIP: 61.9%) than when it is at the low level (increase in DIP: 23.4%). Our comple mentarity analysis further suggests that BA culture has stronger complementing effects because the increase of innovation performance is larger when BA culture is at the high level, compared to the increase when BA skills are at the high level.

## Discussion and implications

In this section, we discuss our findings. We then present our contributions to theory, followed by our implications for practice. Finally, we discuss the opportunities for future research.

## Discussion of findings

The objective of our research is to understand how and why business analytics capabilities complement customer involvement capabilities to achieve disciplined autonomy for digital innovation. The moderating relationships supported by our empirical findings address the how question. To address the why question, we explicate two mechanisms that underpin the moderating relationships. By mechanism, we mean a causal process that explains the occurrence of empirical events (Mingers and Standing, 2017).

First, BA skills-complemented customer involvement is underpinned by a top-down mechanism in which analytical skills guide decision making for customer involvement by integrating internal and external information. Companies’ customer involvement often suffers from fragmented data. For example, a company implementing BA initiatives for their customer engagement reported that “the biggest challenge was the fact that no one paid attention to what happens with the data and what has emerged over the last five, six, seven years of operational systems. There existed very few individual examples with both a clean logic and a clean data model and an appropriate link with other systems” (Dremel et al., 2020, p. 7). The holistic insights generated by BA skills provide employees and managers with guiding principles to prioritize tasks, resolve conflicts, and make sense of opportunities and challenges for digita innovation (Boˇziˇc and Dimovski, 2019; Saldanha et al., 2017)

Second, BA culture-complemented customer involvement happens because of a bottom-up mechanism in which BA culture em powers front-line employees with autonomy to make fact-based decisions. Autonomous interactions are driven by the differences in employees’ and customers’ knowledge and capabilities, rather than by formal management (Vessey and Ward, 2013). Forma authority or hierarchical structure tends to stifle employees’ freedom to adapt their behaviors to respond to customers’ requests or feedback (Krishnamoorthi and Mathew. 2018). In contrast. fact-based decision making encourages spontaneous behaviors to address novel or surprising customer needs (Watson, 2016). Furthermore, when strong BA culture is present, innovative ideas are more likely to emerge because employees are open to learning-by-doing and are willing to share knowledge (Storm and Borgman, 2020).

Our top-down and bottom-up mechanisms shape digital innovation in different ways. The top-down mechanism, facilitated by technical BA capabilities, aligns digital innovation with a company’s vision. The bottom-up mechanism, facilitated by cultural BA capabilities, enables digital innovation to adapt to external changes. Our results of hypotheses testing and complementarity analysis suggest that BA culture plays a stronger role in complementing customer involvement capabilities for digital innovation. Our results also suggest that BA skills can directly improve digital innovation performance $( \beta = 0 . 2 2 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , whereas BA culture cannot (β = $- 0 . 0 6 8 , p > 0 . 0 5 )$ . Taken together, the empirical evidence demonstrates that superior technical skills are necessary but insufficient for innovation (Watson, 2016). Although BA culture does not realize value on its own, it is “a secret source for creating business value with analytics” (Kiron et al., 2014, p. 9) because it has strong complementing effects on companies’ existing capabilities.

We further examine the interaction effects of BA skills and BA culture. and the interaction effects among BA skills. BA culture. and customer involvement capabilities on digital innovation performance. Our results $( \mathrm { B A S } \times \mathrm { B A C } \colon \beta = - 0 . 1 0 7 , p < 0 . 0 5 ; \mathrm { B A S } \times \mathrm { B A C } \times \mathrm { C I C } ;$ $\beta = - 0 . 0 4 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ show that these interactions negatively influence the performance of digital innovation. The negative effects suggest that, when both mechanisms are strong, they may conflict with each other in influencing customer involvement activities because employees tend to avoid central control when their local autonomy is strong (Queiroz et al., 2020). Given that BA culture has stronger complementing effects, instead of letting top-down control play a major role in influencing customer involvement, companies need to encourage bottom-up autonomy most of the time and use global insights to assist local interactions when employees need guidance.

## Theoretical contributions

Our research advances the existing knowledge of BA value realization, customer involvement, and digital product innovation. First. our research contributes to BA value realization by theoretically and empirically investigating the complementary use of BA and by theorizing two mechanisms of realizing BA value. The complementarity view offers greater potential for business value realization because capability complementarity is less likely to be imitated or replicated, but extant research focuses on BA as an antecedent that enables other capabilities (Chiang et al., 2018; Mikalef et al., 2020a). We explicate two mechanisms for BA complementarity. Spe cifically, the top-down mechanism explains that BA value realization requires global guidance facilitated by BA skills, while the bottom-up mechanism explains that BA value realization requires local autonomy empowered by BA culture. Our results further suggest that BA culture is the secret source of generating value because BA culture does not directly improve performance on its own, but it is stronger in complimenting a company’s existing capabilities. Both the bottom-up and top-down mechanisms address the dearth of understanding on how the socio-organizational and technical aspects of BA create value (Dremel et al., 2020)

Second, our work contributes to the research on customer involvement by explicating the contextual conditions that augment the effects of customer involvement capabilities on innovation. Customer involvement is not always beneficial because emphasizing customer requirements or feedback may make companies overly driven by customers or less likely to take initiatives to innovate (Cu and Wu. 2016: Ittner and Larcker. 1997). The recent development of the IS literature demonstrates that technical BA capabilities moderate the relationship between customer involvement and innovation because companies are more informed when they have datadriven insights (Saldanha et al., 2017). Our research advances this stream of literature by examining the moderating role of cultural BA capabilities. We suggest that BA culture plays a stronger moderating role than BA skills $\begin{array} { r } { \mathsf { d o } , } \end{array}$ corroborating the insights from the industry that “a strong analytics culture is the lynchpin in moving from competitive parity to competitive advantage” (Kiron et al., 2014, p. 10).

Third, our work contributes to digital innovation research by addressing how to shape the duality of digital product innovation. The literature on digital innovation is dominated by technology adoption because the early stage of the IS field focused on the efficiency of operations and decision making (Yoo et al., 2010). Given the profound impact of digital products on our society, researchers have started to examine how to shape digital products in the marketplace. However, we still lack empirical studies (Kohli and Melville, 2019). Our research suggests that superior market performance of digital products lies in the duality view (Saldanha et al., 2017; Vessey and Ward, 2013). The complementarity of BA and customer involvement capabilities addresses the duality of digital innovation from a disciplined autonomy perspective. Specifically, BA skills help employees make informed decisions that align innovation with company goals. BA culture empowers employees to autonomously make data-driven decisions for innovation to adapt to externa changes.

## Practical implications

Our research offers implications for practitioners. First, companies need to encourage collaboration between the BA and customer involvement functions to enhance digital innovation. Analytics-based innovation is more likely to happen if BA and other functional employees frequently interact with each other. To facilitate such interactions, leaders must establish and nurture cross-functional teams to engage with customers. Benefiting from the collaboration, companies can effectively respond to customer requirements and also proactively influence customer preferences for their product development vision.

Second, compared with strengthening technical skills, companies need to focus more on explicating organizational values that support and promote data-driven decision making to establish a BA culture. Unlike BA skills that can be gained by hiring analytics professionals, BA culture needs to be developed internally rather than acquired externally. Small or start-up companies may easily develop a data-driven culture because their organizational structure is less rigid and hierarchical. Established companies, however, may find it challenging to promote a data-driven culture because they often have a traditional, hierarchical structure. Therefore, established companies need to place more emphasis on fostering a BA culture by encouraging shared beliefs to promote and value decision making based on analytics insights.

Third, companies need to develop an organizational mindset to balance the needs of responding to customer needs and developing their own vision of product development. The balanced view is important to ensure that digital innovation achieves short-term competitive advantage and long-term viability. Innovators often face the dilemma of balancing the flexibility of coping with chang ing needs in the marketplace and the stability of legitimizing a vision for growing innovation. To address the dilemma, leaders should empower front-line employees to make data-driven decisions and provide high-level principles for them to follow.

## Limitations and future research

Our research has several limitations that offer opportunities for future research. First, our research suffers from the issue of respondent bias and perceptual scales because we collected cross-sectional data using single informants in the IT industry. We encourage future research to collect longitudinal data from various industries. By doing so, future research may extend our research findings by examining changes in BA and customer involvement capabilities over time. Furthermore, the complementary role of BA skills and BA culture may vary across IT and non-IT industries.

Second, we used only the survey method to test our model empirically. The survey method allows us to establish the statistical power and provides opportunities to generalize our findings to a large population. We encourage future research to adopt other research methods such as the case study method to validate or advance our research findings. For example. future research can use the case study method to extend our variance model to a process model. By doing so, researchers can explore how to coordinate the proposed top-down and bottom-up mechanisms to develop and enhance digital innovation over time.

Third, we focused only on the customer involvement capabilities that we consider critical for interacting with BA capabilities to shape digital innovation. Future research could examine other organizational capabilities in a different context. We expect our BA complemented value realization mechanisms to be generalizable across contexts. For example, researchers may apply our mechanisms to examine the interaction of BA and supply chain capabilities for manufacturing performance, which will provide insights into how BA facilitates supply chain management activities.

## Conclusion

Capability complementarity offers unique competence to address the duality of innovation, but the process of using BA and its complementary capabilities to realize business value remains elusive. Our study provides researchers and practitioners with a path to moving from BA-enabled value realization to BA-complemented value realization. Our findings demonstrate that BA skills complement organizational capabilities via a top-down process, whereas BA culture complements organizational capabilities via a bottom-up process. When both capabilities are strong, their effects may conflict with each other. Because BA culture has stronger com plementing effects, managers need to empower employees’ autonomy most of the time and provide high-level guidance when they need it. Managers also need to value and foster BA culture in their company because a data-driven culture cannot be bought externally but developed internally. We believe that our BA-complemented implications are important for organizations to shape digital inno vation using both the socio-organizational and technical aspects of BA.

## Appendix B. The potential for common method variance

To mitigate common method variance (CMV), we reduced informants’ evaluation apprehension and minimized their social desirability bias by allowing them to anonymously answer our survey questions (Podsakoff et al., 2012). To assess the presence of CMV, we performed two tests (i.e., Harman’s single-factor test, marker variable assessment)<sup>1</sup>. First, we conducted Harman’s singlefactor test by entering all constructs into a factor analysis using principal component analysis method (Podsakoff et al., 2003). The test result showed that the single factor accounted for 43.2% of the cumulative variance. Therefore, CMV may not be a major concern in our research.

Second, we applied Lindell and Whitney’s (2001) marker variable assessment using “job tenure” (i.e., the number of years that an informant has been in their position) as our marker variable. The correlation between the marker variable and other variables was insignificant. After removing the marker variable, all significant correlations among our key independent and dependent variables remained significant. Overall, the results of our tests suggest that CMV does not affect our research findings.
