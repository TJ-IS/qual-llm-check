# Strategic roles of IT modernization and cloud migration in reducing cybersecurity risks of organizations: The case of U.S. federal government

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Security risks Legacy IT systems IT modernization Migration to the cloud U.S. federal government

## A B S T R A C T

Many organizations run their core business operations on decades-old legacy IT systems. Some security professionals argue that legacy IT systems significantly increase security risks because they are not designed to address contemporary cybersecurity risks. Others counter that the legacy systems might be “secure by antiquity” and argue that due to lack of adequate documentation on the systems, it is very difficult for potential attackers to discover and exploit security vulnera bilities. There is a shortage of empirical evidence on either argument. Routine activity theory (RAT) argues that an organization’s guardianship is critical for reducing security incidents. However, RAT does not well explain how organizations might guard against security risks of legacy IT systems. We theorize that organizations can enhance their guardianship by either modernizing their legacy IT systems in-house or by outsourcing them to cloud vendors. With datasets from the U.S. federal agencies, we find that agencies that have more legacy IT systems experience more frequent security incidents than others with more modern IT systems. A 1%- point increase in the proportion of IT budgets spent on IT modernization is associated with a 5.6% decrease in the number of security incidents. Furthermore, migration of the legacy systems to the cloud is negatively associated with the number of security incidents. The findings advance the literature on strategic information systems by extending RAT to explain why the “security by antiquity” argument is not valid and how organizations can reduce the security risks of legacy IT systems through modernization and migration to the cloud.

## Introduction

Information technology (IT) systems are strategic assets that reflect key business practices of organizations (Comella-Dorda et al. 2000). As business and technology environments evolve, organizations must co-evolve by modernizing their IT systems to reflect contemporary business practices. However, it is expensive for the organizations to modernize their existing IT systems, and eventually, some of the strategic, mission-critical IT systems become legacy systems that are too fragile to change but also too important to discontinue (Comella-Dorda et al. 2000; Furneaux and Wade, 2011, 2017). These legacy systems can negatively affect the organi zations' agility. flexibility, and innovativeness (Nagi, et al. 2011: Ravichandran. 2018). They can also increase the risks of operational

IT disruptions (Tae et al., 2020).

Beyond such deficiencies in legacy systems, this study asks: How does an organization’s reliance on legacy IT systems affect security incidents in the organization? Does migration of the legacy IT systems to the cloud reduce security incidents? As summarized in Table 1, prior studies on information security have not yet tackled these questions, despite their relevance and importance for both public- and private-sectors. We seek to provide theoretically grounded and empirically validated answers to these questions. Orga nizations in a range of industries such as banking, transportation, or healthcare rely on legacy systems (ComputerWeekly, 2019; Healthcare IT News, 2017; Tae et al., 2020; Tozzi, 2017), which carry a large amount of sensitive information and power critical business processes. Data from Privacy Rights Clearinghouse (PRC) shows that security breaches are occurring against organizations with increasing frequency, magnitude, and sophistication (Kolkowska et al, 2017). Given that information is one of the most valuable strategic resources in organizations and security breaches could cause substantial financial loss, it is paramount for the organizations to protect their information assets from growing cybersecurity threats (Hu et al, 2007; Hedstrom, ¨ et al. 2011).

Among such organizations, we choose the U.S. federal government as the context of our empirical inquiry. Many federal agencies rely heavily on decades-old legacy systems and are struggling to modernize these systems (Pang, 2017). Moreover, the number of cybersecurity incidents in the federal government has risen from 5500 in 2006 to more than 35,000 in 2017 (U.S. Senate, 2019), exposing federal agencies to a variety of grave risks to national security. For example, in November 2018, the U.S. Postal Service discovered a vulnerability that exposed account information of more than 60 million users (Nextgov, 2018). In June 2015, the U.S. Office of Personnel Management (OPM) announced that attackers abroad breached its federal human resources (HR) database and stole sensitive information on 22 million people. The stolen information included top-secret security clearance and background checks of federal employees, contractors, and their family members (The Washington Post, 2015). A subsequent investigation by Congress found that a 30-year-old mainframe system written in COBOL for the personnel database was too technically obsolete to encrypt the sensitive information (Nextgov, 2015).

Information security professionals have conflicting arguments as to whether legacy systems are to blame for such security incidents. On the one hand, there is a growing concern among some federal IT officials that agencies rely on too many decades-old legacy systems, most of which were not originally designed for security (U.S. Government Accountability Office, 2019). They argue that the legacy systems are too vulnerable to contemporary cybersecurity threats. On the other hand, other industry practitioners and government officials point out that the legacy systems may be more “secure by antiquity.” This is because many legacy systems are black-boxed IT systems on which there is little or no documentation. New generations of attackers have too little knowledge on such antiquated systems to discover and exploit their potential vulnerabilities (FCW, 2013). We build on routine activity theory (RAT) to frame the link between legacy IT systems and cybersecurity incidents and the mechanisms by which organizations can guard against cybersecurity risks to the legacy systems. We argue that the “security by antiquity” argument is not valid and that the organizations can reduce security risks of legacy IT systems through IT modernization or the migration of legacy systems to the cloud.

We test the proposed theory with a dataset from 24 federal agencies at the top of the federal hierarchy that are responsible for dayto-day administration of the U.S. federal government (e.g., Department of Homeland Security, Department of Energy, and Department of Transportation).<sup>1</sup> We obtain data on security incidents at these federal agencies from the Federal Information Security Management Act (FIMSA) Annual Reports to Congress in 2012–2015 (Office of Management and Budget. 2016). We use the Federal IT Dashboard to obtain data on federal IT investments. We find that federal agencies that spend a larger proportion of their IT budgets spent on IT modernization experience significantly fewer security incidents. Specifically, a 1%-point increase in the share of IT modernization in IT budgets is associated with a 5.6% decrease in security incidents. Furthermore, federal agencies that migrate more IT systems to the cloud experience substantially fewer security incidents.

## Theoretical background

## Related literature

In their research curation, Hui et al. (2016) categorize prior IS research on information security into four themes: (1) security investments. (2) risk management. (3) behavioral compliance. and (4) market effects of security. A recent review of the cybersecurity literature also uses similar categorizations (Walton et al., 2021). Our study relates primarily to the first theme of Hui et al. (2016) by examining how IT investments (IT modernization, migration to the cloud) affect cybersecurity incidents (e.g., Angst et al., 2017; Kwon and Johnson, 2014).

To identify a gap in this theme, we review relevant quantitative empirical studies for the antecedents for security breaches. Table summarizes the related studies. Among these studies, ours falls into the category of research that evaluates the effectiveness of IT and security investments in preventing security incidents. Our reading of this stream of research suggests that prior studies do not adequately address how legacy systems affect cybersecurity incidents and how organizations can reduce the security risks of legac systems. For example, Kwon and Johnson (2014) examine how security investments affect data breaches in the healthcare sector. They find that proactive security investments prior to the discovery of a security incident are more effective than reactive security in vestments made in reaction to an incident. Angst et al. (2017) differentiate symbolic and substantive security investments in the healthcare industry and find that substantive investments by hospitals are able to deter security breaches, while symbolic investments are not. Sen and Borle (2015) find that, counterintuitively, greater investments in information security are associated with a higher risk of breaches. Li et al. (2019) investigate the effectiveness of IT security strategic posture, measured by the extent to which a firm’s IT security investments deviate from an industry norm.

Our study differs from the previous studies in Table 1 in three ways. First, we focus on the cybersecurity implications of general IT investments (which would subsume IT security investments). Second, we theorize the link between legacy IT systems and cyberse curity incidents to resolve the debate in practice on whether the legacy systems are more secure by antiquity. Third, we extend the guardianship dimension of RAT to theorize how organizations can guard against cybersecurity risks of legacy IT systems by modernizing legacy IT systems or migrating them to more capable guardians in the cloud.

## Routine activity theory

Prior IS literature has drawn upon RAT to frame organizational conditions under which cybersecurity incidents can occur (Hughes and Cybenko. 2014: Wang et al., 2015: Liao et al., 2017: Wang et al., 2019: Luo et al., 2021). RAT explains that opportunities for information security incidents emerge in an organization if the three following conditions are met simultaneously: (i) there is a supply of likely offenders; (ii) there is a suitable target that is valuable, visible, and accessible; and (iii) there is lack of effective guardianship that protects the suitable target (Cohen and Felson. 1979: Felson and Clarke. 1998). Likely offenders who are motivated to commit crimes look for potential suitable targets that are valuable, accessible and visible to them Capable guardians can prevent the offenders from approaching and harming the targets. A crime is unlikely to occur when any one of the three conditions is not met. A motivated offender would not be able to commit a crime if a potential victim is unidentifiable, inaccessible, or not of value, and the offender must avoid deterrence by the capable guardian. Prior IS studies use this framework to explain digital crimes. For example, Wang et al. (2015) explain that an IT application experiences more frequent unauthorized access attempts if it has higher target value, higher visibility, and higher accessibility and contains information that has lower inertia.

We build on RAT in theorizing how legacy systems affect how the three conditions converge to create opportunities for cyberse curity incidents. Likely offenders could be external attackers or malicious insiders who have a motivation to gain unauthorized access to federal IT systems. Offenders also can be unsuspecting insiders who make mistakes or errors to unintentionally disclose sensitive information to unauthorized parties. Targets are IT systems that manage critical information of federal agencies. Legacy systems can have security vulnerabilities due to their lack of native security features. Offenders can view such legacy systems as suitable targets, as they contain valuable information with visibility and accessibility. Regarding guardianship, RAT-based studies assume that a foca organization, such a federal agency, is the guardian of the sensitive information. They argue that the agency can enhance its guardianship by investing in technologies for security protection (e.g., firewalls, intrusion detection systems, virus and malware detection software) (Wang et al., 2015; Liao et al., 2017; Luo et al., 2021). However, RAT does not consider whether such guardianship becomes ineffective over time if the agency fails to make necessary investments regularly (Hughes and Cybenko, 2014). We do not yet know if the recommended routine protections enhance the guardianship of modern IT systems only or the legacy IT systems as well.

This presents an opportunity to not just apply RAT but to also extend it in the context of legacy IT systems. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, RAT-based studies have not yet theorized how external governance of IT systems affects the guardianship of sensitive information. This question presents an additional opportunity to extend the guardianship dimension of RAT to inform practice with respect to whether migration of legacy systems to the cloud reduces or increases security risks.

## Hypothesis development

The effect of legacy systems on security incidents

## Arguments for security-by-antiquity

Whether legacy IT systems are more or less secure than modern systems has been a subject of enduring debate in practice. Some professionals argue that legacy systems were not designed for security, and hence, they have more security vulnerabilities. Others counter this argument by coining the term “security-by-antiquity,” in which the legacy systems are too antiquated to have any documentation and there are few or no human experts left who understand the internal workings of the systems. Thus, a likely offender who is motivated to breach the legacy systems may not be able to decipher how the systems work and what kinds of security vul nerabilities they may have. For example, Rep. Darrell Issa, former Chair of the Oversight and Government Reform Committee in the U. S. House of Representatives, argued that systems written in COBOL are “pretty much hack-proof” and “most hackers are not even old enough to understand the language” (FCW, 2013). Many IT practitioners express similar opinions.<sup>2</sup>

The proponents of “security-by-antiquity” use three major arguments to justify why legacy systems might be more secure than modern systems. First, the accessibility of legacy systems is more limited than that of modern systems. Decades old systems were not originally designed to provide access points to many users. Thus, in terms of RAT, accessibility to the legacy systems is low (Hughes and Cybenko, 2014). Second, legacy systems are often undocumented or poorly documented. How they were originally designed and developed and how they were subsequently updated and maintained remains obscure to likely offenders, making them less visible to the offenders. Third, most tools used by the original developers of the legacy systems have become obsolete and discontinued (e.g., operating systems, programming languages, and hardware standards). There is also lack of adequate documentation on those tools. Potential offenders are likely to face high costs if they want to learn about the underlying technologies, standards, and tools of the legacy systems. Thus, the threat capabilities of the likely offenders are limited for the legacy systems (Hughes and Cybenko, 2014).

## Arguments against security-by-antiquity

We however argue that legacy systems are likely to be less secure than modern systems.

Target value and likely offenders: Legacy systems have accumulated a large amount of valuable information over the years, a fact that makes them too valuable for an organization to discontinue (Comella-Dorda et al. 2000). As RAT argues, such valuable infor mation makes the legacy systems valuable targets for likely offenders (Ransbotham and Mitra, 2009). For example, the U.S. Internal Revenue Service (IRS) still maintains the Individual Master File, which was developed more than 50 years ago with assembly language code. This file is still used in the processing of income tax filings and refunds of taxpayers every year (Nextgov, 2016; FCW, 2015), which is one reason why it is a frequent target of attackers (Associated Press, 2015). The large amount of valuable information in legacy systems attracts motivated offenders, both inside and outside an organization.

Target visibility: From an enterprise IT architecture perspective, organizations which rely on many legacy systems often have complex architectures sometimes described as “spaghetti” systems (Ross et al., 2006). Various legacy systems that have been accu mulated through mergers, acquisitions, and internal restructurings are connected to each other in an ad hoc manner. Without sufficient standardization and integration, the legacy systems are loosely connected with each other and to other modern systems. Such complex enterprise architectures are more suitable targets for likely offenders. It is well recognized in the literature that connection and transition points between different systems are potential sources of vulnerabilities (Fisher, 1984; Oladimeji et al., 2011). Baskerville et al. (2018) argue that security vulnerabilities emerge at “junctions where data cross over boundaries into, out of, and between systems” (p. 4). Due to compatibility and interoperability problems, the legacy systems often communicate with each other or with modern systems through connectors and converters. When information flows through many such points, attackers have more op portunities to intercept and exploit these flows. In terms of RAT, these points increase visibility to likely offenders. On the other hand, more modern and integrated systems have fewer transition points and thereby fewer visible vulnerabilities.

Target accessibility: Legacy systems are not designed to handle complex identity and access management controls. In the past era ot mainframes, the assumption was that there was a limited number of users, who were located within a secure, trusted perimeter of an organization. In modern organizations, however, tens of thousands of geographically dispersed employees, contractors, customers, and business partners need access to the systems. Thus, the organizations need capabilities to authenticate the identities of numerous users and to segregate their potentially conflicting access privileges. Many legacy systems are not designed to address such complex access control requirements of the present era, thereby increasing accessibility to them (Hughes and Cybenko, 2014). It is expensive or infeasible to upgrade the legacy systems to accommodate emerging new security control requirements that address vulnerabilities in identity and access controls (August and Tunca, 2006, 2008). As RAT argues, such vulnerabilities in access controls could make the legacy systems suitable targets for attackers.

In addition, the longer a legacy IT system is maintained, revised, and updated, the more complex it becomes, and the more defects and vulnerabilities it is likely to develop. The IS literature on software development and maintenance indicates that software complexity is negatively associated with quality. Banker and Slaughter (2000) find that software that is more frequently updated develops more defects. Harter et al. (2000) also find that software complexity increases defect density. With each change, the legacy system becomes more complex due to new requirements, decision paths, rules, logics, and calculations (Harter et al., 2012). As noted, maintenance activities on the decades-old legacy system are often not documented well. In general, change management in operational IT systems is a source of risk as it can accidentally introduce errors and vulnerabilities. If the changes made to legacy IT systems are not documented, the errors and vulnerabilities they introduce can remain undetected and provide more access points to likely offenders

A security incident is more likely to occur when software has more defects and is error-prone. For example, defects in identity and access control components create security holes into the entire network of systems. Likely offenders take advantage of such holes Hence, complexity and entropy accumulated throughout modifications to the legacy systems make them more defective, leading to higher accessibility (Hughes and Cybenko, 2014; Mitra and Ransbotham, 2015). It might be costly for software vendors to provide patches for vulnerabilities in old legacy systems (Arora et al., 2008, 2010), even when the vendors still exist (Ransbotham et al., 2012). The software development lifecycle literature argues that a structured development process that takes into account security consid erations significantly reduces software defects and security vulnerabilities (Krishnan and Kellner, 1999: Banker and Slaughter, 2000; Harter et al., 2000, 2012; Krishnan et al. 2000). However, many legacy systems were developed decades ago before these method ologies became available.

Capable guardians: There is a lack of capable guardians who can protect legacy systems from attackers. Along with the absence of adequate documentation, there is a shortage of experts who have tacit knowledge of the internal details of the legacy systems, making it difficult and costly to institute modern security governance and control mechanisms to protect them (Ransbotham and Mitra, 2009). The professionals who originally designed the legacy systems have been retiring (CIO, 2016). Thus, federal agencies have been losing the tacit knowledge of these professionals on the legacy systems. The loss of tacit knowledge, combined with the lack of explicit knowledge in documentation, can weaken the federal agencies’ guardianship of these legacy systems. Some IT managers seek to build “workaround” security protections around the legacy systems. However, such workarounds sometimes exacerbate the complexity of the overall security infrastructures and create further security vulnerabilities of their own

Decades-old legacy systems are unlikely to have been designed for strong security protection. The security awareness of both developers and users was low or absent decades ago. Even if the legacy systems were originally designed for security, their security standards and solutions are unlikely to have adapted to the dynamically changing security threat landscape. For example, as of 2010, American Express’ website could handle a maximum password length of only up to eight characters. Moreover, it was not able to allow the use of special characters in passwords.<sup>3</sup> Many experts believed that such limitations were due to the technical shortcomings in the authentication function of old mainframe systems,<sup>4</sup> As RAT argues, the lack of such routine protections weakens guardianship of sensitive information and makes legacy systems targets for attackers.

In summary, our criticism of the “security-by-antiquity” argument covers all three conditions of RAT to justify why legacy systems are less secure than modern systems. According to RAT, there is a sufficient supply of likely offenders, inside and outside an orga nization, who are motivated to target the legacy systems because these systems manage a wealth of sensitive information accumulated over decades (value). Moreover, the legacy systems are suitable targets because they are not designed for security to start with and they have many security vulnerabilities that provide opportunities for unauthorized access (accessibility, visibility). Finally, the guard ianship of legacy systems is not effective due to the design limitations of the legacy systems and lack of explicit and tacit knowledge on their internal workings. Thus, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 1. Organizations that have a larger stock of legacy systems are likely to experience more security incidents

The effect of migrating IT systems to the cloud on security incident

Having applied RAT to the in-house governance of legacy systems, we now extend RAT to external governance in order to theorize whether the migration of legacy systems to the cloud would increase or decrease security incidents.

Capable “external” guardian: Cloud vendors have more resources and capabilities to build effective guardianship of valuable infor mation than other organizations such as federal agencies. As the IS outsourcing literature finds (Grover et al., 1996; Ang and Straub 1998: Levina and Ross. 2003), outsourcing vendors use common IT platforms to achieve economies of scale and scope in the production and delivery of IT services to a large number of client organizations. Thanks to economies of scale and scope, it is economically more feasible for the vendors to use dedicated information security teams to protect the clients’ systems over the common IT platforms. By comparison, a client organization is unlikely to have adequate resources to afford even a fraction of the dedicated cybersecurity team of the vendors, In addition, the vendors are better able to attract, motivate, promote and retain top security talent, which is necessary as the security threat landscape dynamically evolves. In comparison, the in-house legacy system environment of a client organization is unlikely to offer attractive and sustainable career paths for security professionals who look for opportunities to continuously develop and advance their professional skills and knowledge. In the legacy environments, IT professionals spend most of their careers in maintaining and operating specific legacy systems and have fewer opportunities to develop skills for emerging new technologies. For example, according to the Office of Personnel Management (OPM), as of 2015, almost 40% of the federal IT workers have been in the government for more than 20 years. Their skills and knowledge in modern IT systems are likely to be limited. The cloud vendors have more modern IT en vironments and offer more opportunities and incentives to the highly-skilled cybersecurity force (Hui et al., 2013)

Target accessibility: Migration of legacy systems to the cloud requires standardization of IT interfaces in the client organization, which can in turn make it easier for the cloud vendors to effectively guard information flows at the access and interaction points around the enterprise architectures. To be able to connect to the cloud and make use of its common standardized IT services and interfaces. a client organization needs to adhere to the standards mandated by the vendors. Thus, migrating legacy systems to the cloud often requires the standardization of the IT interfaces in the client’s enterprise architectures (Tanriverdi, et al., 2007). As we discussed above, such standardized IT systems have fewer access points for likely offenders than unstructured legacy systems. The highly standardized interfaces with the client make it easier and less costly for the cloud vendor to apply common security governance and control mechanisms to guard the sensitive information exchanged through those interfaces.

The foregoing development extends the guardianship dimension of RAT to include not just in-house guardianship but also guardianship by third-party outsourcing vendors. It provides plausible reasons that migration to the cloud decreases security risks in an organization. A cloud vendor is resourceful and capable in building and continuous updating its “effective guardianship.” Migration to the cloud entails modernization and standardization of the legacy systems, decreasing target accessibility. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2. Organizations that migrate their IT systems to the cloud are likely to experience fewer security incidents.

## Empirical methods

## Data and measures

Our unit of analysis is a U.S. federal agency. The sample covers 24 large federal agencies that are under the purview of the U.S. Office of Management and Budget (OMB) by the Chief Financial Officers (CFO) Act (Table 2). These “CFO Act” agencies include al cabinet departments such as the Department of Defense and the Department of Justice as well as several independent agencies including the National Science Foundation and the Social Security Administration.

## Dependent variable: Security incidents

From the FISMA Reports to Congress, we find the annual number of security incidents in the 24 federal agencies in Fiscal Years (FY) 2012–2015.<sup>5</sup> Table 3 lists the types of security incidents tallied in the FISMA Reports. We exclude denial of services (DoS) incidents because the presence of legacy systems is unlikely to be related to vulnerability to DoS. We use the log-transformed number of incidents as our main dependent variable. While the data in the FISMA reports is very rich, it is only available for 2012–2015, limiting our sample size to 94. To overcome this limitation, we also utilize incident records from Privacy Rights Clearinghouse (PRC), which tallies breaches of personally-identifiable information (PI) in business and government organizations since 2005. for supplementary ana: lyses.<sup>6</sup> It collects information on known breach incidents from the media, law enforcement, and other public sources. While the PRC data is not as rich as the FISMA data as it only focuses on PII breaches, it spans a longer period of 2005–2016, allowing us to utilize a bigger sample. We identify the PII breach incidents that occurred in the 24 federal agencies from the PRC records. We also categorize them into intentional and unintentional breaches (Table 3).

## Independent variables

We use the Federal IT Dashboard (http://itdashboard.gov/) to obtain data for IT modernization and migration to the cloud. Table 4 provides descriptions of our independent and control variables.

IT Modernization: The IT Dashboard tracks IT investment patterns of the CFO Act agencies. It reports the amount of IT spending that goes to development, modernization, and enhancement of IT systems (DME). DME investments include “costs for projects leading to new IT assets/systems and projects that change or modify existing IT assets to substantively improve capability or performance; implement legislative or regulatory requirements; or meet an agency leadership request.” The rest of the IT spending is devoted to maintenance and operation of legacy systems. As an inverse proxy for the stock of legacy systems in an agency, we measure IT modernization (IT Modern ) as the percentage of [DME spending] to [total IT spending] in five previous years (FY t-1 \~ t-5). We present robustness checks with a three-year and a seven-year window below.

## Control variables

We control for the overall sizes of (i) IT investments (IT Invest) and (ii) security-related IT investments (Security Invest). We control for the number of suspicious network activities as an indicator of target attractiveness (Target Attract) (Ransbotham and Mitra, 2009). Security vulnerabilities of a federal agency should depend on its IT capabilities. We use the federal HR database from the OPM to measure the profiles of IT employees in federal agencies as proxies for IT capabilities. Specifically, we control for their average length of tenure in the federal government in years (IT Tenure) and the share of IT employees with a bachelor’s degree or higher to all agency IT employees (IT Edu).

We control for the size of agency budgets (Budget) and the numbers of agency employees (Employee). We also include controls for agencies’ organizational characteristics such as geographical dispersion and functional diversity, which affect attractiveness of the agencies as targets. We use the OPM data to measure how many employees work in each of the 50 states and Washington, D.C. Using this data, we calculate the geographic dispersion of a federal agency (Disperse) with an inverse of Herfindahl index (1 -  (Agency employees in state i / Total agency employees)<sup>2</sup>). We measure the functional diversity of the agency in a similar way. In the federal budgets published by the OMB, each budget item is designated to one of the functional categories such as national defense, energy, transportation, and general government administration. Using these functional budget categories, we calculate an inverse of Herfindahl index of agency budget (1 –  (Agency budget in function i / Total agency budget)<sup>2</sup>) to measure the functional diversity of the agency (Diversity).

We also control for organizational complexity of an agency, which could increase security vulnerabilities. Many cabinet agencies have sub-agency bureaus (e.g., the Drug Enforcement Administration under the Department of Justice and the Food and Drug Administration under the Department of Health and Human Services). We compute a Herfindhal index of the bureau budgets (Complex). As older agencies are likely to have more legacy systems, we control for the ages (in years) of the agencies. Finally, security vulnerabilities could be associated with agency functions. For instance, agencies that perform more defense or law enforcement re sponsibilities are more likely to be targeted by likely offenders. To account for this possibility, our estimations include five indicators for agency functions (Table 4). Table 5 provides descriptive statistics, and Tables 6 and 7 provide correlation tables

## Estimation model specification

We estimate the following equation:

$$
\operatorname{Log} (Y _ {\mathrm{it}}) = \alpha + \beta X _ {i t} + u _ {i} + v _ {t} + \epsilon_ {i t}\tag{1}
$$

where $Y _ { i t }$ is the number of security incidents at agency i in FY t, X is a matrix of covariates, u is agency fixed-effects, v is year fixedeffects, and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is idiosyncratic residuals. As a primary estimation method, we use Driscoll and Kraay (2006) fixed-effects estimation for spatial autocorrelation. Since the federal agencies are under the umbrella of the U.S. government, $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ could be contemporaril correlated with other peer agencies. The estimations also account for temporal autocorrelation in residuals for two lagged years.

In our estimation, endogeneity is unlikely to be a serious concern. Security incidents in year t are unlikely to significantly affect IT modernization in the previous five years. In addition, prior research shows that migration of IT systems to the cloud is driven primarily by a cost-savings motivation (Han and Mithas, 2013). To the best of our knowledge, federal agencies rarely cite security advantages as a motivation for migration to the cloud. Nevertheless, we will address any endogeneity concern with respect to the cloud with instrumental variables in the section to follow. Agency and year fixed-effects should control for unobserved heterogeneity that affects both security incidents and IT investment patterns in federal agencies; hence, simultaneity and omitted variable bias are not a serious concern. We do not have a reason to believe that IT investments, federal budgets, or agency headcounts are measured with significant measurement errors that are systematically correlated with residuals. One concern in our estimations is a small sample size, and we will address this issue with various robustness checks below

## Hypotheses testing

Table 8 presents the main estimation results. Column 1 lists the result for the total security incidents, and the remaining columns present the same analysis with each breach category. The coefficient of IT Modern is negative and significant in all but one column. providing support for H1. This analysis corroborates the notion that a larger proportion of legacy IT systems to the overall IT asset base causes more security incidents in federal agencies. Table 8 also shows that the coefficient of Cloud is negative and significant in al columns except one. This finding is in accordance with H2 that migration to the cloud serves as an actionable guardianship and mitigating mechanism for security risks.

From the coefficient of IT Modern in Column 1, we calculate that a 1%-point increase in the share of IT modernization in IT budgets is associated with a 5.6% decrease<sup>7</sup> in total security incidents. Column 2 shows that an increase in IT modernization leads to a reduction in improper usage incidents. As explained above, the lack of built-in identity and access management features in many legacy systems makes it difficult to effectively control users’ access and segregate their privileges in the systems. The impact of IT modernization is strongest for social engineering incidents (Column 4), which include phishing; a 1%-point increase in the share of IT modernization in IT budgets is related to a 9.2% reduction in social engineering incidents. Likewise, IT modernization is negatively related to security incidents involving malicious code attacks (Column 7). It is also interesting to see that investments in IT modernization are negatively associated with policy violation incidents (Column 6). which include mishandling of personal infor mation without proper encryption. It appears to be challenging to fully protect personal information that flows throughout complex enterprise architectures tangled with a myriad of legacy systems. Surprisingly, the coefficient of IT Modern is positive and significant for unauthorized equipment incidents (Table 8, Column 5), which implies that federal agencies struggle to physically secure modern portable devices such as laptops, tablets, mobile phones, and USB thumb drives. IT modernization entails a major change management process as the agency adopts more modern technologies such as laptops, mobile phones, and thumb drives. Our findings indicate that the agency is not able to guard against these risks effectively. Migration to the cloud, on the other hand, significantly reduces un authorized equipment incidents, providing evidence of the cloud’s superior guardianship. This illustrates a critical role of capable external guardianship in cybersecurity protection.

As to the control variables, the coefficients of IT Invest are negative and significant in all columns in Table 8, indicating that agencies with a greater percentage of IT expenditures over total budgets experience fewer security incidents. While intuition suggests that more digitized federal agencies are likely to have more vulnerabilities, the agencies with more IT spending seem to have greater IT capabilities that can better protect the IT infrastructures. The coefficients of Disperse are estimated to be negative and significant in all columns in Table 8. This shows that agencies whose employees are spread around the county suffer from fewer security incidents. Intuition might suggest that a federal agency with more geographic locations is subject to higher security risks due to larger network presence (Ransbotham and Mitra, 2009). In addition, maintaining secure environments in many dispersed locations would be more expensive. However, the results in Table 8 indicate that federal agencies that are geographically more centralized are less secure. In such agencies, valuable information is likely to center around a few locations as well. Hence, from the RAT perspective, the systems at a more concentrated agency seem to be more attractive and accessible targets for likely offenders.

From Table 8. it is also notable that the coefficients of IT Tenure are positive and significant in all columns but Column 6. In other words, the longer tenure IT employees of a federal agency have in the government, the more frequent security breaches it suffers from. The coefficients of IT Edu are negative and significant in Columns 2–5, meaning that when the agency’s IT workers are more educated, it experiences fewer incidents in improper use. As of 2015, only about 50% of the federal IT workers have a bachelor’s degree, and 30% of the IT employees are in the federal government for more than 20 years. These findings illustrate the importance of recruiting young, educated IT professionals who are more familiar with modern technologies and new cybersecurity threats to the federal government. They can augment IT capabilities and cybersecurity protection levels of the federal agencies, which can function as effective guard ianship as in RAT.

Our supplementary analyses with the PRC dataset in 2005–2016 provide intriguing results (Table 9). The coefficient of IT Modern is found to be insignificant. To see if the effect of IT modernization on PII breaches might have changed over time, we add the time dummies and the interactions with IT Modern in Table 9. Columns 2 and 6 demonstrate that the effects of IT modernization are not significant in 2005–2010 but become significant in 2011–2016. Column 2 illustrates that a 1%-point increase in the proportion of IT spending in modernization leads to a 1% decrease in the total number of PII breaches after 2011. It is well known that malicious attacks have become more pervasive and sophisticated in the recent vears. The results in Table 9 provide the important insight that complex legacy systems have become increasingly more vulnerable to security threats against PII. Moreover, the significant impact of IT modernization in Column 6 suggests that many legacy systems are not designed to effectively detect and deter human errors that lead to unintentional security breaches. For example, one common unintentional breach is where an employee mistakenly emails sensitive information to recipients who are not authorized to see it. Modern and integrated IT systems are likely to better detect and auto matically block such a transmission of sensitive data, a guardianship capability that legacy systems do not have.

## Robustness checks

To demonstrate the robustness of our findings, we conduct a range of sensitivity analyses as follows. First, we re-estimate the model with alternative IT modernization variables. As mentioned in Table $^ { 4 , }$ IT Modern is measured by the share of IT modernization expenditure to total IT spending for a five-year window. Tables 10 and 11 provide the estimation results with a three-year and a sevenyear window, respectively. We do not obtain substantially different results from Table 8. In Table 12, Column 1 and 2, we use two alternative explanatory variables. In Column 1. in calculating the share of IT modernization spending, we exclude security-related IT investments, including expenditures related to identification and authentication, access control, and incident response. On average, these security-related IT investments only share 2.33% of the total federal IT spending. Excluding such spending does not change our main findings substantially. In Column $^ { 2 , }$ instead of the proportion of IT modernization over total IT expenditures, we use the tota amount of IT modernization spending for the previous five years. This estimation generates similar results.

Second, since the dependent variables are count variables (the number of security incidents), we estimate the model with a con ditional fixed-effects negative binomial regression. Table 12, Column 3 shows a consistent result with Table 8.

Third, while migration to the cloud is primarily motivated by cost savings, superior guardianship against security vulnerabilities of legacy systems could be another motivation. If this is the case, the relationship between cloud spending and security incidents could be endogenous. To address this concern, we adopt instrumental variables (IV) from the Federal Data Center Consolidation Initiative. In this program, the OMB and the Federal CIO Council have encouraged federal agencies to consolidate in-house data centers since 2010.<sup>8</sup> It explicitly aims to achieve cost savings in energy and real estate of the federal data centers. We obtain the number of closed data centers, the amount of cost savings from data center closures, and the number of data center optimization goals that each agency meets from the Data Center Consolidate Report to Congress by the Government Accountability Office (U.S. Government Accountability Office. 2016). Using these measures as IVs for Cloud, we find in Table 12. Column 4 that the coefficient of Cloud is negative and significant.

## Discussions and conclusion

This study theorizes and tests the validity of a long-held and widely-contested belief that legacy IT systems are “secure-by-an tiquity.” We find that antiquity does not make the legacy IT systems more secure but in fact significantly increases the frequency of security incidents. We also find that federal agencies that migrate more of their legacy IT systems to the cloud enhance their guardianship against these security risks and experience fewer security incidents.

The ongoing debate about whether legacy IT systems are prone to security risks or secure by antiquity implies that the generic logic and explanations of RAT are not sufficient to resolve the debate. By systematically applying RAT in the legacy IT system context, we develop our theoretical argument that greater reliance on legacy IT systems satisfies the three criteria of RAT and increases the fre quency of security incidents. Our empirical findings support this hypothesis, advancing our theoretical understanding of how and why legacy IT systems increase security incidents. Regarding an organization's guardianship of sensitive information, RAT-based studies to date have considered only the guardianship provided by an organization. Although other organizations often distribute and manage their sensitive information in their digitally extended networks of business partners, RAT has not incorporated the guardianship provided by external business partners. In this study, we extend the guardianship dimension of RAT to include not only the guard ianship internally provided by the focal organization but also the guardianship provided by its external business partners such as cloud vendors. Our findings show that the cloud vendors can be more capable guardians of sensitive information of federal agencies. The IS literature discusses a range of strategic advantages of the cloud such as cost reduction, flexibility, and agility (Armbrust, et al., 2010 Marston, et al., 2011; Chen and Wu, 2012). This is the first study to tackle the cybersecurity implications of migrating IT systems to the cloud and relying on the guardianship of cloud vendors as well. Future IS studies can build on our extension of RAT to study how the guardianship of business partners might affect information security in an organization’s digitally extended network.

## Implications for practice

Our theory and findings have important implications for practitioners who make strategic investment decisions on information systems. As explained above, there is an enduring, unresolved debate in practice as to whether legacy IT systems are less or more secure than modern systems. Our study provides a theoretically grounded and empirically validated answer to this question; the legacy systems create significant information security risks for organizations. Senior managers who assume that the legacy systems are secure by antiquity could be exposing their organizations to material harm. Our study suggests that they can mitigate the cybersecurity risks of the legacy systems by allocating more resources to IT modernization and by migrating the legacy systems to the cloud.

IT managers are advised to abandon their conventional wisdom of security-by-antiquity and take security threats posed by legacy enterprise architectures more seriously. Our empirical analyses raise a wake-up call that old and technologically outdated systems cause a wide range of security incidents including policy violations, social engineering, or unintentional disclosures of sensitive in formation (Tables 8-9). While it is risky and expensive to replace old, obsolete legacy systems that are deeply embedded into core business processes (Furneaux and Wade. 2011. 2017). this study provides IT managers with a strong justification for discontinuation of the legacy systems. This study informs that it is a strategical imperative for an organization to modernize decades-old legacy systems and to consistently monitor and patch the vulnerabilities from the antiquated legacy infrastructures.

## Limitations and future research directions

This study carries a few limitations. First, the FISMA reports do not report the number of security incidents at the sub-agency bureau level, such as the Transportation Security Administration or the Federal Aviation Administration. In addition, the FISMA dataset does not provide incident-level information such as causes of incident or extent of damage. Incident information at a more granular level would have afforded us a more in-depth analysis on the relationship between mitigating mechanisms and different types of security incidents that have different costs, scope, and impacts. Second, the FISMA dataset covers only the four years from 2012 to 2015. In spite of the small sample size, however, we still observe sufficiently significant impacts of IT modernization and cloud computing. In addition, the supplementary analyses with the PRC data that covers 2005–2016 (Table 9) and a range of robustness checks (Tables 10- 13) assure that our findings are credible. Third, we were not able to obtain more detailed information in security breaches, other than incident categories (Table 3) and the number of incidents in each category. We could not find out, for example, the magnitude of security incidents, the extent of damages, information on potential attackers, or which system was targeted and compromised. For this reason, we were not able to calculate economic impacts or returns from investments in IT modernization.

The public sector provides a range of interesting and promising opportunities for future IS and security research. For example, one can examine how political environments affect security vulnerabilities in governments. Given the political ramifications from a se curity failure, IS researchers can study how political environments in the legislative branch influence security investments or capa bilities in the executive branch. It would be also interesting to study the relationship between IT outsourcing and security risks, since outsiders such as third-party contactors who have access to internal systems can pose security threats. This risk is clearly exemplified by the case of Edward Snowden, who was an external contractor for the National Security Agency when he exposed its surveillance programs. Researchers can also study how high-profile incidents of PII breaches such as ones in Target and the OPM affect security investments in both for-profit firms and governments. While we explain that IT modernization mainly mitigates security incidents due to the known risks, we do not have granular data to distinguish which incidents in our sample were due to known vulnerabilities or new, emerging threats. We recommend future studies to make this distinction and formally test how the mitigating mechanisms in our study influence security breaches that arise from previously unknown, emerging threats.
