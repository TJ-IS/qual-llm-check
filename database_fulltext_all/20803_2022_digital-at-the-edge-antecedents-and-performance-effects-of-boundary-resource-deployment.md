# Digital at the edge – antecedents and performance effects of boundary resource deployment

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Boundary resources Application programming interfaces Digital business ecosystem Firm performance Panel data regression

## A B S T R A C T

With business ecosystems digitalizing by the force of digital innovation, the deployment of boundary resources (such as application programming interfaces: APIs) becomes a strategic op tion across contexts. We distinguish between boundary resources that provide access openness and those that provide resource openness, and theorize the antecedents and consequences of their deployment. Employing panel data regressions to a longitudinal cross-industry dataset, we find that the digital knowledge base of the focal firm and the existence of potential digital com plementors drive boundary resource deployment. Such deployment benefits firm performance depending on the firm’s market power. From our empirical analysis, we reveal a differentiated perspective on the quality of the confined openness provided by boundary resources as well as the embeddedness of their deployment in the rationales and motivations of the associated actors in digital business ecosystems. We complement the existent theoretical framework on boundary resources and provide valuable insights to managers reflecting about deploying boundary re sources in a beneficial way.

## Introduction

Today’s largest companies by market value – such as Apple, Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, or Facebook (Forbes, 2019) – excel at leveraging their digital knowledge and that of other actors in digital business ecosystems (Nischak et al. 2017) by deploying boundary resources such as application programming interfaces (APIs) (Yoo et al., 2010; Parker and van Alstyne, 2018; Song et al., 2018). Through these APIs. external contributors are allowed to access the assets made available by deploving firms in a manageable and controlled manner to either utilize them in their contexts or develop new digital offerings by building upon them (Boudreau, 2012; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). Such benefits from boundary resources are being recognized and leveraged not only by firms from the digital space (Iyer and Subramaniam, 2015a, 2015b). Companies across industries that are eager to create and scale digita innovation – such as retail (Best Buy, 2020: Macys, 2020: Walmart, 2020), transportation (FedEx, 2020: UPS. 2020), and manufacturing (Svahn et al., 2017; Ford, 2020; John Deere, 2020) – are also increasingly reflecting upon deploying APIs (Yoo et al. 2010; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). However, deploying boundary resources also incurs some downsides. For instance, the introduction and governance of APIs requires resources that could otherwise be invested in self-development or other distributive activities, while the returns are uncertain (Eaton et al., 2015). Furthermore, with boundary resources (as opposed to, for example, opensource settings), openness is confined (Karhu et al., 2018). which may limit innovation and growth potentials and reinforce path dependencies. Therefore, decisions about deploying boundary resources are far from trivial.

In the evolving IS research stream related to the topic, boundary resources are considered an important means by which firms “open up,” in a manageable and controlled manner, to external audiences to stimulate innovation and growth within digital business eco systems (Yoo et al., 2010; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). In particular, firms can deploy boundary resources to provide two types of openness (Karhu et al., 2018). Access openness refers to leveraging the deploying firm’s existing assets by allowing external audiences to utilize and integrate them in other contexts. For instance, eBay deploys a broad range of APIs for access openness that lets external parties utilize its transaction services (e.g., managing auctions) or incorporate its data (e.g., bidder information) – all of which drive the exposure of eBay’s assets and thus increase revenues (Iyer and Subramaniam, 2015b; eBay, 2020). By contrast, resource openness relates to leveraging external innovation capabilities by exposing the deploying firm’s assets that enable external audiences to diversify the firm’s offerings. As an example, the agricultural and construction manufacturer John Deere deploys diverse APIs for resource openness that provide distinctive technological capabilities (e.g., access to machine data) for developing new services regarding logistics management and agronomic solutions (John Deere, 2020).

Although previous research generated important qualitative insights into the design of boundary resources, the ecosystem interactions revolving around them, and value-creating mechanisms in digital contexts (e.g., Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013; Eaton et al., 2015; Karhu et al., 2018), the factors that drive their adoption are as unclear as the performance outcomes, especially empir ically. Filling such research gaps becomes increasingly important for two reasons: the business relevance and strategic value for boundary resources increases across contexts; and the different types of boundary resources may lead to different performance effects and the driving forces for their deployment may vary in correspondence with a firms’ capabilities and those of its environment (Yoo et al., 2010; Eaton et al., 2015; Karhu et al., 2018). From an academic perspective, indications about the strategic rationales and consequences for deploying firms help to gain a more complete and nuanced understanding of boundary resources (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013; Karhu et al., 2018; Parker and van Alstyne, 2018) and their role in enabling openness and subsequent interaction in digital business ecosystems (Dahlander and Gann, 2010; Dahlander et al., 2021). From the perspective of managerial practice, insights into whether and when the benefits of deploying boundary resources (as one particular strategic option) outweigh the associated costs is of vital importance (Yoo et al., 2010; Iyer and Subramaniam, 2015b; Jacobides et al., 2018). Such insights might particularly enrich the reasoning of managers in doubt about the efficacy of deploying boundary resources. Therefore, this study is concerned with the following research questions:

(1) How do firm-related and context-related factors influence boundary resource deployments; and

(2) When are boundary resource deployments beneficial for the firm?

To provide answers to these questions, we create a theoretical framework drawing on contingency theory (Weill and Olson, 1989), a lens that has proven valuable in investigating the antecedents and consequences of deployment decisions in multiple IS contexts (Teo and Pian, 2003; Sharma and Yetton, 2007; Shao et al., 2016). To investigate our predictions, we employ panel data regressions to a longitudinal dataset of Standard & Poor’s 500 (S&P 500) firms between 2010 and 2018. We exploit the ProgrammableWeb API database and focus on the employment of APIs as our operationalization of boundary resources. The findings suggest that digital knowledge and the presence of potential digital complementors in the industry positively affect the decision to deploy boundary resources (APIs). Moreover, we find that the firm’s digital knowledge particularly drives the deployment of APIs for access openness, while the potential digital complementors in the industry particularly drive the deployment of APIs for resource openness. Regarding the performance implications of boundary resource deployments, we could not find that the deployment of one type of the boundary resources increases firm performance on average. However, we observe that the deployment of APIs for access openness increases firm performance for firms with high market power. Our additional analysis regarding the drivers behind the API performance relation further reveals that while the deployment of both APIs for access openness and APIs for resource openness has a positive impact on a firm’s revenue, only firms that have high market power and deploy APIs for access openness can achieve greater operational margins.

This work contributes to boundary resources research by providing the first large-scale empirical evidence shedding light on the contextual factors that lead companies across industries to deploy boundary resources (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013; Karhu et al., 2018: Gawer. 2021). Furthermore. our findings reveal when firms decide to establish different types of openness (i.e., access ys. resource openness) through boundary resources and under what conditions they profit from such a strategic decision (Dahlander and Gann, 2010; Dahlander et al., 2021). Furthermore, this research has important implications for managerial practice by informing managers about relevant contextual conditions and potential consequences of deploying boundary resources.

## Background

Digital innovation and digital(izing) business ecosystems

Owing to the force of digital innovation, defined “as the creation of (and consequent change in) market offerings, business processes, or models that result from the use of digital technology” (Nambisan et al., 2017, p. 224), product and industry boundaries are progressively dissolving, thus forcing firms to cope with new competitive and cooperative dynamics (Yoo et al., 2010). These dynamics are reshaping business ecosystems and the way firms need to operate within them. Driven by the generative and convergent nature of digita innovation, companies are increasingly dependent on value co-creation and co-capture with heterogeneous and widely dispersed players (Yoo et al., 2012; El Sawy and Pereira, 2013).

As a result, digital innovation exhibits three specific key traits that refer to, on the one hand, its distributed and combinatoria nature, and on the other hand, the key role of digital platforms (Yoo et al., 2012). Regarding the former, innovation within a layered modular architecture can be characterized as distributed, dispersing not only control over product components across multiple firms, but also product knowledge across heterogeneous disciplines and communities (Yoo et al., 2010). An illustrative example is smart phones, where large numbers of distributed and heterogeneous third-party developers can access different components (e.g., photo and video cameras) of the product to diversify its functions (e.g., third-party applications) over a broad range of application areas (Boudreau, 2012; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). Furthermore, digital innovation is combinatorial in that new digital products or services are increasingly created based on the (re)combination of existing modules with digital capabilities, which, in turn, has become a new source of innovation (Selander et al., 2013; Henfridsson et al., 2018). For example, after Google introduced an API to its Maps service, it was (re)combined by external developers with numerous already-existing location-based databases and, thus, created a variety of new digital offerings (Yoo et al., 2012). Finally, firms are increasingly designing and introducing technological platforms (e. g., product or service platforms) that reduce transactions’ costs, facilitate connections, and create network effects between multiple distinct groups of actors (Eisenmann and van Alstyne, 2006; Parker et al., 2016). These platforms can be differentiated based on their main function, which is either focused on (1) facilitating transactions of existing goods or services or (2) stimulating complementary innovation on the platform through external development (Cusumano et al., 2019; Gawer, 2021)

Accordingly, the key traits of digital innovation are fundamentally reshaping the business ecosystems in which companies find themselves, challenging them to consider how to leverage external actors for distributed and combinatorial innovations (Boudreau. 2012; Parker et al., 2017) or leverage their assets through scaling their distribution in digital settings (Gawer, 2014, 2020). One strategy in this regard is represented by the deployment of boundary resources, such as APIs, which can be utilized to either distribute the firm’s assets in its respective digital business ecosystem (Cusumano et al., 2019; Gawer, 2021) or permit external audiences the development of complementary innovations upon the provided resources (Yoo et al., 2012; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013).

## Boundary resources

Boundary resources enable resource sharing and stimulate external contribution (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013; Eaton et al., 2015). In the emerging IS research stream, so far, scholars have revealed valuable qualitative insights in three directions: (1) the design of boundary resources; (2) associated mechanisms through which value is created; and (3) the ecosystem interactions revolving around them.

In terms of design (1). prior work derived qualitative insights about how firms can design boundary resources to manage the delicate tension between the coexisting and contradicting forces of resourcing (extending the scope and diversity of the firm’s offering) and securing (improving the firm’s control) in digital business ecosystems (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). Accordingly, as firms balance between opening up toward external contributions and maintaining control over strategic assets, they are dynamically renewing and modifying their boundary resources and the associated provision of assets (e.g., software or infrastructure) towards external audiences. Therefore, previous research particularly emphasizes the dynamic nature of boundary resources by delineating how the multifaceted actions of the external actors in digital business ecosystems are also influencing and (re)shaping the design of boundary resources in a co-creation process called distributed tuning (Eaton et al., 2015).

Regarding value-creating mechanisms (2), previous studies pointed toward how boundary resources such as APIs help companies. after a certain period of openness and growth, to monetize created value by exerting more control over strategic assets (Parker et al., 2017; Parker and van Alstyne, 2018). More recent studies also revealed that APIs can serve as mediators for interactions and exchange in digital contexts, thus offering new distribution and commercialization potentials for digital offerings (Gawer, 2021; Dahlander et al., 2021). In this context, scholars have also revealed how non-focal firms are creating value from the utilization of focal boundary re sources through the processes of capability search (using external resources to improve their own innovation capabilities) and capability redeem (using external capabilities to distribute and monetize products or services) (Selander et al., 2013). In contrast, research also sheds light on how firms can defend themselves from hostile activities (e.g., strategic exploitations) by non-focal actors through the modification of existing and introduction of new boundary resources (Karhu et al., 2018).

Considering ecosystem interactions (3), the emerging research provides insights on how the introduction of boundary resources affects the dynamic evolution and structure of digital business ecosystems. In this regard, recent studies delineate how APIs are ful filling dual functions that simultaneously affect application innovation and imitation, depending on the market conditions and product complexity in the associated ecosystem (Song et al., 2017). Furthermore, related research also sheds light on how the introduction of new APIs influences the structure and interaction of the established digital business ecosystems (Um and Yoo, 2016). In particular, researchers revealed how new APIs dynamically reshape the established ecosystems from single hierarchical structures to horizontall coordinated and multilayered hierarchies that span across different domains and are connected with other digital business ecosystems.

In summary, deploying boundary resources allows firms to leverage external participation and contribution through opening up assets to external audiences in digital business ecosystems (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013; Gawer, 2021). Hence, by deploying boundary resources, firms are able “to define openness and facilitate complementors’ work” (Karhu et al., 2018). Accordingly, based on Karhu et al.’s (2018) distinction, two particular types of boundary resources can be discerned by relating the different forms of openness they provide toward external actors. One type, boundary resources for access openness, enables firms to distribute their existing digital offerings among external audiences and to utilize or integrate the provided assets in their business activities or contexts (Selander et al., 2013; Gawer, 2021). As an example, Visa provides multiple of its financial service solutions through APIs to scale their distribution among users in their digital business ecosystem (Visa, 2020). The second type, boundary resources for resource openness, refers to opening up assets that enable external parties to diversify the deploying firm’s offerings through heterogeneous development activities (Boudreau, 2012; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). For instance, Amazon allows external complementors to tap into the distinct capabilities of its voice assistant Alexa to create new and diverse voice-activated “skills” (e.g., digital services), thus diver sifying the assistant’s service ecosystem (Amazon, 2018).

Although valuable knowledge has been created, there are, to date, no insights on contextual conditions serving as antecedents that lead companies to deploy boundary resources, and whether and when such decisions pay off regarding financial benefits (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013; Dahlander et al., 2021). Additionally, previous studies were predominantly conducted in the IT industry (e.g., Apple’s iOS or Google’s Android platform) and thus paid less attention to investigating other contexts that are affected by digita innovation even though the decision to adopt boundary resources is becoming increasingly relevant across industries through on-going digitalization (Yoo et al., 2010; Yoo et al., 2012). Addressing these gaps is particularly important due to the existence of different types of boundary resources. Accordingly, boundary resources can be instantiated for distinct purposes (i.e., facilitation of interactions vs. complementary innovations) and relate to different types of openness (i.e., access vs. resource openness) toward external audiences (Karhu et al., 2018; Gawer, 2021). As to these differences, the deployment of the specific types of boundary resources may receive varying influence by contextual driving forces and also lead to different consequences. Therefore, the deployment decision becomes more complex. To date, the antecedents and financial implications of deploying these different types of boundary resources and the specific types of openness they afford are still effectively unexplored (Dahlander and Gann, 2010). Such insights can yield a more comprehensive and differentiated perspective on boundary resource deployments and their role in digital business ecosystems (Gawer 2021; Dahlander et al., 2021).

## Theoretical framework and hypotheses development

Firms deploying boundary resources create digital interfaces to expose or provide access to specific resources (Gawer, 2021). Compared to self-development or other forms of external cooperation (such as outsourcing or licensing), boundary resources represent a certain degree of openness to the public (Dahlander et al., 2021). However, they are clearly less open than open-source settings (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). Therefore, by deploying boundary resources, firms aim to both foster generativity and retain control over their resources (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013; Gawer, 2021). The potential benefits notwithstanding, there are multiple downsides to deploying boundary resources. First, creating digital interfaces for external audiences signifies investing valuable resources with uncertainty about the return (Svahn et al., 2017). Second, resources need to be invested to monitor and service the actions of the potentially large number of external actors interacting with the deployed boundary resources (Karhu et al., 2018). Finally, opening up selectively may reinforce path dependencies in innovation trajectories, confine innovation opportunities, or restrict growth potentials (Dahlander and Gann, 2010).

Accordingly, following a contingency perspective (Sharma and Yetton, 2007; Wang and Benbasat, 2013; Shao et al., 2016), we argue that a firm’s particular conditions define whether the firm perceives that the benefits from deploying boundary resources outweigh the costs (Menz and Scheef. 2014). The decision to deploy boundary resources may therefore be driven by the internal and external conditions in which a firm finds itself. While several factors in a firms’ internal and external environment may influence the decision to deploy boundary resources, the key traits of digital innovation (i.e., digital technology platforms, distributed and combinatorial innovation) highlight the importance of specific factors. In particular, distributed and combinatorial digital innovation emerges from the interaction of a firms’ internal digital resources and the surrounding ecosystem, centering on digital technology platforms (Eaton et al., 2015; Henfridsson et al. 2018). Thus, firms that possess valuable digital resources and make them available to external actors may profit from openness. Nevertheless, they may opt against complete openness since they may want to avoid exposing their assets to their competitors (Karhu et al., 2018). On the other hand, to benefit from distributed and combinatoria innovation, companies are dependent on the availability of capable external actors within their ecosystems and that these actors can be attracted to participate. Here. firms that are surrounded by digital ventures may perceive them as potential complementors, which may justify openness. However, they may opt against complete openness because of the potential threats of digital disruption (Skog et al., 2018). These conditions (elaborated on below) may create a particular demand for boundary resource deployment. Furthermore, these conditions may shape the emphasis on boundary resource deployment and the confined openness it affords, that is, a focus on access or resource openness.

To date, it is unclear how deploving boundary resources affects a firm's performance (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson. 2013: Dah: lander et al., 2021). Potentially, boundary resource deployments can, on the one hand, reduce costs by saving internal developer or distributive resources and, on the other hand, increase revenues by the creation of new innovative offerings as well as by extending the reach of existing offerings. In this context, due to their established brands, networks, and large customer bases, firms with high market power may be particularly well prepared to realize those potentials by attracting large audiences that help to develop and distribute their offerings (Boudreau, 2012; Selander et al., 2013; Song et al., 2018). Leveraging internal digital knowledge

The possession of valuable internal resources and the ability to expose them are important drivers of openness (Dahlander and Gann, 2010). At the same time, firms possessing such assets may feel the need to secure them (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). The confined openness provided by boundary resources may therefore be particularly relevant.

In general, firms possessing digital knowledge have also been found to significantly drive digital innovation (Kohli and Melville, 2019: Hanelt et al., 2021b). In particular. because of its combinatorial and distributed nature, digital innovation requires complementarities with other parties. Their realization can be eased by boundary resources, such as APIs (Yoo et al., 2012). Consequently, firms possessing digital knowledge may seek to deploy APIs to enable other actors to search for and redeem their valuable components within the loosely-coupled modular-layered architecture in a controlled way (Yoo et al., 2010; Selander et al., 2013). Furthermore, those firms should be particularly well prepared to actually realize these potentials as they can be assumed to be able to set up boundary resources at comparatively great ease. A firm’s knowledge base indicates its technological capabilities (Vermeulen and Barkema, 2001; Grimpe and Hussinger, 2014). The ability to deploy boundary resources as digital technological artifacts (Yoo et al., 2012) should therefore be related to a firm’s digital knowledge base. Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:

## H1a. Internal digital knowledge is positively associated with the likelihood of future boundary resource deployments.

More specifically, firms with superior digital knowledge bases may particularly opt to deploy boundary resources for access openness. These firms, with their advanced digital knowledge, may have progressed in digital innovation (Grover et al., 2018; Hanelt et al., 2021b). Through deploying boundary resources for access openness, they may attempt to foster network effects and distribution of the components they developed (Karhu et al., 2018; Gawer, 2021). In addition, as firms with superior digital knowledge bases probably have incurred greater expenses (e.g., financial and technological resources) in the past to build up their digital expertise, they might also be more inclined to commercialize it and thus opt for using boundary resources for access openness (Eaton et al., 2015; Hanelt et al., 2021b). In sum, it seems likely that firms with elaborated digital knowledge aspire to utilize them to distribute and scale their digital innovations. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1b. The influence of internal digital knowledge on the likelihood of future boundary resource deployments is stronger for boundary resources targeted at access openness than those targeted at resource openness.

## Leveraging external digital knowledge

The existence of competent actors in the environment may drive a firm toward opening up (Dahlander and Gann, 2010). However, these potential complementors may also quickly turn to competitors (Skog et al., 2018). The confined openness provided by boundary resources may therefore be particularly relevant for firms facing such circumstances.

Boundary resources serve not only as a means to foster the distribution of existing digital innovations, but also to unleash gen erativity, that is, “a technology’s overall capacity to produce unprompted change driven by large, varied, and uncoordinated audi ences” (Zittrain, 2006, p. 1980), in the development of new digital innovations (Yoo et al., 2012). However, to profit from this generativity, potential complementors have to be available. Here, digital ventures seem particularly relevant as they exhibit elaborated digital capabilities (Tumbas et al., 2017). Also, these digital ventures regularly draw from the existing digital infrastructures (Tilson et al., 2010) and components (Yoo et al., 2010) to rapidly build their services, and may therefore be keen to use available boundary resources (Eaton et al., 2015: Huang et al., 2017). Offering boundary resources might attract the creative and rapid scaling streams of distributed and combinatorial digital innovation toward the deploying company (Parker et al., 2017) in a regulated way that avoids resources leaking to potential competitors in an uncontrolled manner (Dahlander and Gann. 2010). Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:

## H2a. The presence of external digital complementors is positively associated with the likelihood of future boundary resource deployments.

In particular, firms that face the emergence of digital entrants may use boundary resources for resource openness to profit from the creative inputs of external actors (Karhu et al., 2018). Thus, these external contributions may help firms diversify their current of ferings on a much greater scale and meet the continuously changing customer demands through a constant flow of new services (van de Ven, 2005; Yoo et al., 2010; Boudreau, 2012). By offering boundary resources for resource openness, firms may attempt to turn digita ventures into co-creators of value – that is, complementors instead of hostile competitors (El Sawy and Pereira, 2013; Skog et al. 2018). Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2b. The influence of external digital complementors on the likelihood of future deployments of boundary resources is stronger for boundary resources targeted at resource openness than for those targeted at access openness.

## Performance implications

Boundary resources provide two specific benefits: leveraging internal digital knowledge through distributing activities of third parties; and leveraging external digital knowledge through the creative contributions of external actors. Meanwhile, the development and provision of boundary resources, as with any other large-scale technological infrastructure, causes expenses such as upfront development and continuous administrative costs (Eaton et al., 2015; Karhu et al., 2018). From an economic perspective, the different types of boundary resources can have a positive influence on the firm’s performance through varying mechanisms. Therefore, we discern and discuss at first the possible performance implications of boundary resource deployments for access openness and subse quently those for resource openness.

The deployment of boundary resources for access openness allows firms to extend the reach and commercialization of the existing offerings (Karhu et al., 2018: Gawer. 2021). The deployment of such boundary resources enables firms to establish new digital dis. tribution channels that help diversify revenue streams from existing offerings (Gawer, 2021; Dahlander et al., 2021) while exploiting network effects in digital business ecosystems (Eisenmann and van Alstyne, 2006; Parker et al., 2016). In addition, boundary resources for access openness can support firms in substituting traditionally capital-intensive distribution channels (e.g., personal sales) and, thus, improving current revenue streams in a cost-effective manner (Tilson et al., 2010; Gawer, 2021). For instance, the pharmacy retail chain Walgreens allowed external audiences to access its photo printing services through APIs and integrate them in other contexts – resulting in greater exposure, higher overall engagement, and more revenues per customer (Iyer and Subramaniam, 2015b). Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:

## H3a. The deployment of boundary resources targeted at access openness is positively associated with future firm performance.

In contrast. the deplovment of boundary resources for resource openness aims for contributions from external parties that are often less costly than self-development (Boudreau, 2012; Boudreau and Jeppesen, 2015). Such boundary resources allow large groups of external developers to keep an existing digital offering fresh and attractive by adding a continuous flow of new functionality (van de Ven, 2005; Yoo et al., 2010; Boudreau, 2012). This can induce stabilizing or even increasing revenues. For example, external developer communities are constantly releasing new applications on Apple’s operating system (iOS), which causes constant evolution of func tionalities on existing devices (e.g., iPhone or iPad). Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

## H3b. The deployment of boundary resources targeted at resource openness is positively associated with future firm performance.

Besides, there might be a further nuance regarding the performance implications of boundary resource deployments. In particular, how deployed boundary resources translate into firm performance also depends on how they are received and embraced by the external environment. Consequently, firms that rank high in market power may signal a certain degree of legitimacy that drives their attractiveness to consumers and complementors in their respective digital business ecosystems (Selander et al., 2013; Eaton et al., 2015; Song et al., 2018). Thus, a powerful market position might influence the deployment of boundary resources

First, firms that deploy boundary resources for access openness and rank high in market power are more likely to reach greater exposure and distribution of their assets through, for instance, network effects among external audiences (Gawer, 2014), thus creating inherently greater potential to improve revenues. In addition, since distribution costs of resources on digital infrastructures are relatively independent from the scale (Tilson et al., 2010), firms with powerful market positions might particularly benefit from such boundary resource deployments. In addition, a powerful market position also increases a firm’s overall acceptance of its digital of ferings (Eaton et al., 2015; Song et al., 2018), allowing the firm to charge higher prices, thereby potentially realizing greater margins. Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:

## H4a. Market power positively moderates the relationship between the deployment of boundary resources targeted at access openness and future firm performance.

Second, firms that rank high in market power and deploy boundary resources for resource openness might be particularly attractive as partners in co-creating value (Dahlander and Gann, 2010; Song et al., 2018). For instance, digital complementors might perceive an established firm’s position, infrastructure, or customer base as a way to rapidly scale up or accelerate their innovation activities (Huang et al., 2017: Song et al., 2018). For example, Apple's diverse technological platforms (e.g., iPhones or iPads) and integrated distribution channels (e.g., iOS App Store) can attract various developer communities and, thus, effectively leverage their heteroge neous knowledge resources and competencies (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013; Eaton et al., 2015). Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4b. Market power positively moderates the relationship between the deployment of boundary resources targeted at resource openness and future firm performance.

## Methodology

## Sample selection

To gain large-scale empirical insights, we focused on a longitudinal sample of firms listed in the S&P 500 index. We decided to investigate the years 2010 to 2018 and focused on the index constituents of our starting year, 2010. We collected data for boundary resource deployments by systematically collecting information from the ProgrammableWeb API directory in April 2019, which is the Internet’s most complete account of information about public APIs (Evans and Basole, 2016; Um and Yoo, 2016). We further collected data on digital patents from the USPTO and on digital start-ups from the CrunchBase database. Financial and ownership data were retrieved from Datastream, board, TMT, and CEO data from the BoardEx database. From potentially 4500 firm-vears, we only include firm-years with available financial and other relevant data for our regressions, yielding a final sample of 3,720 firm-years. For the analysis of performance implications, we needed to exclude the year 2018 due to data unavailability (i.e., future performance), reducing the sample to 3,307 firm years.

## Variables

## Main variables

Boundary resource deployments: To measure boundary resource deployments, we chose APIs as our operationalization of boundary resources. We focused on ProgrammableWeb API directory (Evans and Basole, 2016; Um and Yoo, 2016), which contains all relevant information about company APIs that are described by the pre-defined criteria, such as category, type, provider, introduction date, and other interaction-related and technical properties. To collect all the required information for our analysis. we searched the directory for any API listings that matched our S&P 500 firms and were introduced in the timeframe between 2010 and 2018.

After collecting all the relevant API information, we coded the content of each API entry according to our previous conceptuali zations of openness: access openness and resource openness. An API that was providing access to digital offerings (e.g., software solutions or modules) with the purpose of distribution among external actors and integration in other contexts was considered an API for access openness. Here, keywords like “provides service,” “offers solution,” or “allows integration of software” indicated an API for access openness, while an API that was providing assets capable of diversifying the deploying firm’s offerings and thus being utilized for developing novel services or products was considered as an API for resource openness. Accordingly, terms like “build applications,” “develop services,” or “support development activities” indicated an API for resource openness. After conducting an initial pilot coding of 100 randomly selected APIs by two authors, an intercoder-reliability score of over 85% was achieved. The remaining disagreements among the coders were discussed among all the authors until a consensus was reached.

Based on the collected API’s information and our coding results, we considered boundary resource deployments as a firm’s adoption of an API. We created three variables to measure boundary resource deployments. First, we created a binary variable API that takes the value of 1 if a firm adopted at least one API in the given vear and zero otherwise. Second. we created a binary variable APl access that takes the value of 1 if a firm adopted at least one API for access openness in a given year and zero otherwise. Finally, we created a binary variable API resource, taking the value 1 if a firm adopted at least one API for resource openness in the respective year.

Digital knowledge: To capture firms’ digital knowledge, we focused on digital patent fillings. We follow prior literature in the view that patents can reflect the knowledge base of a firm (Prabhu et al., 2005: Hanelt et al., 2021b). Specifically, we assumed. similar to Hanelt et al. (2021b), that the number of digital patent fillings can be a proxy for a firm’s digital knowledge base. We focused on the data from the USPTO for a firm’s patenting activities. Similar to Firk et al. (2021a), we focused on initial filings in patent classes that reflect digital technologies. Specifically, we considered the following technological classes of the U.S. Patent Classification (USPC) scheme as related to digital technologies. First, we considered the domain of “Communications & Computers" similar to Hall et al (2001). Second. we considered the technological classes that were created after the “Communications & Computers" domain and that are clearly associated with digital technologies, such as “Data processing: software development, installation, and management.” Table A in the Appendix provides a full list of the USPC classes used for our operationalization. Finally, to construct our measure of digital knowledge, we considered the number of digital patent filings over the last two years. To reduce the influence of the outliers, we followed prior studies (e.g., Custodio ´ et al., 2019; Hanelt et al., 2021b) by log transforming the digital knowledge variable.

Digital complementors: We suggested that boundary resource deployment can help unleash the generativity in digital innovation. The presence of potential digital complementors is crucial to realize this benefit. To proxy for the presence of potential complementors, we, similar to Firk et al. (2021b), constructed a measure for the level of digital ventures per industry incumbent. To identify digita ventures, we extracted all firms in the CrunchBase database founded between 2008 and 2017. Thereby, we focused on digital start-ups. Due to their early stage, such digital start-ups are particularly inclined to draw from existing assets and act as complementors for incumbent firms (Eaton et al., 2015; Huang et al., 2017; Tumbas et al., 2017). We classified each start-up’s industry affiliation into one Fama and French 48 industry. We further checked whether each start-up was indeed a digital venture. Specifically, we evaluated the descriptions of the start-ups and classified them as either digital or not, based on a list of keywords such as digital, internet, or online (see Appendix 1 for the full list). Next, we counted the number of digital ventures in the industry and divided it by the number of sample firms in the industry. This procedure induces a ratio reflecting the number of digital ventures by each industry incumbent. We then calculated the two-year average of this number to construct our final digital complementor variable.

Firm performance: We decided to use a market-based and an accounting-based measure of a firm’s performance. We used the firm’s total shareholder return (TSR) as a market-based performance measure reflecting the shareholder stock and dividend returns over a certain period. Specifically, it was calculated as the share price at the end of a period minus the share price at the beginning of the period plus dividends paid, all divided by the beginning share price. To capture the long-term effects of boundary resource de ployments, we decided to measure TSR over a two-year period t and t + 1 (TSR 2y). As an accounting-based performance measure, we used return on assets (ROA). We used the ROA as defined by DataStream. Again, we considered that the performance effects of API adoption might have longer lead times and measured ROA over a two-year period t and t + 1 (ROA 2y).

## Control variables

We selected a set of variables to control for confounding effects. First. we selected control variables on the ownership. board. CEO. and TMT levels to capture the parties potentially involved in the decision to deploy boundary resources. Ownership concentration was included because large blockholders might drive or prevent the decision to employ boundary resources. Technology expertise of leaders could encourage the decision to deploy boundary resources, and thus we added IT board expertise and IT TMT expertise. In addition, we included CEOs age as a control because older CEOs are frequently considered to be less willing to engage in new projects and generally to innovate. Second, we selected several financial and organizational controls to account for potential confounding effects. We included firm size as the larger firms are considered to adopt new technologies earlier. Since a firm’s better economic situation is often associated with more innovative activities, we included ROA to account for the firm’s profitability and TSR to account for capital market perception. For a similar reason, we included leverage, sales growth, and firm risk. We also considered the firm’s diversification. as more diversified firms could have more various opportunities to deploy boundary resources. In addition to diversification, we also accounted for the degree to which a firm targets businesses and consumers as their customers (B2B–B2C mix) because firms with a more mixed customer base may have more options to deploy various boundary resources. Furthermore, we included the firm’s intangible assets and the R&D intensity, as these may be related to a firm’s general innovation activities that may drive benefits of boundary resource employment. Finally, we considered five industry dummies, according to Fama and French. For the analyses of the performance implications, we used the same set of control variables but excluded the two performance measures ROA and TSR as controls. A detailed explanation of the calculation of each control variable and the data source is provided in Table A in the Appendix.

## Empirical methods

To analyze both the antecedents and performance implications of the deployment of boundary resources, we needed to address different empirical challenges in our empirical analyses. Accordingly, we first elaborate on our reasoning behind choosing an esti mation procedure for analyzing the antecedents of boundary resource deployment, and then discuss our reasoning behind choosing an estimation procedure for analyzing the performance implications of boundary resource deployment.

## Estimating the antecedents of boundary resource deployment

To analyze the antecedents of boundary resource deployment, we needed to account for the binary nature of our dependent variables regarding API adoption. The panel structure of our data further allowed us to control for unobserved heterogeneity in our empirical analysis. We decided for a general estimation of equations (GEE) regression with a logit link function since it enables us to account for both within- and between-firm variance to calculate robust estimates, thus tackling the issue of unobserved heterogeneity (Liang and Zeger. 1986: Ballinger. 2004). Specifically, the GEE corrects for the correlation in the dependent variable across firms over time by estimating the correlation structure of the error terms (Gupta and Misangvi. 2018). We preferred a GEE model over alternative panel logit models with random or firm-fixed effects for several reasons. First, logit fixed effects models would drop all observations of firms that did not adopt any API during our time frame. Second. the assumption of random effects models that error terms are uncorrelated over time may be questioned in our panel data set. Third, the GEE fits a population-average model, allowing the coefficient to be interpreted as the influence averaged across the population of the firms, aligning well with our antecedent’s hypotheses targeting population differences.

To operationalize the GEE, we considered the xtgee command in Stata 15. The command requires a specified correlation structure for the dependent variable. As the efficiency of the GEE depends on the selection of an appropriate correlation structure, we followed a careful selection process. We started by running a Wooldridge test for autocorrelation to check whether choosing an autoregressive correlation structure is appropriate. As the test did not show evidence of autocorrelation, we decided to select among the remaining options (i.e., exchangeable, unstructured, or independent) by applying the quasi-likelihood under the independence model criterion (QIC) measure (Cui, 2007). Based on the smallest-QIC criteria, we selected an exchangeable correlation structure, as it yielded the best fit for all three variables of boundary resources (Quigley and Hambrick, 2012; Gupta and Misangyi, 2018). Next, we specified a logit link function of the binomial family and the robust option, considering the Huber–White standard error correction. Finally, we lagged all our independent and control variables to tackle reverse causality issues. Specifically, we tested the following model to analyze our first and second hypotheses:

## Estimating the performance implications of boundary resource deployment

To examine the performance implications of boundary resource deployment, we had to consider potential sources of endogeneity. First, there might be systematic differences between firms that employ and do not employ boundary resources that drive performance differences. To address this issue, we decided to run a firm-fixed effects regression that assigns each firm an individual effect. Thereby, the firm-fixed effects model controls for time-invariant firm-specific unobservable factors, and estimates only time-variant effects within a firm. Prior IS research, therefore, highlights the importance of firm-fixed effects in capturing unobserved firm heterogeneit when studying IS performance implications (Mithas et al., 2012; Pan et al., 2018; Joshi et al., 2019).

Second. as boundary resource deployments is an endogenous choice, and time-variant unobservable factors could drive the decision for boundary resource deployments and a firm’s performance concurrently, we still could face a self-selection bias. To account for this, we decided to include a correction factor, as suggested by Shaver (1998). Specifically, Shaver (1998) proposed including an additional control variable in the model that is based on a first-stage probit model estimating the propensity to engage in a particular strategy (e.g., boundary resource deployments). Following this suggestion, we estimated the probability of deciding on boundary resource deployments while accounting for our second-stage control variables (and the moderator variable market power) and an exclusion criterion. The exclusion criterion should be correlated with the decision for boundary resource deployments, but not correlated with the firm’s performance. We chose the average diffusion of boundary resource deployments in the industry, as this should be related to boundary resource deployments of the focal firm. but should not affect the performance of the focal firm. other than through the impact on deciding for boundary resource deployments or not. Finally, we used the predicted values of the probit regressions to calculate an inverse Mills ratio (Heckman, 1979), which we included as an additional control variable in our firm-fixed effects regressions (i.e., λ boundary resource deployments).

## Findings

## Descriptive results

To illustrate the presence of boundary resource deployment in the listed firms, we provided an overview of the development of APl use and adoption within our sample. Panel A of Table 1 displays the API use in our sample by year and industry. The overview provides at least two interesting findings. First, the results indicate a rapid diffusion of APL use in recent vears. While in our starting vear. <4% ot the firms had an API installed. 15% installed at least one API in 2018. This supports the relevance of boundary resource deployment and the need to better understand the antecedents and implications of this decision. Second, the results support the idea that API use is not limited to technology firms. The service sector, including many technology firms such as Alphabet or Salesforce, has the highest diffusion rate. However, we also find a considerable number of API users in the Manufacturing (e.g., automotive) and the Wholesale & Retail industry. Panel B of Table 1 further illustrates the frequency of adoptions of APIs over time and the differences in this devel opment for the two types of APIs. The results indicate that around 4% of the sampled firms adopted at least one API in each year. Moreover, the results indicate that many firms adopted APIs in more than just one year as the sum of all API adoption exceeded the APIs users. Regarding APIs for access or resource openness, we observed a more frequent adoption of APIs for access openness than of APIs for resource openness. Similarly, in 2018, users of APIs for resource openness accounted for only 7% of our sample, while around 12% used at least one API for access openness.

Robustness of results. We ran several (untabulated) robustness tests. With regard to the antecedents of boundary resource employment, we alternatively ran a panel logit with random effects and also tested alternative correlation structures in the GEE model and found similar results. We also tested whether using a probit model would affect our results and again found similar results. Finally, we considered that some firms may adopt multiple APIs in one year and tested the impact on the number of API adoptions and again found similar results. With regard to the performance effect, we also tested the number of API adoptions in a year and found consistent results. Furthermore, we considered even longer lead times for the performance effect and tested the impact of API adoption over three years. We again found similar results to those reported.

Additional test – drivers of the performance effects. In an additional test, we aimed to further investigate the drivers of the performance effect of API adoptions. Based on our arguments regarding the hypotheses, we expected that APIs could, for example, serve as an efficient distribution channel that increases revenues and profits. While we would expect increases in revenue on average, gaining superior profits might be more difficult. Specifically, adopting and employing APIs are certainly associated with considerable administrative costs that need to be compensated for by additional margins. Regarding APIs for resource openness, this might be particularly difficult as the external contributors also aim to benefit from their participation. Therefore, we decided to investigate the impact of API adoption on net sales alongside the operating margin in the next two vears. Market power could help in the acceptance of APIs and thus increase their sales impact. It may also affect how firms can translate additional revenue into profits. Hence, we also considered the moderating effect of market power in the additional analysis. Table 8 presents the results of the additional analyses.

The results in Table 8 indicate that both types of APIs were associated with significant increases in sales. We also observed that market power positively moderates the relationship between API adoption and sales. Regarding the impact of API adoption on the operating margin, the results show a more nuanced picture. Specifically, we found a positive and significant impact of the adoption of APIs for access openness on the operating margin for firms with a higher market power, while the influence of the adoption of APIs for resource openness is not significant irrespective of the firm’s market power. Overall, although the additional analysis suggests that APIs are effective in increasing revenues, it is difficult to extract superior margins from APIs. While market power could help increase the margin effect of APIs, this holds true only for APIs for access openness. Table 9 summarizes the results and effect sizes of the additional analyses. ## Discussion of findings

Antecedents of boundary resource deployment

Our findings suggest that firms with pre-existing digital knowledge opt to deploy APIs. This result underscores that these firms may be eager to leverage their digital knowledge by sharing it with the public and, concurrently, want to retain some form of control over their valuable assets (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013). Accordingly, the findings indicate that firms possessing digital knowledge do not generally perceive boundary resources as unnecessary as to their own competence or as a particular threat to this competence but value and pro-actively seek the confined openness provided by boundary resources. The high intensity of boundary resource deployments by actors in digital industries, which can be assumed to possess substantial digital knowledge, lends support to this

interpretation (Parker et al., 2017; Karhu et al., 2018).

Interestingly, our empirical analysis revealed that firms that rank high in digital knowledge tend to deploy APIs that provide access openness. In this context, we know that firms that are rich in digital knowledge are particularly apt to pursue digital innovation (Kohl and Melville, 2019; Hanelt et al., 2021b). While internal digital knowledge may help develop digital innovation components, con necting them to external actors is particularly important to account for the distributed and combinatorial nature of digital innovation (Yoo et al., 2010; Yoo et al., 2012). The deployment of boundary resources might be particularly helpful in this regard, as they represent a new means to mediate interactions and exchange in digital contexts (Gawer, 2021; Dahlander et al., 2021). Here, to achieve network effects (Boudreau, 2012; Boudreau and Jeppesen, 2015), APIs may be used to increase the reach of specific offerings with the help of others. In addition, APIs create new distribution and commercialization opportunities for digital offerings (Gawer. 2021: Dahlander et al., 2021), which is why they might be deployed particularly by firms that have previously invested in their digita knowledge and now aim to realize profits from resulting innovations. Our analysis further revealed that firms with digital knowledge do not deploy APIs for resource openness significantly more than other firms. This particular insight might indicate that firms with less digital knowledge are also inclined to deploy APIs for resource openness to learn and draw valuable knowledge from their externa environment (Kohli and Melville, 2019; Hanelt et al., 2021b).

Furthermore, we found that the existence of digital complementors in the field is positively associated with boundary resource deployments. At least two potential explanations emerge. First. the presence of potential complementors, such as digital ventures (Skog et al., 2018), might threaten established firms based on a perception that these firms could be potential competitors or disruptors (Lucas and Goh. 2009: Karimi and Walter. 2015). Driven by that perception. firms may feel especially alert to engage in digital innovation to defend against disruption (Henfridsson and Yoo, 2014), which could drive the decision to deploy APIs (Svahn et al., 2017). Second, it might be that established firms see digital ventures as potential complementors and want to steer them in their direction by setting up APIs (Yoo et al., 2012). Since digital business ecosystems are regularly characterized by the notion of coo petition (El Sawy and Pereira, 2013), it is likely that both aspects are true. Relatedly, the confined openness provided by boundary resources seems to afford the deploying firm a way to cooperate with these actors in a controlled manner

Our analysis further revealed that firms that face the existence of digital complementors in their field tend to deploy particularly those boundary resources that allow for resource openness. This finding indicates that deploying firms seek to leverage the creative potential of digitally sayyy actors in the co-creation of value (Parker et al., 2017). The experiences of an incumbent automobile manufacturer embracing digital innovation provide an illustrative example; to explore new design options, APIs were used to “give third-party developers the opportunity to use in-car resources, such as sensor data, screens, and loudspeaker systems, for developing applications.” Accordingly, firms that are surrounded by digital complementors seem to recognize the resulting opportunities to diversify their digital innovation efforts and thus aim to stimulate external contributions by deploying corresponding boundary re sources (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013; Gawer, 2021).

## Performance implications of boundary resource deployment

As to the consequences of boundary resource deployments, on the one hand, we can derive the insight from our data that deploying APIs, on average, lacks a significant performance implication. This finding could indicate that companies may deploy APIs but probably could not manage such new technologies properly, which might result in a lower realization of the potential economic benefits. For instance, incumbent automotive manufacturers have been found to struggle to realize the potential of APIs (Svahn et al., 2017). Also, some firms might invest in API deployment not for rational reasons but to follow management fashions (Abrahamson, 1991; Baskerville and Myers, 2009) emerging from the omnipresent discourse and uncertainty related to digitalization (Tumbas et al., 2018).

On the other hand, our findings provide a differentiated picture regarding the specific performance effects depending on the firm’s market power and the two types of boundary resource deployments (APIs for access and resource openness). Our results suggest that firms with higher market power deploying APIs for access openness benefit from higher performance, while we do not observe such beneficial effects when deploving APIs for resource openness. The nature of boundary resources can reveal why market power might be a necessary condition for performance outcomes, whereas the varving functions of boundary resources can explain how these outcomes materialize. With regard to the former, boundary resource deployments are just an initial step to profit from co-creation and cocapture of value. The consecutive step is represented by external actors that actively utilize boundary resources to. for instance leverage the provided assets in their contexts (Selander et al., 2013: Parker et al., 2017). Firms that rank high in market power might represent attractive partners for external audiences due to their established brands, customer bases, and the quality of products or services (Eaton et al., 2015; Song et al., 2018). External stakeholders might project not only a high level of legitimacy on these powerfu actors (Eaton et al., 2014) but they also could perceive the chance to profit themselves from these assets or ultimately reach larger audiences within their contexts and related business activities (Karhu et al., 2018; Song et al., 2018). However, as boundary resources have also varying functions with distinct purposes and requirements, their specific effects on a firm’s performance may also vary. Accordingly, the key purpose of APIs for access openness is distributing existing digital innovations to external parties that can utilize them in their contexts (Selander et al., 2013; Gawer, 2021). Consequently, these digital assets need to constitute a certain degree of maturity and quality to provide added value to the targeted audiences, thus providing inherently greater commercialization oppor tunities. In contrast, for APIs for resource openness, the commercialization phase might remain far away (Parker and van Alstyne, 2018; Gawer, 2021). Moreover, regarding the associated costs, developing and maintaining APIs for resource openness could require greater financial and technological resources due to the more complex coordination and governance of heterogeneous complementor activities and contributions (Eaton et al., 2015; Karhu et al., 2018). In addition, complementors could strive to capture larger parts of the generated revenues stemming from their contributions (Eaton et al., 2015; Karhu et al., 2018), which could lower the probabilities of realizing significant performance-related benefits from APIs for resource openness.

Furthermore, our additional analysis provides more insights into why firms with market power particularly benefit from boundary resource deployments for access openness. First, we found that both types of boundary resource deployments are effective for increasing revenues, which is also further augmented by a firm’s market power. This finding parallels our arguments that boundar resources support firms in commercializing and diversifying their digital offerings (van de Ven, 2005; Yoo et al., 2010; Boudreau, 2012) – all of which stabilizes and increases revenues. Second, however, our results also suggest that firms struggle to realize superior margins by deploying boundary resources. In particular, firms cannot benefit from increases in their margins when deploying boundary resources for resource openness, even if their market power is high. This can arise from governing the complex interactions with external actors and, in particular, from their ambition to capture parts of the generated revenues (Eaton et al., 2015; Karhu et al., 2018), thus further limiting the firm’s ability to realize superior margins. Regarding boundary resources for access openness, we also did not find that firms realize superior margins on average. However, firms with higher market power can extract higher operationa margins, as they deploy boundary resources for access openness. Presumably, firms with less market power might be obliged to offer their products at lower price levels to convince audiences of their offerings and thus intend to increase their respective market shares, which, in turn, makes it difficult to extract higher operational margins from API deployment. In contrast, firms with relatively high market power, once again, might benefit from infrastructural control (Eaton et al., 2015) and lock-in effects of customers (Zhu and Zhou, 2012) and, concurrently, from technological infrastructures that unlock economies of scale and thus have greater potential to extract higher operational margins (Tilson et al., 2010).

## Implications

Our study contributes to IS research related to boundary resources in the following ways. First. we provide insights into the internal and external contextual factors that drive firms to deploy boundary resources. With our investigation catering to the strategic ratio nales of deploying firms, we complement work that has particularly focused on the influences of external actors on boundary resource deployment (e.g., Eaton et al., 2015; Parker et al., 2017). Second, complementing work that has described boundary resources as a means “to define openness” (Karhu et al., 2018), we provide insights into which firms opt for which type of openness. Third, we provide a detailed and differentiated investigation of the performance implications of boundary resource deployments, complementing work that has only implicitly or conceptually dealt with financial outcomes (Karhu et al., 2018; Parker and van Alstyne, 2018; Dahlander et al., 2021). From these contributions, we derive the following implications.

The era of digital business ecosystems and digital innovation is said to require increased openness in a firm’s business and inno vation activities (Yoo et al., 2012; El Sawy and Pereira, 2013). Concurrently, opening up reduces a firm’s ability to control its assets and the operations of external actors (Karhu et al., 2018; Parker and van Alstyne, 2018). The concept of boundary resources has been suggested as a theoretical device to deal with and mitigate this tension by enabling resourcing and securing processes (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013; Eaton et al., 2015). Our work supports that boundary resources cannot be treated as a uniform means to enable confined openness but that we also need to differentiate the purpose for which they are deployed as it renders different types of openness (Dahlander and Gann, 2010; Dahlander et al., 2021).

On the one hand, confined openness can target access, which is particularly selected by firms rich in digital knowledge. The deploying firms seem to utilize boundary resources as a measure of exploitative learning (March 1991) to leverage what they have developed internally. Thus, resourcing (by which the scope and diversity of the focal firm’s offerings is enhanced; Ghazawneh and Henfridsson. 2013) is likely to be enforced in a rather limited way, while securing (by which firms establish control over strategio assets) might be pronounced. From a temporal perspective, the deploying firms seem to utilize such boundary resources, particularly in the commercialization (West and Bogers, 2014) or implementation and exploitation (Kohli and Melville, 2019) phase of innovation. In these more mature stages of digital innovation, firms are particularly prioritizing profit-seeking and thus try to build or maintain boundaries that allow them to capture greater rents from their previous investments (Parker and van Alstyne, 2018; Gawer, 2021). Accordingly, boundary resources might be deployed particularly for facilitating the exchange and transactions of established digita innovations (Gawer. 2021: Dahlander et al.. 2021).

On the other hand, confined openness can target resources that are particularly selected by firms surrounded by digital com plementors. The deploying firms seem to utilize boundary resources as a measure of explorative learning (March, 1991) to enable cocreation with external actors. Thus, resourcing may be particularly enforced by the deploying firm (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013), while securing could be less pronounced as valuable contributions stem from external actors and not from the focal firm. From a temporal perspective, such boundary resources may support the initiation and development (Kohli and Melville, 2019) or obtaining and integration (West and Bogers. 2014) phases of innovation where firms attempt to profit from the widespread and diverse knowledge of others to create new offerings. In these more nascent stages of digital innovation, firms particularly aspire encouraging external contributions, thereby diversifving their offerings (Parker et al., 2017: Gawer. 2021). Here. boundary resources may be deployed particularly for stimulating complementary innovations upon the provided resources (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson, 2013; Gawer, 2021).

Overall, distinguishing different types of boundary resources, combined with the antecedents we have investigated, provides a differentiated perspective on the quality of the confined openness provided by boundary resources, the balance of resourcing and securing mechanisms, and their use in innovation processes by the deploying firms. This perspective enriches our understanding about the embeddedness of boundary resources and their design in the rationales and motivations of the associated actors (Eaton et al., 2015).

Relatedly, our study further illuminates the dynamics of coopetition in the digital age (El Sawy and Pereira, 2013). Boundary resources may substantially influence actor roles in co-creation within digital business ecosystems (Biedebach and Hanelt, 2020). Especially, the decision to deploy a particular type of boundary resource by a focal firm may instantiate ties to specific non-focal actors and their associated contributions. Regarding access openness, the contributions of external actors may be more distributive in nature, while the contributions of the focal firm have been rather creative. Accordingly, regarding resource openness, the contributions of external actors are rather creative, while the focal firm distributes an infrastructure for innovation and growth (Eaton et al., 2015; Huang et al., 2017). Thus, by deploying boundary resources, focal actors make a specific offer for a particular type of cooperation with the public (Karhu et al., 2018; Gawer, 2021). However, the degree to which this invitation is accepted by non-focal actors and, therefore, the degree to which potential benefits of the cooperation can manifest for the focal firm are substantially influenced by its market power. Non-focal actors not only search for the best functionality provided by others (Selander et al., 2013) or decide based on customer insights but also examine which digital infrastructure they choose to scale their offerings (Huang et al., 2017; Song et al., 2018). Size seems to matter in digital innovation and the underlying ecosystems, where network effects are aspired to and winnertakes-it-all perceptions dominate innovators’ rationales (Gawer, 2014, 2020). These reflections provide new perspectives on the interactive functioning of distributed and combinatorial digital innovation (Yoo et al., 2010) and the scaling of digital innovation (Huang et al., 2017).

Also, our work provides an interesting contextual differentiation in boundary resources research. Prior literature has predominantly dealt with purely digital contexts (Karhu et al., 2018). Regarding digitalization across industries (Tilson et al., 2010), however, the topic of boundary resource deployment is becoming relevant in all types of business contexts. Our study revealed the widespread dissemination of boundary resources across various industries and different contexts. Particularly in contexts that do not belong to the IT or digital industries, the decision to adopt boundary resources might be embedded in a larger process of digital transformation (Hanelt et al., 2021a). Interestingly, we found that firms with superior digital knowledge (i.e., firms that are advanced on the digita maturity curve) are not more likely to deploy boundary resources for resource openness than other firms, but they are more likely to deploy boundary resources for access openness. These firms do not seem to seek help in developmental processes but seek help in scaling the reach of digital innovation. Conversely, firms that are yet to explore and develop their digital business strategies (Bhar adwaj et al., 2013) might learn in parallel or through the deployment of APIs. However, such firms, based on inexperience and un certainty, might also follow trends and hypes in deploying APIs. Relatedly, history might be influential in this regard in various ways – due to socio-technical or socio-cognitive inertia (Besson and Rowe, 2012). For instance, firms that have achieved success in the past in non-digital contexts might strongly adhere to a supply-chain logic, which might run against the platform logic in digital(izing) business environments (Gawer and Phillips, 2013). Accordingly, the particular organizational surroundings of boundary resource deployment become more relevant in the era of digitalization and provide valuable insights into managerial decisions about and the effectiveness of boundary resource deployment.

Additionally, our study has important implications for managerial practice. In the discourse revolving around digital trans formation, managers might reflect on the deployment of boundary resources such as APIs. First, our study informs managers about the internal and external factors that drive firms to deploy certain types of boundary resources – an insight that is relevant to both assessing the strategic moves of competitors and reflecting on the firm's own situation. Second, our study revealed that setting up APIs lacks a performance effect per se. When the goal is to profit economically from APIs, the right conditions should be in place. Here, firms with superior market power in particular can expect positive returns. This, however, is primarily driven by deploying APIs for access openness. More specifically, although both types of APIs are effective in increasing revenues, only APIs for access openness allow firms with high market power to extract operational margins. Consequently, it is vital that firms consider their market position and bar gaining power when they assess whether the benefits of deploying boundary resources can outweigh the costs. Finally, firms need to understand that in digital(izing) business environments, they are embedded in an ecosystem of increasingly heterogeneous and diverse actors with various requirements and demands. Accordingly, they should focus on optimizing the quality of co-creation on their part and be extra-sensitive to hubris and false assumptions. Furthermore, managers should not perceive APIs as a means to substitute for their own R&D efforts but as a means to gather valuable complements and profit from knowledge (re-)combination (Dong and Yang 2019). Here, digital entrants should not only be viewed as threats but also as potential co-creators.

## Limitations and future research

Despite its theoretical and practical contributions, our study exhibits some caveats worth mentioning. First, we restricted our sample to publicly listed companies in the US market based on the S&P 500 stock index. The adoption and intensity of boundary resource deployment can be influenced by the fact that the most valuable and impactful tech companies in the world (e.g., Google or Amazon) originate from and are headquartered in the US. Future research in other international markets could thus help validate and expand our empirical findings. Second, we acknowledge that our focus on digital start-ups as digital complementors is not without drawbacks. Specifically, more mature firms could probably act as digital complementors. As indicated by the related literature. we expected that digital start-ups are particularly inclined to draw on existing assets and infrastructures and act as complementors for incumbent firms (Eaton et al., 2015; Huang et al., 2017; Tumbas et al., 2017). Future research should further explore the use of and contributions to the boundary resources of peer firms by established digital firms. Third, we focused on APIs as boundary resources. Therefore, the analysis of additional boundary resources (such as software development kits; SDKs) might promise fruitful insights that could extend our understanding of different deploying strategies related to other types of boundary resources.

In our empirical study, we pursued a crisp and clear focus regarding the antecedents and consequences of boundary resource deployments. However, other aspects in this regard remain unanswered. Regarding antecedents, we investigated specific factors that seem particularly important in their relation to the key traits of digita innovation. Thus, future studies should not only investigate additional antecedents but also provide insights into how their interactions might influence a firm’s decision to deploy boundary resources.

Concerning the types of boundary resource deployments, to establish a more holistic understanding of distinct types and char acteristics (e.g., associated technologies) of boundary resource deployments and their relationships, future research should derive more detailed insights through, for instance, the development of specific taxonomies for boundary resources (Nickerson et al., 2013). Furthermore, future studies could investigate how distinct types of boundary resources interact with different target groups, exploring more of the activities and particular needs of the user side of boundary resources (Eaton et al., 2015; Karhu et al., 2018).

Regarding consequences, future in-depth case studies could explore specific factors inside the firm, such as path dependency (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990; Lane et al., 2006) or internal conflicts (Lucas and Goh, 2009; Svahn et al., 2017). They could also examine how the firm's contexts (e.g., industry, digital complementors) exactly influence the effective deployment of boundary resources. In addition, future research can consider developing more insights into particular outcomes on the user side of boundary resources (Benlian et al., 2015; Parker et al., 2017). Finally, future investigations can also attempt to derive long-term effects of boundary resource deployments on firm performance to further complement and advance our understanding of the pervasive and lasting impact of digital innovation and associated technologies on contemporary firms.
