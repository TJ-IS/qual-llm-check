## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Scaling agility Taxonomy development Qualitative research

## A B S T R A C T

Driven by environmental uncertainty, many organizations today attempt to achieve agility at scale. However, given the variety and complexity of these efforts, there is currently a limited understanding in terms of their most relevant configurations, especially over the process of change. Based on an iterative taxonomy development approach grounded in design science, we present a taxonomy to structure, configure, and update scaling agility. The resulting multidimensional classification system offers an integrative view on elements only selectively considered in prior research. While offering a conceptual anchor for further research, we also provide specific configurations covering three different stages of scaling agility.

## Introduction

Scaling agility can be understood as a process of diffusing the initial adoption of agile concepts (e.g. principles, methods and practices) to additional organizational units (Fuchs and Hess 2018). Scaling agility is currently being adopted in a range of industries at different management and operational levels, and thereby shaping new organizational forms (Denning 2016). As organizations seek to scale agility, they attempt to understand and more dynamically exploit new digitalization opportunities for process, service, or product innovations (Bharadwaj et al. 2013). Scaling agility should build superior organizational capabilities to better grasp customer needs and respond timely and effectively to them through accelerated innovation cycles (Denning 2013; Denning 2018b). The change process that it is required to scale agility seems propitious, yet arduous. In fact, a recent report from McKinsey (a management consulting company) indicates only 4 percent of the 2,500 surveyed companies to have succeeded in reaching improved organizationa agility (Smet et al. 2018).

The transition to agility at scale has stimulated many studies in the intersection between organizational management and information systems (IS) research. Recent systematic literature reviews indicate that scaling agility studies have generally dealt with operational and organizational aspects of the transition. However, there is not sufficient research informing our understanding about strategic aspects of the change, in particular on a governance mechanism to support multiple change configurations over time (Dikert et al. 2016; Limaj and Bernroider 2019). Furthermore, extant literature points out that organizational changes in the context of scaling agility follow atypical patterns (Fuchs and Hess 2018), and require customized process models (Jovanovi´c et al. 2017b; Pikkarainen et al. 2012). While valuable knowledge has been offered by these prior studies, no attempts have been made to compile configurations of scaling agility initiatives in today’s businesses on the basis of an overall classification system guiding researchers and supporting business practitioners to handle the required transition processes in appropriate ways.

In an attempt to close these gaps, this study’s aim was to develop a taxonomy to shed light on configurations of scaling agility across different stages of organizational change. In general, such comprehensive conceptualizations are valuable to organize the knowledge about a topic of interest in a meaningful way (Doty and Glick 1994). They are also useful to grasp and analyze the structure of a complex phenomenon (i.e. scaling agility) and theorize about differences and commonalities between concepts (Beaulieu et al. 2015). Against this backdrop, we designed this research study based on the taxonomy development approach proposed from Nickerson et al. (2013. Accordingly, the data used for analysis are a combination of primary and secondary data, comprising 18 in-depth interviews conducted with change agents (i.e., agile coaches and senior executives in the banking industry), and studies from agile and change literature selected through a rigorous, systematic process.

The remainder of the paper proceeds with theoretical considerations of the agile concept for management and organizational change. Next, we outline the research methodology used to develop the taxonomy, before presenting the results. Then, we show how the taxonomy can be used to configure and update scaling agility along different stages of change. Finally, we discuss key implications of the study for IS research and provide actionable recommendations for managerial practice dealing with scaling agility, before pointing out study limitations and an outlook for future research.

## Background

## The agile concept and scaling agility

The ‘Agile’ term was first used in an organizational context from Goldman et al. (1995) in an attempt to represent modern management concepts that would help companies to survive in highly competitive and quickly changing environments. However, since then, the ‘agile’ term has received different interpretations in the literature (Limaj and Bernroider 2019). A prevailing number of studies define ‘agile’ as a method (or practice, or approach), which for instance, can be adopted to “regularly produce high quality software in a cost effective and timely manner via a value driven life-cycle” (Hobbs and Petit 2017, p.4). Others view ‘agile’ as a capability, for instance, commonly referring to it as “the ability of an enterprise to survive and prosper in a competitive and unpre dictable environment by responding quickly and effectively to any kind of change – anticipated or unpredicted – in proper ways and due time” (Nejatian et al. 2018, p.202). Or, more specifically tied to the customer’s perspective, agility allows “to create, react to, embrace, and learn from change while enhancing customer value” (Mikalsen et al. 2018, p.117). Nevertheless, all these streams of interpretations seem to have their roots in the Agile Manifesto (Beck et al. 2001), a document which was originally created to correct the weaknesses of traditional software development by offering a new baseline to design and develop faster and better software. The Agile Manifesto contains four core values and twelve principles, which emphasize responsiveness to change, customer involvement, trusting employees, empowering teams, face-to-face communication, and short product release cycles.

Organizations in a variety of contexts nowadays are changing their way of thinking, operating, and organizing by adopting agile concepts in pursuit of business success (i.e., commonly referred to in the literature as ‘the agile way of working’). Apart from the software industry, agile concepts are being applied in banking, telecommunication, education, retail, and the mass media industry (Limaj and Bernroider 2019). The adoption of the agile way of working can happen at different organizational levels (e.g., individual, team, proiect, or the entire organization). Scaling agility (also referred to in the literature as agile at scale' or agile transformation' or ‘agile transition’) can have many forms (Fuchs and Hess 2018). However, it can be generally regarded as a change process of emulating the agile way of working from a fragment of the organization (e.g., one team or one division) across the entire organization (Dingsøyr et al. 2018). For instance, a recent case of scaling agility in the banking industry has been the transformation of ING bank from a traditional bank to a platform bank (Calnan and Rozen 2019).

Scaling agility is a highly complex process that can be configured in many ways. However, in general terms it can be considered to develop along a non-agile to fully agile continuum associated with different stages of change (Conboy 2009). In this respect, Laanti and Delta (2017) propose five stages of scaling agility: (i) beginner, (ii) novice, (iii) fluent, (iv) advanced, and (v) world-class. Somehow similar, Lal and Clear (2018) consider three stages of scaling agility: (i) prior to agile, (ii) hybrid agile, and (iii) agile. Other studies use various conventions, however, the 3-stage abstraction seems to be the most widely used to describe scaling agility cases in literature (e. g. Dikert et al. 2016; Fuchs and Hess 2018). Hence, in this research we consider scaling agility as a process having an element of temporality that captures three stages of change, namely what we call (i) beginner, (ii) intermediate, and (iii) advanced.

Considering past cases reported in the literature, we understand that there is no clear difference between the three aforementioned scaling agility stages. We also note that different organizations considered in the same change stage might have very diverse con figurations of how they scale agility. Hence, we refrain ourselves from attempting to delimit each stage in terms of what events can happen and rather suggest to envision the differentiation between the stages in terms of the level of change outcome. In this regard, we consider the beginner stage typically to reflect the smallest scale of change and to have a minor to moderate effect on the speed and flexibility of the organizations. In a similar way, we conceive the intermediate stage to reflect considerable organizational changes and to have a substantial influence on organizational speed and flexibility. The advanced stage concludes the journey of the scaling agility process reflecting a completely changed organization, which is fully agile in terms of reaching superior speed and flexibility.

## Prior literature on scaling agility

Extant research on scaling agility generally deals with selective facets of organizational change that are associated with the agile transformation process. For instance, a common approach considered in the literature to scale agility is to focus on the organizationa design aspect and explore the role of scaling models (see Appendix D) to conduct an organizational restructuring by alternating current organizational structures with agile structures (Mikalsen et al. 2018; Razzak et al., 2017). Another dominant approach in prior related research targets organizational culture (Livari and Livari 2011; Tolfo et al. 2011) by considering how employees think, and their basic values and beliefs that need to be aligned with the “agile mindset”. A common approach in support of this change seems to be the recruitment of new staff already familiar with the agile way of working and attached with the required agile mindset (Conboy et al. 2011). Another stream of research on scaling agility considers the organization’s technical infrastructure – for example, by analyzing strategies related to legacy information systems which may hinder agile operations and continuous business model renewal (Lima et al. 2020).

While recognizing that a combination of different change aspects (i.e., dimensions) considered in prior research can be used to configure and reconfigure the process of scaling agility (depending on the stages of change), we also note that scaling agility involves complementary changes of multiple dimensions. However, to the best of our knowledge, the literature has been silent on combining al major change dimensions across different stages of scaling agility. Indeed, even though research has provided extensive findings in terms of organizational change approaches in general (Todnem 2015), the state of research on scaling agility was described as being only “initial” in a recent and comprehensive literature review (Fuchs and Hess 2018, p. 3). We consider the development of a guidin integrative framework as essential to increase the clarity of the scaling agility concept and related change processes.

## A theoretical framework of organizational transformation

Since agile principles and methods need to be applied continuously at the organizational level to a wide range of structures systems, and processes, our main challenge was to offer a guiding framework that is sufficiently generic and flexible to be useful to a wide range of applications but also sufficiently detailed to support the organizational change. For this purpose, in our analysis we broadly refer to a generic approach including context, content, and process perspectives on organizational transformation, which was originally proposed by Pettigrew (1985; 1987; 2012). The strength of this approach is to describe and analyze strategic organizational change in a context and thereby illustrate how and why these changes unfold in a process characterized by alternate stages of radica and incremental change periods. More specifically, three perspectives provide a general source of intertwined questions to guide strategic change on the backdrop of an ongoing legitimization and de-legitimization of ideas. The context perspective captures the “where” of the transformation in terms of the internal or external environment, the content perspective refers to “what” and for “what purpose” the organization is transformed, and the process perspective refers to “how” the transformation is implemented. Therefore, the use of this model should capture both strategic and operational views, as well as their interconnections, which according to Pettigrew are conditions for theoretically sound and practically useful findings. In prior IS research, there is widespread support for using this transformational lens to evaluate IS-enabled change (Bernroider et al. 2013; Stockdale and Standing 2006; Symons 1991). More recently, prior research has drawn on the Pettigrew's model in the areas of strategizing (Concannon and Nordberg 2018) and in particular on the dynamic interlinking of organizational change formulation and execution processes (Chanias et al. 2019), which also apply to this study on scaling agility.

## Research methodology

There have been various attempts in prior research to grasp classificatory efforts – termed most notably as taxonomies, typologies, or frameworks. However, these terms seem to be often confused in the literature and have been used interchangeably (Nickerson et al. 2010). While both Sanchez (1993) and Bailey (1994) seem to agree that taxonomies differ from typologies mainly based on how you derive the classification (viewing the former mainly as conceptual and the later as empirical), other studies use mostly the term taxonomy for either conceptual or empirical derived groupings (Nickerson et al. 2010). The term framework has been also used for organizing obiects (Schwarz et al. 2017). Bailey (1984). however, proposes a more nuanced procedure, which he terms a two-level model (which he extends later into a three-level model) that includes one conceptual typology analysis and one empirical taxon omy analysis (and respectively one combined conceptual/empirical analysis) to reach a final classification. Yet,

Nickerson et al. (2013) point out that Bailey’s approach does not match with design science principles that view artifact devel opment as a “search process” (Hevner and Chatterjee 2004, p.88). Hence, they propose a procedure that is not constrained to a single conceptual and/or a single empirical analysis but combines as many iterations of them as needed until the ending conditions (i.e., rules for termination) are met. They recognize that this procedure could refer to any of the classification forms (i.e., taxonomy, typology, or framework). However, aiming for “common recognition over precision”, Nickerson et al. (2013) decide to call it a method for tax onomy development.

Our strategy for grasping the phenomenon of scaling agility is based on an incremental approach of building cumulative knowledge by applying the taxonomy development procedure proposed from Nickerson et al. (2013). The procedure in itself is not only an artifact (i.e. method) but also results in an artifact (i.e. model) that helps to “provide useful explanations of the nature of the obiects under study or of future objects” (Nickerson et al. 2013). Hereby, we primarily aim to create a useful model but not necessarily an ‘ideal model. which is usually hard to define and unrealistic for IS research as it can change over time (Hevner and Chatteriee 2004), Nickerson’s method is a derivate of design science research and is composed of two main research activities – namely, artifact building and artifact evaluation (March and Smith 1995).

With regard to artifact building, Nickerson et al. (2013) point out three main steps in their taxonomy development procedure. With regard to the first step (i.e., determining the meta-characteristic), we specify that the users of our taxonomy are concerned with the change aspects of scaling agility. This includes in particular organizational leaders (i.e., executives), agile coaches, and researchers across organizational management and IS disciplines dealing with this topic. The purpose of the taxonomy is to identify dimensions and characteristics that elucidate the course of change for organizations transforming to an agile way of working. Thus, in line with our research aim we formulate the following meta-characteristic: Guiding framework of organizational change for scaling agility. With regard to the second step (i.e., determining the ending conditions), in Table A.1 (see Appendix A) we point out the objective and subjective ending conditions that we adopted from Nickerson et al. (2013). In the third step, we iteratively apply conceptual-to-empirical and empirical-to-conceptual approaches until the ending conditions are met. In line with design science evaluation phase, we consider a fourth step, i.e., result validation. The last two steps are described in more detail in the following.

## Taxonomy development and data analysis

We followed the approach proposed by Nickerson et al. (2013 to combine five iterations of deduction (i.e., conceptual-to-empirical) and induction (i.e., empirical-to-conceptual) to build up the taxonomy. In Table 1. we depict each major iteration that sustained the development of the taxonomy by pointing out the approach chosen, the purpose of the iteration, the utilized source (i.e., data), the type of analysis applied, and the transitional result.

For simplicity's sake. before describing the iterations. we firstly describe all sources of data used for the taxonomy development. which combine primary and secondary data gained from qualitative interviews and literature, respectively. The primary qualitative dataset consists of interview transcripts and notes gathered during two taxonomy development iterations (i.e.. iterations 2 and 4). Table 2 describes the considered 18 in-depth interviews conducted with key stakeholders (i.e., people working at a C-level capacity and agile coaches) of organizations’ scaling agility and associates them to the related iteration as well as to different stages of change. Even though agile coaches sometimes referred to their various experiences in different organizational contexts, most responses related to the banking industry of a European Union country. We deemed this focus as appropriate in iterations 2 and 4 because currently many banks are carrving out scaling agility initiatives (Birkinshaw 2018). In addition, the activity patterns of banking organizations are not essentially different from other digital-oriented organizations that spend big on scaling agility and IT change projects (Denning 2018a). Besides, recent research results show that banks are currently following similar change templates as other non-digital born organizations (from other industries) to scale agility (Gerster et al.; Schuch et al. 2020). Furthermore, the other iterations (1, 3, and 5) for taxonomy development serving initial structure development, extension, refinement, and validation purposes had a genera orientation. In particular, 26 secondary cases from agile scaling literature were included. Thus, we assume that the applicability of the resulting taxonomy is likely to extend to organizations heavily relying on IT beyond the banking sector.

The majority of interviews were conducted face-to-face at company sites. while two interviews were conducted via videoconferencing. We started each interview by explaining the research purpose, interview procedure, and ensuring participants that the data collected will be treated anonymously and confidentially and used for research purposes only. All participants agreed to record the interview, which we guided by semi-structured, open-ended questions. This allowed for rich and extensive answers, resulting in a tota duration of 16 h and 46 min of interview recordings. Finally, we transcribed the interviews line by line, which resulted in 187 pages of primary text used for analysis.

The selection process of the considered literature (depicted in Fig. 1) is based on a systematic approach following methodologically rigorous guidelines pointed out in previous research (Okoli and Schabram 2010; Tranfield et al. 2003; Webster and Watson 2002). We developed a research protocol establishing that the selection of articles will be done based on the search conducted in nine research databases (Levy and Ellis 2006) and used a combination of the following keywords: ‘scaling agility'. agile at scale'. digitalization'. ‘organizational transformation’, ‘organizational change’. From the extracted 1,073 articles, we limited our selection to peer-reviewed full papers published in English in the time between 2007 and 2019 which considered agile as a management practice, resulting in 111 selected articles. Of those, we considered 26 articles which used a case-study methodology in our 3rd iteration of the taxonomy development process. Case study research provided in-depth insights into real-life cases illustrating the complex socio-technica processes of scaling agility to develop the taxonomy. In the last (fifth) iteration, we considered the remaining 85 articles selected from the agile literature using other (than case study) research methodologies as part of the conceptual-to-empirical approach. The predominant methodologies used in these articles included frameworks, conceptual models, empirical surveys, and literature reviews, which helped us to compare and unify the concepts of our taxonomy with a more diverse set from the agile literature.

In the first iteration, we conducted a conceptual-to-empirical analysis reviewing a convenient sample of literature from scaling agility and change theory research. This helped us to expand our understanding of the domain of study. Most importantly, it stimulated the alignment of context, content, and process perspectives proposed by Pettigrew (1985; 1987; 2012) with our meta-characteristic, thereby allowing us to create an orientation for the next iterations in which we based the classification of the additionally identified factors (as depicted in Appendix B1).

In the second iteration, we used an empirical-to-conceptual approach and the data gathered in the first eight qualitative interviews (see Table 2) providing insights from real-life agile transformations. The data served as input for our thematic analysis, which we conducted based on recommendations on how to apply the framework method (Gale et al. 2013). Accordingly, we aimed to produce categories (i.e., dimensions and characteristics) that are essential to describe scaling agility (Braun et al. 2019). We started with coding the data through “careful reading and re-reading” to recognize important information that captures the qualitative richness of the phenomenon (Boyatzis 1998, p. 1; Rice and Ezzy 1999, p. 258). Next, by means of the process of abstraction, we organized emerging patterns around the first line of categories explaining scaling agility and allied them with the related context, content, and process perspectives of change (as depicted in Appendix B2). Considering the ending conditions, the taxonomy at this stage could not be considered robust (given the limited number of dimensions and characteristics), hence another iteration was needed.

In the third iteration, in an attempt to examine more cases to allow for further building up the taxonomy we used again an empirical-to-conceptual approach. The input for this phase was 26 research publications using a case study methodology, as previously mentioned, identified though a rigorous selection process from the agile research literature (see Fig. 1). The analysis at this stage used categories identified in the previous iteration as a starting analytical framework (i.e., scheme) for coding the new data. Each time distinctive codes emerged they were grouped into new clusters (i.e., categories). The analysis resulted in the identification of one new dimension and nine new characteristics, while the naming of three existing dimensions was updated (as depicted in Appendix B3) While the taxonomy was more robust than before and met most ending conditions, another iteration was needed considering that we added a new dimension.

In the fourth iteration, we decided to use another empirical-to-conceptual approach, conducting ten more qualitative interviews in an attempt to extend and refine the taxonomy (see Table 2). The goal for each interview was two-fold. In the first part of the interview. we aimed to gather more insights about scaling agility in the case organizations. In the second part, we aimed to gather specific feedback from the interviewees about the current version of the taxonomy by using the so-called member reflections procedure (Tracy

2010). The procedure of analyzing the data at this stage was similar with the previous iteration and resulted in an extension of one new dimension and thirteen new characteristics in total. Hence, another was needed.

In the last iteration, we used a conceptual-to-empirical approach in an attempt to achieve saturation. We considered new objects from agile literature (i.e., 85 articles extracted using the systematic process illustrated in Fig. 1) in addition to the objects from previous iterations, and compared the categories developed in the taxonomy against all objects. The analysis did not provide any new di mensions or characteristic (only one revised label), yet increased the explanatory power of the taxonomy and provided more evidence facilitating taxonomy description. As the taxonomy met all ending conditions, the next step we performed was its validation. In Appendix C we provide exemplary evidence for the codes identified in the taxonomy development process.

## Result validation

While recognizing that the term ‘validation’ has a different meaning and implications in quantitative research, we understand the validity of our qualitative inquiry as how accurately the resulting taxonomy represents participants’ realities and is credible to them (Schwandt 1997). We used a combination of constructive (during the research process) and post hoc strategies to increase the rigor and quality of our result and the credibility of our study.

During the research process, we applied methodological coherence (Morse et al. 2002), using carefully the taxonomy development procedures until meeting the defined subjective criteria (discussed more closely in Appendix A2). Two researchers were involved in prolonged engagement with case participants building trust and close collaboration, which facilitated information disclosure (Creswell and Miller 2000). We used data triangulation by gathering additional reports and relevant documents from the case organizations, in addition to the primary data (i.e., interviews) and also considering secondary cases from literature. We conducted peer debriefing meetings between the three researchers analyzing the data to share and agree on transitional research outputs and pinpoint next steps towards achieving saturation (Creswell and Miller 2000; Morse et al. 2002).

The post hoc procedure consists of member checks (Creswell and Miller 2000). After establishing a complete version of the tax onomy and its related description, we send it for validation to all interviewees (that participated previously in the fourth iteration) asking for their critique (or affirmation). We received largely encouraging feedback with interviewees pointing out the usefulness of the taxonomy to help them understand scaling agility during various stages of change across different organizational settings. There was a note about changing the label of one dimension, which we considered.

In Table 3, we present our resulting taxonomy of scaling agility which we achieved after combining two conceptual-to-empirica iterations with three empirical-to-conceptual iterations based on guidelines for taxonomy development in IS (Nickerson et al. 2013). The taxonomy captures a variety of different temporal states of the transformation process because we reviewed multiple organiza tional cases using primary and secondary data sources informing different aspects and stages of change. ## Change context

Within the change context, the first dimension of the taxonomy, change scope refers to the range of change or the extent that agile concepts (e.g., values, methods, or practices) are to be scaled in the organization. Based on the interviews, we differentiate between organizations focusing on changing only one unit, those conducting a partial change, and those considering the option of an organi zation-wide change. Only one unit means that in essence only IT development teams will be changing the way they deliver services, for instance, by embracing agile values and employing agile software development methodologies. Partial change implies redesigning only a fragment of the organization, sometimes limited to business units and software development – while other units (such as HR, marketing, or legal) follow traditional patterns. One illustrative case is the creation of DevOps teams merging members from business development with operations (Debois 2009), or complementary to this concept, the more recently coined term BizDev integrating business and software development in terms of strategy, people, and functions (Fitzgerald and Stol 2017). Change, in this case, tends to unfold incrementally and is commonly referred to in the literature as a “step-by-step” rollout, or evolutionary deployment (Mikalsen et al. 2018; Pikkarainen et al. 2012). Organization-wide, on the other hand, implies a full reconstruction of all organizational structures, processes, and systems according to the agile principles. Commonly it follows a radical approach to change, also referred to in the literature as a “big-bang” rollout or revolutionary deployment (Fry and Greene 2007; Pikkarainen et al. 2012).

The second dimension, change agent refers to the actor(s) driving agile-at-scale initiatives. In some cases, during our interviews we noted that change was often initiated and directed from people in upper management positions (e.g., CEO, CTO or CIO), or middle management or other internal agile advocates (i.e., employees promoting the agile way of working – early adopters). In other cases, during the interviews, we recognized the role of the agile coach acting as a mentor and suggesting the course of change. This position is typically fulfilled by a consultant with agile expertise hired from outside the organization (Sidky et al. 2007) or by an internal member of the organizations usually playing a dual role (e.g., change manager and agile coach). Based on cases from the literature, we identified the option of the cross-functional team (acting as a change agent) designing, building, communicating, and running the agile transformation (Fry and Greene 2007). It is pertinent to highlight that organizations typically have more than one change agent, especially as the change process evolves.

The third dimension, reference model refers to the main sources of information which organizations utilize to retrieve guidelines for scaling agility. As we focus on the scaling agility of service organizations aiming to leverage the value of IT to produce service innovations, the first frame of reference that we identified from the literature are service planning models, which uphold the governance of service management. For instance, some popular frames of reference used for IT service management are: Information Technology Infrastructure Library (ITIL), which was recently updated (i.e., version 4) to emphasize agile values, practices, and methods; the Control Objectives for Information and Related Technology (COBIT) framework; and The Open Group Architecture Framework (TOGAF). Other compatible frames of reference used to optimize performance include Lean, DevOps, and Kanban (Axelos 2019). The second frame of reference are the so-called scaling models, which provide guidance to expand the agile way of working organizationwide. The most frequent models used to scale agile mentioned in the interviews include Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe), Large-Scale Scrum (LeSS), and Spotify Model, while the literature also mentions Disciplined Agile Delivery (DAD), Nexus, and Holacracy (Hobbs and Petit 2017: Jovanović et al. 2017b: Spearman and Dolman 2016). An overview of these agile scaling models is provided in Table D.1 in Appendix D. Furthermore, from the literature we identified maturity models as a frame of reference offering a measurement system to assess the ongoing organizational change. These frameworks allow organizations to evaluate the level of agile adoption and its effect on organizational performance. The literature proposes a number of different maturity models, while arguably the most recognized is Gartner's maturity model which guides organizations to assess their fit along five levels of agile adoption from non existent to optimizing (Norton 2008). More recent work has focused on creating measures related to specific scaling frameworks. For instance, Turetken et al. (2017) propose a maturity model for organizations to identify the success level of SAFe adoption. Finally, some agile coaches pointed out in the interviews that they were involved in transformations where organizations were not using any external model. Typically, these organizations are trying to avoid an overwhelming change (e.g., in terms of their practices, processes, and organizational structures) but are rather aiming to let go of their old mindsets and habits and shift towards an agile culture.

## Change content

Within change content. the fourth dimension of the taxonomy, structure refers to what type of formal or informal organizational structural changes can appear when scaling agility. Our analysis indicates three central approaches recognized from our interview data: maintaining an unchanged organizational structure; creating a matrix structure; and updating the current structure in a way that fits the model chosen to scale agile. The first approach is typically used when organizations attempt to increase agility by actualizing their values and principles (e.g., according to the agile manifesto) but the structure of the organization remains unchanged. An alternative approach is suitable when organizations have decided to scale agility more substantially but are still somehow unclear about the change content. context, and scope. Hence. they form new teams with existing emplovees. In addition to their current roles and responsibilities, these employees are assigned new roles and responsibilities as needed to work in an agile team. In this way, the organization creates a ‘double’ structure, also referred to in the literature as hybrid or matrix structure. The last approach is usually related to an overwhelming change in terms of the organizational structure as the entire teams, roles, and responsibilities are reshuffled, based either on guidance from specific agile scaling models (e.g. SAFe) or using an in-house customized model (typicall based on combining some elements from different scaling models) that suits an organization’s particular needs.

The fifth dimension, team development, refers to the extent that various parts of the organization work in an integrated and co ordinated way to create an output that is of value for the customer. The conventional approach of organizations is to configure its operations based on functional silos, which consist of individuals or small teams with a specific area of expertise performing process work (e.g., functions) separately from the rest of the organization (Motingoe and Langerman 2019). We recognize from our interviews that organizations attempting to scale agility are shifting from work assignments within functional silos to inter-disciplinary (also referred to as cross-functional) teams. This approach challenges traditional expertise boundaries, stimulates knowledge sharing and collaboration, and shifts the focus from individual to group performance. For instance, in an attempt to reduce miscommunication and interpretation issues, software companies have been transitioning to DevOps teams (Ebert et al. 2016). This team re-organization is compatible with agile principles that stress empowering autonomous, self-organized groups to conduct end-to-end process work. Another approach, suggested in the literature, consists of creating so-called value streams that include all various stakeholders (interna and external) contributing to the entire service value chain activities (Axelos 2019). Agile organizations have individuals contributing to multiple value streams, as the agile structure enables members of an agile team to take part in several agile teams simultaneously.

The sixth dimension, IS modernization, refers to the efforts an organization can make to rejuvenate their legacy assets as part of the scaling agility strategy. Legacy assets refer to system components that are not compatible with the agile way of working. These may include data, functions, or user interfaces related to business logic encoded in any aging software systems constructed to run on various obsolescent hardware types, programmed in obsolete languages (Weiderman et al. 1997). The ultimate goal of system modernization (in the context of digital transformation) is to create a contemporary digital infrastructure supporting agile operations and practices. We differentiate between four options for system modernization. the first three identified from our interview data and the last one spotted in the literature. The first option is to change the system owner. The organization thereby seeks to alter the people responsible for developing the system in an attempt to foster a natural change of the legacy assets. For instance, appointing a new system developer with cutting-edge programming capacity could lead to a changed source code. The second option is to use wrapping techniques allowing to adapt a new service-oriented interface to a legacy component without changing the core system itself. While this option typically requires relatively little effort and resources, it slightly affects the degree of system modernization. The third option, is to use migration techniques in an attempt to iteratively replace legacy system components. In this respect, our data indicate that some or ganizations conceive a hybrid architecture seeking to balance agility with stability through multi-speed IT, hence changing their system only partially. The fourth option, which was spotted in the literature, is to use the so-called redevelopment (or ‘cold turkey’, or ‘big bang’) technique to rewrite the entire legacy system from scratch, typically resulting in the highest degree of system moderni zation (Stonebraker and Brodie 1995).

## Change process

Within change process, the seventh dimension of the taxonomy, refers to the change strategies organizations may follow when attempting to scale agility. Based on cases from the literature, experimenting and pilot projects are utilized by individuals or pilot teams to try out agile practices and methods in an attempt to generate initial feedback about the agile way of working. This process also help decision makers assess to what extent the transformation might benefit the organization and how it can be best planned (Hallikainen 2011). Mirroring headquarters is a strategy typically used in corporations where the branches follow the same patterns of change as the headquarters (i.e., parent company). In particular, based on our interviews, we recognized that branches usually use the same change agent who leaded the agile transformation in the parent company. The snowball effect is a pattern of change that consists of starting small by applying agile transformation to a limited group of people (e.g., one unit or one department). If the change is successful and creates performance improvements, it is then shared with the rest of the organization and the knowledge gathered is applied to transform the remaining structures one after another. High-level planning is a strategic choice to orchestrate agile change in a holistic manner by formulating beforehand a detailed plan that guides the transformation. This plan should be in alignment with the overal corporate strategy and is typically established by upper management (i.e., governors).

The eighth dimension, human capital development, refers to the activities and developmental efforts related to human resources needed to prepare the organization for change and successfully strengthen its knowledge and human-capital base to conform with the agile way of working. Our interview data supports the view that fundamental efforts and communication activities should be directed towards crafting a change mindset. Usually this consists of finding a compelling and meaningful change purpose that resonates with all stakeholders, explaining the reason why the change needs to happen and how it would benefit each of them (beside the organization itself), as well as increasing information transparency and clarity (e.g., about their new roles and responsibilities). This direction towards establishing a sense of emergence and acceptance of change could help bring everybody on board in terms of setting up their mindset to want to change and committing to embrace an agile culture. Tightly connected but distinctive organizational efforts aiming ultimately to shift to an agile culture and practices could be directed towards instilling agile principles and/or reshaping existing em ployees’ skills and capabilities. Coaching and mentoring could be used to educate the organization and foster employees’ learning of agile principles and ingestion of agile values. Training and the creation of communities of practices could incentivize, instruct, and facilitate the absorption of new skills and renewing capabilities essential for the agile way of working. Other efforts could be directed towards updating the recruitment process – aiming to recognize and bring in external talent who already possess an agile mentality as well as the skillset and competencies to work in an agile environment.

## Discussion

Managers across industries face the need to understand and respond to dynamic customer requirements through accelerated innovation cycles (Denning 2013; Denning 2018b). These tasks are particularly challenging given the complexity of transitioning to agility at scale (Jovanovi´c et al. 2017b; Pikkarainen et al. 2012). This paper addresses the issue by providing a scaling agility framework built through a taxonomy development methodology using an iterative analysis of empirical interview data and literature. In what follows, we discuss our contributions from the perspective of IS research and managerial practice given the purpose and natural boundaries of this study.

## Contributions for IS research

The outcome of this taxonomy development study is an artifact $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ model) and as such a high-level abstraction of scaling agility taking place in business reality. More specifically, we offer a structured and integrative view of design elements (capturing in a comprehensive way change facets of a complex process) that have been only selectively considered in prior research. Indeed, previous research has explored success factors and challenges for scaling agility (e.g., Dikert et al. 2016; Kalenda et al. 2018; Pikkarainen et al. 2012); however, these studies generally focus on particular issues without considering the overall change process. By contrast, this study not only links different change facets but also structures them based on the widely-recognized Pettigrew’s context, content, and process approach (1985; 1987). Thus, we consolidate knowledge that has been offered in a fragmented way in the literature and develop a comprehensive taxonomy that can help scaling agility research move forward – for instance, by stimulating theory building (Doty and Glick 1994). One important characteristic of the taxonomy is that by being generic, it allows different configurations in time, thus taking into account the fluid nature of scaling agility. Researchers in the future investigating scaling agility could use the tax onomy to classify important aspects of the case(s) they are focusing on or to better contextualize their study.

Furthermore, we provide an improved conceptualization of scaling agility as a multilevel and continuous process temporally connecting three phases of change with dimensions that are interrelated. Our findings corroborate the study by Fuchs and Hess (2018) who conceptualized scaling agility as a process composed of episodic changes, by specifying three “sequences of agile waves” and providing a rationale on the transition between waves. In this respect, our data analysis shows that when shifting between the stages of scaling agility (i.e., from beginners to intermediate to advanced), some dimensions of the taxonomy seem dependent on time and are likely to change concurrently (i.e., scope, structure, team development, and IS modernization). To illustrate, the efforts of scaling agility in the beginners stage focused on only one unit, an unchanged structure, functional silos, and legacy IS wrapping. Interestingly enough, in our study we found that the respective case did not create new roles (e.g., agile roles) but chose to add new responsibilities to existing roles, which could potentially lead to role overload (Jovanovi´c et al. 2017a). According to Gersick (1991), between change stages the organization needs to have a period of adjustment attempting to find an equilibrium. Next, we identified an intermediate stage characterized by a partial change, a matrix structure, inter-disciplinary teams, and legacy IS migration. As before, Gersick (1991) here suggests another period of adjustment needed to restore a state of equilibrium and be ready for the next wave of change. Finally, the advanced stage of scaling agility organization-wide was linked with a new agile organizational structure, the creation of value streams, and legacy IS redevelopment. This finding seems to support the idea of interrelated dimensions, which sequentially switch in time reflecting also a natural sequence in the evolving stages of the scaling agility process.

However, the relationships of other dimensions than the ones mentioned above with change stages require a more nuanced interpretation. Regarding the reference model. at the beginners stage the illustrated organization (i.e., Bank B) does not use an external model pertaining to their minimal scope of transformation and incremental approach to change, while a scaling model was used in the later stages (i.e., Bank A and C). In comparison, past research reports that organizations following a more radical approach to change implement a scaling agility model already in their beginners stage, such as in case of ING Bank using the “Spotify” model (Calnan and Rozen 2019). Somehow similar, Paasivaara et al. (2018) stress the importance of using a reference model especially when the scope of change is to conduct an organization-wide agile transformation; otherwise the organization lacks a change direction, such as in case of Ericsson large-scale agile transformation. In short, the reference model dimension seems to be mainly related to the scope dimension (which reflects incremental or radical paths to change), rather than time-bound to the stages of change.

Furthermore, previous research has shown the difficulties that organization have in recognizing upfront strategies to guide changes towards greater agility to meet digitalization requirements (Yeow et al. 2018). Clarifving the strategy is deemed important for the effective management of structural transformations and overcoming barriers to change (Hodgkins and Hohmann 2007). At the be ginners stage, we identified two strategies chosen to scale agility: conducting experiments’ and/or pilot' projects. However. based on our fieldwork, other strategies (i.e., ‘mirroring headquarters’, ‘snowball effect’, and ‘high-level planning’) can co-exist or applied in iterations during different stages of scaling agility. With regard to change agent(s), previous studies stress the importance of deter mining the role of key people that instruct change recipients during the scaling agility process (Akkaya and Tabak 2020). Our tax onomy specifies five potential change agent(s), but as seen in our cases their introduction or activity does not seem to have a clear time dependence related to the stages of scaling agility. Analogously, the efforts towards human capital development consist of four distinguished but mutually reinforcing processes conducted in an ongoing manner, hence without a particular attachment to any of the stages. Indeed, our data indicate that changing towards an agile organizational culture is a long and difficult process subject to switches between ‘crafting a change mindset’, ‘instilling agile principles’, ‘reshaping skills and capabilities’, and ‘recruitment’.

Considering the above, the reference model, strategies of change, agent, and human capital development dimensions do not seem to follow a linear connection with the stages of scaling agility; hence they can be considered time-independent based on our observations. Rather, they depend on relationships between the context of change and its content and process over time. Thus, coupled with the timedependent dimensions, they enable adjusting to different courses of change, capturing the complexity of the process in which orga nizations attempt to scale and optimize agility.

## Contributions for managerial practice

The main contribution of the developed taxonomy is that it allows practitioners to sequentially walk through a framework to structure and update their scaling project in terms of using different configurations when transitioning from one stage of scaling agility to another, without being overloaded. In the following, we point out taxonomy-related actionable recommendations to address some of the main challenges reported in previous studies that organizations experience when scaling agility.

First, previous literature associates a high number of scaling agility challenges to unstable leadership roles with regard to decision making (Heikkila et al. 2017: Hobbs and Petit 2017: Q'Connor 2011). In addition. lack of communication and coordination are also mentioned as main barriers hindering the transformation (Dikert et al. 2016: Hobbs and Petit 2017: Turetken et al. 2017). Our tax: onomy helps practitioners by specifying five potential change agent(s) based on which organizations need to define and establish the internal context in regard to boundaries between accountability and responsibility roles. This implies increasing clarity on the structure of key stakeholders, which according to agency theory could also improve the alignment of agents’ own interest with those of the changing organization (Bryant and Davis 2012).

Second, past research highlights that organizations generally lack effective knowledge needed to scale agility (Paterek 2017). Indeed, there has been reports of organizations using only the SAFe framework having difficulties in applying the framework due to a lack of guidance for its customized implementation (Turetken et al. 2017). In this paper, we portray a diversity of reference models, which are reflected in the proposed taxonomy and could be used simultaneously during various stages of change. In particular, we recommend practitioners to use the proposed taxonomy itself as a reference model to complement the external knowledge contained in other existing frameworks and thus help organizations to tailor their scaling agility projects according to their needs.

Third, there are a number of studies reporting people-related issues in terms of conflicts between traditional and agile roles (Conboy et al. 2011; Stray et al. 2018). During our interviews, we recognized that role alternations were typical for the organizations tran sitioning from the initial stage of transformation to an intermediary stage. Past research demonstrates that role overload negatively influences job satisfaction (Virick et al. 2007). In addition, employees’ resistance to change builds as a result of the impact that change has on them (e.g., overload) rather than refusing change per se (Piderit 2000). Thus, in order to minimize the risk of increasing this type of resistance to change, we recommend practitioners to clearly communicate to employees that alternations are only temporary before advancing to legitimize a new structure.

Fourth, there have been important questions asked in past research about how agile teams actually organize and set team boundaries in practice (Hoda et al. 2010; Jovanovi´c et al. 2017a; Stray et al. 2018). On this subject, we surprisingly realized that a ‘functional silo’ is still one dominating form of work existing in organizations that are scaling agility. This result is in line with previous research arguing that organizations that scale agility dedicate limited attention to improve teamwork internally (Zavyalova et al. 2018). Agile team management and coordination plays a crucial role in improving team productivity (Jovanovi´c et al. 2017a; Melo et al. 2013). Hence, we advise practitioners to dedicate more effort to achieve more harmonious ‘inter-disciplinary teams’ or ‘value streams’ as these two other forms of work are more suited to agile organizations.

Finally, another critical challenge related to scaling agility is the difficulty to close the gap between current legacy IS and the desired modernized IS to enable supporting faster and more flexible operations (Mehrizi et al. 2019; Roman et al. 2015). Our taxonomy contributes by capturing a strategic mechanism for IS modernization by means of four actions: changing the system owner, wrapping, migration, and redevelopment. These actions provide ascending impact on changing legacy systems, because they require increasing time and effort. With this in mind, we advise managers to conduct a continuous assessment of the external (and internal) situation to strategically decide on what actions to implement over time to give the company the required contemporary IS to support agile op erations in the short and long term.

## Limitations and future research

This study provides a conceptual structure capturing important conditions needed to assess scaling agility in organizations. In an attempt to avoid being overly eclectic, we focused on the most important differential factors in developing the taxonomy. This entails that some of the choices we had to make affected the boundaries of the taxonomy. For example, we did not include goals and outcomes in the taxonomy since they exhibited very similar and therefore non-discriminating characteristics among our sample organizations. Future research could, however, expand on the actual impact of reported change states during different stages of scaling agility or other performance-related organizational constructs.

Furthermore, it should be noted that each case considered in the taxonomy development process provided a snap shot of the organizational transformation at a given moment of time and process progression embedded in a particular context of a limited number of organizations from the highly digitalized contemporary banking industry. Naturally, banking had a certain influence in the tax onomy development and the three illustrative cases are also banks. However, we developed and validated the taxonomy also in the context of other sectors and indicated that for most dimensions the characteristics are indeed subject to time and evolving contexts This in turn gives rise to alternative process modes during three stages of scaling agility. Future research on explanations of these strategic changes could not only deal with the fluidity and continuity of change dimensions and their relationships. but also with validating the application of the taxonomy in other sectors.

Finally, in the above discussion we provide a refined interpretation that contributes to a better understanding of the scaling agility process. However, what we offer is not conclusive and only based on the data analysis conducted in this study. More research is needed to empirically examine and further clarify the relationship between the dimensions in light of a temporality perspective, in particular, in terms of investigating if specific sequences or configurations are more efficient for a transition to happen. From a conceptual point of view, this would also help to shed more light on the borders between the scaling agility stages.

## Conclusion

The large-scale application of agile methods (here termed scaling agility) has become a prominent phenomenon in organizations striving to become more agile and thereby more rapidly be able to adapt novel digital products or services to customers. The developed taxonomy of scaling agility provides an important conceptual perspective assisting business practitioners to make decisions at different stages of scaling agility. We acknowledge that these strategic changes are not a one-size-fits-all solution (Fuchs and Hess 2018) and require an understanding of different configurations of the change context, content, and process (Pettigrew 1987) as organizations go through different states of radical and incremental adjustments. Our framework corroborates prior research by offering an integrative classification system based on eight interrelated dimensions and 31 characteristics reflecting the change scope, change agents, reference models, organizational structures, team development, IS modernization, strategy, and human capital development. We also provide recommendations for specific problems in the process of scaling agility and present likely connections between configuration elements, which together with the taxonomy can serve as an orientation for both practitioners and future research

Quality of the empirical taxonomy

The taxonomy meets the following quality criteria. First, the taxonomy is formed based on a clear, well-defined meta-characteristic, which is essential in developing a taxonomy (Nickerson et al. 2013). Second, the taxonomy is concise because it contains eight distinct change dimensions and it satisfies the instructions of Nickerson et al. (2013 to limit the taxonomy in seven plus or minus two di mensions. Third, when comparing the dimensions with each other and the characteristics within each dimension it is easy to distinguish unambiguously between them, which points to a robust taxonomy. Fourth, given the current structure and form, it is easy to add new dimensions and characteristics, hence, the taxonomy is easily extendible. Fifth, each dimension and characteristic is clearly defined and described warranting an explanatory taxonomy.
