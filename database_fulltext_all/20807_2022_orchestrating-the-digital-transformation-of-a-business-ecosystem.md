# Orchestrating the digital transformation of a business ecosystem

## A B S T R A C T

The nature, scope, and impact of digital transformation reaches well beyond the boundaries of a single firm. This suggests information systems research should consider how digital transformation unfolds within business ecosystems that consist of multiple interdependent firms, and how this process can ideally be managed. We pursue this research opportunity by introducing orchestration as a concept through which to view digital transformation in business ecosystems, and by presenting empirical insights from a longitudinal in-depth case study that highlights how a focal firm became the orchestrator of digital transformation in its business ecosystem. We explain that becoming an orchestrator of digital transformation occurs through three distinct phases: initiating, opening-up, and integrating. We also identify the interplay of activities by which a focal firm strategizes, mobilizes, and aligns other actors and their resources, as it orchestrates the digital transformation of its business ecosystem. We conclude by outlining how our work serves as an important foundation for future information systems research and offer managerial guidelines outlining how to orchestrate digital transformation processes within business ecosystems.

## Introduction

The information systems (IS) discipline has investigated the phenomenon of digital transformation for some time: from Andal-Ancion et al. (2003) seminal work on digital transformation of industry incumbents, to a more recent review of the field by Vial (2019). Exploring digital transformation continues to represent a major research topic for IS researchers (Burton-Jones et al., 2020; Chanias et al., 2019; Ross et al., 2018; Sebastian, 2017; Wessel et al., 2021), with Rai (2016, p. v) highlighting that our discipline should “be at the forefront of knowledge creation pertaining to transformative digital phenomena”. However, despite the general disciplinary consent that IT can be transformative for firms, society, and individuals alike (Lucas. et al., 2015; Nambisan et al., 2017), as well as the growing body of knowledge pertaining to digital transformation (Burton-Jones et al., 2020; Vial, 2019), two key areas require further development.

First, IS research studying digital transformation predominantly focuses on individual firms (Dremel et al., 2017; Wimelius et al., 2021). distinct outputs (Sun. 2012), or the development of IT artifacts (Venters & Whitley. 2012). These relatively narrow perspectives have been criticized (Barrett et al., 2015; Wang, 2021) because digital transformation tends to involve holistic change amongst human actors and technology and organizations who collectively alter their configurations (Lusch & Nambisan, 2015; Nambisan & Sawhney, 2011; Paquin & Howard-Grenville, 2013). Put differently, digital transformation is not just about the firm or customers or employees or output or technology, but rather inyolyes complex interactions and interdependencies amongst all of these (Jarvenpaa & Tuunainen 2013; Weill & Woerner, 2017). Consequently, advancing IS research related to digital transformation requires the adoption of holistic perspectives (Agarwal & Lucas, 2005), such as business ecosystems (Lusch & Nambisan, 2015: Weill & Woerner, 2017), that do not favor “parts” over the “whole” (Wang, 2021).

Second, empirical insights that adopt business ecosystem perspectives to investigate digital transformation processes are limited, which implies sufficient managerial guidelines on how to effectively manage holistic digital transformations are also lacking. This is surprising, given that firms looking outside their boundary for innovation is a well-accepted strategy (Chesbrough, 2003; Majchrzak & Malhotra, 2013; Palmi´e et al., 2021). Indeed, there is an implicit assumption that customers and other non-firm actors affected by, or involved in, digital transformation processes remain passive; using and consuming only digital technologies. However, digita transformation requires significant processual changes (Hansen et al., 2011) and interactions among people, organizations, and technologies (i.e., Ross et al., 2018; Ross, 2017; Wang, 2021), which need to be managed and coordinated. This implies that it is important to understand how firms may embark on such a journey by orchestrating digital transformation, and how this process may enfold within business ecosystems (Ivarsson & Svahn, 2020; Kohli & Johnson, 2011).

Our present work aims to address this gap in knowledge by investigating how focal firms become orchestrators of digital trans formation amongst other interdependent actors in their business ecosystem. We draw on empirical findings from a longitudinal indepth case study (Yin, 2017) of digital transformation, set in the context of security services, to explain three aspects: the process by which a focal firm becomes an orchestrator of digital transformation in its business ecosystem; how the focal firm draws upon resources, capabilities, and knowledge from other actors in its business ecosystem; and how orchestration of digital transformation processes affects the focal firm and the business ecosystem overall. As such, our work provides two important contributions to the IS literature.

First. we articulate and explain the process of becoming an orchestrator of digital transformation through the three distinct phases of initiating, opening-up, and integrating. We also identify the interplay of activities with which a focal firm strategizes, mobilizes, and aligns with other actors and their resources, as it orchestrates the digital transformation of its business ecosystem. Consequently, we demonstrate that becoming an orchestrator does not simply imply coalescing autonomous firms into a single entity or hierarchica authority (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006), nor that ecosystem actors are inert entities that merely respond to inducements and constraints arising from their network ties. Rather, our present work highlights that becoming a successful orchestrator of digital transformation requires a focal firm to act as the “essential-player” who stage-manages different elements (including conflicts and tensions) of a digita transformation initiative (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006) with the intention to create benefit for all actors involved. Orchestration thereby conceptually differs from planning (Drucker, 1959) or coordination (Crowston, 1997) that aim to achieve benefits for the coordinating entity (i.e., a focal firm) only (Malone & Crowston, 1994). Ultimately, we position orchestration as a concept that can help IS research develop new insights related to digital transformation of business ecosystems and, in doing so, respond to calls for our discipline to investigate how firms undergo digital transformation bevond their own boundaries (Vial. 2019).

Second, our work is significant for managers attempting to benefit from digital transformation within their business ecosystems. Many firms fail to realize benefits from digital transformation (Sutcliff et al., 2019), with over 70 percent of attempted digital transformation programs considered unsuccessful (Rogers, 2016). Against this backdrop, our work can inspire managers to articulate, implement, and pursue their digital transformation journey with others.

## Theoretical background

## Digital transformation of business ecosystems

Digital transformation describes “a process that aims to improve an entity by triggering a significant change to its properties through a combination of information, computing, communication and connectivity technologies” (Vial, 2019, p. 121). As such, digita transformation is not a simple outcome to be achieved, but a process that involves continuous cultural and structural changes, so that the full potential of digital technologies can be achieved when re-imagining organizational structures (Hess et al., 2016). business models (Bharadwaj et al., 2013) or, as we here argue, business ecosystems.

In light of the societal and managerial relevance of digital transformation, IS research has examined a variety of related topics, including: the strategy and cultural change needed to pursue digital transformation (Chanias et al., 2019), the firm’s capability development (Li et al., 2018), reconfiguring and aligning of internal and external capabilities (Hess et al., 2016; Yeow et al., 2018), internal roles (Westerman, 2016), and the role of leadership therein (El-Sawy et al., 2014). Despite the variety of research topics, a common denominator underlying the existing body of knowledge is the implicit assumption that individual firms are seen as the sole actor to both drive and benefit from digital transformation. Notwithstanding the important contribution prior IS research has made to date, the phenomenon of digital transformation has changed since Andal-Ancion et al. (2003) first explored it. Today, the phenomenon of digital transformation is more complex than ever since the nature, scope, and impact of digital transformation reaches well beyond the boundaries of a single firm. Blockchain, crowdsourcing, open source architectures, analytics, and digital platforms represent but some of the developments that imply digital transformation takes place in larger business ecosystems with multiple interdependent actors involved (Weill & Woerner, 2017). The changing nature of the phenomenon therefore suggests that digital transformation research should consider new perspectives that take the multi-actor nature of the phenomenon into consideration (Markus & Rowe, 2018).

The business ecosystem concept (e.g., Moore, 1993) is becoming increasingly popular in IS scholarship and practice, as it can provide a holistic perspective that includes multiple stakeholders or ‘actors’ in the analysis (Wang, 2021). Business ecosystems describe groups of interdependent actors, including a firm's competitors, complementors, suppliers, customers, and regulatory authorities (Iansiti & Levien, 2004a), as well as the interactions between them that collectively contribute to an intended outcome (Kapoor, 2018). The ecosystem concept has already been established in the wider business and management disciplines, where it has been applied to investigate relationships firms have with external partners (Giudici et al., 2017; Katri et al., 2017), cooperation and the resulting competitive advantages (Wilkinson & Young, 1994), and how a shared vision affects business ecosystem performance (Gulati et al.,

2012). While the IS literature only recently began to apply the business ecosystem perspective (Nambisan et al., 2017; Senyo et al., 2022; Wang, 2021), the notion that IS research related to digital transformation should adopt such perspectives has existed for some time (Lusch & Nambisan, 2015; Weill & Woerner, 2017). This is because the business ecosystem perspective enables us to view in teractions between firms and external partners beyond the dyad previous IS research investigating business partnerships (Bharadwa et al., 2013) – i.e., co-creation agreements (Jarvenpaa and Tuunainen, 2013; Breidbach and Ranjan, 2017) and innovation crowd sourcing (Majchrzak & Malhotra, 2013) – typically focused on. Instead, adopting a business ecosystem lens can help describe “the alignment structure of the multilateral set of partners that need to interact in order for a focal value proposition [e.g., a digital solution] to materialize” (Adner, 2017, pp. 40, emphasis added). As such, a business ecosystem perspective can help us understand how groups of individuals or organizations are connected, and how value based on their collective actions may emerge when investigating digita transformation processes (Pena & Breidbach, 2021).

## Orchestrating digital transformation in business ecosystems

Business ecosystems are an increasingly desirable and often even necessary contexts for organizations attempting to engage with and benefit from digital transformation. One reason is because the knowledge required to solve a particular problem pertaining to digital transformation may be located outside the organization, thus the need to address a deficiency in capacity arises (Wang, 2021). In addition, establishing, accessing, or exploring digital capabilities within a business ecosystem may be a strategic choice: for example, when organizations focus on dynamic capabilities for strategic advantage (Teece, 2018). In either case, organizations need to be able to orchestrate the actions of others within their business ecosystem who, for example, possess knowledge and skills not available internally (Ivarsson & Svahn, 2020).

The orchestration concept has already emerged in strategy (Hinterhuber, 2002), management (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006; Libert et al., 2014), supply chain (Wind et al., 2009), entrepreneurship (Boudreau, 2017), service science (Breidbach et al., 2016), and the marketing disciplines (Perks et al., 2017), where it is typically used to describe the actions of dedicated human actors facilitating valuecreating interactions for and between others outside of a traditional customer-firm dvad (Breidbach et al.. 2016). Orchestration has also been associated with firms’ leverage over dispersed resources and capabilities of others, and how ecosystems evolve as focal firms become intermediary “centers of gravity” (Ivarsson & Svahn. 2020). Within the IS literature, however, orchestration is typically linked to digital platforms (Breidbach and Brodie. 2015: Kazan et al., 2018), and is viewed in terms of matching problems and needs with potential solutions (Nambisan et al., 2017). For example, the orchestrating role of digital platforms enable focal actors to connect to and activate their networks, tapping into new sources of value (Libert et al., 2014). Regardless of the disciplinary lens taken orchestration is distinct from planning – the “process of making entrepreneurial decisions” (Drucker, 1959, p. 217) that emphasises “future thinking” (Mintzberg, 1981, p. 320). But it is also different from coordination, which involves the decomposition, docu mentation, and alteration of tasks to create new forms of work (Crowston, 1997) for a coordinating entity (i.e., a firm) (Malone & Crowston, 1994). Orchestration also differs from open-innovation, where external sources like customers are used to create innovation (Chesbrough, 2003). In this article, we build upon this literature (Breidbach et al., 2016; Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006; Ivarsson & Svahn, 2020) and define orchestration as an undertaking by a focal actor that intends to integrate and leverage resources and capabilities of others within a business ecosystem. The role of the orchestrator is therefore to identify, integrate, and stage-manage the interde pendent roles, skills, or actions of other actors within a business ecosystem for intended outcomes - such as digital transformation.

While orchestration represents a relevant concept for IS research and practice alike, we do not yet fully understand how and with what activities focal firms can become orchestrators of digital transformation, and what impact the orchestration of digital trans formation has on business ecosystems. Such insights would be of significant managerial importance as they could provide insights into how firms can reach beyond their boundaries to benefit from digital transformation

## Methods

## Research design

Investigating how focal firms become orchestrators of digital transformation processes amongst interdependent actors in business ecosystems represents an emerging research area. When “little is known about a phenomenon” (Eisenhardt, 1989, p. 548), theory building through case studies is an appropriate research strategy (Yin, 2017). We therefore relied on a longitudinal in-depth case study design to examine the 16-month process an established security-services firm undertook to become an orchestrator of digital trans formation within its business ecosystem.

## Case selection

Case selection was not concerned with representing an overall population, but driven by a criterion-based theoretical sampling approach (Miles & Huberman, 1994). This ensured our case represented orchestration of digital transformation in business ecosystems as our phenomenon under investigation. We followed Yin (2017) in developing a case protocol to screen potential cases. First, we aimed to select an organization within the wider professional services context, since operations here tend to be based on a comparatively conservative stance towards digital technology (Breidbach et al., 2013), thus making ‘true’ digital transformation particularly well observable (Westerman et al., 2014). Second, we aimed to identify an organization undergoing a digital transformation process that was deeply intertwined with, and reliant upon, its business ecosystem actors. Third, we ensured the selection of an organization that was able and willing to introduce us to its business ecosystem actors for data collection. We assessed six organizations, of which three were potentially suitable. After conducting screening interviews with all three, we selected “Alpha Security”, a leading security services firm.

## Research context

Alpha Security was founded in Australia in the late 1990 s and has grown to over several thousand employees. They offer pro fessional security services ranging from mobile security patrols, security officers and guards, and security logistics, to personal con cierge offerings to customers in the banking, education, government, and telecommunication sectors. Alpha Security’s motivation to engage in digital transformation processes was driven by its senior management, who saw it as an opportunity to address long-standing productivity challenges in the security services sector, and to ensure its services remained competitive in a fast-changing market.

Alpha Security’s digital transformation journey is centered around two projects the firm was unable to conceptualize, implement, and roll-out alone: Project Omega and Project Sigma. Resolving this problem required Alpha Security to obtain external knowledge and expertise and, in the process, become an orchestrator of a business ecosystem. Project Omega aimed to develop a data analytics system to collect and analyze real-time data to support security patrols. Specifically, Project Omega intended to address growing customer concerns of not having access to real-time data, including limited information related to the location of security guards. In addition, Project Omega aimed to address technical challenges Alpha Security’s guards were facing during their daily patrols (i.e., personal safety tokens to be used in emergencies). Project Sigma aimed to address growing customer concerns related to how CCTV cameras were serviced. Project Sigma aimed to solve this challenge through a platform that intended to support collaboration and communication between Alpha Security’s technicians, customers, and suppliers.

In order to identify and delineate the boundaries of Alpha Security’s business ecosystem, we built on the definition by Iansiti and Levien (2004b) as well as Kapoor (2018) that business ecosystems describe groups of interdependent actors who collectively contribute to an intended outcome (i.e., advancing security services with digital technologies). As such, a focal firm’s business ecosystem may include a firm’s competitors, complementors, suppliers, customers, and regulatory authorities (Iansiti & Levien, 2004b). Fig. 1 pro vides an overview of Alpha Security’s business ecosystem, highlighting Alpha Security as the focal firm, its main competitor (i.e., Beta Security), complementors (i.e., Charlie Technology, Echo, Foxtrot Com, Gamma Technology), customers (Customers A, B and C), and its key supplier (Supplier A).

## Data collection

Data collection commenced in September 2017 and concluded in May 2019. The longitudinal nature of the study implied that, throughout the 16-month period, we maintained close contact with Alpha Security and its business ecosystem actors. Specifically, one of Alpha Security’s employees acted as our ‘central point of contact’. S/he had deep knowledge of the organization, having already spent seven years there. S/he was involved in and understood the firm’s digital transformation initiatives, and introduced us to relevant stakeholders. In addition, ongoing discussions with her/him helped us contextualize emerging findings. We engaged through 106 emails, 46 phone calls, and 25 personal meetings throughout the study.

First. we commenced data collection by developing an initial set of interview questions using existing literature, vet avoided academic language, thus allowing participants to express ideas in their own words (Coviello, 2005). As recommended by Yin (2017), our questions evolved as new insights emerged. Each interview lasted approximately 60 min and took place in-person at Alpha Security’s head office, as well as at other sites where digital technologies were deployed. Our interviewees included representatives from both Alpha Security and its ecosystem partners, which ensured that we captured sufficient data about the digital transformation process and interplay amongst all actors involved. We completed 16 formally-scheduled in-depth interviews, and an additional 10 ‘real-time in terviews’ on-site (Barkey & Kunda, 2001), which enabled us to probe into how and why participants completed certain activities or made certain decisions that we observed or participated in, and resulted in a total of 26 interviews.

Second. observations of formal meetings as well as informal interactions between Alpha Security and other actors of its business ecosystem provided complementary insights into the context of our study (Klein & Myers, 1999). We spent a total of nine hours on site for formal observations of training sessions, meetings, or technology demonstrations that were organized for employees of Alpha Security by partners of its business ecosystem, In addition, we spent approximately 60 h on-site at three different Alpha Security offices for meetings, discussions, and impromptu observations of interactions between business ecosystem actors. We recorded all 69 h of observations using detailed field notes and memos (Angrosino & Mays de Perez, 2000), which allowed us to map the roles of each partner involved in the digital transformation journey.

Finally, we presented emerging findings to senior management on three separate occasions throughout the duration of the study (seven hours in total) to report on the progress of the study, obtain feedback, and gain deeper insights – thereby validating the progress of our study. These sessions typically resulted in Alpha Security or its partners providing us with additional internal documentations (Yeow et al., 2018), which we used to map the progress of the digital transformation journey. For instance, we obtained access to documents outlining benefits of a digital solution to customers, and organizational diagrams that mapped interrelationships between all business ecosystem partners. Overall, the quantity and quality of data collected compares favourably to other studies investigating digital transformation (Vassilakopoulou & Grisot, 2020; Wimelius et al., 2021), and especially those employing longitudinal research designs (Malaurent &. ## Data analysis

We analyzed our data following recommendations by Yin (2017), Miles and Huberman (1984), and Eisenhardt (1989) to support the theory-building process that unfolded through the interplay of data analysis, literature review, and generation of insights (Eisenhardt & Graebner. 2007). Throughout data collection and analysis, to ensure rigour, we adopted an insider/outsider approach with one researcher collecting field data (e.g., through interviews and observation), while others reviewed emerging findings (Davison & Martinsons, 2016). We debriefed regularly to discuss emerging findings (Gioia et al., 2010), and structured our analytical process using Yin’s (2017) stages of compiling, disassembling, and reassembling.

## Findings

In what follows, we describe how Alpha Security emerged as the orchestrator of digital transformation with and amongst other actors in its business ecosystem. We structure our findings across the three phases of initiation, opening-up, as well as integration, highlighting the activities performed in each phase, while also reporting on outcomes as they pertain to Alpha Security and its business ecosystem actors, resulting from each phase.

The initiation of alpha security’s digital business ecosystem transformation

Developing a future vision for alpha security

Alpha Security’s digital transformation journey was initiated by its senior management, who responded to changing customer expectations and the resulting need to upgrade Alpha Security’s then-outdated technical infrastructure (i.e., digital CCTV cameras, remote sensors, and analytics had become available to the security services industry). Undertaking a digital transformation journey was viewed as the much-needed opportunity to address long-standing cultural challenges that plagued Alpha Security and the securityservices sector more broadly. Specifically, the organization had not changed its operations for decades and was overall comparatively risk averse. Consequently, Alpha Security’s values and organizational structure manifested in a relatively conservative stance, which also reflected industry norms. The CEO of Alpha Security and a Senior Operations Executive explained to us:

‘We can provide a much better solution [technology], but because policies haven’t kept up with technology it becomes an obstacle. There are cultural barriers, and it takes time, consistent messaging and being able to produce a compelling argument for change. [CEO. Alpha Security].

‘Culture is the challenge in this industry, I have only been in this industry for 18 months and hear ‘that’s not what we do’. [Operations Executive, Alpha Security].

In 2017, Alpha Security’s senior executive team initiated an internal strategizing process that would eventually result in the firm becoming an orchestrator of digital transformation processes within its wider business ecosystem. However, at the time, the team commenced this journey by identifying two technical priorities: first, the creation of a real-time data analytics system to support security patrols in the field (referred to in this paper as ‘Project Omega’); second, a digital platform to automate workflows for Alpha Security’s staff and to improve customer experience (referred to as ‘Project Sigma’):

## Assessing internal capabilities

Once Alpha Security formally defined the obiectives of Project Omega and Project Sigma, it began to assess its internal capabilities. This was to understand if, how, and to what extent Alpha Security could mobilize the resources needed to internally develop the Omega and Sigma projects. This audit revealed two concerns. First, Alpha Security did not have the capabilities, resources, or knowledge

needed to successfully complete the projects:

‘We have few people in the business who are capable of managing a complex technology project; therefore, we need to […] attract those people who can’. [CEO, Alpha Security].

Second, Alpha Security recognized it was a technology laggard. Senior management shared with us that employees throughout the organization resisted change and were uncomfortable with digital technologies. Alpha Security’s management team thus realized that this would be a major hindrance to the success of Project Omega and Project Sigma:

‘Culture plays an important part of transformation […] A lot of people are […] resistant to change, and you have to bring them on the journey’ [CEO, Alpha Security]

Consequently, Alpha Security acknowledged that it had to challenge the traditional inward logic of organizational development and reach beyond its boundaries for help. However, senior management also realized that Alpha Security’s conservative culture prohibited any external party from engaging with its workforce. The prerequisite to any inter-organizational collaboration, therefore, was to first reframe the organization’s mindset.

## Reframing the organization’s mindset

In reframing Alpha Security’s organizational mindset, senior management intended to help employees develop a better under standing of digital technologies, but also to appreciate interactions and cooperation with other firms. Senior management initially intended to achieve this by highlighting functional benefits that could stem from the involvement of external service providers:

‘Third party solutions are usually the better way to go, it is a lot cheaper. They [third party providers] are exposed a lot more industry, developing this type of product […] You get better value for money compared to internally developing the product’. [IT Employee, Alpha Security].

However, despite initial attempts to reframe the organization’s mindset, it became evident that merely highlighting functiona benefits (i.e., better ‘value for money’ of an ICT solution) was not sufficient. In fact, employee resistance persisted, with one participant later reflecting on it as ‘technophobia’:

‘The challenge, for the employee, is overcoming technophobia, overcoming the fear that ‘big brother is watching me’. That’s the challenge of the average employee. Resistance comes from ‘I don’t like technology’; ‘I don’t want to use it’. It is fear of technology’ [Manager, Beta Security].

Alpha Security’s senior management subsequently began to consult all employees to help them overcome technophobia. For example, we observed ‘town hall’ meetings where employees asked management questions and offered input on how new technologies could be used. This helped refine the narrative, which shifted from functional benefits associated with technical solutions to the advantages that working with external partners could have for Alpha Security’s employees: e.g., a shortened transformation period, reduced need for retraining, business growth (which translated to employment security and professional development opportunities), and improved working relationships amongst staff. One HR Manager explained the role of employee consultations to us:

‘Take the time to consult with people, ease them into the change, announce the change, talk about why, give them background infor mation […]. Even notify them what the impacts might be, [it] can soften the blow’ [HR Manager, Alpha Security].

One employee whose role eventually changed significantly because of Project Sigma explained to us later how consultations with Alpha Security’s senior management had helped them understand the challenges the organization was facing in 2017, and how it led to them eventually appreciating the fact that all employees were – together - part of a larger digital transformation journey:

‘[CEO] explained everything and took us on the journey - why are we getting to this point as the business grows, and what’s the benefit. That is what all [employees] care about.’ [IT Employee, Alpha Security].

Alpha Security had initiated its digital transformation by recognizing that external actors from its wider business ecosystem were needed and by generating internal legitimacy to work with them. Now, the next step involved opening-up the business ecosystem.

The opening-up of alpha security’s digital business ecosystem

## Developing consensus

The second phase in Alpha Security's digital transformation journey required the firm finding ways to open-up’ its business ecosystem, meaning to initiate new collaborations related to digital transformation with partners with whom Alpha Security had previously engaged only on unrelated topics. Opening-up the business ecosystem meant that Alpha Security had to gain the support and assess the willingness of other firms to undertake the digital transformation journey with them. Alpha Security’s approach therefore shifted from its inward focus in the initiation phase, towards being outward focused. Specifically, as the focal firm, Alpha Security had to develop consensus amongst all actors in its existing business ecosystem (i.e., customers and suppliers) about the ob jectives it intended to achieve with projects Omega and Sigma, and to ensure that whatever digital solution was to be co-created would have the support and commitment from every-one involved. Alpha Security initially developed such consensus by aiming to under stand the willingness of other actors to partake in the digital transformation journey - a process that required open and honest communication:

‘Open and honest communication is critical because change can be good, change can be bad, but if every-one [across the business ecosystem] is fully engaged, then you can provide feedback without the fear of being singled out’ [Operations Manager, Alpha Security].

One of the key challenges Alpha Security encountered in opening-up its business ecosystem, was the ‘domino effect’ that projects Omega and Sigma had on business ecosystem actors. For instance, during an on-site observation, a representative of Beta Security explained that Foxtrot Com’s (an existing actor in Alpha Security’s business ecosystem) had to be incorporated into the projects to ensure the digital solution could operate on Customer C’s site (an Alpha Security client):

‘We had to test Foxtrot Com’s infrastructure to ensure the solution could be functional and operational at Customer C site. [Manager, Beta Security].

At this point, Alpha Security’s senior management understood that it had to become the focal firm to orchestrate a digital trans formation journey amongst both the actors of its existing business ecosystem and also new actors, thus effectively growing its business ecosystem.

## Engaging new business ecosystem actors

In early 2018, Alpha Security began selecting firms that could fill existing capability gaps. However, Alpha Security selected new actors in an ad hoc manner by asking if a potential partner could complete the task at hand and/or have the capability needed. This decision was driven by Alpha Security’s employees who, at this point, were under substantial pressure to advance projects Omega and Sigma:

‘I do not care who the company is, if they are an IT business within [our business ecosystem] or outside. As long as I can achieve what my manager wants.’ [Operations Manager, Alpha Security].

As the number of actors in Alpha Security's business ecosystem grew, benefits began to materialize. Alpha Security's attempt to ‘scale-up’ the size of its business ecosystem meant it could co-develop new digital solutions with customers, software providers, and even a competitor. For example, by mid-2018, Alpha Security was collaborating with competitor Beta Security to ensure Project Omega met Customer C’s needs. However, this process was not void of tension, misalignment, and conflict. For instance, miscom munication between Alpha Security and competitor Beta Security resulted in the development of a digital solution that did not meet initial specifications. We observed this tension during a meeting between Alpha Security and Beta Security staff, where Alpha Security’s staff members criticized the new system. After the meeting concluded, a Beta Security employee explained to us:

‘We spoke to upper-level management at [Alpha Security]. We told them what it [Project Omega] does, and they hear us. They then tell someone else, and they tell someone else. It is human nature that the story changes slightly. By the time it gets to the guys on the ground what they thought they were getting is slightly different from what is being installed. [Manager, Beta Security]

Alpha Security eventually learned it had to ensure ‘fit’ between the capabilities of a new ecosystem actor (i.e., Beta Security) and the digital outcomes to be achieved (i.e., the configuration of the ‘man down’ app used by security patrols). Resolving this challenge meant Alpha Security had to find better ways to match business ecosystem actors to digital solutions.

## Matching business ecosystem actors to digital solutions

In late 2018, Beta Security could no longer implement all the functionalities that Alpha Security requested. Beta Security therefore suggested to introduce Charlie Technology, one of its partners, to the project since they had the technical capabilities needed. From the perspective of Alpha Security, the ability to trust external companies to implement and operate their digital solutions was pivotal to the success of their digital transformation journey from this point onwards. The following quotes ascertain the role of trust among the ecosystem actors.

‘We trust [Charlie Technology]. They make the changes and work with us, and they trust that we will promote their product.’ [Manager, Beta Security].

‘Partnership is built on trust, and that goes back to integrity. You got to do what you say you are going to do, when you are going to do it. You can lose trust auick. and it takes twice as long to gain the trust back’ [Operations Executive. Alpha Security]

The new-found ability to match individual actors to digital solutions consolidated Alpha Security’s role as the orchestrator of the business ecosystem. For the ecosystem actors, it meant they had input into, and would absorb the benefits of, the development of digital solutions, Technologically, it meant Projects Omega and Sigma would serve the ecosystem, rather than individual actors only

## The integration of alpha security’s digital business ecosystem actors

## Obtaining feedback

In early 2019, Alpha Security recognized that “no digital solution is perfect” (IT Employee, Alpha Security), and therefore decided to expand its digital transformation journey bevond projects Omega and Sigma. In this sense, Alpha Security's digital transformation approach shifted from an initial ‘one-off’ attempt intended to address immediate market needs, to a constant undertaking that became integral to the business ecosystem Put differently, in early 2019 orchestrating was no longer just a process of initiating a digital transformation journey, but rather something that had emerged as Alpha Security’s identity. The firm had become an orchestrator of digital transformation. The key enabler underpinning this shift was the willingness of all business ecosystem actors to initiate and participate in feedback processes to refine digital offerings. For example, Alpha Security and Charlie Technology jointly developed a new security app. Initially implemented as a minimum viable product (MVP), this project became pivotal to gathering feedback about digital user experiences, which helped Charlie Technology to improve its services. Alpha Security’s management explained to us that their ability to provide other business ecosystem actors – like Charlie Technology – access to their customers to obtain such feedback was key to transforming security services at the business ecosystem level:

‘It is very important to review everything. We need suggestions to keep it running, both from the company bringing [Project Omega] in [Charlie Technology], and the company that is operating it’ [Operations Manager, Alpha Security]

Alpha Security subsequently institutionalized the ‘digital review process’, a novel initiative that would allow its partners to implement and test digital technologies within Alpha Security’s customer base. By orchestrating these interactions, Alpha Security established a new source of knowledge for its ongoing digital transformation activities. For example, Alpha Security later worked with Gamma Technology to include financial accounting functions:

‘[Project Sigma] has always been designed for small to medium sized businesses, which didn’t require that accounting capability. We worked with them [Gamma Technology] over the last few months to introduce accounting capabilities, reports, and the more advanced stuff. [IT Employee, Alpha Security].

However, despite the immediate benefits that the digital review process provided, the multiple perspectives provided by all actors involved often led to conflict. For instance, during a meeting between Alpha Security and Gamma Technology that focused on which technology feature to implement, we observed that the increasingly complex network of actors and interactions meant that bargaining and negotiation were needed to ensure ecosystem stability.

‘Unfortunately, we [Alpha Security] cannot decide which solution to implement during this meeting as we need to talk to our customers, our suppliers, and different departments within Alpha Security. We can then provide you with an answer’ [Observations, Alpha Security & Gamma Technology meeting].

## Integrating new capabilities

In mid-2019, Gamma Technology suggested that Echo Technology develop an API which would provide additional accounting capabilities for Alpha Security’s customers. Pursuing such an opportunity had only become possible because Gamma Technology was open to the idea of utilizing additional IT solutions from outside their firm. However, as the focal orchestrating firm, it became Alpha Security’s role to identify, vet, and manage any prospective actor attempting to become part of the business ecosystem. In this sense, Alpha Security not only attracted new digital capabilities by becoming an orchestrator of digital transformation processes in its business ecosystem, but also developed substantial expertise in orchestrating, and now applied this skill for the benefit of others. For example, we obtained documentation from Alpha Security which outlined Gamma Technology's future technology development roadmap. In mid-2019, the first conceptual references were made that outline how Echo Technology’s systems could be integrated, and suggestions were made for Alpha Security to orchestrate the process between the actors involved. A technical staff member explained:

‘[We] may integrate Echo Technology’s CRM systems with Gamma Technology’s system to improve internal processes’ [Technician, Alpha Security]

## Empowering business ecosystem actors

Finally, the benefits associated with digital transformation presented themselves not only to Alpha Security, but also manifested for other actors in the business ecosystem. Following Alpha Security’s example, other actors began to pilot and test new digital appli cations independently. For example, Beta Security collaborated with Charlie Technology to create a ‘lone worker function’, a persona safety feature embedded into radios used by security guards, which was then adopted by Beta Security’s clients.

‘We developed the ‘lone worker function’ with them [Charlie Technology]. We work here in Australia on our sites, and it helped them with their clients in America [Manager, Beta Security].

Alpha Security used its role as the orchestrator to empower all business ecosystem actors by providing opportunities to build, pilot, and test new digital applications within projects Omega and Sigma. In this way, projects Omega and Sigma – as both technology artifacts and the business ecosystem – were constituted by one another, allowing for ongoing ‘co-creation’ activities of digital solutions in ways that could not be previously achieved:

‘We are co-creating. From our point of view, it is hard to find a client like [Alpha Security…] who is willing and happy to do so. [Manager, Beta Security].

Finally, we consider it noteworthy to highlight that we did not observe an ‘end state’ of Alpha Security’s business ecosystem, but instead documented its evolution towards a ‘steady state’ that will likely continue to evolve over time.

## Discussion

Our empirical findings highlighted how Alpha Security emerged as the orchestrator of digital transformation in its business ecosystem, and what impact this had on the business ecosystems more broadly. As such, our work responds to calls in the IS literature to empirically investigate digital transformation through a business ecosystem lens (Lusch & Nambisan, 2015) – that is, by exploring the phenomenon beyond a focal firm’s boundaries (Vial, 2019). Fig. 3 depicts a model that summarizes the process by which Alpha Security became an orchestrator of its business ecosystem and articulates how orchestration of digital transformation took place here.

## Theoretical contributions

We identified three distinct phases associated with the orchestration of digital transformation of a business ecosystem: the initiation phase, the opening-up phase, and the integration phase. Collectively, these three phases describe two things: i) how a focal firm be comes an orchestrator of digital transformation; and once matured in this role, ii) how it continues to orchestrate the digital transformation of its business ecosystem.

First, the initiation phase captures how a focal firm emerges as an orchestrator, highlighting the need to achieve internal legitimacy for digital transformation as a prerequisite. Second, the opening-up phase captures how the focal firm commences its role as the orchestrator of digital transformation, pulling together and leveraging the resources and capabilities of others within its existing business ecosystem. Importantly, the focal firm needs to achieve external legitimacy for digital transformation amongst the actors within its business ecosystem. Finally, within the integration phase, the focal firm matures in its role as orchestrator of digita transformation, engaging continuously with other business ecosystem actors, including those outside of its established business ecosystem, to integrate new capabilities and empower all actors involved.

While our model in Fig. 3 depicts the execution of the nine activities that encompass the three phases in a linear manner, the order in which each activity is presented may be context-dependent. In fact, the assumption that digital transformation processes involving actors in different industries or business ecosystems will unfold in this exact order is unlikely. We therefore theorize more generally that becoming an orchestrator and successfully orchestrating digital transformation processes requires a focal firm to i) strategize, ii) mobilize and iii) align resources and actors within a business ecosystem, and to continue to do so over time (i.e., through the initiation, opening-up, and integration phases) as the focal firm and ecosystem actors continuously adapt to internal and external change.

Strategizing resonates with traditional approaches to strategic thinking in individual firms (Mintzberg, 1981; Warner & W¨ager, 2019), but affects both the focal orchestrating firm as well as other business ecosystem actors who are ‘being orchestrated’. When orchestrating digital transformation processes, strategizing activities enable all actors in the business ecosystem to identify any misalignment between existing and required resources (Yeow et al., 2018), and to shift cultural beliefs and practice on how in teractions with an external environment should take place (Tushman. 2017) - thus ultimately inverting the traditional inward logic of organizational change (Parker et al., 2017). Conversely, mobilizing aims to encourage and facilitate collaboration between previously disconnected actors in a business ecosystem, while aligning aims to institutionalize digital transformation in a business ecosystem context. By becoming an orchestrator of digital transformation, focal firms are able to not only benefit from digital technologies in a single instance, but can continue to align and realign other business ecosystem actors and resources over time (Teece, 2007), thus achieving a perpetual transformative state. In this way, the orchestration of digital transformation is not an end-state, but rather an ongoing process that requires the (re)shaping and reconfiguration of IT and business resources (Warner & W¨ager, 2019), thus empowering focal firms and their business ecosystems to exploit and seize emergent digital opportunities.

We consequently position orchestration as a concept that is distinct from planning (Drucker, 1959), coordination (Malone & Crowston, 1994), or open innovation (Chesbrough, 2003). While the latter are inward focused in providing benefits for a focal firm only, we demonstrate that orchestration intends to bring together independent actors for mutual benefit. We further argue that orchestration is also distinct from concepts commonly referred to in the strategic IS literature discussing digital transformation – including agility (Fuchs & Hess, 2018), reorientation of firm culture (Chanias et al., 2019), strategy (Bharadwaj et al., 2013), and leadership (El-Sawy et al., 2014). Though orchestration encompasses elements such as the reorientation of a firm's culture. these represent individual activities within orchestration processes more broadly.

In conclusion, our study provided important new theoretical and empirical insights pertaining to the orchestration of digita transformation processes in business ecosystems. Focusing on the case of Alpha Security allowed us to identify and explain the means with which some actors – i.e., orchestrators – can simultaneously control, yet empower, other business ecosystem actors in digita transformation settings. Our work ultimately challenges assumptions that a firm’s ability to successfully engage in digital trans formation is contingent on culture (Chanias et al., 2019), strategies (Bharadwaj et al., 2013), or leadership (El-Sawy et al., 2014) only. Instead, by focusing on the role and impact of orchestration in business ecosystems, we reinforce the notion that there is an increasing blurring between digital strategy, business strategy, and the IT function (Galliers, 2011). Orchestration may thereby represent the necessary concept by which firms can shrug off their ‘deep structure’ (Gersick, 1991)—organizational structures, practices, values, and identities—that can inhibit established market incumbents from fully utilizing digital technologies (Sebastian, 2017). In what follows, we provide suggestions for practitioners interested in implementing an orchestration strategy.

## Implications for practice

Many organizations fail to successfully undergo a digital transformation journey (Sutcliff et al., 2019), with over 70 percent of attempts considered unsuccessful (Rogers, 2016). While failure is common whenever IT paradigms change (Lucas & Goh, 2009), firms that successfully undergo digital transformation journeys outperform others in their industry by nine percent (Westerman et al., 2017). However, these success stories are often attributed to start-ups or prominent high-tech firms. Unfortunately, these success stories and subsequent lessons are not always transferable to industry incumbents burdened with inflexible legacy systems (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015) and a less digitally-adept workforce (Sebastian, 2017). Our study provides new insights into how such an industry incumbent successfully underwent a digital transformation journey by orchestrating the process and by involving other actors in its business ecosystems. New implications for practice arise from here.

First, orchestration represents an alternative approach to undertaking digital transformation. Rather than attempting to overcome intra-organizational constraints like inflexible legacy systems (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015) or a less digitally-adept workforce (Sebastian, 2017), we suggest managers identify and understand the digital capabilities of others in their existing business ecosystem. We aim to raise awareness that orchestration represents a unique approach with which managers can stage-manage their own digital trans: formation journey. Our study of Alpha Security demonstrates how the firm was able to acquire resources, capabilities, and knowledge faster than building these internally. Our study also demonstrates how Alpha Security was able to build shared values in their business ecosystem, and how this challenged the traditional inward logic of internal development (Parker et al., 2017). In this way, the notion of orchestration is different to the phenomenon of co-innovation of products in dyadic partnerships (i.e., Bharadwaj et al., 2013), as it involves not just opening-up but also integrating business ecosystem actors.

Second, it is important to formulate, articulate, and implement a digital transformation strategy in conjunction with all actors in one’s business ecosystem, thus avoiding misunderstandings that can arise, for example, from stereotypes individuals may hold (Huetten et al., 2019). We highlight the importance of building shared understanding, internal legitimacy, and aligned business goals. This is important because, whilst the benefits of becoming an orchestrator of one’s own business ecosystem are evident, they need to be communicated to others who may, at first, be apprehensive.

Finally. becoming an orchestrator requires a mindset shift from inward to outward looking. This shift may be a very disruptive for some incumbent firms (Ivarsson & Svahn, 2020) but is likely easier for new market-entrants. Ultimately, we intended to provide practitioners with inspiration on how to invert traditional inward-looking lenses and practices, and how digital transformation efforts can be achieved by becoming orchestrators instead.

## Limitations and future research

Our study was conducted in Australia and in the security services industry. We therefore cannot argue that the findings of this study are context independent. Nonetheless, we suggest that the case and context of Alpha Security and its business ecosystem is repre sentative of the challenges experienced by many industry incumbents undertaking digital transformation journeys (Sebastian, 2017) We therefore provide analytic, rather than statistical, generalization (Breidbach & Tana, 2021; Neu & Brown, 2005). As with al qualitative studies, the empirical data that we collected was subject to interpretation during analysis. Finally, while we opened up the boundaries of the digital transformation process, we did not consider other influences such as professional identities, broader interorganizational projects, and joint ventures (Jones & Lichtenstein, 2008) – although these are captured in part by the framing pro vided in this paper. Ultimately, this study focuses on an emerging phenomenon, and tells the story of how Alpha Security emerged as the orchestrator of digital transformation in its business ecosystem. We thus posit that the concept of orchestration opens up new

directions for future strategic IS research to address.

First, future research could investigate what ‘ideal’ levels of orchestration may look like (Paquin & Howard-Grenville, 2013), and how these may lead to different outcomes within business ecosystems. Answering this questions could help us understand how and under what conditions orchestration can help digital innovation materialize in distributed settings (Nambisan et al., 2017). This is important for managerial practice, to help firms encourage its business ecosystem actors shrug off traditional boundaries and participate in digital transformation processes, whilst managing trade-offs and tensions (Boudreau, 2017)

Second, future research should explore the orchestration of digital transformation in business ecosystems throughout different settings and through quantitative approaches, including machine learning (Antons et al., 2021). Future studies could examine orchestration as a long-term activity to provide insights beyond the ‘steady-state’ we reported on. An equally promising avenue for research lies in the acceptance of the orchestration activities. Like other studies (Tian et al., 2021), our organizations saw value in business ecosystem orchestration. Other studies could examine instances where ecosystem actors resist this approach, for example by exploring instances of failure but also potentially unethical implications that may arise (Breidbach & Maglio, 2020). Another fruitfu avenue could be to identify and investigate business ecosystems where orchestration activities are performed by all ecosystem actors, with changing levels of commitment, resources, and power over time (Simeonova et al., 2020).

Third, future research could emphasize the role of the IT artefact more strongly by examining how digital technologies enable/ support orchestration (Nambisan et al., 2017), or by asking how digital technologies can shape the nature of the orchestration process and its outcomes. For instance, recent research suggests that the unique properties of digital technology allow orchestration to be a much more viable strategy for focal firms undertaking a digital transformation (Ivarsson & Svahn, 2020), with digital platforms likely providing a fruitful context to do so (Breidbach and Ranjan, 2016; Breidbach and Brodie, 2016). On the flip side, the question of how legacy systems act as ‘invisible chains’ that prohibit orchestration of digital transformation should also be considered. This is because firms face the tension of building on their technological foundation whilst continuing multiple practices that depend on them (Wimelius et al., 2021).

## Conclusions

As the nature, scope, and impact of digital transformation reaches well beyond the boundaries of a single firm today, new concepts are needed to view the phenomenon in these larger systemic settings. We introduced orchestration as such a concept and presented empirical insights highlighting how a focal firm became the orchestrator of digital transformation in its business ecosystem. Successfu orchestration requires to simultaneously control, yet empower, others in digital transformation settings as the boundaries of digita strategy, business strategy, and the IT function continue to blur. Ultimately, we like to think that orchestration represents the necessary concept that enables other researchers to generate new knowledge about digital transformation, while enabling firms to leave ingrained organizational structures, practices, values, and identities behind that can inhibit their ability to utilize and benefit from new technologies.
