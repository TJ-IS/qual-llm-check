## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: IS use IS resources Network embeddedness IS outsourcing Organization size Meaningful use

## A B S T R A C T

This research examines how organizations can achieve effective information systems (IS) use by aligning internal IS resources with embeddedness in the IS outsourcing network. Leveraging the empirical opportunity of large-scale organization-wide meaningful use attestation of electronic health records, we conducted a longitudinal analysis of US hospitals from 2013 to 2017. We found that an organization’s network embeddedness amplifies the positive relationship between IS re sources and effective IS use. We also identify different impacts depending on network embedd edness types and organization sizes. We found positive moderating effects of structural and positional embeddedness. However, junctional embeddedness has no direct or moderating effect on IS use. In addition, the moderating effects manifest differently for different-sized organizations – i.e., structural embeddedness has a stronger positive moderating effect for large organizations, while positional embeddedness’ positive moderating effect is stronger for small organizations. We also found heterogeneous direct effects of structural and positional embeddedness on organiza tional IS use. Solely depending on structural embeddedness will result in lower IS use, which is more prominent for small organizations. Positional embeddedness has a negative direct effect on small organizations’ IS use but positively relates to large organizations’ IS use. This research highlights the role of network embeddedness in facilitating IS use and offers a nuanced under standing of its impacts. Our findings also provide practical implications for organizational IS use through strategic IS outsourcing.

## Introduction

A global trend is occurring around organizations' digital transformation, in which organizations are increasingly implementing information systems (IS) to transform business models, support operations, and drive strategic decision-making. The theoretical puzzle regarding whether and how IS improves organizational performance has consistently garnered IS research’s attention (Kohli and Grover, 2008; Melville et al., 2004). One central thesis addressing this puzzle is that, rather than IS adoption, the actual use and assimilation of IS (or IT functionalities) play a more critical role in driving firm performance (e.g., Devaraj and Kohli, 2003; Liang et al., 2007; Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006; Wang et al., 2012). However, as Lin et al. (2019) indicated, studies grounded in large-scale empirical tests of IS use at the organizational level are scarce due to the difficulty of collecting organization-wide IS use data. Leveraging the national Electronic Health Record (EHR) meaningful use attestation, recent studies have empirically shown the effects of IS use on reducing IT security breaches (Kim and Kwon, 2019; Kwon and Johnson, 2018) and improving healthcare quality (Lin et al., 2019). However, little has been done to understand IS use’s antecedents at the organization-level – i.e., how organizations can improve IS use through organization-level strategic moves.

Prior IS strategy research often treats IS use as a dimension of organizations’ IS capabilities and emphasizes the dependencies of IS capabilities on organizations’ IS resources (Caldeira and Ward, 2003; Wang et al., 2012). In other words, IS resources directly provide IS functionalities or indirectly facilitate the actualization of these functionalities through learning and experience diffusion, leading to greater IS use (Kettinger et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2012). However, “IS resources may not always be used optimally” due to reasons such as “lack of incentives and aspiration” and “lack of knowledge” (Hedman and Kalling, 2003, p. 55). Hence, we focus on the contingenc that may explain how organizations can better transform IS resources into effective IS use.

Organizations are increasingly outsourcing their IS applications to external vendors to leverage their expertise and help smooth the implementation process. In turn, the extensive outsourcing activities of organizations in the same sector result in a complex organization-vendor network. Such an IS outsourcing network embeds knowledge and experience that may facilitate organizations’ IS use (Chellappa and Saraf, 2010). However, organizations’ capabilities of assimilating knowledge and experience from the network – i. e., contracted vendors and other organizations through the shared vendors – depend on the organizations’ embeddedness in the network. Organizations with higher embeddedness in the organization-vendor network can directly access more knowledge and experience from their contracted vendors: also, contracted vendors diffuse other client organizations' knowledge and experience to the focal organization through indirect connections (Chellappa and Saraf, 2010). Hence, this research examines the primary research question: How does embeddedness in the outsourcing network influence organizations’ conversion of IS resources into IS use?

Furthermore, organizations’ institutional context sets a boundary condition that may hinder or facilitate “successful IS imple mentation” (Sharma and Yetton 2003, p. 539). At the organizational level, most institutional characteristics may be intertwined with or explained by organization size. Relevant to the IS use context, organization size not only infers the resource availability and IS budget (Ang and Straub, 1998; George et al., 2005), but also signifies the organizational structure and complexity, and accordingly, the scope, complexity, and diversity of IS needs (Sambamurthy and Zmud, 1999; Lee et al., 2019). Thus, different-sized organizations will likely have different needs and capabilities for assimilating external knowledge and experience. Hence, different-sized organizations may need different types of network embeddedness to effectively absorb external knowledge and experience from the IS outsourcing network. Therefore, we propose the second research question: How do the effects of embeddedness in the organization-vendor network on organizations’ conversion of IS resources into IS use differ in different-sized organizations?

We examine our research questions using a longitudinal analysis of US hospitals from 2013 to 2017. Results confirm that IS re sources are positively associated with the likelihood of organizations’ effective IS use, which is amplified when organizations have greater embeddedness in the IS outsourcing network. In addition, we distinguish three major types of network embeddedness that are conceptualized and commonly used in prior literature: number of direct connections (i.e., structural embeddedness), connections to other structurally embedded organizations (i.e., positional embeddedness), and bridging other unconnected organizations (i.e., junctional embeddedness) (Grewal et al. 2006). We found that structural and positional embeddedness amplify the positive rela tionship between IS resources and IS use. However, junctional embeddedness does not significantly affect the relationship between IS resources and effective IS use.

We also found different impacts of structural and positional embeddedness in different-sized organizations. While structural embeddedness amplifies the positive relationship between IS resources and effective IS use for both small and large organizations, positional embeddedness only amplifies the relationship for small organizations. Also, we found that structural embeddedness has a negative direct effect on effective IS use, which is more prominent for small organizations. Positional embeddedness has a negative direct impact on small organizations’ effective IS use, but a positive direct effect on large organizations’ IS use.

This research contributes to the IS use literature by highlighting the role of embeddedness in the IS outsourcing network in un derstanding organizational IS use. Our findings regarding network embeddedness types and their differential impacts in different-sized organizations add nuances to our understanding of the impacts. We also offer practical implications to business managers on trans forming IS resources into effective IS use through strategic IS outsourcing.

## Theory and hypotheses

## IS resources and IS use

Building on prior IS strategy research (e.g., Bharadwaj, 2000; Kettinger et al., 2013; Ross et al., 1996; Wade and Hulland, 2004), we define IS resources as an organization’s technical IS assets and IS-related human capital that are crucial to its development of IS ca pabilities in seizing opportunities and reacting to threats. IS resources provide or support IS functions that are essential to cultivating the organizational effective IS use (Kettinger et al., 2013). The effective use of IS emerges in employees’ processes by applying their knowledge, integrating with others' experiences, and coordinating with others' actions (Wang et al.. 2012).

For example, in the healthcare context, systems – such as clinical decision support systems, computerized physician entry, and physician visualization dashboards – offer functionalities embedded in the clinical processes. Organizations with more IS resources that are core to the business process can enable the IS functions to digitalize business processes and coordinate them. Other IS resources such as IT emplovees, administrative systems, and IT devices support actualizing those functionalities afforded in the business process embedded systems. Employees need to access the systems through IT devices such as computers and networks. Administrative systems improve the IS and human resources accountability, creating an organizational culture emphasizing effective IS use. Organizations with more IT employees are more capable of offering training sessions and educating employees about the “best practices” of using IS in the workplace. IT employees also provide essential troubleshooting and maintenance activities when other employees have tech nical issues with IS use.

In sum, IS use is essentially the process in which an organization transforms IS resources into IS capabilities embedded in the business process. Hence, IS use cannot exist independently; rather, it depends on an organization’s available IS resources (Wang et al., 2012). An organization’s IS resources enable its exploitation of IS functionalities, facilitation of learning, and diffusion of experience, thereby developing more effective IS use. Therefore, we propose the baseline hypothesis that:

Hypothesis 0 (H0). Organizations with more IS resources are more likely to achieve greater IS use

However, prior IS strategy literature demonstrated that solely relying on IS resources might not be sufficient to trigger IS use (Caldeira and Ward, 2003; Hedman and Kalling, 2003). Organizations need to align their IS resources with other resources and ca pabilities (e.g., Caldeira and Ward, 2003; Melville et al., 2004), suggesting a contingency perspective to model organizational IS use. Given that organizations can assimilate information, knowledge, and resources from their IS vendors (Caldeira and Ward, 2003), we theorize how organizations’ embeddedness in their IS outsourcing networks influences their capabilities of transforming IS resources into IS use.

## Moderating effects of embeddedness in the IS outsourcing network

Organizations often outsource their IS to vendors to reduce system development costs and improve implementation efficiencies. An organization’s selection of IS vendors also has strategic implications for its effective IS use (Caldeira and Ward, 2003). Specifically, IS vendors are often responsible for customizing system modules for organizations’ specific needs, improving the effective use of systems. In addition, IS vendors usually work with several client organizations simultaneously, accumulating experience and facilitating IS use through training sessions and sharing implicit knowledge. Such IS use experience and best practices can be passed on to and assim ilated by other clients (Chellappa and Saraf, 2010; Chi et al., 2010). From the social network perspective, members with social ties can access similar experiences and knowledge (Kane et al., 2014). Accordingly, members’ abilities to acquire social resources in the network influence their absorptive capacity to prompt IS use based on their vendors’ knowledge and experience. We thus examine organizations’ embeddedness in the organization-vendor network, which models different types of players – i.e., client organizations and IS vendors – and their contractual relationships.

Prior literature has developed theoretical mechanisms and empirical measures of different categories of network embeddedness depending on the nature of inter-organizational exchanges (Gulati. 1998: Gulati and Gargiulo, 1999: Uzzi, 1996). To reflect an actor's network embeddedness, Grewal et al. (2006) have synthesized the literature and identified three subconstructs: structural, junctional, and positional embeddedness. Grewal et al. (2006, p. 1045)’s conceptualization focuses on the “architecture of network ties.” Spe cifically, structural embeddedness focuses on the extent to which an actor directly connects to others in the network. Junctionaì embeddedness highlights the actor’s connections to others who are not connected. Positional embeddedness captures the degree to which an actor connects to other structurally embedded actors in the network. Each type of network embeddedness helps an organization access different types of experience and knowledge from external actors in the network, thus affecting an organization’s conversion of IS resources into IS use through different network-based theoretical mechanisms.

Structural embeddedness emphasizes an organization’s direct connections to others in the organization-vendor network (Kane and Ransbotham, 2016). In other words, in an organization-vendor network, an organization with higher structural embeddedness links to a larger pool of vendors. The direct link between an organization and a vendor will help transfer knowledge and experience of how to use IS appropriately. For example, IS vendors often have customer service teams which have responsibilities of conducting training sessions for the client organization and providing manuals for using the system. However, different vendors may have heterogeneous levels of expertise in the best use of their systems. Thus, the larger number of vendors an organization can reach through the structura embeddedness, the greater the transfer of knowledge and experience. Also, by accessing different IS vendors in the network, the client organizations can perform a vis-a-vis \` comparison and solidify the list of vendors who provide the most appropriate knowledge and experience. Therefore, structural embeddedness can facilitate an organization’s capabilities of converting IS resources into IS use through direct access and transfer of the organization's contracted vendors' experience and knowledge

Junctional embeddedness captures the extent to which an organization takes bridge positions between two actors in the network (Faraj et al., 2015). In an organization-vendor network, the organization with higher junctional embeddedness indicates that it has established direct ties to vendors with fewer shared clients. Also, there will be more shortest paths passing through the organization if it takes a high junctional embeddedness position in the network (Sasidharan et al., 2012). Thus, organizations with higher junctiona embeddedness have stronger abilities to control the flow of knowledge and experience in the network (Farai et al.. 2015). In addition. organizations with higher junctional embeddedness can leverage their bridging positions to “experiment and innovate” because they can access non-redundant knowledge and experience from vendors (Yan et al., 2015; Sasidharan et al., 2012, p. 661). Thus, junctiona embeddedness can facilitate an organization’s capabilities of transforming IS resources into IS use by controlling the local network’s flow of knowledge and experience. and by accessing non-redundant knowledge and experience from disconnected vendors in the network

Positional embeddedness reflects an organization’s connections to others who have stronger connections in the organizationvendor network (Kane and Borgatti, 2011). In our context of the organization-vendor network, organizations need to outsource their information systems to vendors with a larger client base to acquire a higher level of positional embeddedness. These wellconnected vendors implement their systems in various client organizations, and therefore have stronger capabilities of accumu lating knowledge and experience to facilitate IS use. Furthermore, the large number of IS implementation cases form a decent pool of trial-and-error practices, thereby enriching the quality of the well-connected vendors' knowledge and experience, In turn organi: zations with high positional embeddedness can benefit from the well-connected vendors’ accumulated knowledge and experience to better facilitate their transformation of IS resources into IS use. Hence, positional embeddedness can facilitate an organization’s ca pabilities of transforming IS resources into IS use by accessing richer and higher-quality knowledge and experience from well-connected vendors in the network.

Although the three subconstructs of network embeddedness demonstrate different mechanisms, they all strengthen organizations capabilities of transforming IS resources into IS use. Therefore, we hypothesize that.

Hypothesis 1 (H1). Organizations’ embeddedness – i.e., structural embeddedness (H1a), junctional embeddedness (H1b), and positional embeddedness (H1c) – in the IS vendor network amplifies the positive relationship between IS resources and IS use.

## Moderating effects of organization size

Organization size has been identified as an essential contingency influencing the effectiveness of IS outsourcing strategies (e.g., Lee et al., 2019). The central thesis around organization size is the strong inferences of an organization’s slack resources – i.e., larger organizations often have more resources, larger budgets for IS outsourcing, larger scope and complexity of IS projects, and a larger number of vendors (e.g., Ang and Straub, 1998). Given the notable differences between small and large organizations, different di mensions of network embeddedness may affect their transformation of IS resources to IS use differently. Thus, we further theorize the moderating effects of organization size on the impacts of network embeddedness.

Large organizations often have a more complex organizational structure, decision-making, supply chain relationships, and business processes. Correspondingly, large organizations tend to have more complex, broad scope, and diverse information systems; also, the interdependencies among their systems are highly complex (Sambamurthy and Zmud, 1999; Lee et al., 2019). Facilitating the effective use of complex and diverse systems often needs knowledge and experience from different domains. A single vendor may not be sufficient to provide a wide range of knowledge and experience. Thus, to fulfill large organizations’ need for diverse and complex knowledge and experience, it is crucial to have a larger pool of vendors (i.e., high structural embeddedness) because an organization can acquire different knowledge and experience from different vendors. In addition, given the complexity and diversity of information systems, large organizations need to absorb external knowledge and experience from the organization-vendor network more effi ciently. A critical indicator of such efficiency is the non-redundancy of knowledge and experience acquired from the network, which is mainly manifested by an organization’s junctional embeddedness. Also, a higher level of junctional embeddedness in the network helps large organizations access external knowledge and experience in a more timely manner because of their bridging positions in the network, which fulfills large organizations’ need to access diverse knowledge and experience promptly. Hence, compared to small organizations, structural embeddedness and junctional embeddedness – by providing an organization with a larger number of con nected vendors and non-redundant experience and knowledge – have stronger impacts on large organizations’ transformation of IS resources into IS use. Therefore, we hypothesize that.

Hypothesis 2a (H2a). Structural and junctional embeddedness have a stronger positive impact on the relationship between IS resources and IS use for large organizations than for small organizations.

In contrast, small organizations have a simpler and smaller scope of information systems needs and limited resources and budgets (George et al., 2005). Accordingly, the external knowledge and experience that small organizations need to effectively convert IS resources to IS use are more concentrated and in a smaller scope. Importantly, small organizations should effectively allocate their limited resources and budgets when selecting IS vendors. Thus, one strategy small organizations can take is connecting to those vendors who are well connected in the network. In doing so, small organizations can concentrate their resources and budgets on the few well-connected vendors, which helps keep their vendor set smaller and focused while still able to access the rich externa knowledge and experience accumulated by the well-connected vendors. Hence, compared to large organizations that have substantia resources and budgets for IS projects, absorbing knowledge and experience from well-connected vendors in the network (i.e., posi tional embeddedness) is more important for small organizations to effectively transform IS resources to IS use. Therefore, we hypothesize that.

Hypothesis 2b (H2b). Positional embeddedness has a stronger positive impact on the relationship between IS resources and IS use for small organizations than for large organizations

We used three dimensions to measure hospitals' IS resources: IS budget. IS emplovees, and IS applications, IS budget measures hospitals’ total dollar amount of budget to the IS projects each year. IS employees indicates the total number of full-time equivalent IT employees in a hospital each year. IS applications count the total number of applications, including both clinical and administrative IS applications, that are active in use in each hospital in a year. Given the multi-dimensional nature of the construct, we created a composite second-order variable ISResources by following Anand et al. (2020). Specifically, ISResources is measured as the unweighted sum of the standardized values of IS budget, IS employees, and IS applications. We used standardized values because of the scale difference among the three second-order measures. We also winsorized each dimensional measure to be within three standard de viations from the sample mean to account for potential outliers

We built the two-mode affiliation network where hospitals and vendors are nodes and hospitals have contract relationships with their EHR vendors. We focused on EHR systems because the meaningful use attestation has focused on EHR adoption (Lin et al., 2019). Also, EHR systems are critical IS resources that facilitate the digitalization and sharing of health information within and across healthcare providers (Mishra et al., 2012). We included EHR systems that are used in Atasoy et al. (2018). As discussed in the theoretical development, we used three subconstructs to measure network embeddedness: structural, junctional, and positiona embeddedness. Structural embeddedness measures a hospital’s degree centrality – i.e., total number of EHR vendors in our research context. We used betweenness centrality to measure junctional embeddedness, which refers to the number of paths between different nodes on which the hospital lies. We operationalized positional embeddedness as a hospital’s eigenvector centrality in the network. Similar to the construction of IS resources measure, we used the unweighted sum of the standardized and winsorized values of degree centrality, betweenness centrality, and eigenvector centrality to measure NetworkEmbeddedness.

We used the number of staffed beds in a hospital to measure its Size. We also included additional time-variant control variables that may influence hospitals’ meaningful use attestation. The variable Age is measured as hospitals’ number of years since their founded date. The variable HealthSysSize controls for the number of health entities affiliated with the same healthcare system. All these var iables are transformed using the logarithm to address the highly skewed distributions. ## Empirical approach

We estimated panel data fixed effect models to examine our hypotheses. Although the dependent variable is ordinal, we used the linear ordinary least square (OLS) model because of its strength in testing interactive effects and controlling for time-invariant het erogeneity. We included hospital fixed effects to control for time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity. We also specified a set of year dummies to control for time-specific unobserved heterogeneity. To empirically examine the moderating effects, we add the in teractions between IS resources and network embeddedness. We also lagged the dependent variable by one year, which helps avoid the reverse causality concern. Specifically, we estimate:

Based on this model, we can empirically examine the baseline hypothesis (i.e., H0) using the statistical sig nificance of $\beta _ { 1 } .$ . The statistical significance of β supports the moderating effect of network embeddedness on the relationship between IS resources and IS use (i.e., H1). In H1, we theorized different mechanisms regarding the moderating effects of different types of network embeddedness. We empirically test it by individually examining each dimension’s moderating effects. Specifically, we replace the composite second-order construct of network embeddedness with its three subconstructs $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , structural embeddedness, junctional embeddedness, and posi tional embeddedness). To avoid multicollinearity concerns and better interpret results, we add the subconstructs’ interaction term with IS resources in separate models. In other words, we add one interaction term in each model separately.

To empirically test H2, we constructed two subsamples based on hospitals' size – i.e., small hospitals are those with fewer than 100 staffed beds, and others are labeled as large hospitals. We run the model using three subconstructs for both small and large hospita samples. Based on the estimations, we can empirically examine if the moderating effects of each type of network embeddedness are different for small and large organizations.

Another unexpected result we found in the empirical analysis is the negative direct effect of network embeddedness on meaningful use attestation $( \beta = - 0 . 0 1 0 7 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ , as shown in Column (2) in Table 3). One possible explanation is that although prior literature demonstrated the importance of external technical expertise in successful IS use (Thong, 2001), entirely relying on external IS sourcing in “filling gaps of resources and capabilities” inhibits an organization’s IS use (Caldeira and Ward, 2003, p. 135). When looking at the different dimensions of network embeddedness, Column (1) in Table 4 shows that only structural embeddedness negatively influences hospitals’ meaningful use attestation $( \beta = - 0 . 0 2 9 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Thus, when other things are equal, solely relying on contracting to a large number of IS vendors will hinder the organizations’ capabilities to achieve effective IS use. The negative direct effect of structura embeddedness further demonstrates the importance of aligning external knowledge and resources with internal IS resources to achieve effective IS use.

Table 5 presents the results for the moderating effects of organization size. The results demonstrate that the coefficients on the interaction term between IS resources and structural embeddedness are positive and significant for both small $( \beta = 0 . 0 0 8 3 7 , p < 0 . 1 )$ and large organizations $( \beta = 0 . 0 0 9 0 2 , p < 0 . 0 5 ) ;$ also, the magnitude for large organizations is greater than small organizations. Thus, structural embeddedness has a stronger positive moderating effect on the relationship between IS resources and IS use for large or ganizations than for small organizations. However, the coefficient on the interaction term between IS resources and junctional embeddedness is negative for small organizations $( \beta = - 1 . 0 3 4 , p = \mathtt { n } . s . )$ but positive for large organizations $( \beta = 0 . 1 1 1 , p = \mathtt { n } . s . )$ Although this result generally confirms a stronger positive moderating effect of junctional embeddedness for large organizations than for small organizations, both are statistically non-significant. Hence, H2a is partially supported. We also found a positive and sig nificant coefficient on the interaction term between IS resources and positional embeddedness for small organizations $( \beta = 0 . 9 4 2 , p <$ 0.1). However, the coefficient on the interaction term between IS resources and positional embeddedness for large organizations (β = − 0.508, p = n.s.) is negative and non-significant. Thus, positional embeddedness has a stronger positive moderating effect on the relationship between IS resources and IS use for small organizations than for large organizations. Therefore, H2b is supported.

As shown in Table 5, we also found that structural embeddedness has a stronger negative impact on the effective IS use in small hospitals than in large hospitals. In addition, the results show that positional embeddedness negatively influences small hospitals meaningful use of IS, but positively influences large hospitals’ meaningful IS use.

The different results regarding organization size demonstrate that small organizations are at a disadvantage in solely relving on external networks to promote effective IS use. One possible reason is that smaller organizations often “have limited resources and skills” (George et al., 2005, p. 217) that can be leveraged to implement IS functions and facilitate their meaningful use. The lack of external resources pushes an organization to pursue meaningful use through internal IS resources investments. Furthermore, smallersized organizations and those that are not bounded by a large corporation have advantages in greater flexibility and speed (Ebben and Johnson, 2005; Hagel and Singer, 1999) in organizational restructuration and IS implementations, thus are better able to rely on internal IS resources in achieving meaningful IS use. In contrast, the IS implementation in larger organizations tends to be broader and more complex. Evidence also demonstrates that larger organizations face more difficulties in being agile in IS implementation (e.g., Dybå and Dingsøyr, 2008). Accordingly, larger organizations often need to absorb the external IS vendors’ resources in implementing and actualizing IS functions.

## Discussions

In the current digitalized business environment, organizations actively invest in IS and undertake the digital transformation initiative. The effective use of the implemented digital systems is pivotal in achieving competitive advantage and digital leadership (Devaraj and Kohli, 2003; Lin et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2012). Filling a significant gap in understanding IS use’s antecedents at the organizational level, we theoretically predicted and empirically confirmed how organizations can achieve effective IS use through internal IS resources and external embeddedness in the IS outsourcing network. We also add insights on the heterogeneous effects of different network embeddedness types and the impacts on different-sized organizations.

This study makes three major theoretical contributions. First. it extends the IS strategy literature by shifting the attention to the upstream research investigating IS use’s antecedents at the organizational level. The existing literature has focused on how organi zations’ IS investments and IS use influence their performance (e.g., Kohli and Grover, 2008; Melville et al., 2004; Wang et al., 2012). Although IS use has been identified as a core mechanism explaining the link between IS investments and firm performance (Devara and Kohli, 2003; Lin et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2012), we know little about how organizations can strategically facilitate IS use. This research is among the first to theorize how IS resources and embeddedness in IS outsourcing networks influence organizations’ IS use. While prior IS strategy literature has recognized the impact of IS resources on IS use (e.g., Caldeira and Ward, 2003; Kettinger et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2012), we further demonstrate its contingencies on IS outsourcing networks. More importantly, we delineate that structural and positional embeddedness amplifies the relationship between IS resources and effective IS use, while junctional embeddedness has no influence on the relationship. We also found the differential impacts of different network embeddedness types for organizations of various sizes. Specifically, structural embeddedness has a stronger positive moderating effect on large organi zations. Positional embeddedness demonstrates a stronger positive moderating effect for small organizations. These nuanced findings enrich our understanding of the complex impacts of IS resources and different types of network embeddedness on the effective IS use of different-sized organizations.

Second, this research contributes to the prior IS use literature by shedding light on how organizational strategies influence IS use. This study differs from previous IS literature that examines individuals’, systems’, and groups’ characteristics in determining individual IS use (e.g., Burton-Jones and Grange, 2013; Burton-Jones and Volkoff, 2017; Hao et al., 2018; Walsh et al., 2016). We leverage the nationwide meaningful use provision to investigate hospitals’ meaningful use of EHR systems. The meaningful use attestation offers the large-scale objective measure of EHR use at the organizational level (Lin et al., 2019). This research also examines the top-down factors of organizational strategies as the antecedents of IS use. By doing so, we deliver the central thesis that organi zations can facilitate effective IS use by aligning internal and external strategies. We also delineate the heterogenous effects of different network embeddedness types in different-sized organizations. In doing so, our findings reach a fine-grained view on the combination of IS resources and network embeddedness for producing IS use.

Third, this research contributes to the prior EHR use literature. Previous individual-level EHR use literature has investigated the assimilation (Baird et al., 2017; Mishra et al., 2012), effective use (Burton-Jones and Volkoff, 2017), adoption (Hao et al., 2018), and deep structure use (Romanow et al., 2018) of EHRs by physicians. Also, past organizational-level studies have examined how the economic considerations (Ozdemir et al., 2011), social contagion (Angst et al., 2010), and knowledge transfer in socioeconomic networks (Peng et al., 2014) influence healthcare providers’ adoption of EHRs. This research adds to the EHR use literature by demonstrating how organizations’ strategies (i.e., IS resources and IS outsourcing network embeddedness) influence their IS use.

Our results also provide significant practical implications for business and IS executives. Following our baseline hypothesis testing results, we recommend that organizations should align their IS resources with proper embeddedness in the IS vendor network to achieve effective IS use. Specifically, businesses can consider supplementing internal IS resources with a stronger structural and po sitional embeddedness, i.e., the number of ties to other organizations and other structurally-embedded organizations in the network. For example, outsourcing IS applications to vendors that are highly embedded in the vendor network can help an organization to better transform IS resources into effective IS use. Additionally, our findings recommend different strategies to small and large organizations For example, compared to large organizations, small organizations can make efforts to connect to vendors that are highly embedded in the network (i.e., positional embeddedness). However, solely relying on such positional embeddedness will negatively impact smal organizations’ effective IS use.

Although this research makes significant contributions to theory and practice, it is worth noting the limitations and suggesting future research guidelines. First, this study investigates how organizational-level strategies facilitate IS use. Future research can further theorize how the employee-level and organizational-level IS use interact with each other by developing a multi-level theory. For example, future research might take a configurational perspective to theorize the different configurations that lead to effective IS use; i. e., precisely what configurations of organizational IS strategies, system characteristics, and employee attributes produce a high IS use level? Second, we developed the theoretical model based on the logic of resources and inter-organizational relationships. Future research can go beyond such a framework and further explore other organizational strategies, such as IS governance structure, to enrich the theoretical insights. Future research also can theorize the alignment of IS and business strategies in achieving high IS use. Third, this research adopts a single research context of hospitals’ use of EHR systems, which may limit the generality of our findings We encourage future research to develop organizational theories of IS use in other contexts.
