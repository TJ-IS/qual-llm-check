# Does adopting inner source increase job satisfaction? A social capital perspective using a mixed-methods approach

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Inner source Open source principles Social capital theory Job satisfaction Structural equation modelling Interview study Mixed-methods

## A B S T R A C T

Internal boundaries between information systems divisions have traditionally caused friction and frustration, and negatively affected IT staff job satisfaction. Adopting open source development principles and processes within a corporate environment, what has been labeled ‘inner source,’ is now part of a larger management strategy to break down silos and promote innovation, collab oration, cooperation and communication across divisions of an organization. In this article we argue that inner source emphasizes the importance of relationships between developers and teams interacting across intra-organizational boundaries. We posit that inner source helps to create social capital, which in turn links to higher levels of job satisfaction. We use a sequentia mixed-methods approach to test a theoretical model by combining quantitative survey data collected at one IT organization with qualitative data based on interviews with professionals from six organizations that adopted inner source. The results suggest that employees who participate in inner source projects develop more social interaction ties with others and have higher levels of shared understanding of software projects. Furthermore, we confirm that social interaction ties and a shared understanding link to a higher degree of trust. Finally, this study provides evidence that social capital mediates the link between participation in inner source and job satisfaction, implying that an inner source strategy can offer benefits to organizations. Enriched with the findings from the qualitative interviews, we then identify a number of implications for managers.

## Introduction

The adoption of open source software (OSS) development and governance practices within an organization, known as ‘inner source,’ is now part of a larger management strategy (Morgan et al., 2021; Stol and Fitzgerald, 2015; Stol et al., 2022) that focuses on changing the typical enterprise silo-based mentality and creating new channels of interaction and collaboration between anvone within an organization (Morgan et al., 2021; Stol et al., 2011). Inner source has been linked to increased software reuse, knowledge sharing, and product innovation (Capraro and Riehle, 2017; Morgan et al., 2011; Riehle et al., 2016; Vitharana et al., 2010). These output-oriented benefits to increase operational productivity have prompted a wide range of software organizations to consider adopting the open source development model internally (Cooper and Stol, 2018; Fitzgerald, 2011): including Bell Laboratories (Gurbani et al., 2006), HP (Dinkelacker et al., 2002; Melian and Mahring, ¨ 2008), Nokia (Lindman et al., 2008), Philips (Wesselius, 2008), and SAP (Riehle et al., 2009). While there is a considerable body of Information Systems (IS) literature that has focused on OSS, it has primarily considered only certain aspects of the OSS phenomenon, such as organizational adoption of OSS products (Marsan et al., 2012), OSS developers (Carillo et al., 2017; von Krogh et al., 2012), achieving business value with OSS (Morgan and Finnegan, 2014), and OSS project success (Sutanto et al., 2021). There is a dearth of work within the IS<sup>1</sup> field on the internal use of open source development principles and processes.

Inner source has expanded from its initial focus on software development practice to a broader organizational focus (Morgan et al., 2021). Several studies have suggested that adopting inner source implies a cultural rather than a technical change (Melian, 2007; Morgan et al., 2021; Neus and Scherf, 2005; Stol et al., 2014). These views suggest that beyond the output-oriented benefits mentioned above, adopting inner source may bring both organizational and individual benefits such as more resilient relationships among de velopers, a motivated and empowered workforce, and consequently, developer job satisfaction. However, the pertinent literature has largely neglected the outcomes of inner source on individual developer levels (Edison et al., 2020), and especially the mechanisms by which software developers benefit from organizations’ inner source strategies have remained underexplored. Hence, despite the positive notions such as code reuse and better knowledge sharing that come with inner source as a concept (Morgan et al., 2022; Riehle et al., 2009; Vitharana et al., 2010), far less is known about how IT developers are affected by a company’s inner source adoption. A better understanding of the effectiveness of inner source approaches for software developers’ job satisfaction is important to hel organizations and managers in their decisions to adopt such far-reaching organizational principles and practices.

This study investigates the impact of inner source participation on software developers’ job satisfaction, a relation that has been largely neglected in previous studies of inner source. Prior IS literature has established a positive link between innovative software development practices, such as pair programming (Balijepally et al., 2009) and agile development (Tripp et al., 2016), and developers job satisfaction. Job satisfaction is associated with developers’ increased well-being and morale, reduced burn-out and absenteeism, and reduced employee turnover intention (Sharma and Stol, 2020; Wegge et al., 2007; Wright et al., 2007), suggesting it is a variable of great interest to managers. However, there is little rigorous evidence that inner source has positive influences on software developers job satisfaction. The focus on developer relationships across intra-organizational boundaries (between divisions and teams) further suggests the importance of social capital, which has been described as “the goodwill that is engendered by the fabric of social relations and that can be mobilized to facilitate action" (Adler and Kwon. 2002). We posit that inner source helps to build social capital – which in turn helps to facilitate better communication to improve the information flow, knowledge sharing, and collaboration mechanisms that enable developers to finish tasks quicker and reduce overall friction and frustration within the development process. These are all key aspects of IS development that should lead to job satisfaction, suggesting that social capital created through inner source plays a centra role in increasing job satisfaction. Hence, the objective of this study is to investigate the mediating role of social capital between developer participation in inner source and job satisfaction.

Drawing on social capital theory, we develop a theoretical model. We test this model using a mixed-methods approach that combines quantitative archival version control system data and survey data among 155 software developers in one large company, as well 22 interviews with 17 personnel in six companies that have adopted an inner source strategy. This study makes a number of novel contributions. First, this article is. to the best of our knowledge. the first to present a study of the inner source phenomenon in the strategic IS literature, which differentiates it from prior work that has primarily focused on an operational level of software product development, Second, most studies on inner source are either descriptive case studies or experience reports (Dinkelacker et al., 2002: Gurbani et al., 2006; Gurbani et al., 2010; Wesselius, 2008), or present reviews, taxonomies and research agendas (e.g., Capraro and Riehle, 2017; Caroll et al., 2018; Edison et al., 2020; Morgan et al., 2021; Stol et al., 2014). While valuable in their own right, these studies do not rise to a level that facilitates theorizing and theory testing. Given the lack of theoretical glue lamented by others in this area (Edison et al., 2020), our theory-driven study is a novel contribution that can help to establish a cumulative tradition for the inner source literature. Third, while benefits arising from social capital have been extensively studied (Ali-Hassan et al., 2015; Chou and He, 2011; Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998), antecedents of social capital have received limited attention (Lee et al., 2015; Payne et al., 2011). Thus, in this article we propose inner source participation as one such mechanism through which IS developers can build social capital. Further contributing to social capital theory, the fourth contribution of this article is a demonstration that social capital serves as a mechanism that mediates the relationship between inner source participation and job satisfaction.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. In the next section, we discuss the emergence of inner source as an organi zational strategy, and present the theoretical foundation for the study. We then propose a theoretical model by developing a set of six hypotheses. These hypotheses are evaluated using a mixed-methods approach (Venkatesh et al., 2013), first using structural equation modeling based on a combination of data gathered from two separate sources, followed by an interview study involving informants from six organizations that have adopted inner source initiatives, to corroborate and enrich the quantitative findings. We conclude the article with a discussion of the results, limitations, and implications for managers.

## Background and related work

## Inner source as a management strategy

Similar to other concepts such as agile methods and design thinking, inner source has gradually transformed from a software development specific focus into a broader management strategy (Morgan et al., 2021). At its core, inner source rests on three key principles underpinning the open source way of working (Riehle et al., 2009): egalitarianism, that is, anyone can contribute; meri tocracy, that is, contributions and decisions are evaluated on the basis of their merits, not the status of the contributor; and self-or ganization, where developers decide the way they work and what they work on, rather than being assigned tasks. These open source principles were also seen as promising in overcoming various coordination challenges of distributed development in larger companies (Ågerfalk and Fitzgerald, 2008; Fitzgerald, 2006; Gurbani et al., 2006; Stol and Fitzgerald, 2015), which increased in popularity in the late nineties (Lacity and Willcocks, 1998; Merali et al., 2012). During this period, numerous blue-chip companies experimented with the idea using different terminology, such as “internal open source” at Bell Laboratories (Gurbani et al., 2006), Progressive Open Source at Hewlett-Packard (Dinkelacker et al., 2002), and “Community Source” at IBM (Vitharana et al., 2010).

Many software organizations are organized in units (e.g., teams, business units), which do not share the source code of their products (Cooper and Stol. 2018: Stol et al., 2014). A key point of contention in such traditional settings is code ownership: teams commonly believe that only they are sufficiently qualified to work on their code, implying a lack of trust (Cooper and Stol, 2018, p.64). In practice, inner source implies that organizations create an open’ collaborative environment within the boundaries of an organization (Morgan et al., 2021; Riehle et al., 2009; Schlagwein et al., 2017; Stol et al., 2011), in which developers can share projects with others beyond their organizational unit, and likewise, they can choose to contribute to projects that are owned by other organizationa units. One reason that developers do this is to reduce their dependencies on others (Cooper and Stol, 2018; Stol et al., 2011; Wesselius, 2008). Inner source empowers developers to increase their self-directed work, allowing them to overcome organizational bottlenecks, and reduce frustration that stems from such bottlenecks.

More recently, inner source has emerged as a more strategic-level decision to change the way organizations develop software (Morgan et al., 2021), and an increasing number of organizations are starting to adopt inner source initiatives (Stol et al., 2022). However, inner source has been largely ignored in the IS literature as a specific organizational strategy to IS development. This is surprising, given the increasing attention on inner source from organizations worldwide across different sectors (Stol et al., 2022). There are some notable exceptions. Vitharana et al. (2010) reported a qualitative case study of IBM’s internal open source initiative and found that it facilitates increased information sharing and leverages a wider (internal) community of developers, which in turn helps to improve software reuse. Morgan et al. (2011) investigated inner source from an open innovation perspective, and more recently the use of inner source to create internal platforms (Morgan et al., 2021). Two other papers presented prescriptive frameworks for adopting inner source (Sharma et al., 2002; Torkar et al., 2011). Notwithstanding, there is a general lack of theory development in the inner source literature with a recent literature review concluding that “theorising the inner source approach is important since there is lack of cohesion, cumulative tradition and clarity” (Edison et al., 2020, p.15).

## Social relationships in inner source

Relationships are key to software development (Cramton and Webber, 2005; DeMarco and Lister, 1987). Large software projects involve numerous people and teams who must communicate, collaborate, and coordinate to deliver a high-quality product (Strode, 2016). In traditional organizations, these collaboration networks tend to be rigid and align with organizational structures and boundaries (Brass et al. 2004): developers belong to an organizational unit and are typically assigned to work on projects within that context (He et al., 2007). These organizational “silos" or “fiefdoms" (Hirschheim and Newman. 1991) may lead to a lack of trust towards, and conflict with, other teams (Cilliers and Greyvenstein, 2012: Hemon et al., 2020: Stol et al., 2011: Wesselius, 2008) Dependencies frequently exist between software components that are developed by different units (Strode. 2016): producers and consumers of components may have different assumptions and views of what the component should do (Grinter, 2003; Stol et al., 2011). Furthermore, organizational boundaries may cause significant friction (Grinter, 2003), as each unit may have different pri orities, constraints, and budgets (Riehle et al., 2016; Stol et al., 2011).

Inner source facilitates the building of an organization-wide internal developer community that transcends intra-organizational boundaries (Riehle et al., 2009; Stol and Fitzgerald, 2015). Rather than being confined to a specific unit that works on assigned tasks, inner source emphasizes collaboration and communication across units, thus reducing friction between these organizationa units (Gurbani et al., 2006: Wesselius. 2008). Developers participate in an inner source initiative on a voluntary basis: that is, developers are encouraged, but not required, to share and contribute to projects owned by other units. Inner source initiatives can also foster empowerment of developers (Cooper and Stol. 2018: Riehle et al., 2009). Rather than sending a request to develop a certain functionality to the unit that owns a component (a so-called “feature request’). developers are empowered to collaborate to overcome this bottleneck (Stol et al., 2011) by developing certain functionalities themselves and offering the result to the component owner (a socalled “pull-request”).<sup>2</sup>

A central premise in this study is that IS developers participating in inner source projects build up social capital as it implies more as well as intensified social interaction with developers within the organization but beyond their own unit. The term ‘social capital’ is defined as: “the sum of the actual and potential resources embedded within, available through, and derived from the network of relationships possessed by an individual or social unit” (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). In other words, it is relationships that make people’s jobs, activities, and daily life more efficient. The social capital metaphor has since been widely used, notably in management literature, but also in IS research (Ali-Hassan et al., 2015; Chou and He, 2011; Daniel et al., 2018; Hsu and Hung, 2013; Huysman and Wulf, 2006; Merali et al., 2012; Urquhart et al., 2008; Zimmermann et al., 2018). Previous work has shown social capital to be effective in facilitating exchange of resources between different organizational units and improving the effectiveness of cross-functional teams (Adler and Kwon, 2002). Both exchange of resources and cross-functional teams are relevant concepts to inner source, and hence we argue that social capital theory is a highly suitable lens for studying the inner source phenomenon. Rass et al. (2013) theorized the mediating role of social capital in the relationship between open innovation and firm performance, though this was not substantiated with empirical data. In this paper, we argue that inner source helps to build social capital; by participating in inner source projects, developers increase their “network of relationships” with other developers, transcending intra-organizational boundaries (Brass et al., 2004).

## Inner Source, social Capital, and job satisfaction

Social capital theory has predominantly been used to explain the emergence of intellectual capital, job performance, innovation, and organizational performance (e.g., Ali-Hassan et al., 2015; Rass et al., 2013; Chou and He, 2011; Wasko and Faraj, 2005). While there is some nascent work that highlights the positive effect of social capital on job satisfaction (Flap and Volker, ¨ 2001; Ommen et al., 2011; Shin and Lee, 2016), this relationship has not been studied within the IS context. Job satisfaction is broadly defined as (Morris and Venkatesh, 2010, p. 145): “the extent of positive emotional response to the job resulting from an employee’s appraisal of the job as fulfilling or congruent with the individual's values." Job satisfaction has been linked to several organizational benefits. including employees’ intentions to remain with an organization (Locke, 1969; Maier et al., 2013; Sharma and Stol, 2020) and organizational performance (Kessler et al., 2020). Developers leaving an organization is a key concern in the software industry given that the associated loss of knowledge and expertise is challenging and costly to replace (Brooks Jr, 1995; DeMarco and Lister, 1987; Quan and Cha, 2010). For that reason, IS researchers have focused on finding mechanisms that stimulate job satisfaction (Joseph et al., 2007; Tripp et al., 2016).

While many demographic factors, job related factors, and organizational factors determine job satisfaction (Joseph et al., 2007). most IS research in this area has focused on aspects of job design to explain the emergence of job satisfaction (Morris and Venkatesh, 2010: Thatcher et al., 2002: Tripp et al., 2016). While the principles underpinning inner source suggest some changes to developers workflow, the nature of the work (designing and developing software) does not fundamentally change. Rather than focusing on the job design of developers in inner source, we focus on the role of social capital that is built while collaborating across organizationa boundaries. We argue that it is not the participation in inner source projects itself that leads to job satisfaction, but rather that this relationship is mediated by social capital. The traditional organizational structures that shape interpersonal networks (Brass et al. 2004) place limits on the social interactions that take place, and the outcomes of such interactions such as trust. This is directly relevant to IS development, because development of large software products is usually decomposed into components, assigned to specific organizational units. This, however, inevitably leads to dependencies, which in turn can lead to friction in the development proc ess—and that in turn frequently leads to developer frustration (Stol et al., 2011). Inner source removes such constraints, allowing and encouraging developers to participate in projects that are of interest to them (Cooper and Stol, 2018; Riehle et al., 2009), or that they believe can resolve critical dependency bottlenecks (Stol et al., 2011). Hence, reducing friction and frustration through this process has the potential to increase job satisfaction.

## Hypothesis development

We propose a theoretical model that rests on a chain of effects; we argue that inner source participation induces development of social capital. which subsequently leads to software developers' job satisfaction. As argued above, the importance of social relationships in software development suggests a social capital lens. We draw on Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998)’s conceptualization of social capital. which comprises three dimensions: the structural. the cognitive, and the relational dimension. The structural dimension represents the “configuration of connections between actors” (Zimmermann et al., 2018). The cognitive dimension refers to “shared representations, interpretations, and systems of meaning” (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). This dimension includes a range of aspects related to having a shared mindset and a subsequent ability to communicate effectively. Finally, the relational dimension represents personal relationships that people may develop over time “through a history of interactions” (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998, p. 244). While there is consensus that these three dimensions build the core of social capital, each of these dimensions have appeared in various guises in the IS literature (e.g., Ali-Hassan et al., 2015: Chiu et al., 2006: Chou and He. 2011: Wasko and Farai, 2005). For example. relational social capital has been operationalized as “trust” (e.g., Ali-Hassan et al., 2015) as well as “reciprocity” (e.g., Wasko and Faraj 2005).

We largely follow the conceptualization by Chiu et al. (2006) because, similar to their study, we also focus on a single developer community for our quantitative study. In line with Chiu et al. (2006), we operationalize structural capital as the social interaction ties between people, i.e., the interactions that developers have within an inner source community. Such network ties are beneficial to information flow and knowledge sharing (Bolino et al., 2002), which is important in large-scale software development. To oper ationalize cognitive social capital, we focus specifically on shared vision and shared language. The relational dimension includes several aspects of relationships between people, such as trust, norms, and identification (Fukuyama, 1995; Wasko and Faraj, 2005). In the context of IS development teams, an essential attribute of emergent relationships is trust (Sarker et al., 2011), and prior work on inner source has emphasized a lack of trust between teams as an obstacle (Cooper and Stol, 2018; Riehle et al., 2016). For that reason, and following others (e.g., Ali-Hassan et al., 2015), we operationalize relational social capital as trust. We adopt Mayer et al.’s (1995) definition of trust as “the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party.” This definition is commonly adopted in IS research and in essence can be summarized as “the expectation that others will behave as expected” (Jarvenpaa et al., 1998).

Following Nahapiet and Ghosal (1998), we note that structural and cognitive social capital are prerequisites of relational socia capital; that is, trust requires the presence of social ties and shared understanding. Fig. 1 portrays the theoretical research model with the chain of effects, proposing social capital as a key mechanism of transforming inner source participation into developers’ job satisfaction. We develop our hypotheses step by step in the following.

Adopting inner source implies acknowledging the principles of egalitarianism, meritocracy, and self-organization (Riehle et al., 2009). In traditional team-based structures, developers would primarily collaborate with their team members. Prior research has shown that ‘insufficient developer networking’ can lead to a lack of collaboration among teams (Riehle et al., 2016; Wesselius, 2008).

The fact that anyone can contribute regardless of hierarchical levels (egalitarianism) together with contributions being evaluated based on merit instead of developer reputation or standing (meritocracy), enables interactions between developers who formerly may have been unaware of each other, simply because they belonged to different organizational units. Members from any organizationa unit can choose to participate (self-organize) in shared projects, thus establishing new social relationships with colleagues that they may never have met before.

In line with social capital theory (Kwon and Adler, 2014), newly emerged inner source-initiated relationships among developers can become of high strategic value both to the organization and for the individual developer. Having a social tie with another person indicates shared membership of some group, which may be formal or informal. Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998, p. 244) acknowledge that this may be an “impersonal” linkage, but it suggests the potential of who could be reached when mobilizing someone within the network. By participating in inner source projects, developers increase their collaboration network with potentially anyone within an organization through shared projects. In so doing, developers will naturally expand their ‘network of co-contributors’ throughout the organization. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1a (H1a). Developers who participate more in inner source projects develop more social interaction ties with colleagues.

A major source of difficulty in large-scale IS development is the need to manage dependencies between different components; a component is created by one team and likely to be used and integrated by another team (Grinter, 2003; Riehle et al., 2016; Stol et al., 2011: Strode et al., 2006). This situation often leads to several challenges. First, the consumer of the component is often unaware of the assumptions that the component producer made (Riehle et al., 2016: Stol et al.. 2011). Second, components often miss critical features that the consuming team needs (Stol et al., 2011; Wesselius, 2008), because the producer is not fully aware of the consuming team’s requirements or operating environment. These issues suggest a lack of shared understanding among teams (Riehle et al., 2016; Strode et al., 2006). In general, IS developers tend to focus on their own tasks. Inner source changes that because working collaboratively on components implies more extensive communication about the requirements of those components. Tsai and Ghoshal (1998) suggested that a shared vision is “a bonding mechanism that helps different parts of an organization to integrate or to combine resources,” and that “a shared vision and/or a set of common values help develop this dimension of social capital, which in turn facilitates individua and group actions that can benefit the whole organization.” This also means an increasing awareness and learning of other teams’ requirements and constraints (Stol et al., 2011). In that process of learning, software developers get a better perspective about how shared components can serve the whole organization rather than only a specific business unit (Gurbani et al., 2006; Stol et al., 2014). Furthermore, developers from different business units might also diverge in their use of language and terminology; an increased leve of interaction through shared projects is likely to align the language that developers from different business units use. In line with social capital theory, we posit that, as developers collaborate with others on an inner source project, they will exhibit an increased leve of cognitive social capital manifested as a shared vision (i.e. a better understanding of the organization’s overall goals) and shared language (i.e. a better understanding of communication habits), which together we refer to as shared understanding

Hypothesis 1b (H1b). Developers who participate more in inner source projects experience a higher level of shared understanding

Tsai and Ghoshal (1998) pointed out that “trusting relationships evolve from social interactions,” because trust is an emergent property of relationships; through continued social interactions, relationships grow over time. Trust also plays a key factor in IS development, as mentioned earlier (Sarker et al., 2011). Costa and Peiró (2009) describe trust succinctly as being the “key to the development of cooperation and in the coordination of expectations, interactions and behaviors among individuals.” As most orga nizations are divided into business units, each often as a profit center, collaboration is usually hampered because each business unit has their own focus and interests (as well as a balance sheet) that they pursue. These foci lead to the well-known downsides of a silo-based mentality such as distrust of contributions from developers elsewhere (Cooper and Stol, 2018) and the “not-invented-here" syndrome (Antons and Piller, 2015). It is the lack of interactions (and indeed social ties) that prevents the opportunities to build trust. In line with the network literature (e.g., Krackhardt, 1992), strong ties among developers across multiple organizational units emerge through frequent and close interactions, and are known to engender trust. Thus, as the social capital lens suggests that trust results from socia interactions (Tsai and Ghosal, 1998), we argue that inner source facilitates easier intra-organizational social interactions among software developers, which in turn helps to build trust. This is consistent with prior research that linked strong bonds among proiect members, or what Singh et al. (2011) have labeled “cohesion,” to emergent attributes such as trust (Coleman, 1988). Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2a (H2a). The more social ties a developer has with others in an inner source community, the more trust that developer has in members of that community.

Similarly, we expect that developers who share a shared understanding of how they work together, of the software components they work on, and use the same cognitive representations in the discussions they have about their work and how their software can contribute to the success of the whole organization, will also exhibit a higher level of trust towards one another. Specifically, the cognitive dimension of social capital is characterized by shared language, shared rituals, shared narratives, and shared visions (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). In software development, these forms of social capital lead to states of harmony in a context that is otherwise characterized by the possibility of opportunistic behavior (e.g., co-workers could hide software code from others, knowledge hoarding, etc.). The cognitive dimension of social capital further implies, for example, a shared understanding of advantages and disadvantages of development approaches, agreement on annotating and documenting software code, and developing software to wards shared goals (Goldbach and Benlian, 2015). Hence, these facets can be considered as shared understanding among developers, which constitutes the basis for mutual communication. Shared understanding implies a high quality of exchanged information (because misunderstandings are reduced), which has been linked to both an increase in trusting beliefs (which includes a sense that others in a “transaction” are honest and sincere) and a decrease in perceived risk (which includes the potential of experiencing “loss” when working with others) (Nicolaou and McKnight, 2006). Hence, we propose:

Hypothesis 2b (H2b). The more a developer experiences a shared understanding with others, the more that developer trusts other members in the inner source community.

We further posit that developers who can trust others in the organization have a higher sense of job satisfaction. As briefly mentioned, many organizations are organized into isolated business units, while at the same time developers from different units must frequently collaborate to integrate each other's software components. or deliver specific functionality in time due to product dependencies (Riehle et al., 2016; Stol et al., 2011; Wesselius, 2008). If a producing unit delays its component deliveries to a consuming unit. then the consuming unit must deal with the consequences: for example. product releases may have to be delaved in turn (Stol et al., 2011). This ‘breaking of promises' can lead to a sense of distrust towards other teams (Riehle et al., 2016). Such product release delays may be consequential for product teams as a whole, potentially leading to great frustrations and break-downs of relationships, which in turn is likely to affect a person's level of iob satisfaction. This notion is also echoed by Festinger's (1957) cognitive dissonance theory, which suggests that individuals strive for harmony in their beliefs and perceptions, which can be increased by high levels of trust (Kim et al., 2009). Hence, we argue that developers who can trust their peers will experience less frustration and in turn, the experience a higher level of job satisfaction. Hence, given the benefits developers derive from their relations with coworkers, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3 (H3). The more a developer trusts other members in the inner source community, the higher that developer’s job satisfaction.

Finally, we argue that social capital mediates the relationship between inner source participation and job satisfaction. That is, participation influences job satisfaction through the development of social capital, which contrasts with the suggestion that partici pation in inner source projects corresponds directly to a higher level of job satisfaction. It is not participation in inner source projects itself that affects developers’ job satisfaction, but rather the ability to draw on a network of relationships. Recalling Nahapiet and Ghoshal’s (1998) definition of social capital cited earlier, it is the participation in inner source that leads to “actual and potential resources embedded in the created network of relationships,” which in turn link to a higher level of job satisfaction. Inner source affords any developer (egalitarianism) to choose which projects they contribute to (self-organization), and to build relationships with developers from other organizational units. Developers participate in activities such as making contributions and giving feedback to evaluate others’ contributions to determine whether these should be integrated (meritocracy). These activities establish socia interaction ties and, as we argued before, this in turn helps to build trust. We also argue that participating in inner source affords developers to converge in their understanding of the organization’s software system designs, learn new technologies and expertise, and get recognition for their expertise (Riehle et al., 2009). Expertise is a form of shared knowledge that is acknowledged by others to be valuable, and which can be trusted by others. The ability to trust others means fewer conflicts (as long as that trust is not broken), and that in turn makes for a less stressful and more eniovable work experience. which we argue translates into job satisfaction.

The proposed mediating effect of social capital is in line with previous work on interpersonal networks, which are at the core of social capital. Organizational structure is a key force in shaping these interpersonal networks (Brass et al., 2004); that is, most or ganizations feature formal organizational structures such as teams and business units, roles, and task assignment. These forma structures thus influence which networks are built (Brass et al., 2004). The inner source principle of self-organization releases these constraints by encouraging developers to choose which shared project to join, which in turn helps to forge new interpersonal networks At the same time, interpersonal networks have also been linked to job satisfaction, though with various outcomes; as Brass et al. (2004) point out. “interactions are not always positive." Again. interactions that follow from constrained networks due to organizational structures (teams, business units) may be necessary, which could explain any negative relationship to job satisfaction. In inner source, however, the network constraint is released, and so developers can choose to no longer participate in inner source projects where the might face “the obnoxious coworker” (Brass et al., 2004, pp. 798). Thus, in sum, inner source participation links to an increase in socia capital, comprising social interaction ties, shared understanding, and trust, which contributes to job satisfaction. This leads to the final hypothesis in our model:

Hypothesis 4 (H4). The relationship between inner source participation and job satisfaction is indirect, serially mediated by (a) social interaction ties and trust and by (b) shared understanding and trust.

## Research method

We adopted a mixed-method approach (Ågerfalk, 2013; Creswell, 2014; Venkatesh et al., 2013) which is defined as a “class of research where the researcher mixes or combines quantitative and qualitative research techniques, methods, approaches, concepts or language into a single study” (Johnson and Onwuegbuzie, 2004, p. 17). Several forms of mixed-method approaches exist such that either the quantitative part complements the qualitative one, or vice versa (Venkatesh et al., 2013). We adopt a sequential mixed-methods approach to test and substantiate our theoretical model by combining quantitative data collected through a survey matched with behavioral data at one organization that had an active inner source initiative, with qualitative interviews with 17 inner source practitioners. Hence, the primary purpose for conducting a mixed methods approach was to corroborate and enrich the insights gained from the quantitative study by additional qualitative analyses, following recommendations for the use of mixed methods (Venkatesh et al., 2013). We use triangulation to confirm the findings by situating the findings from the quantitative study within the results obtained from the qualitative analysis

## Quantitative Method: Survey design and data collection

To test the research model in Fig. 1, we first conducted a survey at SysCom (a pseudonym), which is a large organization with approximately 850 engineering staff distributed across several offices within a single country. The organization develops multiple software systems in the defense sector. To protect the identity of the firm, we do not report its geographic location or the software products they produce. SysCom started an inner source program in late 2017 and maintains its own private installation of the GitLab version control system. All developers within the company have a GitLab account, and all source code developed by SysCom is managed through the platform, which is accessible to all staff. Similar to how organizations vary in their adoption of development methods (Fitzgerald. 1997), inner source initiatives also vary across organizations (Cooper and Stol, 2018: Gurbani et al., 2010) However, a quintessential attribute is that any developer is free to create projects and to make contributions to other inner source projects (Cooper and Stol, 2018; Riehle et al., 2009; Stol et al., 2014).

We collected data from two separate sources that were combined for the analyses. First. we conducted a survey using an internal corporate platform based on Microsoft SharePoint to gather data on the social capital constructs (social interaction ties, shared un derstanding, and trust), job satisfaction, and demographics. We also asked respondents to provide their GitLab user ID. All engineering staff were invited to fill in the survey and were assured of confidentiality of the provided information, in particular because we also requested their GitLab user ID. To assess respondents’ inner source participation, we then gathered their activity data from the corporate GitLab installation until the date we started the survey, using the provided GitLab user IDs. We collected 155 usable re sponses (a response rate of ca. 18 %) for our analyses. Table 1 presents descriptive statistics of the sample.

For all but one of the constructs we relied on measurement instruments that have been validated in prior studies. Given the nascent state of quantitative research on inner source as an organizational strategy to IS development, no measurement instruments exist for the degree of inner source participation. Below we explain how we operationalized inner source participation.

Appendix A lists the data collection instrument for the developer survey. All instruments except inner source participation (described next) used a 7-point Likert scale.

Inner Source Participation. We developed a three-item instrument that captures key activities in inner source. These items included the number of pull requests (PR) that the developer has merged,<sup>3</sup> the number of PRs that the developer has contributed, and the number of projects that a developer has shared (‘inner-sourced’) with others. The first item is the number of PRs that a developer merged, which indicates that the developer has integrated a contribution from someone else into the code base. The second item is a measure of contributions made to other teams’ source code repositories than a developer’s own team. The third item is a measure of repositories that a developer has shared with others.<sup>4</sup> All three indicators were collected from SysCom’s version control system, rather than through the developer survey. Such data is therefore not opinion or based on memory, but represents a trace of actual developer activity. The values of these variables tend to be very skewed; i.e., a relatively small number of people tend to have high numbers, whereas a majority tends to have smaller numbers in terms of contributions, etc. For example, some studies of open source have suggested that 80 % of contributions are made by 20 % of contributors (Mockus et al., 2002). Following Hahn et al. (2008) and Peng et al. (2013), we used the logarithmic values of these variables.

Social Interaction Ties. We operationalize structural social capital as social interaction ties. To measure a developer’s social inter action ties, we drew selected items from an instrument by Chiu et al. (2006). The original instrument contained four items. However, we excluded the item “I know some members in the SysCom virtual community on a personal level” because some relationships may remain virtual only (i.e., colleagues may never meet), and so we deemed this statement to be not useful in the context of this study. Similar to open source communities, developers in inner source settings may also not meet in person.

Shared Understanding. To measure the cognitive dimension of social capital, we selected and adopted five items from an instrument from Chiu et al. (2006), who measured cognitive social capital by means of two latent variables: Shared Vision and Shared Language. As a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) indicated a lack of discriminatory validity between these two constructs, we combined their items into a single latent variable that we labeled Shared Understanding.

Trust. We follow Costa and Peiro ´ (2009) and Lee et al., (2015) in conceptualizing the relational dimension of social capital as trust. Trust was measured using three items adapted from Chiu et al. (2006).

Job Satisfaction. We measured job satisfaction using an instrument of four items adopted from Longo and Mura (2011). We chose this measure as it had been used before in the context of other forms of “capital” (e.g., Longo and Mura, 2011) and because it captures job satisfaction from a holistic perspective.

Control variables. We included three demographic control variables: age (measured in years; minimum 25, maximum 63), tenure with the company (measured in months; minimum 4, maximum 420), and gender (coded as a binary value; 112 men, 43 women).

## Structural equation modeling

We conducted structural equation modeling (SEM) with IBM SPSS AMOS version 28, using maximum likelihood estimation. Using SEM is suitable as our conceptual model involves latent variables, as well as parallel and serial mediation (Iacobucci et al., 2007). SEM is considered a “gold standard” for analyzing models with latent variables (Hoyle, 2022). A potential issue is that models cannot converge due to a small sample size, and alternative methods exist to address this, including Factor Score Regression (FSR) and the Croon Method (Devlieger and Rosseel, 2017). However, both methods suffer from limitations in terms of model constraints or bias in the parameter estimates. For that reason, FSR is not advised (Devlieger and Rosseel, 2022, p. 317), and the Croon method does not allow correlated residual errors (Devlieger and Rosseel, 2017, p. 37). The latter emerged to be a necessary part of our model, as we discuss below. Our model converged without issues.

Evaluation of the model according to commonly used fit measures led to reasonable results, though not optimal $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f } = 2 . 1 7$ (247.16/114). CFI = 0.94. TLI = 0.92, NFI = 0.89. RMSEA = 0.087: the value of SRMR (0.11) fell bevond the desirable cut-off of 0.10 (Kline, 2016). We inspected the modification indices (MI) to identify potential sources of misfit in the model. Making changes to a model on the basis of MIs must be done with great care and caution. Scholars have suggested that such modifications can be allowed under certain conditions, namely if such modifications are minimal and theoretically justifiable, and such modifications do not lead to significant changes to model parameters (Bowen and Guo, 2012; Byrne et al., 1989). MIs indicate sources of misspecification between data and model; as such, they can also be treated as a reminder to the researcher what relationships should be reconsidered. In this case, the MI suggested a correlation between the error terms of social interaction ties and shared understanding, which represented the structural and cognitive dimensions of social capital, respectively. As Byrne et al., (1989,p. 460) pointed out, allowing error terms to correlate is justified when these parameters represent non-random measurement error that can be ascribed to method effects. In this case, structural and cognitive social capital, representing two dimensions of social capital, were assessed using the same survey in strument. Following Tsai and Ghoshal (1998), both are modeled as antecedents of trust, which is based on the relational dimension of social capital. Furthermore, previous research suggested interactions exist between different dimensions of social capital (Hsu and Hung, 2013). Thus, we argue there is theoretical justification to correlate the error terms of social interaction ties and shared un derstanding. The resulting model improved to a good fit (χ<sup>2</sup>/df = 1.91 (246.69/129), CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.077, SRMR = 0.06).7

For analysis of the mediation hypothesis (H4), we follow Hayes’s (2009, 2018) recommendation to rely on a bootstrap procedure to evaluate the significance of the indirect effect. The bootstrap procedure generates a sampling distribution of the indirect effect from which a confidence interval can be derived. No assumptions regarding the distribution are made, nor does the inference computation involve the standard error (Hayes, 2009, pp. 412). We relied on the bias-corrected percentile-based bootstrap procedure with 10,000 samples and a 95 % confidence level.

For example, it is clear that many other factors affect job satisfaction (e.g., opportunities for promotion, pay and benefits, autonomy), which were not within scope of this study. We return to this when we discuss the limitations of this study.

A central line of reasoning in our theory development is the importance of social capital. and its mediating role in participation in inner source on the one hand, and job satisfaction on the other. The conceptual model depicts a situation, in which a parallel mediation (an effect goes through more than one parallel path) and serial mediation (mediation occurs over multiple “steps”) are simultaneousl present. We report overall direct effects, overall indirect effects, and indirect effects through social interaction ties as well as shared understanding. We ran two separate analyses to evaluate the mediation hypothesis (H4); once using SPSS AMOS version 28, and once using the PROCESS macro for SPSS (Hayes, 2018). The results of these analyses are shown in Table 6.

The analysis with AMOS indicates that the direct effect of inner source participation on job satisfaction is not significant (β = 0.01, p =.92, CI: [–0.25, 0.26]). In contrast, the mediation analysis shows that the indirect effect of inner source participation on job satis faction (through all parallel and serial mediators) is significant. The results of the bias-corrected bootstrap procedure (recommended for evaluating mediating effects (Hayes, 2018), with 10,000 bootstrap samples) indicated a significant effect, with an unstandardized coefficient of 0.08 and a 95 % confidence interval [0.01, 0.19]. Following Zhao et al. (2010) and others (see Hayes, 2018), the sig nificance of this indirect effect indicates a mediating effect.8 Hence, we conclude that the data support H4.

## Robustness checks

We conducted additional mediation analyses with the PROCESS macro for IBM SPSS (Hayes, 2018), which is a specialized set of scripts to conduct mediation and moderation analyses and also capable of calculating specific indirect effects for sub-paths of a paralle mediation. PROCESS defines a large number of built-in models or templates, which can be populated with the variables of interest. While specialized for mediation and moderation analyses, PROCESS does not work with latent variables, which is why we used factor scores generated by AMOS based on the CFA. Using PROCESS Model 80 (Hayes, 2018, pp. 606), the results were consistent with the analyses conducted using AMOS (see Table 6). The table also shows that the direct effect of participation on job satisfaction is not significant, indicating full (rather than partial) mediation (Zhao et al., 2010).

As our dataset comprises observational data rather than experimental data, any claims of causality must be justified and grounded in strong theory. To gain further confidence in our results, we conducted a robustness check using Propensity Score Matching (PSM) to exclude selection biases and applied the instrument variable approach using 2SLS to minimize the threat of endogeneity. The results of these additional robustness tests are presented in Appendix C.

The interviews also provided supporting evidence for Hypothesis 1b, which suggested that participating in inner source is bene ficial in creating a shared understanding among developers. For example, an interview respondent in LifeCom described how many dependencies existed between the components that different teams were developing in parallel. LifeCom used inner source to move towards cross-functional teams that worked on specific customer features and leveraged the company’s code repository system to open up each team’s code in a shared space. The company aimed to align development activities in a way that encouraged conversation across teams, built around a shared customer-orientation. This helped to create shared ownership and reduce duplication among software com ponents, as well as reduce “territoriality,” i.e., a sense of exclusive ownership of a product. The Head of Inner Source at LifeCom remarked:

“Multiple teams now come together to define features they want shipped to the market and also work together to come up with a single timeline.”

Interview respondents in FinCom also outlined the historic tensions that existed between back-end and front-end developers who, faced with intense time pressures and work schedules, were convinced they needed to maintain sole ownership of their code to ensure quality. FinCom hoped they could trigger more communication and collaboration across the organization by creating more trans parency and standardizing the way components were developed. Like Lifecom, they set up an enterprise code repository for teams to store and share components easily and effectively. As a result of these practices, collaboration improved between front-end and backend teams, as each learned more about interdependencies in their work. This learning across the board helps to establish a shared understanding of both the process and the systems being developed.

“Now there is less time and less frustration spent on: who has been doing this? Who is responsible for that? Where is the code gonna go? How are we going to figure out who owns it? There are so many different systems that have to work together. Now everybody gets the opportunity to actually integrate what they learn from having new people look at their systems.” (Technical Leader, FinCom)

Similar experiences were reported at HealthCom who maintain a shared platform component that is reused by different business units. This shared component is designed with several layers of abstractions, which are necessary to make the component suitable for a wide range of divisions. This, however, makes integration more complicated, and business units would not always appreciate the need for the design, which they perceived as unnecessarily complex. Inner source allows developers to participate in joint projects involving other business units which has helped to establish a better understanding and appreciation of the software design. Interview re spondents from HealthCom described how inner source has resulted in remarkable change for the company as there is much more awareness, and efficiency now that everyone can participate in the shared project. A Software Team Lead from HealthCom again emphasized the importance of learning across teams:

“Sharing resources and capabilities in the form of knowledge, expertise, experience of others etc. is extremely beneficial for developers as it promotes learning across teams.”

This emphasized that there was an increase in developers’ awareness of other teams and projects, and that developers had a shared vision of the potential to recognize opportunities to achieve synergies. Furthermore, the mindset of collaboration that emerges with inner source has resulted in developers having what one respondent from HealthCom described as “having a lot of drive to look beyond their own work. " At HealthCom. working with developers across department borders has resulted in increased organizational learning as developers from different departments learn more about the constraints and domain knowledge of different units

Several interview respondents remarked on how onboarding and developer mobility improved in their respective organizations as developers had become familiar with, and contributed to, shared projects of other teams. This increased shared understanding allows them to become productive more quickly after moving to another business unit. Furthermore, several respondents noted that inner source brings together people who have similar interests and shared goals

“With a large organization it’s very difficult to align the stars and have a joint collaboration project. However, with inner source you have this alignment of interests around a shared goal …. And that leads to a lot more creativity.” (Software Engineer, TechCom)

## Social interaction Ties, shared Understanding, and building trust

When focusing on building trust, either through an increase in social interaction ties (H2a) or through an increased level of shared understanding (H2b), the interviews also offered corroborating evidence.

Several interview respondents explained that the purpose of adopting inner source was to empower developers to address bot tlenecks in the software development process, and this would depend to a considerable degree on mutual trust. A common issue in organizations that are organized into business units, each of which operates as its own profit center, is that these units are evaluated based on their operational performance; managers compete for resources within the organization, serving as “barons” who are pro tective of their “fiefdoms” (Hirschheim and Newman, 1991). This is particularly problematic for those organizations where each working hour must be ‘billed’ or attributed to a specific project. Time spent on another team’s code therefore comes at the cost of the contributing team’s performance, and managers tend to be reluctant to spend ‘their’ budget on other teams’ projects. Hence, a number of initiatives were put in place to foster relationships. All of the organizations in this study established “core teams,” similar to those commonly found in open source projects (Mockus et al., 2002), to oversee and champion the adoption of inner source and help build trust between people. When discussing knowledge sharing and building relationships across team boundaries, interview respondents from NewsCom outlined how they communicate the value of inner source through a platform that comprises a directory of inner source projects, blogs to share stories, and tools to support collaboration. The inner source manager at LifeCom described how they organize weekly “TechTalks,” live streamed to everyone in the company, where teams from different business units get a chance to talk about their work, provide demos on new features and updates, and learn from each other. The company also introduced internal social media tools to encourage coordination and sharing of information. Interview respondents from TelCom described some of the initiatives they put in place to build relationships and trust, including a community portal, active and responsive forums, regular hackathons, and many face-to-face workshops. The Head of Strategy Implementation at Telcom discussed the importance of building a community of people across the distributed organization:

“It’s a new cultural change in a lot of enterprises where you move from this idea of a written communication and a web portal, doing things remotely, to bringing people from all over the company together. It’s very important to continually do that…. Continually evolve the community.”

The results of the interview analysis also suggest the importance of a shared understanding for building trust (supporting H2b). For example, a Development Manager at HealthCom discussed the importance of explicitly making inner source a company strategy, and being clear in what the vision is for a shared asset (i.e., an inner source project) so that business units can rely on that:

“It is important to have a clear unifying vision so that people don’t waiver off in different directions and send mixed messages to the rest of the teams.”

For TelCom, the establishment of a new governance structure, a flexible community portal and infrastructure, and core teams that aimed to build communities around components were vital to promoting a shared understanding. FinCom introduced the role of Trusted Committer – individuals who would evaluate contributions to inner source projects to ensure common standards were maintained. Furthermore, this role changed hands every two weeks, to ensure that all engineers would learn how to serve in this role, as well as to allow engineers to return to development. Management noted sharp quantifiable gains in efficiency among participating teams after six months, which, importantly, helped to change the prevailing mindset, reduced silo-thinking, and established new trust relationships. Individuals and teams became more likely to mentor others, as they knew everyone’s contributions would become part of a common asset.

An informant at LifeCom described how many people in the organization were initially cynical about inner source, as there was much concern over ‘who is to blame’ if code goes wrong. Thus, introducing inner source required the gradual build-up of trust to encourage individuals to become open to working with each other. Establishing governance processes and guidelines on how people should behave, dedicated core teams, tools that ensure transparency and communication, and incentives that reward and recognize contributions proved vital. Informants from NewsCom reported remarkably similar experiences; to advocate the inner source initia tive, certain members of staff were identified as champions who helped spread the word and build a community. Sharing of infor mation and experiences also helped to build trust, as a Software Team Leader at NewsCom explained:

“There are no shortcuts to building trust. We put people in place that have open personalities in terms of how they approach and view others' contributions. That helped. So you need champions who have the energy, passion and desire... Also having internal blogs where stories are shared, tools that are available to make inner sourcing easier and directories of inner source projects helps build trust.”

## Trust and job satisfaction

The interview results also provided corroborating evidence for the link between trust and job satisfaction (H3). A major problem with large-scale IS development is the need to integrate components and resolve dependencies. Teams are responsible for delivery of components, and due to concerns about the quality of contributions they are frequently protective of these assets. This means that developers from outside a team usually do not have permission, nor would they be trusted, to make changes. Likewise. teams that are dependent on components by other teams may be distrustful of those components (as mentioned briefly, they are “not invented here”). Inner source makes it easier to collaborate, communicate, and offers transparency that can help developers to identify and manage software dependencies, and allow others to make meaningful contributions. The Head of Strategy Implementation at TelCom explained how, before their inner source initiative, the process of working with other teams to address dependency issues took considerable time due to issues of ownership and a distrust towards people from other teams; much time would be wasted on finding information and waiting for people to respond to requests. Inner source helped to speed this up, allowing people to connect directly and empowering people to make cross-team contributions. The contributed code would still be subject to peer review by the owning team, but because of the continuous interactions and information sharing (including feedback) between the owner of a component and contributors, the resulting contribution would be of high quality.

“With inner source, we could break the dependency chains and the dependencies between different backlogs of different teams, by allowing people to contribute code back. People can do things for themselves. This led to much more efficient development, better code quality and overall job satisfaction.” (Head of Strategy Implementation, TelCom)

The creation of an inner source program at TelCom was credited for stimulating a change towards a more ‘open’ culture that reduced territoriality, fostered trust, and encouraged and empowered individuals to share, extend, and improve each other’s work. A Software Engineer at TechCom described how transparency led to a higher level of sharing and inclusivity, emphasizing their importance to instill trust between teams and promoting overall employee satisfaction:

“We share everything, really everything. Not just work products, but also decision making, communication, anything really and this is very rewarding.”

An interview respondent from FinCom remarked that “transparency is the secret sauce” to building trust, something that was also echoed by many of the other interview respondents. This increase in trust coincided with several other factors, including pride in the job, personal growth, cross-unit collaboration, increased product awareness, and increased productivity, with interview respondents reporting that these factors contributed to a higher sense of employee satisfaction

## The mediating role of social capital

Lastly, our analysis also found supporting evidence for Hypothesis 4, which suggests that social capital mediates the relationship between inner source participation and job satisfaction. While practitioners are typically not familiar with methodological nomen clature such as “mediation,” we specifically probed for indications of mediation.

As the results presented above suggest, IS professionals report that connections with others are key to making their lives easier. Organizations in this study leverage the enterprise infrastructure to increase transparency, allowing developers to connect and work in open ways. This involves full accessibility to all source code by default, providing support through Q&A forums, feedback mechanisms such as peer review, “hack weeks,” and other ways to share knowledge including wikis and mailing lists. As it provides full openness, developers have an opportunity to talk about their work, establish new interactions with people in other divisions to collaborate on shared interests, and receive feedback from potentially anyone within the organization. This ability to connect to others and help others ultimately translates to higher levels of reported job satisfaction, as one Team Lead at NewsCom shared:

“I gain satisfaction from trying to help anyone that wants to engage in inner source projects.”

The human connection, the ability to connect to others, and make the job at hand easier or more fulfilling speaks to the power of social capital. Interview respondents from HealthCom and LifeCom both described how inner source enabled them to build and share software more easily and effectively, and helped them to eliminate dependencies and ensure teams were not waiting on each other to complete critical pieces of work. The inner source manager at LifeCom commented:

“With inner source, developers not only care about their own benefit and their own purpose but now think about the overall purpose and growth of the company… and help others to move up the ladder as well.”

All interview respondents reflected on how inner source promoted ‘a much happier workforce’ and something that is simply ‘fun to do.’ Speaking to the mediating role of social capital, people enjoy interacting and collaborating with others with similar interests.

“People view it as a bonus to engage in inner source. They are really enthusiastic about it. There are so many individual stories that are testament to this, I’ve lost count.” (Software Team Leader, NewsCom)”

In sum, inner source gives developers the freedom to work on different projects and identify or select particular tasks beyond their specific focus area: “they are very invested in or passionate about and will push forward to deliver high-quality products or solutions for the organization” (Software Engineer, TechCom). Another respondent from TechCom added that the idea of having people sharing ideas and artifacts they produce by being part of a community is extremely rewarding because:

“you feel a real sense of completion, you feel happy. And it’s not about my salary level you know or my relations with my boss, it’s about what I’m doing for the company. It’s very satisfying and a good feeling in my opinion” (Software Developer, TechCom)

Several informants mentioned that inner source is an “advancement in their career,” and was generally the preferred mode of working. One informant at TechCom, who started an inner source initiative several years ago reported:

“It’s not really necessary to do so much promotion about inner source anymore at this point. It pretty much has a mindshare. Developers just really want to work this way. They are excited by it.” (Team Leader, TechCom)

In conclusion, the interview excerpts support the notion that starting an inner source initiative increases collaboration among IS developers from different departments, which increases their level of social capital. This streamlines the work, and also comes with other benefits, all of which contribute to developers’ job satisfaction.

## Discussion and conclusion

This study is one of the first to conduct an empirical investigation of inner source as a strategy for managing IS development using a mixed methods approach. We investigate the link between inner source participation by software developers and job satisfaction, and how this link is mediated by social capital. We summarize our findings from the multi-method study in Table 7, and then discuss the contributions and implications of the study for IS management practice and research in the following sections.

## Implications for practice

The findings of our study have a number of implications for practice. As proposed in our theoretical model, participation in inner source projects helps to establish social interaction ties with others.

Much of the friction in large-scale software development stems from internal boundaries between units (“fiefdoms” (Hirschheim and Newman, 1991)) within organizations (Grinter, 2003). This friction is as old as the IS development profession, starting with the divide between development and operations. from which the DevOps movement has emerged in the last decade or so (Hemon et al 2020). In a similar vein, an inner source strategy provides a forum to connect developers from different organizational units to each other; without inner source, those developers may not have an opportunity to work together and potentially remain in isolation. Inner source represents a strategy that seeks to break down the ‘silo’ structure present in many organizations (Cooper and Stol, 2018; Riehle et al., 2016; Stol and Fitzgerald, 2015). Peng et al. (2013) found that co-membership among project teams helps to build ties in open source communities, which facilitate the flow of knowledge and expertise. We posit that similar mechanisms may apply to an inner source setting, though further research is needed to study this in detail. By facilitating staff to connect and collaborate on projects that they are interested in, we argue that this can help to establish awareness, understanding, and perhaps some level of rapport between staff who would otherwise not have collaborated. Such collaborations can lead to synergies and new, innovative products, as happened, for example, at TechCom, leading to a major innovation award. This potential should be of great interest to managers in all organizations.

Furthermore, we also found that participation in inner source projects helps to increase shared understanding. Software being an intangible product whose design largely exists in people’s minds, aligning understanding among developers is key to speeding up the development process or to onboard new people. For managers, these are clearly important aspects as this could translate in faster product delivery because teams who work on different components will better understand how these fit together. These findings are in line with previous observations that inner source helps to improve intra-organizational mobility of staff (Riehle et al., 2009).

We also found that social interaction ties and shared understanding are positively associated with relational trust. While socia interaction ties are likely to contribute to the emergence of trustworthy relations between people, it is the cognitive dimension of social capital that is more important; sharing an understanding of software projects is a stronger predictor to develop trustworthy relations among developers. This is likely to positively impact the performance of IS developers, and in turn, the performance of teams (Chou and He, 2011).

Furthermore, trust has been linked as a positive factor to team performance and knowledge sharing (Chiu et al.. 2006: Sarker et al.. 2011). Organizational division into separate teams (or “silos”) can lead to a blame culture; this is the key premise underpinning the DevOps movement in the IT industry which addresses the gap between development and operations divisions that exists in many organizations (Hemon et al., 2020). We propose that inner source as a strategy to overcome internal organizational boundaries in creases transparency, helps to encourage collaboration, and reduces the friction that results from unresolved software or knowledge dependencies (Grinter, 2003; Riehle et al., 2016). This is of particular importance to mid-level managers who are concerned with the performance of their specific unit, a frequently-heard challenge (Stol et al., 2022). While developers within a team may spend re sources on “other” projects, not owned by their unit, this has beneficial outcomes in terms of increased level of shared understanding and trust within the organization.

Our findings also reveal that developers who exhibit higher levels of trust in members of the inner source community exhibit a higher level of job satisfaction. This strengthens the evidence for the important role of trust in IS development (Sarker et al., 2011). Job satisfaction is an important predictor for retention, reduced burn-out and absenteeism, and organizational performance (Sharma and Stol, 2020; Wegge et al., 2007; Wright et al., 2007), which should be of great interest to managers. Our study suggests that inner source can contribute towards a higher level of job satisfaction.

The data also bear out that there is no direct link between inner source participation and job satisfaction. Rather, this study presents empirical support for social capital (as we operationalized it) as the mediating mechanism. However, given the limited magnitude of the relationship, other mediating mechanisms may exist; this is an opportunity for further research.

## Implications for research

Our results hold theoretical implications as well as future research avenues for inner source as a strategy and social capital theory (Lin, 2002; Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). An inner source strategy enables (but does not require) developers to contribute and share code with others on shared projects. While in this study we have adopted an output-oriented focus on participation, other types of participation – such as cross-team communication (via email or through dedicated platforms) – could also define a building block of inner source participation. We suggest future research to consider alternative facets of inner source participation to complement this study’s finding.

Concerning implications for social capital theory, we find complementary evidence that social interaction ties and shared un derstanding inform trust (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). Following previous work (Tsai and Ghoshal, 1998), we also confirm a stronger link between the cognitive dimension of social capital (as represented by shared understanding) on the relational dimension of socia capital (as represented by trust), compared to the structural dimension of social capital (as represented by social interaction ties). In addition to this replicated support of social capital theory in an inner source context, we also add new insights. Previous studies have often treated social capital as an exogenous construct, or focused on value-related outcomes such as knowledge sharing, task completion, or value creation (Chou and He, 2011; Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998; Tsai and Ghoshal, 1998; Wasko and Faraj, 2005; Zimmermann and Ravishankar, 2014). Only few studies have linked social capital to job satisfaction (Longo and Mura, 2011), or have considered its emergence (Maak, 2007). To this end, our study makes two complementary theoretical contributions. First, by showing that inner source helps to build relationships among developers, we add to the scarce literature on antecedents of social capital in an IS context. Second, social capital theory originated by highlighting the value of social relations in everyday social life (Jacobs, 1965; Lin, 2002), and has since been applied to understand internal functioning of organizations (Tsai and Ghoshal, 1998). This study provides evidence that an increase in social capital is linked to job satisfaction. This should be of great interest to organizations and managers as this has been linked to several benefits – including employees’ intention to stay with an organization and organizational performance (Kessler et al., 2020).

## Limitations and future research directions

A number of limitations of our study must be acknowledged. First, concerning the quantitative study, no prior instrument for inner source participation existed, and so we developed our own instrument; both Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability measures scored very high, suggesting internal consistency (see Table 3). While inner source initiatives across organizations vary in context and set-up (Cooper and Stol, 2018: Gurbani et al., 2010), we specifically focused on code contributions and sharing in our operationalization of inner source participation, which is among the main tasks of developers. However, other indicators could be identified for inner source participation, such as communication on mailing lists and other fora: we suggest this would be a fruitful avenue for future empirical studies of inner source and related scale development efforts.

Second, while we drew on social capital theory as a foundation for our central argument, not all concepts that have previously been identified as part of social capital theory (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998; Tsai and Ghoshal, 1998) were captured; each of the three dimensions of social capital (structural, cognitive, and relational) was measured by only one variable of interest. Future work could consider including other variables often associated with social capital, such as norms and identity, as well as other measurement operationalizations. This quantitative element of the study also relied on sample data. As no experimental set-up was used (involving an intervention, a treatment group, and control group), we cannot draw definitive conclusions regarding the causal relationships of our hypotheses. Thus, we cannot fully exclude the possibility that job satisfaction is the reason that developers participate in inner source initiatives. However, the quantitative study uses data from two separate data sources: the company-internal version control system (which captured developer activity) and a developer survey. Developer activity had thus been recorded over a period of time before the developer survey was conducted, and job satisfaction was measured after this period of activity. Furthermore, a PSM procedure (see Appendix C) indicates further robustness of the findings. The qualitative findings did not reveal any suggestion that participation was an outcome of a high level of job satisfaction, in support of full mediation of social capital. Notwithstanding, we did observe limited $R ^ { 2 }$ values, indicating that other variables are at play in explaining the three dimensions of social capital as well as job satisfaction, which could be identified and studied in future work. Future research could investigate the possibility of a reciprocal relationship between inner source participation and job satisfaction over time as it has been done for other organizational variables such as pride (cf. Kraemer et al.. 2020). Future work also could consider other mediating variables

Third, we focused on inner source adoption in large organizations as most organizations that have adopted inner source are large (Stol et al., 2022). Future research could focus on experimental or longitudinal studies in small and medium-sized enterprises to complement and extend our findings.

Furthermore, the interview results clearly spoke to the main themes found in social capital: social interaction ties, shared un derstanding, and trust. The qualitative findings provided confirmatory insights that confirmed that inner source participation is a mechanism to establish new interaction ties that otherwise would not have happened, and develop a shared understanding of the software product and goals, both of which in turn helped to build trust among members of the inner source community. Any mention of job satisfaction would follow a discussion of these themes; that is, none of the participants suggested that people participating in inner source would generally be happier in their job without invoking these themes. However, qualitative data is necessarily subject to interpretation by the researcher; for example, we could not exclude the possibility that people who participate in inner source ini tiatives in general have a different life attitude, and that they exhibit higher levels of job satisfaction for other reasons. Furthermore. the purpose of the interviews was to find corroborating evidence for the role of social capital, rather than identifying challenges; previous work has already investigated challenges in inner source (cf. Stol et al., 2011; Riehle et al., 2016). While our interview guide provided prompts on benefits and how inner source may help to build relationships, respondents were overwhelmingly positive in their judgment, and we detected no negative attitudes.

Finally, our mixed method approach combines the benefits of generalization through multiple observations with the benefits of indepth analysis of rich interview data. However, our quantitative part relies on a single company, representing one specific case. Therefore, future quantitative research could go beyond such an approach and gather data from multiple organizations.

## Conclusion

Inner source – the use of open source development and governance principles within an organization – is an organization-wide strategy that helps to overcome silo-based mentality and processes in large organizations. This article presents a multi-method study of the mediating role of social capital in the relationship between developer participation in such inner source projects and job satisfaction. We find – through combining survey and internal platform data – that participation in inner source is not directly related to job satisfaction; instead, we find support for our central proposition that social capital (comprising structural, cognitive, and relational dimensions) increases with inner source participation, and subsequently increases job satisfaction. A set of qualitative in terviews at six other organizations further lend support to our findings. In sum. we show that inner source initiatives have benefits on individual developer levels such that it enlarges social capital and job satisfaction – both much needed to increase the workforce's productivity and to lower turnover of highly skilled developers. Given an increasing level of attention on inner source as a strategy to IS development, this study is a timely contribution to this nascent area within information systems research

## APPENDIX B. ASSESSING COMMON METHOD BIAS

Among the most widely used approaches to assess common method bias (CMB) is Harman’s single-factor test (Malholtra et al., 2006), which we conducted in the following way. We adopted a CFA approach, in which all items are loaded onto a single factor. In this test. we only included those items that were part of the self-assessment survey (excluding the items from the version control system) as they share a common method. The fit of this one-factor model was very poor (χ<sup>2</sup>/df ratio of 10.9 (837.15/77), RMSEA = 0.25, CFI = 0.56, SRMR = 0.15, TLI = 0.48, NFI = 0.54); a poor fit suggested that CMB was not present.

While commonly used, Harman’s single factor test has been criticized (Podsakoff et al., 2003), and so we interpret these results with caution. Furthermore, we also employed the unmeasured latent method construct (ULMC) method (Podsakoff et al., 2003; Williams and McGonagle. 2016). Using this approach, an additional construct is added to the CFA model. which itself has no indicators (hence it is ‘unmeasured’). Factor loadings are specified from this construct to all items in the model. This additional ‘method construct’ controls for the common method bias that might exist: if CMB is present. then this typically reduces the factor loadings of the substantive factors of interest. The variance of the CMB is then represented by the factor loadings of the method construct (Podsakoff et al., 2003, p. 891). Comparing the initial CFA model to one with the additional method construct then allows comparison of the factor loadings—if these loadings deviate considerably from the original loadings, then this hints at the presence of CMB. As the exogenous construct in our study (participation) was measured through a different data set, we excluded this construct in the ULMC procedure. Deviations below 0.20 are generally considered unproblematic (Archimi et al., 2018; Gaskin, 2017); in our analysis, the largest deviation between factor loadings was 0.10. Overall, these results suggest that concerns of CMB do not loom large for this study.

As parameters, we used the nearest neighbor algorithm and a ratio of treatment to control of ‘1’, i.e., treatment and control group should have the same size (Ho et al., 2011), and used our control variables together with social interaction ties, shared understanding, and trust as independent variables in the logistic regression part. The resulting sample size was 76: 38 ‘pairs’ of matched respondents, with each pair consisting of one respondent assigned to the treatment group, and one assigned to the control group.

As the PSM procedure almost always results in a smaller sample size, the use of SEM for the robustness analysis was not feasible given the 76 observations. Also, given the small sample size and thus lower statistical power, we consider 90 % confidence intervals as statistically significant. For these reasons, we used the PROCESS macro (Haves. 2018) as we did to test the mediation hypothesis (H4). The results of the regression analysis with PROCESS (Model 80) are in line with the results of the original sample with 155 developers, confirming the robustness of the results. In particular, as with the original sample, participation in inner source is positively related to social interaction ties $( B = 0 . 5 2 , p < . 0 1 )$ and shared understanding (B = 0.23, p <.1), while social interaction ties $( B = 0 . 1 6 , p < . 0 5 )$ and shared understanding $( B = 0 . 7 2 , p < . 0 0 1 )$ ) are positively related to trust. Finally, trust is positively related to job satisfaction (B = 0.26, p <.05), while neither social interaction ties, shared understanding, participation in inner source, nor the controls are signifi cantly related to job satisfaction.

As a second test to address endogeneity concerns, we applied the instrument variable technique (Sande and Ghosh, 2018). The idea of the instrument variable approach is to find a variable Z outside of the model that is correlated with the independent variable (i.e., relevance criterion) while at the same time uncorrelated to the error term (i.e., exclusion criterion). While the relevance criterion can be tested with correlations, the exclusion criterion can only be argued based on theory. In addition, as the instrument variable technique was developed for regressions with a single exogenous variable. applving the instrument variable technique for mediation typically requires two instrument variables for treatment and mediator variable(s) (Frolich ¨ and Huber, 2017). Our dataset did not contain any suitable instrument variable for mediating variables. For the exogenous variable “inner source participation,” we used the binary variable “has chatroom handle,” which indicated whether a developer has an account in the chatroom (that is part of SysCom’s inner source initiative) or not, as an instrument variable because it is correlated with our measure of inner source participation (r = 0.308), and unlikely to be correlated with the error term (i.e., unlikely to have an influence on job satisfaction). We then run a twostage least square (2SLS) approach with regression analyses using IBM SPSS. The results of inner source participation on all down stream variables (i.e., social ties, shared understanding, trust, job satisfaction) remain stable when the fitted values are included. Hence, it is unlikely that the results are biased by endogeneity.
