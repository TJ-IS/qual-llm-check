# The effect of stimulus modes and associative distance in individual creativity support systems

Ross A. Malaga

Department of Information Systems, UniÕersity of Maryland, Baltimore County, Baltimore, MD 21250, USA

Accepted 6 March 2000

## Abstract

Individual creativity support systems ICSS have relied primarily on word stimuli for improving idea generation andŽ . creativity. However, there is evidence to suggest that combining word stimuli with picture stimuli may be more effective. A theoretical model for this combination, based on the dual coding theory and the theory of associative creativity, is presented. A laboratory experiment was conducted in order to validate the model. Picture and word stimuli were used to elicit ideas in response to a specific task. These stimuli were either closely or remotely associated with the problem task. The number of ideas generated and creativity of those ideas were analyzed. While the theoretical model has not enough support, the results indicate that the use of picture stimuli outperforms words for generating creative ideas. The implications of the results are discussed with regard to the design of future ICSS and future research directions. q 2000 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

## 2. Creativity and ICSS

Creativity may be defined as ‘‘the ability to produce work that is both novel and appropriate’’ 62 , <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> p. 3. Many models of creativity have been proposed <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 2,10,11,29,33,66 . While they differ to some extent, they agree that the following aspects of creativity are important: 1 the creative process, 2 the role ofŽ . Ž . individual creative ability, and 3 the role of theŽ . creative environment.

First, creativity is a process with multiple phases. In general, the creative process includes: 1 problemŽ . structuring, 2 preparation, 3 idea generation, and Ž . Ž . Ž . 4 evaluation. During the first phase, the problem becomes known and is clearly defined. In the second phase, the individual or individuals in the case of aŽ group recalls or gathers the information relevant to. solving the problem. The third phase, idea generation, is where ideas are produced. In the evaluation phase, the potential solutions that were developed in phase three are tested against some criteria to determine to what extent they solve the problem.

Second, all individuals have a natural creative ability. This ability is influenced by many factors including: biology 41 , personality 1,11 , motiva- <sup>w x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tion 1 , and training 1,12,64 . Some of these <sup>w x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> factors, such as biology, cannot be influenced. However, other factors, especially training, can be influenced to lead to an increase in individual creative ability 12,64 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Third, the environment in which the creative process occurs may influence creative performance. For example, many studies have shown that a group idea generation environment i.e., brainstorming may leadŽ . to more and better ideas 7,44 . Recent studies have<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> shown that brainstorming groups may encounter problems that inhibit idea generation 15,16 . Re-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> searchers have manipulated the group idea generation environment by adding group decision support systems GDSS . This technology has led to excel- Ž . lent results in a number of studies 13,14,22,23,<sup>w</sup> 30,55 .<sup>x</sup>

In addition to creativity models, many researchers <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 1,15,16,38,42,55,62 agree on the definition of a creative product. Amabile 1 , p. 35, for instance,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> states, ‘‘a product or response will be judged as creative to the extent that a it is both a novel andŽ . appropriate, useful, correct or valuable response to the task at hand . . . ’’ Gilhooly 26 , p. 123 defines <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> creative products as ‘‘both novel and acknowledged to be valuable or useful in some way’’. In addition, there is a well-known adage attributed to Osborn <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 50 , which states that quantity of ideas leads to quality ideas.

Almost all creativity researchers agree that the three fundamental measures of creative products are: Ž . Ž . Ž . 1 idea fluency, 2 novelty, and 3 feasibility. Idea fluency refers to the ability to generate a large number of ideas in response to a given creative task. Novelty was defined as the originality and uniqueness of an idea. Feasibility refers to whether the idea can realistically be implemented. While idea fluency is a fairly objective measure, novelty and feasibility are subjective. Thus, domain experts are typically used to judge those measures.

## 3. Theoretical background

Most of the commercial and academic ICSS make use of only word stimuli in order to help the user generate new ideas. However, words may not be the best stimulus mode to use. There is much evidence to support the notion that mental imagery plays an important role in idea generation 47,48,59,60 . In<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> addition, it is known that individuals who have a natural ability to generate and manipulate mental images may prefer to use pictorial stimuli in problem-solving tasks 31 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

## 3.1. The role of imagery in creatiÕe thinking

Much of the early literature on the role of mental imagery in creative thought was based on anecdotal evidence 25,60 . One famous story relates the devel-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> opment of the special theory of relativity to Einstein imagining himself traveling beside a light wave. In another story, Kekule discovers the ring structure of the benzene molecule after dreaming about a snake swallowing its own tail.

More recently, experimental studies have shown that mental imagery and the use of pictures does play a role in creative thinking. Most of the problems examined to date have been those that require a high degree of mental manipulation of images and those that are highly spatial in nature 59 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Shepard 60 suggested a number of reasons why<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> imagery influences creative problem solving:

1. The use of imagery provides an alternative to the traditional, language-based way of thinking.

2. The rich content provided by imagery and its link to external sources may suggest ideas and trends of thought that are not fully preserved by language 59 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

3. Images are more likely to produce positive affect and motivation than language.

There is some empirical evidence to support Shepard’s ideas. For example, mental imagery may play a role in alleviating functional fixedness. Duncker 17 suggested that in attempting to solve <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> certain problems, subjects became fixated on a certain type of solution. This concept is embodied in the old adage, when you have a hammer all problems look like a nail. The fixated subjects did not consider other types of solutions, even when the other solution was better.

Kaufmann 32 has speculated that mental images <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> are processed by the brain in a parallel fashion; allowing for the simultaneous consideration of the entire problem thus reducing functional fixedness. Alessandro Antonietti in Ref. 36 performed a Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. series of experiments designed to determine the effect of pictorial representations and mental imagery on overcoming functional fixedness. He had subjects solve mathematical word problems. He found that use of mental visualization had a significant effect in overcoming functional fixedness. In another experiment, he found that the use of pictures was useful in solving problems that have many steps.

A possible theoretical explanation for these results and the body of anecdotal evidence can be found in Paivio’s dual coding theory and Mednick’s associative theory of creativity.

## 3.2. Dual coding theory

Dual coding theory as put forth by Paivio 51,54 :<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

. . . is based on the assumption that memory and cognition are served by two separate symbolic systems, one specialized for dealing with verbal information and the other for nonverbal information. The two systems are presumed to be interconnected but capable of functioning independently. Interconnectedness means that representations in one system can activate those in the other, so that for example, pictures can be named and images can occur to words. Independence implies, among other things, that nonverbal imaginal and verbal mem-Ž .

ory codes, aroused directly by pictures and words or indirectly by imagery and verbal encoding tasks, should have additive effects on recall.

Paivio further theorized that each system verbalŽ and nonverbal has its own associative network.. Since the systems are interconnected, verbal and nonverbal associations may occur. Paivio referred to this type of association as referential reference 53 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> The interaction between direct association verbal toŽ verbal, nonverbal to nonverbal and referential refer-. ence has made empirical study of these effects very difficult. However, some studies 68 have revealed<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> evidence to support these effects.

Fig. 1 adapted from Ref. 54 provides an exam-Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. ple of dual coding theory. In the figure, the image of the dog causes an associative response in the image system. The word ‘‘dog’’ causes a similar response in the verbal system. Each word in the verbal system may have a referential relation in the image system. Not all words and images will have a referential relation. It should be noted that the word ‘‘dog’’ and an image of a dog might produce different associative responses. For example, an image of a German shepherd may result in an association with a guard dog. This association may then lead to the image of a guard in uniform.

Based on experimental data, Paivio 52 has fur-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ther concluded that the use of pictures and words together have an additive effect on the ability to recall an associate from memory.

Numerous studies, dating back over 100 years to Galton 24 , have shown that there are differences in<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> individual verbal and nonverbal abilities 34,68 . In<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> general, individuals who are better ‘‘ visualizers’’ Ž . often referred to as high imagers will prefer to use imagery strategies in problem solving. Low imagers will generally prefer to use a verbal strategy 31 .

While dual coding theory has its opponents Ref. Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 58 , for example , many empirical studies have pro-. vided positive evidence for the theory 52 . Many of<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> the early studies dealt with a subject’s ability to manipulate mental images and recall associates based on lexical or pictorial stimuli 34,52,59 . More recent<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> studies 35 have used positron emission topography <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Ž . PET scans to show that the cerebral hemispheres differ in their ability to process lexical and pictorial material.

## 3.3. AssociatiÕe theory of creatiÕity

The associative connections suggested by the dual coding theory may allow for the generation of new ideas. Margaret Boden 5 states, ‘‘many cases of<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> creativity depend, at least in part, on the mind’s associative power’’. The idea of associative thinking provides the basis for many models of creativity <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 25,33 and for many idea generation techniques <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> 12,27,28,50 . For example, Bouchard 6 , p. 12 states that brainstorming is ‘‘facilitated by other group members who provide unique stimuli which constantly prod the associative mechanism, thereby generating more and better suggestions’’.

Associative theory contends that mental states are joined to each other in such a way that one state ‘‘tends to evoke the other’’ 63 . For example, think-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ing about a fork may tend to evoke the mental state for knife, spoon, or food. Mednick 46 contends that<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> associative thinking may lead to new ideas.

The associative response might not end after one association. For example, a person might make an associative connection between fork and ill-fated cruise ships. The connection occurs through a series of associations forkŽ trident Poseidon the Poseidon Adventure . Mednick speculated that the. greater the ‘‘distance’’ between associative elements, the greater the possibility of a creative response.

An important aspect of Mednick’s theory of associative creativity is the concept of associative hierarchy. An associative hierarchy is the strength and organization of an individual’s associations. Consider the example in Fig. 2, which shows two associative hierarchies around the word table. Mednick predicts that those individuals with relatively flat associative hierarchies are more creative than those with steep hierarchies. The person with a steep hierarchy has a very strong association between ‘‘table’’ and the typical responses of ‘‘chair’’ and ‘‘cloth’’. It is difficult for this person to recall the more remote associates i.e., leg and food . According to Med-Ž . nick, these remote associates are more likely to lead to a non-dominating or creative response. The person with a flatter associative hierarchy may still have a strong associative connection between ‘‘table’’ and ‘‘chair’’, but this stereotypical connection is not overly dominant. This person will more easily recall the remote associates. Associative hierarchies for highly creative and less creative people may contain the same elements in the same order. However, when asked for a creative associative response the person with the steep hierarchy will stop responding before the person with the flat hierarchy. For example, the person with the steep hierarchy in Fig. 2 might respond ‘‘chair’’ and ‘‘cloth’’ to the stimulus word ‘‘table’’. The person with the flat hierarchy might continue responding through ‘‘food’’ and off the right side of the figure.

In order to test creative ability Mednick developed the Remote Associates Test RAT . The RATŽ . presents a subject with a group of three words. The subject is required to find a fourth word that serves as an associative link between the words. For example, a subject might be presented with the words: rat, blue, and cottage. The associative link between these words is ‘‘cheese’’ 46 . Mednick et al. 45 and<sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> others 8 have conducted empirical investigations <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> that show a strong correlation between scores on the RAT and creative ability. In a study of brainstorming sessions, Forbach and Evans 21 found that the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> number of ideas generated was positively correlated with scores on the RAT. Recent books and articles have added evidence to support the associative theory 40,61 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Mednick also noted that ‘‘visualizers’’ and ‘‘verbalizers’’ would tend to produce different types of associations. He theorized, along the lines of Paivio, that individuals would have different associative hierarchies for visual and word stimuli. ‘‘Visualizers’’, those people who are adept at generating and manipulating mental images and, thus, have a relatively flat visual associative hierarchy, will have difficulty in producing creative solutions if the problem has a highly verbal component. ‘‘Verbalizers’’, those people who are not adept at generating and manipulating mental images and thus, have a relatively flat verbal associative hierarchy, will have a similar difficulty handling problems that have a highly visual component.

## 4. Hypotheses development

ICSS have used primarily word stimuli to promote idea generation. However, the conclusions from the psychological literature suggest that use of multiple stimulus modes, specifically words and pictures, may produce better results. Based on the Dual Coding Theory, the use of both pictures and words will lead to the recall of associates along two separate, yet interconnected, chains. According to the associative theory of creativity the increase in the number of associative chains should lead to an increase in the number of ideas generated.

Hypothesis 1. The use of both word and picture stimuli in an ICSS will result in a greater number of ideas being produced for a specific problem task than the use of an ICSS that uses only one stimulus mode or no stimuli.

ICSS that use only word stimuli may not provide the best idea generation mechanisms for those people who are ‘‘visualizers’’ high imagers . The associa-Ž . tive theory of creativity and Dual Coding Theory indicate that these people have relatively flat visual associative hierarchies and relatively steep verbal associative hierarchies. Therefore, ‘‘ visualizers’’ are more likely to produce a non-dominating response when exposed to visual stimulation. Since it cannot be known without testing whether a person is a ‘‘visualizer’’ or ‘‘verbalizer’’, providing both word and picture stimuli will allow individuals to generate ideas using their flattest associative hierarchy. This should lead to the generation of ideas with a high level of creativity.

Hypothesis 2. The use of both word and picture stimuli in an ICSS will result in a higher leÕel of creatiÕe ideas being produced for a specific problem task than the use of an ICSS that uses only one stimulus mode or no stimuli.

The role of the associative distance of the stimuli must also be considered due to the implications for the design of ICSS. Mednick theorizes that remote associates will generate more creative ideas. However, since remote associates are harder to recall than close associates, remote stimuli may lead to the generation of fewer ideas than close associates. The following hypotheses relate to associative distance:

Hypothesis 3. The use of close associates as stimuli in an ICSS will result in a greater number of ideas being produced for a specific problem task than the use of an ICSS that uses remote stimuli or no stimuli.

Hypothesis 4. The use of remote stimuli in an ICSS will result in a higher leÕel of creatiÕe ideas being produced for a specific problem task than the use of an ICSS that uses close stimuli or no stimuli.

## 4.3. Experimental task

The problem task used in this experiment was: ‘‘Produce a list of as many new delicious ice cream flavors as possible’’. The subjects were specifically told to describe ice cream flavors that did not already exist and that they would personally like to eat. While this problem may seem and probably is very Ž . simple it was chosen for a number of reasons. First, most people have had some experience with ice cream and require no specialized knowledge in order to develop new ideas concerning ice cream flavors. Second, the task is open-ended. Third, the creativity of the ideas could be simply judged by a group of ice cream lovers. These people required no specialized knowledge or training. Finally, while the basic problem is simple, it does emulate some real world problems. Many organizations are involved in developing new products or variations of currently existing products.

## 4.4. Software used in experiment

Microsoft Access ${ \bf 7 7 } ^ { \mathrm { T M } }$ was used to develop the software used in the experiment. Access was chosen due to its ease in creating a graphical user interface and its database support. The database was used to store stimulus words and images. For those treatments that used stimuli, the user interaction screen was divided into three sections see Fig. 3 . A state- Ž . ment of the problem task appeared at the top of the screen. The right side of the screen contained an area for subjects to type their ideas. The left side of the screen displayed the stimulus.

The stimulus changed based on an internal timer and user action. Each stimulus was displayed for a minimum of 10 seconds. After the timer had expired, the next stimulus was displayed when the user pressed the TAB key. Therefore, if the timer had expired and the subject pressed the TAB key to move the cursor to a new idea text box, the next stimulus in the database was displayed. In addition, the user could advance the stimulus display by pressing the TAB key before typing anything in the current idea text box, as long as the timer had expired. This method for advancing the stimulus was suggested by a pilot study group.

## 4.4.1. Stimuli used in the software

Before choosing the specific stimuli, both words and pictures, to use in the above-mentioned software an extensive review of the literature was undertaken. However, the previous body of knowledge provided no guidance in this matter.

The central problem with using associative stimuli is that every subject will produce different associations. Therefore the following criteria were used to select stimuli for the software.

<sup>Ø</sup> The use of abstract stimuli was avoided. Abstract terms e.g., love, truth, happiness, etc. do notŽ . lend themselves to an easily understood pictorial representation. In addition, abstract pictures are difficult to describe in words. Therefore, in order to match pictorial stimuli with word stimuli, abstract stimuli were avoided.

<sup>Ø</sup> The use of stimuli with a weak pictorial element was avoided. In order to directly compare words and pictures with the same semantic meaning, stimuli words that did not have a strong pictorial element were excluded.

<sup>Ø</sup> The use of stimuli directly associated with the problem domain was avoided. Mednick 46 points<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> out that this type of association is unlikely to produce original ideas.

<sup>Ø</sup> Stimuli that are closely associated or remotely associated with the problem domain were used. According to Mednick 46 , remote associations are <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> most likely to produce original ideas. However, it was anticipated that subjects would have difficulty generating ideas using remote associates. Therefore, closely associated elements were also used.

The stimulus elements were chosen using the following process.

<sup>Ø</sup> A group of subjects from the overall subject pool were asked to produce a list of direct associations in response to the problem domain. This was accomplished by asking the subjects to write down the first thing they think of when they hear the problem domain in this case when they hear the Ž term ice cream . This task generated a list of 20 . non-duplicate responses.

<sup>Ø</sup> The list of direct associates was then be presented to another group of subjects randomly chosen from the overall subject pool. This group was asked to produce a list of new direct associations in response to the original list. The result of this process was a list of 323 non-duplicate close word associations to the original problem domain. The word stimuli used in the experiment were either an individual word or an adjective–noun phrase.

<sup>Ø</sup> Two raters were then asked to determine which of the close word stimuli had a strong pictorial element. Each rater performed this task individually. The results were then compared and the raters asked to resolve any differences. The final list included 195 closely associated stimuli with a strong pictorial element.

<sup>Ø</sup> Each of the 195 close associate words was queried against a database of 50,000 pictures. Where a picture existed it was added to the Access database.

<sup>Ø</sup>Choosing random words from the dictionary generated the remote associates. As each word was chosen it was queried against the picture database. If a picture was found, then the word and picture were added to the Access database. If no picture was found, then the word was discarded.

A normal distribution of subjects over each of the four main treatments was assumed with respect to inherent creative ability, high and low visualization ability, and typing speed and accuracy.

## 4.6. Experimental procedures

The experiment was conducted over four consecutive Saturday mornings. Experimental sessions for treatments using the same stimuli mode were, as the instructions for these treatments were the same. For example, all of the treatments that used pictures only were tested at the same time.

Upon arrival at the experiment site, a computer laboratory, the experiment proceeded as follows:

<sup>Ø</sup> Subjects were assigned to a computer running the experimental software.

<sup>Ø</sup> The initial screen asked subjects to wait for verbal instructions. The experimenter provided verbal instructions on the use of the software and showed a demonstration on a display in the front of the room.

<sup>Ø</sup> The subjects were then given a short practice task to work on in order to ensure they knew how to use the software.

<sup>Ø</sup> When all subjects had indicated they were comfortable with the software, the experimenter presented the experimental task.

<sup>Ø</sup> Subjects were given 7.5 min to generate ideas.

Subjects were aware that there was a time limit, but did not know the amount of time allocated for the task.

<sup>Ø</sup> At the end of 7.5 min, subjects were given the opportunity to complete typing any idea they were working on.

<sup>Ø</sup> Subjects were then thanked for their participation and dismissed.

<sup>Ø</sup> The results from each workstation were copied to floppy disk and collected.

No major problems were encountered while conducting the experiment.

## 6. Discussion

The sections below discuss the impact of the study. Section 6.1 describes the conclusions. Section 6.2 points out the limitations of this study. Section 6.3 suggests how the study should impact future ICSS design. Section 6.4 suggests future research directions.

## 6.1. Conclusions

Based on the results of the experiment, there is evidence to support the notion that stimulus mode plays a role in the efficacy of ICSS. Specifically, the use of picture stimuli, either by itself or in conjunction with word stimuli, leads to ideas with a higher level of creativity than the use of just word stimuli. The cause of this does not appear to stem, as anticipated, from the dual coding theory. If dual coding was an underlying mechanism the use of both picture and word stimuli would have outperformed either type of stimulus alone. However, significant effects were observed only for treatments that used picture stimuli.

In order to understand this finding, a deeper discussion of the role of imagery in creative thinking is necessary. The anecdotal evidence predicted the significant performance of pictures, either alone or combined with words — specifically, that the use of imagery plays an important role in the creative process.

One possible explanation is that stimulus words might promote functional fixedness, while pictures help alleviate it and no stimuli is neutral. This contention is supported by a recent experiment on word blocking conducted by Smith et al. 61 . In this <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> experiment, the subjects were asked to complete word fragments, L D ER for example. Prior to<sub>– –</sub> completing the fragment subjects were shown a positive stimulus the actual solution , a negative stimu-Ž . lus a word similar to the actual solution , or anŽ . unrelated stimulus. For example, the positive stimulus for L D ER was LADDER, the negative stimulus was LEADER and the unrelated stimulus was MEMBRANE. Smith found that the use of negative stimulus words acted to support fixation.

A closer look at the ideas generated in the experiment seems to indicate that subjects were fixated on stimulus words. This was determined by matching ideas with the stimulus that appeared when the idea was typed. For example, many of the ideas generated when the stimulus word ‘‘pie’’ was displayed made mention of pie i.e., cherry marshmallow pie, appleŽ pie ice cream, and apple pie with whipped cream .. None of the ideas generated when a picture of a pie was displayed made mention of pie.

There is also evidence to support the concept that associative distance of stimuli may impact the creativity level of ideas generated by using an ICSS. Specifically, the use of either close or remote associates outperformed the control. This finding does support the theory of associative creativity in general, as the use of stimuli led to more creative associations than the control. However, it does not lend support to Mednick’s theory of associative hierarchies.

It does not appear that stimulus mode or associative distance had a significant impact on the number of ideas generated. However, this result may be due to the artificially imposed time limit. That limit may not have been long enough for the subjects to completely exhaust their associative hierarchies. Previous creativity experiments have found that the imposition of a time limit can affect the experiment results <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 1,62 .

## 6.2. Limitations

The study contains a number of limitations. First, the artificially imposed time limit may have led to a lack of positive results for the number of ideas generated. Second, no effort was made to control for inherent creative ability, imagery ability, or typing speed. This was not done due to the limited amount of time for which subjects were willing to volunteer. It was felt that a larger sample size without controlling for these variables was preferable to a smaller sample size that did control for these variables. It was assumed that with a large sample size these variables would be normally distributed among the various treatments. However, there is no way to confirm the assumption. Third, the experiment used a relatively simple creative task and no time was provided for preparation or incubation. Thus, the conclusions cannot be generalized to more complex tasks.

## 6.3. Implications for design of ICSS

The results of this study reveal important considerations for the design and development of future ICSS. The large amount of anecdotal evidence combined with the results of this study suggest that future ICSS should use picture stimuli. While the reason why picture stimuli outperform word stimuli is not clearly understood, it appears that picture stimuli may help alleviate functional fixedness. In addition, since there were no significant differences in the performance of close vs. remote associates, either type could be used successfully when com-Ž bined with picture stimuli in future ICSS..

The use of picture stimuli has an impact on the underlying ICSS technology. Many stimuli based ICSS are based on relational database technology. However, this technology may not be capable of handling an associative ICSS that uses picture stimuli. The one-to-many structure of the relational model limits the complexity of associative links between pictures. For example, consider a picture of a rose. This picture may contain many attributes that could lead to an associative connection. For instance, the fact that a rose is a flower may lead to associative connections with a picture of a daisy, an iris, and a lily. However, the picture also has a color attribute Ž . red , which may lead to associative connections with a fire engine, an apple, and the planet Mars. Thus there is a need for a database system that can handle these complex relationships. Two methods that appear promising are the use of object-oriented database systems OODBMS and hypermediaŽ . databases based on World Wide Web WWW tech-Ž . nology.

OODBMS were designed to handle complex images. By treating each image as an object complex, many to many, relationships can be formed. This would allow the user to pursue various associations based on the attributes of the picture object. For example, a user looking at the picture of a rose could choose to proceed by connecting with the flower associates or the red associates.

An alternative to OODBMS is the technology underlying the World Wide Web WWW . ThisŽ . technology may be particularly useful for handling picture-based associative ICSS. Through the use of hyperlinks the WWW is capable of dealing with complex many to many relationships. In addition, through the use of hotspots on the pictures, users would be able to follow associative links based on various image attributes. For example, when placing the cursor over a hotspot on the image of the rose the user would be asked if they wanted to hyperlink to the flower associates. Placing the cursor over a different hotspot would enable to the user to hyperlink to the red associates. It should be noted that the WWW in general is not recommended as an ICSS, as a recent study shows that other types of stimuli provide better results 43 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

## 6.4. Future research directions

While this study does show that picture stimuli are better than word stimuli when used in an ICSS, the exact reason for this is not well understood. A series of laboratory experiments should be conducted in order to validate the results described herein and to further examine the role of picture stimuli in an ICSS.

First, inherent individual abilities are known to play a major role in the creative process. Therefore, this study should be repeated controlling for individual creative ability and imagery ability. This can be accomplished by using standard creativity and imagery tests. Individuals who are not highly creative, and thus, have steep associative hierarchies, may benefit more from remote stimuli than those individuals who have flat associative hierarchies. In addition, high imagers should respond better to picture stimuli than low imagers.

Second, the study should be repeated with no set time limit. Instead of a time limit the experiment should ask subjects to generate responses to a set number of stimuli. The stimulus would only change when the subject has exhausted all responses based on that stimulus. It is anticipated that the picture stimuli will lead to a greater number of ideas generated than word stimuli or a control, due to the greater number of associations produced by pictures.

Third, an associative ICSS based primarily on picture stimuli should be developed and tested. The development of a general use ICSS based on picture stimuli is not a simple task. IdeaFisher, which is based on word stimuli, took over 20 years and US\$4 million to develop. It contains almost 700 connections with the word ‘‘red’’ alone. In total, IdeaFisher contains over 65,000 connected words and concepts Ž . http:<sup>rr</sup>www.ideafisher.com . However, in order to test the concept of a picture based associative ICSS a domain specific system should be built and tested. Such a system could use a relatively small number of pictures and connections.
