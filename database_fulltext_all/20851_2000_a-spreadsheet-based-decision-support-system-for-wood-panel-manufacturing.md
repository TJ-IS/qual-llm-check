## Abstract

Wood paneling manufacturers face a number of complex decisions when trying to allocate production resources and combine various raw materials to meet production goals. While various linear programming formulations for this problem have been proposed, these models are often difficult to use and maintain for real-time decision making in a dynamic shop floor environment. This paper describes an MS Excel-based decision support system for wood panel manufacturing. The system is easy to use and maintain yet gives shop floor personnel access to powerful optimization capabilities useful for fine-tuning production processes in the face of changing supply and price situations. q 2000 Elsevier Science B.V. ## 1. Introduction

Wood-based products are preeminent in most societies. Driven by the increasing population and increasing per capita consumption 10 , demand for<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> such products grew considerably over the last 30 years. The United Nation’s Food and Agriculture Organization FAO estimates that total globalŽ . roundwood production increased from 2,463 million $\mathrm { m } ^ { 3 }$ in 1970 to 3,358 million $\mathrm { m } ^ { 3 }$ in 1994 2 —<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> roughly a 1.3% increase per year. While some predict that sufficient wood fibers will be available in the future globally 5,6 , others project significant<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> gaps in supply. Apsey and Reed 1 quantify this gap <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> as some 400 million $\mathrm { m } ^ { 3 }$ in 2010 and nearly 600 million $\mathrm { m } ^ { 3 }$ in 2020, and Nilsson 7 puts the numbers<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> at 800 million $\mathrm { m } ^ { 3 }$ and 900 million $\mathrm { m } ^ { 3 }$ for the same dates, respectively.

Wooden panels are comparatively thin, flat sheets used by the manufacturing and construction industries to produce a variety of products such as furniture parts or housing. These sheets, except for plywood, are most often made out of small, reconstituted wooden material. Oriented Strand Board Ž . Ž . OSB , Medium Density Fiberboard MDF , or Particle Board are examples of such materials. Over the last 30 years, wood-based panel consumption experienced even more pronounced growth than wood products in general. From 1970 to 1994, the worldwide consumption of wooden panels increased by approximately 2.9% annually, to a total volume of <sup>3</sup> 126.4 million m in 1994 2 . According to FAO, the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> rate of growth is expected to stay above 2.0% annually for the next decade, resulting in an estimated consumption of 172.6 million $\mathrm { m } ^ { 3 }$ in 2010. For this reason, wood panel producers face increased demand for their raw material and upward pressure on raw material prices.

Wooden particle boards are manufactured from small pieces of wood i.e. chips, flakes, splinters, Ž strands, shreds, shives , which are bound together by. the use of an organic binder. Such particle boards made up almost half of all wooden panels consumed Ž . 46.6% in 1994 3 . Particle boards can be produced<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> from many different types of wooden raw materials. Softwood or hardwood logs, chips, shavings, sawmill residuals, and sawdust can be used in various quanti ties for the production of particle boards. Due to this ability to substitute one source of wooden raw material for another, producers can be optimistic that they will be able to find the raw materials needed for their production in the future. However, since the wooden raw material for particle boards makes up approximately 35% of the total production cost 4 , raw<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> material prices have a major impact on particle board prices and the profitability of the board producers. Prices for different types of raw materials i.e. logs,Ž chips, shavings, sawmill residuals, sawdust fluctuate. over time due to changing supply and demand for a specific form of wooden raw material, opening the door for producers to optimize their raw material mix with respect to cost while maintaining adequate product quality.

This paper describes a decision support system Ž . DSS which allows particle board manufacturers to use linear programming to find the minimum cost raw material mix while respecting all quality related constraints. Gfeller 4 was one of the pioneers who<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> employed linear programming techniques to solve the particle board raw material cost minimization problem in the early 1980s. The model integrated in our DSS is based on Gfeller’s work but separates purchasing and production costs in order to better account for the differences in treatment of particular particles. ## 2. The decision problem

Particle board is a commodity product. Therefore, price is a major determinant of the competitive position of a producer. However, buyers also demand that specific quality requirements be met. The quality of particle board is dependent on many variables including raw material composition of the middle and the surface layers of the panel i.e. the woodŽ species used and the types of the wooden raw material processed, such as logs, chips, shavings, sawmill residuals, and sawdust , the quantity of raw material. per unit volume i.e. specific gravity , the densityŽ . profile within the thickness of a board, the moisture content of the raw and processed material, type and quantity of binder per unit volume, press time, and surface finishing. Whereas some of these variables cannot be easily influenced the binder character-Ž istics would be one of them , producers normally . have full control over others. For example, producers can choose among different species, they can decide among different types of raw material such as logs, chips, shavings, sawmill residuals or sawdust, and they have control over the specific gravity and the density profile of the panels.

In particle board manufacturing, raw material optimization is constrained not only by the availability of a given wooden raw material and specific panel quality requirements, but also by the processing capacities a particle board plant possesses. For example, to break down wood in the form of long logs requires different types of equipment than to prepare, say, sawdust. Furthermore, a long log will in most cases be broken down to particles that have a different shape compared to the ones made from sawdust. Indeed, to make a quality panel, particles of different shapes and from different species have to be mixed according to each producer’s recipe. Additionally, not only does each raw material type have different prices which change over time, but the costs incurred from the preparation and use of different types of material vary widely too. Thus, the complexity and dynamic nature of this problem make it an ideal candidate for a DSS development effort.

For the example-case presented in this paper, we assume two products are produced: a furniture grade particle board and a construction grade particle board. Each of these two panels consists of a middle layer and two surface layers, which are symmetrical. A panel may have several stratums of different materials, compositions, and specific gravity to enhance its mechanical properties. Each panel’s layers, however, will always be symmetric from the middle axis to keep the panel straight. To control the panel quality for each of these layers, different density profiles and raw material requirements can be specified. There are eight different types of raw materials available. Prices and quantities available are variable reflecting existing market conditions. Furthermore, the producer knows the maximum capacity of her<sup>r</sup>his bottleneck equipment. For this example, we assumed that nine equipment capacities were the determinants of the overall production capacity. As pointed out earlier, a critical quality characteristic of particle boards is the density and composition of the individual layers of a board. For example, to produce furniture quality particle boards, the surface layers should not contain particles that are too large to obtain a smooth surface. Construction quality boards, whose mechanical properties are of high importance, should preferably be produced from softwoods and long and thin particles in the surface layers. Thus, a producer is not able to completely substitute a given raw material type by others in its daily operations. Should one type of raw material experience significant price increases in the short term, a producer would try to replace this raw material by increasing the use of several other available raw materials without violating the equipment capacity and board quality constraints. Thus, for the linear optimization, the capacity of the bottleneck equipment must be known and taken into account. Appendix D lists the equipment capacity constraints assumed for this example.

## 4. The model

A producer wants to manufacture particle boards at minimum cost. Therefore, the objective function is to minimize the total cost incurred to produce a given amount of particle boards at a preset quality level. For this purpose, the per unit cost of a particular type of raw material combined with its per unit production cost is summed up to the total production cost for all materials, all panels, and all layers. Mathematically, this corresponds to:

$$
\min \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {8} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {2} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {2} \left(\mathrm{RC} _ {i} + \mathrm{PC} _ {i j k}\right)\tag{1}
$$

where ${ \mathrm { R C } } _ { i }$ is the raw material cost for the ith raw material and $\mathrm { P C } _ { i j k }$ is the production cost for the ith raw material in the jth panel and the k th layer.

This objective function is subject to four groups of constraints. Appendix E presents the model formulation as it was developed for the MS Excel based program. It shows the individual and general constraints and the changing cells which Solver uses to derive a solution.

Thirty-two general constraints are required to state that no raw material quantity can be a negative number constraints 70–101, Appendix E . AnotherŽ . nine constraints ensure that the capacity requirements for the production equipment is no larger than 100% constraints 61–69, Appendix E . To assureŽ . the panel quality, 11 constraints per panel layer are needed constraints 17–60, Appendix E . Finally, Ž . another 15 constraints secure compliance with the raw material quantities available constraints 1–16,Ž Appendix E . Thus, the objective function is subject . to a total of 101 constraints.

For the model to function properly, some preliminary data calculations and conversions must be made. For example, since raw material quantity is expressed in metric tons, but the allowable amount of a specific raw material for a given layer is specified as a percentage of the layer’s density, minor calculations had to be made to find the allowable quantity in metric tons. These, and other necessary calculations of straightforward nature, are not shown specifically.

## 6. Managerial analysis

Our DSS not only allows a manager to find the optimum solution in this case minimum cost , butŽ . also enables the analysis of the data and the simulation of situations when some input data is changed. This enables a manager either to make scenario analyses or to search for ways to further decrease the cost of producing the product. These capabilities can be used for cost minimization, the optimization of contribution margins, the planning of future investments, or even to find the best production program. To illustrate these capabilities, we consider an example focusing on the minimization of production costs.

A plant manager facing the task to produce a given amount of particle board panels will strive to minimize the cost to produce these panels. Although the LP-model of the Lignum Optimizer identifies the least cost solution, a plant manager might want to determine what additional savings could be achieved by loosening some of the constraints imposed by the model. Of course, the shadow prices on Solver’s sensitivity report provide some insights into the marginal values of binding resources. However, the ‘what-if’ capabilities of a DSS may be of greater use since it provides immediate feedback on the merits of any new assumptions imagined by the manager and does not require the manager to have specialized knowledge about the proper interpretation of shadow prices 8 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

For this example, we focus on the raw material supply Appendix A and equipment capacity Ap- Ž . Ž pendix D . A manager might first want to look at. these two parameters in isolation to gain a better comprehension of their individual relevance. In a later stage, the manager might look at some combined effects, such as when the supply of a particular material and the processing capacity for that material would be changed concurrently. On the other hand, a manager might also want to look at the worst-case scenario. For this case, she or he would simply decrease some of the raw material supply or the equipment capacity. By altering the individual input parameters of the model, the manager can observe the influence of any given scenario.

## 7. Conclusions

Particle board producers strive to produce their product at the lowest cost possible because particle board panels are commodity products where price is a major factor for customers when making purchasing decisions. Production costs of particle board panels are dependent on many variables, of which the most important are the raw material prices and availability, the panel quality specifications, and the equipment capacities available. Due to the large amount of variables involved, finding the optimum solution by simulation or other empirical techniques is almost impossible. Therefore, an MS Excel based DSS known as Lignum Optimizer was created which allows particle board plant managers to find the lowest cost solution for their production requirements. The program allows for real-time decision making on all MS-DOS compatible computers running MS Excel 5.0 or higher. The program features user friendly GUIs, self-directed step-by-step tutorials, and information tracking features. A password protection system allows managers to assign different levels of accessibility for different users, thus protecting critical areas from unwanted users.

The Lignum Optimizer DSS allows plant managers, shop floor personnel, or other individuals with a need to minimize production cost of particle board panels to evaluate different scenarios relating to their production and thus to make the best decisions possible under existing situations. Given the availability and power of today’s desktop and laptop computers, spreadsheet-based DSS developed using VBA in Excel offers a viable means for enabling better decision making in business — even for those unfamiliar with the underlying optimization and modeling tools.
