# Automated learning of patient image retrieval knowledge: neural networks versus inductive decision trees

Olivia R. Liu Sheng <sup>a</sup>, Chih-Ping Wei <sup>b,)</sup>, Paul Jen-Hwa Hu <sup>c</sup>, Namsik Chang

<sup>a</sup> Department of Management Information Systems, College of Business and Public Administration, UniÕersity of Arizona, Tucson, AZ, USA <sup>b</sup> Department of Information Management, College of Management, National Sun Yat-Sen UniÕersity, Kaohsiung, Taiwan <sup>c</sup> Accounting and Information Systems, DaÕid Eccles School of Business, UniÕersity of Utah, Salt Lake City, UT, USA d College of Economics and Business Administration, UniÕersity of Seoul, Seoul, South Korea

## Abstract

Retrieving a patient’s prior examination images that are relevant to the current ones is a critical component in radiologists’ primary examination reading services. The important role of such image retrieval support will be greatly accentuated in digital radiology practice. Radiologists’ knowledge of patient prior image retrievals is rooted in their interpretation and application of the pertinent underlying medical<sup>r</sup>radiological knowledge as well as in their clinical training and experiences. At the same time, this knowledge may vary with individual practice preferences and styles, and may dynamically evolve over time. The complexity and dynamics suggest that patient image retrievals are a promising area for artificial intelligence-based automated learning techniques. Automated learning of patient image retrieval knowledge can provide continuous knowledge repository update support in an image retrieval knowledge-based system. However, the implementation of the learning techniques needs to address several challenges that include missing and noisy data, as well as multiple decision outcomes. Two techniques based on salient automated learning paradigms, neural network and symbolic learning, are investigated. Specifically, we describe the design or extension of each learning technique to address the unique characteristics of patient image retrieval knowledge and compare the resulting learning performances. The results show that the knowledge derived from the automated learning methods can achieve effective image retrievals that are comparable to those based on a knowledge-engineer-driven approach. q 2000 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

Health care professionals, including general practice physicians and specialists, are becoming increasingly dependent on imaging modalities for their clinical decision making and patient management. The growing role of radiological examinations demands effective management of a library of patient images of an unprecedented volume, storage media choices, and retrieval requirements. Advances in both medical imaging and information technology have made remarkable contributions to transforming radiology from a film-based to a digital practice with which examination reading services becoming less constrained by temporal or geographic considerations. The progressive realization of the digital radiology practice increasingly requires proper patient image retrieval support to radiologists’ primary examination reading.

When interpreting newly generated radiological images, a radiologist usually applies his or her knowledge to determine, retrieve, and make reference to some relevant prior examination images of the same patient for diagnosis and comparison purposes. To alleviate the time and physical demand upon reading radiologists that can result from a search of the relevant patient images, many health care institutions have taken an image pre-fetching approach by applying some primitive rules of thumb to pre-fetch relevant patient prior images for radiologists’ reading. However, most of the real-world image pre-fetching practices are not sophisticated enough to meet various examination reading scenarios. As a consequence, radiologists would end up spending a considerable amount of time searching for the missing pieces of the relevant images. The results of such image searches may translate into decreased productivity, lower work satisfaction, and, in some cases, reduced service quality.

Retrieval of relevant patient prior images is usually dynamic and knowledge-intense; thus, representing a promising application area for artificial intelligence AI techniques, which include knowledge-Ž . based systems KBS and automated learning Ž . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 9,12,15,16,19,29,31 . Radiologists’ knowledge of patient prior image retrievals is rooted in their interpretation and application of the pertinent underlying medical<sup>r</sup>radiological knowledge and in their clinical training and experiences. At the same time, this knowledge may vary with individual practice preferences and styles, and may dynamically evolve over time. The dynamic and individualized nature puts a considerable damper on the applicability and the resulting effectiveness of the knowledge-based approach because of the need for constant and often costly knowledge maintenance and updates. Furthermore, a major overhaul of the knowledge repository may become necessary when transferring the resulting KBS from one health care institution to another. In addition to the knowledge maintenance requirements, a knowledge-based approach also needs to address several other challenges. First of all, a manual knowledge acquisition that centers around knowledge engineers and domain experts is highly time- and resource-consuming. The resulting rules may be incomplete, subjective, and even error-prone. In addition, engineering patient image retrieval knowledge is not exempt from the common problems in knowledge engineering that include rationalization, recall bias and difficulty in verbalizing the target knowledge 13,15,16 . In contrast to the problems of and costs involved with the knowledge-engineer-driven approach, AI-based automated learning techniques may represent a more effective and possibly more economical alternative to patient image retrieval.

Knowledge of patient prior image retrieval has several characteristics, including multiple input attributes with missing values, and multiple and inconsistent decision outcomes. These input<sup>r</sup>output characteristics jointly pose a special challenge to automated learning. The literature of automated learning has separately addressed similar characteristics in several independent studies rather than simultaneously in one application. As a consequence, limited discussions and treatments have been offered. This research aims at developing and evaluating viable alternative solutions to learning as well as the subsequently continuous maintenance of patient image retrieval knowledge that satisfactorily meet the discussed input<sup>r</sup>output characteristics. Specifically, two prevailing learning techniques will be explored; namely, backpropagation neural network 25 and<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> decision tree induction 22,23 . Because of its re-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> puted ability to deal with noisy, conflicting and partial data as well as to generalize to situations not encountered previously, backpropagation learning is computationally effective with data patterns of various complex classifications and predication problems <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 1 . In addition, the use of numerically coded neuron-like input and output nodes enables a backpropagation neural network to deal with multiple input attribute values and decision outcomes. On the other hand, decision tree induction learning produces classification rules in the form of decision trees comparable to human reasoning. This particular learning approach has been successfully applied to a considerable array of applications. However, all of the algorithms that have been developed for decision tree induction are appropriate only for applications involving a single decision outcome; that is, each instance belongs to one and only one decision class. Thus, the resulting single-decision-tree-induction

Ž . SDTI algorithms cannot be readily applied to patient prior image retrievals which usually involve multiple decision outcomes i.e., multiple image re-Ž trievals . Extending from a SDTI algorithm, a multi-. decision tree induction MDTI technique capable of Ž . handling multiple decision outcomes will be developed.

## 2. Background overview and literature review

Primary reading, in this study, refers to a radiologist’s interpretation of newly taken radiological examination images by reading or scanning the im-Ž . ages as well as provided examination information that includes the Consultation Requisition Form Ž . Ž . CRF and the Examination Form EF to generate, evaluate, refine and finally conclude the examination findings 9,15,16 . During the primary reading pro- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> cess, a reading radiologist applies heuristic knowledge to determine the relevance of a patient’s prior images with respect to the current ones by using such information as physicians’ clinical questions, the patient’s status and clinical history, imaging modalities, and the locus of interesting radiographic signs. The relevant prior images primarily serve comparative and confirmatory purposes in radiologists’ primary reading.

Several approaches to prior image retrievals have been extensively investigated by researchers and clinicians alike. A statistics-based approach to patient image retrieval was proposed by Kishore et al. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 10 . Because of its complete reliance on usage statistics, this approach lacks a desirable medical<sup>r</sup>radiological foundation. Furthermore, the image retrieval patterns identified are not sophisticated enough to meet the dynamic image reference requirements of the radiologists’ reading in various examination reading scenarios.

In parallel, several knowledge-based patient image retrieval approaches have also been explored. As depicted in Fig. 1, such approaches acquire and embed radiologists’ heuristic knowledge in the patient image retrieval knowledge-base KB , whichŽ . works in conjunction with the knowledge-based system to decide and select relevant images from the included image databases. To improve the image request turnaround time, the relevant images can be pre-fetched and stored in high-speed local archive devices that are located close to radiologists’ image viewing workstations. Reduced examination reading time, together with possibly improved diagnostic accuracy, may increase the work satisfaction of radiologists.

Several knowledge representation schemes for image retrieval knowledge have been proposed. Common examples include production rules 12,19,29 ,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> decision tables 31 , and object-oriented rule-based<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> schemes 15 . Regardless of the knowledge represen-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tation scheme employed and the sophistication of the underlying KB, these knowledge-based approaches require manual knowledge acquisitions that are primarily driven by knowledge engineers. The knowledge-engineer-driven approach is heavily dependent on the interactions between knowledge engineers and domain experts and, as a consequence, can be highly time- and resource-consuming. The literature has identified several limitations and drawbacks of such an approach, including rationalization, recall bias, and difficulty in verbalizing the underlying knowledge 13,15,16 . Furthermore, the need for continu- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ous updating and customization of the patient image retrieval knowledge, especially when supporting a different group of radiologists within or outside institutional boundaries, demands an efficient and effective knowledge engineering approach.

## 4. Design of backpropagation network learning

A backpropagation network is a multi-layered, fully connected, and feed-forward neural network <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 25 . Training of the network includes two stages: forward pass and backward pass. In the forward pass, activation flows from the input layer, through the hidden layer, to the output layer. In the backward pass, the output resulting from the forward pass is compared with the target known output. Based onŽ . the differences between the resulting and target output, error estimates are computed for the output layer nodes. The weights connected to the output nodes are adjusted so as to reduce the error estimates. Both error propagation and weight adjustment are performed in a backward manner and terminate when the weights stemming from the input layer have been adjusted. Important design decisions for a backpropagation network include the encoding schemes for input attributes and decision outcomes, the choice of network topology, and the determination of parameter tuning for the learning rate and the momentum factor.

## 4.1. Encoding scheme

The encoding scheme represents the input attributes and the decision classes using numerically coded input and output nodes. Two alternative encoding schemes have been proposed: local and distributed representation 8,21 . With a local represen- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tation scheme, an independent input or output nodeŽ . is used to represent each value of the underlying input attribute or decision class . With a distributedŽ . representation scheme, a cluster of input or outputŽ . nodes collectively represent all possible values of the underlying input attribute or all decision classes .Ž . For example, if an input attribute can assume four possible values say, Ž $a _ { 1 } , \ a _ { 2 } , \ a _ { 3 } ,$ and $a _ { 4 } )$ , a local representation requires the use of four separate input nodes; that is, one node for each possible value, whereas two input nodes would be sufficient in a distributed representation scheme e.g., 00 forŽ $^ { a _ { 1 } , }$ 01 for $a _ { 2 }$ , 10 for $a _ { 3 } ,$ , and 11 for $a _ { 4 } )$ .. Compared with a distributed representation, a local representation is capable of representing multiple values for an input attribute as well as for multiple decision outcomes. Therefore, a local representation scheme was adopted in this study to represent both the input and the output layer.

## 4.2. Network topology

The topology of a backpropagation network designates the number of input nodes, output nodes, hidden layers, and hidden nodes on each hiddenŽ . layer. The number of input or output nodes isŽ . jointly determined by input attributes or decisionŽ classes and the adopted encoding scheme. To man-. age possibly incomplete input information, some input attributes, such as disease nature and phase, may include an additional attribute value, unknown. Thus, the resulting backpropagation network for learning patient image retrieval knowledge contains a total of 64 input layer nodes and 26 output layer nodes.

The backpropagation networks’ representational power in discriminating outcome decisions and in coping with problem complexity varies with the number of hidden layers. By and large, a single-hidden-layer network can manage most non-linear discriminant decision problems and closely approximate any of them. A two-hidden-layer network, on the other hand, can effectively deal with any non-linear discriminant decision problems. Beyond this, additional layers do not make significant contributions to the network’s representational power 20,30 . Mean-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> while, the marginal contributions of hidden layers in excess of one have also been empirically validated. For instance, a study by de Villiers and Barnard 4<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> shows that the difference between an optimally trained backpropagation network with one hidden layer and that with two hidden layers was statistically insignificant. Thus, a single-hidden-layer backpropagation network was chosen for this study because of its capability in approximating nonlinear discrimination problems and generating learning performances comparable to those of multiple hidden layers.

An inappropriate number of hidden nodes can cause a backpropagation network to under-fit or over-fit the training data. As the number of hidden nodes increases, the training error rate usually decreases i.e., moves away from under-fitting due toŽ the increased network’s flexibility in fitting the data ,. then flattens out, and eventually turns up i.e., show- Ž ing signs of over-fitting 30 . Thus, the choice of an . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> appropriate number of hidden nodes requires a series of experiments.

## 4.3. Parameter tuning

Parameter tuning in a backpropagation network is essentially about selecting an optimal learning rate and a momentum factor. The learning rate denotes the effect of errors on weight changes and therefore can influence the network’s stability and convergence rate. A lower learning rate usually can increase the likelihood of finding a global maximum at the cost of the convergence speed. On the other hand, a higher learning rate can quicken the convergence process at the risk of generating oscillations between local minima 30 . The learning rate can be<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> constant or time-variant, also known as Search-then-Converge 5 . With Search-then-Converge, the learn-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ing rate remains constant $( \mathrm { i } . { \mathrm { e } } . , \ r _ { 0 } ,$ an initial learning rate for a period of time. $T \ ( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ . , search time . When the number of epochs t exceeds T, the learning rate begins to decrease. That is, the learning rate becomes $r _ { 0 } / ( 1 + t / T )$ after T epochs.

A momentum factor, which determines the effect of past weight changes on the current weight change, is included in the network to accelerate its convergence process and, at the same time, to minimize the likelihood of oscillation and to avoid local minima <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 2,30 . Similar to learning rates, a momentum factor can be constant or time-variant. A time-variant momentum factor can assume an initial value say, zero Ž . from epoch 1 to epoch d, increase by a pre-determined increment say, 0.05 for every subsequentŽ . d epochs until it reaches a terminal value say, 0.9 ,Ž . and maintain that terminal value for the rest of the training 24 . Similar to an appropriate number of <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> hidden nodes, decisions on the learning rate and the momentum factor rely on experimental results. The experimentation for determining the number of hidden nodes, learning rate and momentum factor for this study will be deferred until Section 6.

## 5. Design of MDTI learning

That radiologists’ image retrieval knowledge is usually associated with multiple decision outcomes makes SDTI techniques inappropriate. To overcome this inapplicability, a MDTI learning technique was developed based on a simple and widely used SDTI technique, ID3 22,23 . Rather than building a single <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> decision tree, the MDTI technique constructs multiple decision trees, each of which corresponds to a particular decision class. As such, the number of the resulting decision trees equals the number of decision classes. Interested readers are referred to Refs. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 3,14 for discussions of the theoretical foundation and empirical illustrations.

To construct a decision tree for a respective decision class, the original training instances need to be re-assigned into two revised decision classes, positive P and negative N . As shown in Table 3, aŽ . Ž . training instance will be assigned to the revised decision class P if it has a decision outcome for the decision class corresponding to the decision tree constructed; otherwise, the other revised decision class N will be assigned. Algorithm 1 details theŽ . basics of the proposed MDTI. Based on the hypothetical data set shown in Table 3, a graphical illustration of decision tree construction using the MDTI technique is provided in Fig. 4. Because of the binary nature of decision classes in each revised data set, only the attributes capable of critically distinguishing the instances i.e., decision class P versusŽ decision class N were included in the respective. decision tree construction.

Noise may appear in input attribute values as well as in decision outcomes. Constructing a decision tree with noisy data, in input or output, will result in an idiosyncratic knowledge structure whose performance on a separate testing data set is doomed 11,30 . Approaches to correcting idiosyncratic knowledge construction, which largely results from over-fitting the training data, can be classified into two broad categories: pre-pruning or post-pruning. With a pre-pruning approach, decision tree expansion is controlled; that is, an expansion is allowed only when it can significantly contribute to discriminating the outcome decision classes. A post-pruning approach operates in the reverse direction, allowing a decision tree to expand fully, then working from the bottom of the tree upwards by trimming the weak branches until no such branches remain. Either approach can improve the error rate recorded with the separate testing data sets at the expenses of error rates occurring during the training sessions.

The particular pruning method adopted for the MDTI was Critical Value Pruning CVP , which is aŽ . pre-pruning-based technique 3,14 . With CVP, ex-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> pansion of a decision tree halts at the node where the Ratio of the Dominant Class RDC is greater than aŽ . pre-specified threshold value. When this takes place, the dominant class is assigned to the terminal node. In order to minimize type II errors also known asŽ misses; that is, prior examinations actually referenced by radiologists but not suggested by the knowledge , the CVP method was not applied to . nodes whose dominant class is the revised class N.

The effectiveness of the selected pruning method Ž . CVP deteriorates as the number of decision classes increases. The presence of a large number of classes can make the RDC of each node small. In the worst case, the distinction between a pruned and a fully expanded decision tree becomes unclear. In the MDTI technique, the effectiveness of the CVP method can be maximized because of the binary nature of the decision classes. As the threshold value decreases, the size of the resulting decision tree will become smaller i.e., more generalized . However, the Ž . threshold values should be cautiously controlled because their small values may introduce under-fitting problems.

Prominent generic treatments of missing input information include 1 replacing with the meanŽ . values of the other training cases of the same decision class, 2 discarding the cases with the missing Ž . values, and 3 creating surrogates for the attributes Ž . whose values are missing 30 . Most of the missing <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> input information in patient image retrievals can be characterized as not applicable, and therefore the first two treatments become inappropriate or impractical. Thus, the third treatment was adopted by creating a surrogate, called unknown value, to replace the missing values.

## 6.2. Effect of the number of training examples

An important aspect of evaluating learning techniques is their learning performance with a small training data set. Several previous studies have empirically demonstrated that the ID3 technique tends to be more effective than backpropagation networks in situations where relatively few training data are available 6 , while others have presented contradic- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tory results 27 . This inconclusive result demands <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> further study of the effect of the number of training examples on the learning performance of the investigated techniques.

Fig. 7 shows the learning performance of both techniques as a function of the size of training examples. Ten train-and-test evaluation experiments were conducted for each training size. In each trainand-test experiment, N data set s were selectedŽ . from the 10 previously divided data sets for training purpose and the remaining served as the testing set, where N)20<sup>s</sup>the target training size since each data set contained 20 examples. The retrieval threshold value for the backpropagation network was set to 0.2 for all experiments, while the critical value for the MDTI technique was 0.55. The selection of these two values was based on the compatibility of recall and precision rates when both techniques were evaluated with the training size of 180.

As shown in Fig. 7, even with moderate fluctuation, the recall and precision rates of both techniques generally improved when the number of training size increased. When the size of training examples grew from 20 to 180, the recall and precision rates of the MDTI technique improved by 14.29% and 6.76%, while those of the backpropagation technique increased by 13.7% and 8.65%, respectively. The MDTI technique performed better than the backpropagation network technique in the recall rate at any level of training size. When the training size was 80, the precision rate of the backpropagation network technique was slightly higher than that of the MDTI technique. However, the MDTI technique generally outperfomed the backpropagation network technique, especially when given only a small number of training data i.e., fewer than 80 .Ž .

The MDTI’s tendency to perform better than its counterpart on small training data may be explained by the MDTI’s binary nature of decision classes having been less susceptible to the problem of small disjuncts 7 . As demonstrated by Holte et al. 7 ,<sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> leaf nodes in a decision tree that covers only a small number of training examples tend to have much higher error rates than those with a large number of examples. When given a small number of training examples, fewer decision classes in a decision tree results in more training examples per decision class and, in turn, higher classification accuracy than the backpropagation network technique. This effect was also empirically validated by Shavlik et al. 27 on <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> chess data in which only two decision classes exist.

## 6.4. Incremental learning capability

By incremental learning, we mean the ability of a learning technique to continuously maintain and update the existent knowledge with new stimuli. Incremental learning is an essential property of effective learning, especially in situations where the resulting knowledge repository needs to be adapted to the dynamics and constraints of the real world. The iterative training nature of the backpropagation network technique makes it inherently well-suited to incremental learning. By training an already-trained network with new examples for an appropriate number of iterations, learning in the backpropagation network technique can be performed incrementally <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 27 . However, determining an optimal iteration number can be non-trivial or, in some cases, problematic. The network, on the one hand, may fail to assimilate the new examples fully in a few iterations and, on the other hand, may lose the generalization obtained from previous examples with a large number of iterations. The decision on optimal iterations is highly application-dependent and requires extensive experimentation. In contrast, decision tree induction techniques are usually non-incremental. Several incremental methods for ID3-based decision tree induction techniques have been proposed 28 and an <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> incremental MDTI technique has also been developed 3 . These incremental methods usually involve<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> deriving a decision prediction for a new example using the existing decision tree and then restructuring or expanding the decision tree if the prediction differs from the actual decision.

Although incremental learning is achievable in both the backpropagation network and MDTI techniques, more research is needed to compare the efficiency and effectiveness of the respective incremental learning. Moreover, developing new incremental learning methods for both techniques and evaluating the resulting performance also deserve further research efforts.

## 6.5. Interpretability

The backpropagation network technique represents a holistic approach to learning by encoding the knowledge in the weights between nodes. Explaining the knowledge that is embedded in the network structure is difficult, if even possible. Thus, deriving interpretable rules from a backpropagation network is not likely. The MDTI technique, on the other hand, is capable of generating interpretable rules from the resulting multiple decision trees. Two exemplary rules generated by the MDTI technique are as follows.

Rule a1: If Availability of the most recent examination whose examined anatomical portion and imaging modality are the same as those of the current examination<sup>s</sup>yes

Then Retrieve the most recent one with the same examined anatomical portion and imaging modality.

Rule a34: If Mass-Type <sup>s</sup>Benign-tumor and Patient-Source<sup>s</sup>ICU

Then Retrieve the three most recent three examinations with the same examined anatomical portion and related imaging modality.

The MDTI technique is superior to the backpropagation network technique in interpretability. However, a decision tree is difficult to interpret when it becomes too large 26 . This decreased interpretabil-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ity problem is accentuated by the MDTI technique, which captures and represents the underlying knowledge using multiple decision trees. As such, the interpretability of the trees decreases because a semantically integrated knowledge element is fragmented into and represented by multiple decision trees. To improve the interpretability of the MDTI technique, merging multiple decision trees into a single decision tree is essential and needs to be addressed by future research.

## 7. Conclusion and future research directions

Artificial intelligence learning techniques have the potential to provide intelligent information systems with continuous adaptability in response to new stimuli from the diverse and dynamic real-world environment. However, existing learning solutions are limited in their ability to cope with the complex input and output characteristics of radiologists’ image retrievals. Effective learning of patient image retrieval knowledge needs to address several challenges, which include multiple input attributes with possible missing values as well as multiple and inconsistent decision outcomes.

In this study, we adopted a backpropagation learning algorithm and also developed an MDTI technique to perform the target learning. Compared with a knowledge-engineer-driven knowledge acquisition approach, which took a knowledge acquisition team consisting of two knowledge engineers and several domain experts several months to complete, both learning techniques were more efficient, in terms of time and other resource requirements. In addition to reducing the time requirements by several orders of magnitude, these two techniques were able to achieve an image retrieval accuracy comparable to that accomplished by the manual knowledge acquisition approach, manifesting their effectiveness and efficiency in this particular learning application. Comparisons of the investigated techniques show that the MDTI technique can result in a higher retrieval accuracy than that of the backpropagation network technique even with a small number of training examples. Backpropagation also suffers from longer training sessions and limited interpretability. Additional empirical studies using a larger data set are needed to validate these findings.

This study benefits not only patient image retrieval research and practice but also other learning applications with identical or similar input and output characteristics. Continuing research should be strategically aligned to further the generalizability and effectiveness of the developed learning methods. Some ongoing and future research directions are briefly discussed as follows.

Developments and empirical evaluations of incremental learning methods for both techniques are instrumental. Automated learning is critical to a broad implementation of knowledge-based image retrieval support. In this light, incremental learning becomes an important requirement in knowledge maintenance and in increasing the life span of the knowledgebases. Dynamic and automated parameter tuning and network architecture adjustments to fit new data better are also important. Topological design and parameter value decisions for backpropagation networks have demanded and consumed considerable experimentation effort. Thus, developing intelligent agents to facilitate the design and value-setting process is also essential and will have large theoretical and pragmatic impacts.

In addition, performing a longitudinal empirical evaluation of the developed learning techniques in different radiology practice environments is both essential and desirable. By and large, AI applications in health care usually need to meet stringent regulation and moral standards. An on-line case sampling mechanism would significantly facilitate such evaluation studies. Expanding the developed learning techniques to other applications with similar characteristics, including clinical case selection for clinical education and training, similarity-based medical information retrieval, and guideline-based patient management decision making, also represents interesting directions for future research.
