## Abstract

By using panel data spanning a period of 18 years and by estimating a general cost function in a cost minimization setting, we conducted an analysis of information technology IT investments and production performance in the healthcareŽ . industry. The span of years from 1976 to 1994 in the healthcare industry is characterized by major regulatory changes and several organizational changes such as technological IT and medical advances and structural changes multi-hospitalŽ . Ž systems MHSs , vertical integration, etc. . This offers a unique setting to study the effect of regulation on the performance<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> . of the entire hospital and also of each input factor used in the production of services in the hospital. The cost function approach and the panel data technique allow us to test various hypotheses regarding technical change, substitution, and complementarity effects between IT investments, medical capital investments and labor expenses, and the role of regulation on the cost structure of hospitals. We find that while IT labor expense rose at an increasing rate due to regulatory effects, hospitals were moving in the right direction towards cost containment.<sup>2</sup> q 2000 Published by Elsevier Science B.V.

## 2. Background and literature review

## 3.1. DRG and management adjustment

The enactment of the DRG-related reimbursement legislation led to major changes in hospital management. The most important change is that hospitals moved from revenue-maximization to a cost-minimization strategy 38 . By paying a fixed fee for a<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> diagnosis irrespective of the actual charges incurred, the government forced hospitals to select cheaper ways to provide for patient care. Therefore, we will find that hospital costs increased with time in the period prior to DRG implementation and that hospital costs decreased with time in the periods after

DRG implementation. In the overall period, the effects may nullify showing negligible change in hospital costs. If the costs of production decreases over time, the production unit is said to have undergone technical progress. On the other hand, if costs increase over time, the unit is said to have undergone technical regress 26 . Technical progress or regress<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Ž . should not be confused with technological progress Ž .or regress which is a reference to the state of technology. ## 4. Econometric model

Having discussed how the hypotheses may be tested, we now turn to the problem of estimating the cost function. In general production theory, a production function which relates the quantity of outputŽ to a set of input quantities is estimated under a. cost-minimization or profit-maximization framework. The dual to the production function approach is to estimate the cost function. To obtain efficient and consistent estimates of the parameters of the cost function, it is jointly estimated with the cost share equations 21 , pp. 576–612 . As we have already <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Ž . shown, the advantage of using the cost function approach is that useful measures such as the rate of technical progress, input substitution or complementarity, etc., is easily derived.

Changing regulatory environment and differences between hospitals with regard to organizational variables such as for-profit status, teaching status, case Ž mix ratio or severity of cases, etc. motivate the use. of panel data as opposed to pooling the data. See Refs. 9,43 , etc., on the importance of organizational <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> variables on the behavior of hospitals. We introduce firm-specific and year-specific dummy variables in the estimation of the cost function thereby factoring out possible confounds and providing more efficient and consistent estimates for the parameters of the cost function.

The cost function is specified in the translog form. The translog form is based on the second-order Taylor series approximation for any functional form and therefore, is one of the most flexible, general, and versatile forms for estimating cost functions 15 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> The translog form is well behaved and satisfies all properties required of cost functions such as the existence of second-order differentials 40 . On the other hand, we must note that the translog function like all other parametric forms provides the best approximation of the functional form at the mean values and deviates from the actual functional form as we get further away from the mean values 26 . In<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> addition, the model will also suffer from specification error depending on the particular choice of inputs and outputs. $$

## 5.1. Calculation of capital stock and prices

The data contain annual capital investments in depreciated terms. We take the depreciated capital value for each year and divide it by the depreciation rate for that year and the resulting value is taken to be the capital stock value for the previous year. A difference between the calculated depreciated amount and the actual depreciated amount is taken to be depreciation amount from new investments. Depreciation from new investments is in turn divided by the depreciation rate of the year and added to the capital stock. While this calculation procedure may lead to some noisy data for the initial few years, we effectively determine the capital stock for each year for each hospital that is close to the actual stock for the later years. Unlike previous studies, which have inaccurately used annual investments as a measure of the productive stock, our calculations lead to a measure of capital stock of the following form.

$$
\mathrm{CS} _ {t} = \mathrm{CS} _ {t - 1} + \mathrm{NI} _ {t} - D _ {t}\tag{14}
$$

where CS is capital stock, NI is the new investment, and D is the depreciated capital. Thus, the perpetual inventory method, Eq. 14 enables us to determine Ž . the useful productive capital available during a particular year in a hospital 3 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

The prices of capital are calculated using the formulations in Ref. 11 . The intuition behind the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> calculation is that the price of capital can be derived as the Adiscounted value of the stream of services Ž . and, hence, implicitly the rents that the asset will provideB <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 3 . where $q _ { t }$ is the price index of the capital asset, $r _ { i t }$ is the rate of return of the entire hospital based on its income and prices of all inputs, and $d _ { t }$ is the depreciation rate in time t. The results of the calculation of the prices are given in Table 2.

On examining the Morishima elasticities, we obtain similar results. Substitution between input factors is indicated by negative values in this case. IT capital is found to be substituted with the remaining three input factors prior to DRG but not so after DRG implementation.

## 7. Conclusions and future research

In this paper, we studied the effect of regulation on hospital costs with particular reference to the DRG legislation. We find that hospital costs differed greatly between the pre-DRG and post-DRG periods. DRG legislation led to a lower level of technical regress in hospitals. This implies that cost containment regulation has met with their intended objective to some degree. Without the competitive pressures in the private sector, non-profit organizations are more prone to inefficiencies of operations. Under such economic conditions, this study shows that IT enables some degree of efficiency in production. We do find that IT was a substitute for other input factors in the post DRG period. In spite of the regulatory changes, we find that IT investments yielded positive returns to hospitals. We plan to analyze the contribution from data processing and data communications separately in a future paper. One limitation of the paper may be the dependence of the results on accounting data. As mentioned earlier in the paper, hospitals resorted to cost allocation procedures before and after DRG implementation to maximize reimbursements and so, the resulting capital stock and labor cost allocation could have been noisy see, for example Ref. 16 , for how costsŽ <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> were allocated to out-patient side after DRG implementation . It is difficult to say how the results will. be affected if the data were devoid of manipulations due to cost allocations. The parametric techniques that we employ in this study enable us to determine several measures of productivity, efficiency, and organizational behavior. However, non-parametric studies require fewer assumptions in the formulation and future research using these techniques could yield additional information on organizational behavior in hospitals.
