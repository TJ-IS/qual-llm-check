## Abstract

We study the behavior of prices, efficiency and wholesale buyer and seller profitability using paid human subjects in a two-sided sealed bid–offer SBO spot market bidding mechanism on a three-node radial supply system. Market powerŽ . issues are addressed by comparing three with six remote generation firms. Key features of the supply<sup>r</sup>demand environment were developed through the authors’ interactions with industry executives in a series of workshops sponsored in our laboratory: demand cycled through three levels, with a large spike of must-serve uninterruptible peak power requiring buyers to bid more than their resale values and lose money to obtain adequate power if they were to avoid outage penalties;Ž . off-peak generator companies had excess must-run baseload capacity and had to bid at or below marginal cost to avoid shut-down penalty costs; at mid-level shoulder demand both sides of the market were loss-free. In this stressful environment, efficiency is remarkably high, prices highly competitive, and we observe no significant difference in the measured performance criteria between three and six firms. q 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. ## 1. Economic environment of power systems

Power delivery systems have a number of characteristics whose combination is somewhat unusual and that require consideration and generic representation in any experimental examination. Some of those characteristics reflect the historical state of technology and the historic institution of AAmerican styleB Ž . rate-of-return regulation. Engineers have, in the past, often listed many of these characteristics as AuniqueB, but that is to some extent an exaggeration, although clearly the market design problem in electric power must come to terms with the physical realities of the industry. All industries vary with respect to the market institutions that serve them because it is appropriate for such institutions to be tailored, through evolution, to variations in the economic and physical realities of different industries. The revolution in thinking about electric power during the past half dozen years is the widespread recognition that it is feasible for the structure, price and performance of the industry to be regulated by markets. This was unthinkable a decade ago. In spite of this progress, the form and extent of such market discipline is controversial because of the incompleteness of the state of our knowledge as to what market institutional forms are most appropriate.

## 1.1. Coordination and simultaneity of production and consumption

Electric power is a dramatic example of just-intime production. On-line generators provide the energy needed instantaneously for all consumptive uses, including losses in delivery due to line resistance. This is the most demanding characteristic of the industry—a characteristic tied to its relative nonstorability. Of course energy is stored in the system in the form of coal inventories, water reservoirs, oil and gas tanks and stocks of nuclear fuel, but these must be converted into mechanical energy and the latter into electrical energy as rapidly as surges and declines in use require.

It is not correct, as sometimes alleged, that this characteristic is unique: the transportation and hotel accommodation industries also supply nonstorable products that must meet instantaneous demand conditions. A room not rented, or an airplane seat not filled is lost forever at insignificant out-of-pocket cost as compared with its potential value. You cannot build up inventories of rooms<sup>r</sup>seats for peaks, and build down for troughs. Consequently, capacity is determined by peak load conditions together with market rationing where the latter depends on the price elasticity of demand. This explains the aggressive use of weekly and seasonal peak load pricing, brought naturally by competition, in these industries. Under deregulation, similar forces will shape the electrical industry. What is unique to alternating current power is the need for all generation sources interconnected on the same high voltage grid to be synchronized. This requires at least some of the committed generation capacity a few percent to be Ž . on automatic control from central dispatch centers for load following operations. Hence, the spot market price, location-adjusted for losses throughout the network, must be closely tied in with dispatch and the allocation of marginal loads among load following generation units. The demands this puts upon the communication and control system are far from trivial, although in this study we abstract from such important operational issues in order to focus on the market performance and design side of the equation.

## 1.2. Flexible Õs. inflexible generation

Power stations vary from relatively inflexible, high capacity, baseload, coal fired and nuclear energy units to flexible hydro and gas turbines of small to medium capacity that can readily follow load variations or even provide quick-start sources of emergency power. Because of the slower ramp Ž . acceleration<sup>r</sup>deceleration rates of baseload units, flexible load following units are needed to keep supply instantaneously equal to demand. Historically, this has meant that back-up capacity to meet demand must be sufficient if there is an unscheduled outage by a large generator or a transmission line or their associated connectors and transformers. This capacity has been provided by Aspinning reservesB; i.e. enough surplus capacity is made available by multiple generators that are on line, and spinning, loaded at or above their minimum loaded capacity, but below their maximum capacity. In Norway,Ž which is essentially an all-hydro power system, reserves—water impoundment—are provided by bringing additional hydro turbines quickly online as needed. Although in this study we abstract from. explicit treatment of spinning reserves, generator inflexibility is one of the centerpieces of the environment we create.

## 1.3. Demand side adjustment

An alternative to this Asupply sideB adjustment to unscheduled excess demand has always been to shed load; i.e. interrupt the delivery of power to customers whose demand is not sensitive to continuity of supply for example, in Australia, some 15% of peak Ž demand in some areas can be interrupted by temporarily shutting off hot water heaters or other interruptible loads . Historically, regulatory incentives in . the States have strongly mitigated against such demand side adjustment. The political process responds negatively to Aloss of lightsB, and positively to new supply side investment rewarded with a AfairB rateof-return.

With deregulation, we expect these incentives to change drastically, and for there to be an increase in contracts providing inducements for many customers to allow their loads to be interrupted at peak times or under other temporary excess demand conditions. Some power companies have already computed the great value in allowing the demand side to respond to price signals, and are beginning to negotiate more interruptible contracts. Although we plan to study this issue with experiments designed to allow subject wholesale buyers to invest in greater interruption capacity, the experiments reported below are designed to capture the current economic environment of deregulation. This environment is one of limited demand interrupt capability but also one with substantial baseload capacity built with technologies driven by pronounced economies of scale. But new turbine, and combined cycle, gas technologies yield cost-efficient units at much lower capacity levels— down to 200 MW—than is represented by currently installed large baseload capacity units. The forces of obsolescence and age will almost certainly replace the existing stock of baseload units with smaller, low cost, and more flexible generator capacity. Consequently, we examine an experimental design that reflects, in our opinion, an environment likely to be impacted by change in the next decade. One implication of these changes, which is not studied here, is the possibility of Adistributed generationB—the location of new capacity near customer loads 9 . This<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> holds forth the promise of by-passing the transmission and distribution grids, subjecting the Awires businessB to forms of competition similar to those now emerging in the telephone and television industries. Such a development—historically unthinkable —would undermine the classic presumption that transmission and distribution are inherently natural monopolies. The problem of regulatory reform is to find mechanisms that allow these changes to occur naturally in response to market forces, and to minimize political<sup>r</sup>regulatory resistance to such change.

## 1.4. Variation in load and marginal cost oÕer time

The daily cycle in the consumption of power from a nighttime low to a daytime peak places a corresponding load on generator output, given minor customer load interruption. This cycle is generically similar in almost all local utility systems. Fig. 1 reproduces a typical week’s load cycle showing the pronounced peak to off-peak variation during weekdays, with a lower amplitude cycle for weekend days.

Variation in load induces a corresponding supply side variation over time in a utility’s Asystem lambdaB—the marginal cost of the most expensive generator required to meet the load. The cyclical nature of this measure of energy cost intensity is shown in Fig. 2 for a mid-western utility during a hot August week. The flat peaks result from factors such as load interruption and<sup>r</sup>or importation of power from sources external to the utility.

The experiments reported here will use a stylized representation of a demand cycle, which is expected to induce a corresponding variation in the generator load, similar to one of the daily cycles in Fig. 1 and, through the spot market, a variation in price generically similar to the marginal cost cycle in Fig. 2.

## 3. Mechanism for a smart computer-assisted exchange

In this paper, we examine a call market system for organizing a spot market for energy in a transmission network; i.e. the market is AcalledB at a particular point in time when all accepted bids to buy and offers to sell become binding contracts. This is in contrast to the standard double auction rules used in securities, commodity and foreign exchange markets. In these double auction markets, trading is continuous except for opening and sometimes mid-Ž day calls , the highest bid price and lowest ask price. have priority over all other bids and asks, and yield a bid–ask spread. Contracts occur in real time as either the standing highest bid is accepted by some seller or the standing lowest offer is accepted by some buyer. The bid–ask spread can be narrowed at any time when a new bid higher then the standing bid ask isŽ . submitted or a new ask lower than the standing ask is entered.

It is natural to think in terms of a call market for electricity, since spot trades between regulated utilities have typically been organized as hourly bilateral exchanges. In our sealed bid–offer exchange, bids specifying a bid price and quantity $( b _ { i } , Q _ { i } ) _ { }$ , and asks specifying an ask price and quantity $( a _ { j } , ~ Q _ { j } )$ , are submitted privately, the bids are ordered from highest to lowest according to bid price yielding the Ž demand schedule , the offers are ordered from lowest. to highest according to ask price the supply sched-Ž ule , and the market cleared at a price—the competi-. tive equilibrium CE price—that equalizes the de-Ž . mand and supply. Bids lower than the CE price are rejected; while asks higher than the CE price are rejected. ## 4.2. Generator characteristics in the industry

Turbine generators are characterized by declining average variable fuel costs per unit of output up to Ž . maximum capacity, and by increasing marginal cost starting at a specified engineering minimum loaded capacity and ending at maximum capacity.<sup>2</sup> Engineers consider the range of declining marginal cost as an inoperative region and this criterion is used to define minimum loaded—efficient—capacity.<sup>3</sup> Minimum loaded capacity is commonly at least 40–50% of maximum loaded capacity. Furthermore, marginal cost frequently varies little from 40–50% of capacity up to 100% in low variable cost baseload e.g. coalŽ . <sup>4</sup> fired units. For this reason, we approximate facility marginal costs with step functions in the experiments, each step representing the approximated constant marginal cost for some generator unit.

Generators, particularly baseload units, also have high avoidable start-up costs. Avoidable fixed startup costs can range upward to US\$18–19,000 see Ž Appendix A or more. It is costly to put a generator . through a decommit<sup>r</sup>recommit cycle, because of start-up fuel, stress costs, and the cost of lost load. Generation companies see bilateral contracting as the institution that will solve this commitment<sup>r</sup>revenue problem, but large buyers are keenly aware of how this inflexibility impacts generator vulnerability in contract bargaining. We examine only a spot market’s ability to function in this environment, to establish a competitive baseline, but we recognize that futures markets, as well as contracting,<sup>5</sup> are sure to play a significant role in conjunction with spot markets under deregulation.

## 4.4. Representing demand

Industry<sup>r</sup>regulatory thinking is conditioned by the idea that demand varies insignificantly with price, and this is essentially correct in the short run, particularly in regions with little or no tradition for interruptible power contracts. Econometric studies, however, have typically found demand to have more substantial elasticity over longer periods. The industry is adamant, however, in insisting that they currently have large Amust serveB demand at a fixed regulated retail price with only limited capacity to interrupt demand except in emergencies. Even in emergencies, to interrupt customers involuntarily is seen as incurring large political costs with the public and with the state regulatory commissions. Although, as noted earlier, we want to study the transition from this rigid demand environment to one with increasing interruptible contract arrangements, the current study models demand very closely along the lines of current industry perceptions. Since that perception has been, and is, in a state of change, we would not regard our current model as applying very far into the future.

Our experiments model demand with four wholesale buyers who we can think of as local distribution companies serving residential, commercial and industrial customers, a few of whom can be interrupted, and who, as a consequence, receive a price discount. This segment of demand, however, can be interrupted at the discretion of the individual bulk buyer, who buys for the account of, and delivery to, these resale customers.

## 6.10. Discussion

The market power results are contrary to the conventional wisdom in economics. Why do we get no significant decrease in market power when moving from three to six generation companies?

We should emphasize first that the results are not without precedent in previous experimental studies of contestability with only two firms subject to declining average total and variable cost 2,3,5 . InŽ . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> this literature, it was found that when two firms cannot formally collude, although they may attempt tacit collusion by price signalling, competition tends to rule. In decreasing unit cost environments, the last unit sold is the most profitable, and firms producing undifferentiated products must avoid selling insufficient capacity to recover their sunk cost. This results in exceptionally vigorous price competition. Fig. 4 shows that average total and average variable costs rise only modestly when moving from off-peak through mid-level to on-peak demand.

Similar forces are at work in this electric power environment using the sealed bid–offer auction rules. Firms are under pressure to see their generator offers accepted at commitment levels that cover both sunk and avoidable fixed cost. This avoidable cost risk is not mitigated when there are only three firms, since sellers individually experience twice the level of sunk and avoidable fixed costs as compared with six firms. Buyers face similar risks in meeting must-serve demand. The effect of this avoidable cost risk in the sealed bid–offer auction is to provide stronger incentives for generators to enter supply revealing offers, and for buyers to submit demand revealing bids. Agents get just one offer or bid submission vector per trading period, and underrevelation increases the risk of incurring these avoidable fixed costs. The chart in Fig. 3 compares revealed and true supply and mid-level demand. Note that units near the CE are offered and bid close to true costs and values.

We would caution against any temptation to extrapolate these results beyond the specific conditions we study, except to proffer hypotheses subject to further testing. Thus, formal collusion is effectively prohibited—subjects cannot talk during any experiment. In effect, we achieve the conditions of a perfect antitrust legal environment. However, the experimental literature studying the effect of providing an Aopportunity to conspire,B has tended to show that such cartels are unstable 4 . Here, the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> supply<sup>r</sup>demand environment is heavily weighted against generator profitability off-peak, and against buyer profitability on-peak. This leads to missed trades, and lower efficiency than in more conventional value<sup>r</sup>cost environments compare the highŽ efficiencies reported in Ref. 1 . Finally, there are <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. no transmission constraints to bifurcate the market, and weaken the conditions for contestability on either side of the constraint. Thus, a constraint on line 1 left-hand side in Fig. 3 , would mean that aŽ . monopoly generator would be operating on the right for sales in excess of the constrained deliveries from the left.
