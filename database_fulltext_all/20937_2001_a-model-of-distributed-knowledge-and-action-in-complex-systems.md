## Abstract

This paper presents a model of distributed knowledge and action in complex systems, and discusses the implications of the model for decision support. The model represents three essential components of complex systems knowledge: 1 causalŽ . relationships that form the complex system, 2 operating procedure directives and goals that constrain the system operatorsŽ . actions, and 3 system state trajectories over time. System knowledge is formalized in a computer model that predicts andŽ . explains human interventions. The model extends previous conceptual studies, and serves as a framework for a more general model that is potentially applicable to other complex task environments. q 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. ## 1. Introduction

This paper describes the design and architecture of a computational model of distributed knowledge and action in complex systems, and the implications of the model for decision support in these task environments. A complex system, such as the Nuclear Power Plant NPP environment under study Ž . here, apportions responsibility for decision making and action across the individual constituents i.e., Ž actors and knowledge sources that comprise the . system 8 see Fig. 1 . Those constituents include<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Ž . the following.

Ž . 1 Physical processes, which react to inputs and produce outputs based on the causal nature of the processes e.g., as temperature rises in a contained Ž vessel, pressure also rises ..

Ž .2 Automated interÕentions, which react when a specified set of system parameters crosses a pre-determined threshold e.g., a safety valve closes whenŽ the pressure and<sup>r</sup>or fluid level inside of a tank falls below some value ..

Ž . 3 Management goals, which indicate what the process is to accomplish.

Ž . Ž . 4 Operating procedures or programs , which guide human intervention in the system.

Ž . 5 Human operators, who respond to observed events in the context of: a management goals, Ž . which might be facilitated or threatened by system events, and b operating procedures, which directŽ . certain actions based on system conditions and events. The term operator in this sense refers generically to professionally trained, knowledgeable human interactors with complex systems in various domains 8 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

The structure of the complex system implements a type of decision-making-by-proxy that supports the distribution and communication of knowledge. Management goals and procedures, for example, are operational surrogates for management at various levels, and are intended to assure that the system functions within pre-specified constraints and toward pre-specified objectives. Automated interventions and procedures, in turn, communicate and implement the prescribed actions of design engineers under varying system conditions.

‘Knowledge’ in this context is composed of the explicit knowledge of the individual system constituents, as well as the tacit knowledge that ensues from interactions between the operators and other constituents of the system as indicated in Fig. 1 .Ž . The tacit knowledge provided by interactions is important to a more complete understanding of complex systems and their behaviors. Operator interaction with procedures is particularly illustrative of tacit knowledge leading to explicit system behavior. For a complex facility such as the NPP, emergency procedures are necessarily written to deal with an expected state of the world when, in fact, the choice of the appropriate action to be taken requires knowledge of the real state of the world. Consequently, previous research in procedure execution in various complex system domains, including airline and NPP operations, indicates that operator interventions are influenced, but not necessarily determined, by the directives of applicable operating procedures <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 6,7,15,19 . That is, at some times an operator will follow a procedure, while at other times she<sup>r</sup>he will not.

Pre-specified procedures have inherent limitations. They are usually written by the design engineers who must anticipate all possible states of the process and its environment when specifying what actions the operator should take. They also must be written based on assumptions about the knowledge available to the operator when actions must be taken. It is not surprising that inherent limitations in procedures would impose upon operators additional cognitive and response planning requirements beyond those stipulated explicitly in the procedures. Roth et al. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 18 identify a number of these execution-time requirements, including the need to construct an adequate situation assessment and an accurate mental model of the physical plant. Most important to this research, however, they argue that operators must be able to act when procedural guidance is lacking or ambiguous. Operators must have Athe ability to balance multiple goals when explicit procedural guidance is not available,B and Athe ability to take discretionary action to mitigate or prevent more severe consequencesB <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 11, p. 497 .

Similar issues arise in the design and execution of airline cockpit procedures. Degani and Weiner 6,7<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> note that pilots, like NPP operators, will deviate from procedures when they believe those procedures are not appropriate to the situation at hand. They argue: AManagement . . . must recognize the danger of over-procedurization, which fails to exploit one of the most valuable assets in the system, the intelligent operator who is ‘on the scene.’ The alert system designer and operations manager recognize that there cannot be a procedure for everything, and the time will come in which the operators of a complex system will face a situation for which there is no written procedureB <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 6, p. 302 They conclude: A . . . even in this highly proceduralized system, there is room for individualism, and . . . individualism can, and should be tolerated in these human–machine systemsB <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 6, p. 311 .

Each of these studies argues that both procedures and human cognition play an important role in the proper functioning of a complex system, and that both are important components of system knowledge. Decision support in this environment, in turn, requires an examination of the various knowledge sources, and construction and presentation of a situation assessment based on that examination. More precisely, it requires a means of determining appropriate operator actions given a current system state, and by implication, the circumstances under which operators should or should not deviate from procedures.

## 2. Research objectives and contributions

Recently, O’Leary 10, p. 58 suggested that al-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> though expert systems can be an important constituent of knowledge management KM systems,Ž . their potential for KM has only begun to be tapped. He argues 10, p. 58 :<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> AWe need additional research to expand the use of artificial intelligence AI andŽ . knowledge-based systems in KM. We need to know what forms of knowledge representation appear to work best for particular types of knowledge and how AI can be further integrated into KM systemsB. Similarly, Mitchell and Sundstrom 8 have argued<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> for more formal representations of organizational knowledge — particularly in complex systems — which would serve as the basis for a more efficient dissemination of knowledge in an age of increasing turnover in the workplace.

This research pursues a similar agenda in extending the prior cognitive studies of procedure execution in complex systems described above. We propose a generic representation scheme, or ontology, that models the explicit knowledge structures and their interactions, and then validate the model in a computer simulation that explains and predicts human interventions in real-world scenarios. An ontology provides a description of concepts in a domain, and the relations among those concepts, within which application-specific knowledge bases can be constructed 3,11 . Although the ontology proposed here <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> was developed primarily in the domain of NPP operations, it nevertheless is applicable to other process control environments and to other complex systems in general. The objective of the model is to provide the basis for a DSS that can provide to operators the knowledge and planning expertise of system designers, as represented in procedural and physical models, as well as the knowledge of intervention strategies gleaned from other operators.

The model contributes to an understanding of complex system knowledge and behavior in three ways. First, at its most basic level, the model uses procedures and potential procedure pathways to make predictions about future operator behaÕior, and to explain the behaÕior once its obserÕed. For example, if Step 4 in an NPP operating procedure has just been completed, and conditions are appropriate for the execution of Step 5, then in all likelihood the operator simply will execute Step 5. An explanation of why the operator executed Step 5, in turn, can be generated from knowledge of the hierarchy of goals that the step is intended to achieve or helpŽ achieve . In domains that are strongly constrained by. procedures, this approach typically is most effective in predicting and explaining operator behavior. This approach also is in concert with Allison’s 1 model<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> of organizational processes, which maintains that many observed behaviors of governmental and other organizations can be explained directly in terms of the output of organizational procedures.

Second, although procedure-compliant behavior tends to occur most commonly, the most interesting aspects of operator behavior come in procedure deviations. The model therefore supplements the predictiÕe capabilities of procedures by indicating when operators might deÕiate from the procedures, and then, after the deÕiation is obserÕed, explaining why the operator chose to deÕiate. As noted, an operator deviates from a procedure because the procedure cannot anticipate every situation that might arise during their execution. Procedures are sometimes based on default, and potentially incorrect, assumptions concerning the state of the world at execution time. Acting on incorrect expectations creates a incongruity between the management goals that the operator seeks to achieve and the goals that will be achieved as a result of rote procedure execution. This in turn can motivate an enlightened procedure executor to deviate from the procedure in order to avoid the expected adverse consequences that might be incurred by the organization if the procedure were to be executed as written. The model sheds light on the interplay between the current state of the complex system, the prescribed series of steps in the current procedure, management goals, and the operator’s situation assessment and response planning processes.

## 3. Research method

The research methodology employed in this research closely resembles the methodology proposed by Mitchell and Sundstrom for studying decision making in complex systems 8 . They describe a<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> multi-phase process that entails 1 preliminary ob- Ž .

servation, 2 hypothesis formation and model con-Ž . struction, 3 evaluation using empirical data, 4 Ž . Ž . formulation of domain-specific conclusions, and 5Ž . formulation of domain-general conclusions. This type of methodology, as they argue, has high face validity not only for the specific area under study, but also for the broad class of problems and tasks to which the problem under study relates. They suggest that a number of recent studies of complex systems operation have followed at least portions of the methodology e.g., see Refs. 6,26 . Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>.

The methodology of the current study borrows generally from cognitiÕe task analysis, a knowledge acquisition technique that attempts to observe, understand and model the factors, both cognitive and environmental, influencing behavior generated during performance of a task under study 9,16,17 . The<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> modeling effort was undertaken in two iterative stages. The first stage was an empirically driven task analysis that sought to understand and model the cognitive processes employed by people who are working on procedure-influenced tasks. The analysis explored 1 all of the environmental influences andŽ . constraints impinging on the actor’s decision processes — including but not limited to procedural constraints — and 2 the nature of the reasoning Ž . processes and structures used by the individual to plan, re-plan, and navigate toward achievement of desired goals within the constraints imposed by the task environment. The second stage, drawing from an initial understanding of how the various constituents of the task influenced and constrained operator behavior, entailed the construction of a new model that explains the behavior described in the first task model. Construction of the explanatory model was guided by AI-related theoretical and empirical work in areas such as explanation and plan recognition.

The general task analytic research strategy was further decomposed into five iterative phases: 1Ž . review of the theoretical and descriptive domain literature specifically describing the tasks under study, 2 review of the theoretical literature relatedŽ . to procedure-constrained behavior, 3 transcriptionŽ . and analysis of observed subject behavior using concurrent protocol analysis, 4 consideration of expla-Ž . nation-based research in cognitive science, AI and organizational behavior that contributes to the explanatory model, and 5 construction of a concep- Ž . tual model, followed by a computer model, based on the results of Steps 1 through 4.

Data collection began with existing concurrent and retrospective protocol data, collected by Roth et al. 19 , of several teams of operators involved in<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> simulated NPP emergencies. Our intent was to build upon Roth et al.’s cognitive investigation and analysis of operator behavior, particularly those aspects of behavior pertaining to procedure deviations. This required a reexamination of their data with the purpose of identifying the specific cognitive processes and structures that might serve as constituents of a computational model of the behavior.

The emergency scenarios had been constructed so that resolution of the plant emergency would require more than simple navigation of the prescribed procedures. The sequence of plant behaviors comprising the scenarios was coded into a high-fidelity NPP training simulator, and then presented to several NPP operator crews. The carefully controlled sequence of events in the simulation provided an effective mechanism for observing and modeling the situation assessment and response planning behaviors of operators in the context of preformulated procedures.

The model was enhanced and generalized using the same iterative method to explore application of the evolving theory in different domains. NPP operations were chosen as the initial basis for the model because the domain presents many of the issues of procedure-influenced decision making explored in this research. Nevertheless, those issues are generalizable to other domains as well. For example, the model was later extended and applied to behavior in a legal<sup>r</sup>regulatory environment, in which actors engaged in merger activities were required to act within constraints imposed by various merger and acquisition laws and regulations. Regulations in this regard are analogous to procedures, because they stipulate a series of actions required in order to achieve a particular goal or goals. Because the regulatory domain in this case encompassed the banking industry, the dominant ‘procedure’ was the Bank Holding Act. The actions of banks in attempting to navigate and Ž perhaps sidestep regulations in pursuit of a specific. goal — i.e., to merge with or acquire another bank — is reminiscent of the behavior of operators who are constrained by NPP procedures. Using similar theoretical constructs to explain seemingly different human behavior across heterogeneous domains is a partial validation of the generalizability of the computational model. The implications of the general model are discussed later.

## 4. Computer model of procedure-constrained behavior

The computational model of complex system behavior was constructed from analysis of empirical data gathered in a human-mediated, process control environment — i.e., NPP operations. The empirical data included observations of changes in the complex system state, relevant procedures and the rea soning of operators, as evidenced by their utterances and actions, during simulated plant emergencies.

As noted, the goal of the model is to predict, and then explain, the behavior of the complex system based on a description of the organizational processes. The model receives as input a series of process-induced events, and produces as output an explanation of the events and<sup>r</sup>or a prediction of future actions resulting from the event. The model’s focus on an explanation of operator action is intended to demonstrate that it can understand the contextual features of the process control environment, to the degree that it can predict behavior. From a decision support perspective, while the explanation generated by the current model is directly useful for ex post evaluation by management, the underlying theory embodied in the model is perhaps more important to the types of sophisticated decision support that are the eventual goals of this research. That is, an understanding of complex process behavior, which includes procedure-driven human intervention, is essential for providing real-time advice both to management and to human operators. In short, the model described in this section serves as a theoretical prerequisite for decision support in process control. We return to this issue in Section 7.

## 4.1. IndiÕidual knowledge structures

Explicit in the model are two sets of planning structures made necessary by the imposition of organizational procedures. The first set is comprised of Ž . 1 the goals, plans and actions of the procedure itself — based largely on the procedural model in Pople’s EAGOL system 12,13 — coupled with 2<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Ž . the ad hoc reactive plans of the procedure executor Ž . i.e., the operator , which can lead to actions taken outside of the directives of the procedure. The second set of planning structures is comprised of a decomposition of the executor’s reactive plan into Ž . 1 the restoration plan seeking to achieve, maintain or reestablish a management goal, and 2 theŽ . implementation plan, which seeks to carry out the restoration plan in the context of procedure constraints. The implementation plan focuses on the nature of the violation, if any, and the rationale underlying the action s taken in the restoration plan. The goal ofŽ . the implementation plan is to avoid any adverse consequences that might arise as a result of actions taken to maintain the important goals of the system.

The model is comprised of constituents of both procedural and human planning, which include:

v the p-goal hierarchy — the goals and actions Ž . i.e., steps or directives represented by a procedure.

v the procedure pathway s( ) — an extrapolated series of steps through the procedure leading to a specified goal in the hierarchy. Depending on the state of the system, there might be several alternative pathways through a procedure at a particular point in time.

v the m-goal hierarchy — the operational orŽ management. goals held by individual human operators. An important assumption is that, in a process control environment, the operators goals are to achieve management’s goals.

v the r-goal hierarchy — the ad hoc restoration goals and actions generated by operators in response to actual or anticipated m-goal violations.

The structure and purpose of the goal hierarchies are consistent in some aspects with Rasmussen’s 14<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> abstraction hierarchy framework, which has been proposed for a number of task domains, including those pertaining to complex systems and process control environments. Vicente and Rasmussen 23<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> note that humans use an abstraction hierarchy to reason through problems, first establishing broad goals that subsequently serve as constraints on future actions. Both the abstraction hierarchy and the goal hierarchies developed here provide a basis for recognizing goal violations. In an abstraction hierarchy, goal violations are defined as violations of normalcy constraints imposed by the hierarchy. Similarly, the m-goal hierarchy provides a set of constraints on normal system processing that are used to recognize system failures. However, the hierarchies also differ in an important way. The abstraction hierarchy is a functional description of a plant, and as such is not intended to convey actions or operations. By contrast, the p-goal and r-goal hierarchies are action hierarchies, in that they prescribe a set of operator activities in response to an observed goal violation. The action hierarchies developed here thus are congruent with the current focus on operator behaviors, specifically those dealing with procedure violations.

The current model provides a series of linkages between the goal hierarchies, which it uses to navigate from a violated management goal in the m-goal Ž hierarchy to a set of potential restoration actions in. Ž the r-goal hierarchy , to the procedure step s corre- . Ž . sponding to the restoration action s — if any. TheseŽ . linkages allow the model to anticipate whether man agement goal violations and associated restoration actions might cause an operator to deviate from the procedure.

Fig. 3 shows a portion of the p-goal hierarchy for a procedure. Goals are indicated by labels such as achieÕe, attempt and obtain, while methods for attaining those goals are indicated by labels such as direct and check. For example, ‘Engage BIT’ is an action intended to achieve the intermediate goal ‘attain-additional-flow’, which in turn contributes to stabilizing coolant level in the pressurizer ‘press-Ž level stable’ , and ultimately contributes to establish-. ing adequate charging.

An actor in turn might justify execution or avoidance of a procedure step, particularly in the context of a condition violation, by attempting to support or refute the goal-based assumption linked to a procedure step.

## 4.3. Process model

The knowledge structures have been incorporated within a computer model written in LISP and implemented on a Sun workstation running the Unix operating system. As shown in Fig. 5, the computer model essentially is comprised of two sub-models: Ž . 1 a plan generation model, which constructs a set of possible operator behaviors, in the form of plans and actions that an operator might execute within some type of procedural constraint, and 2 aŽ . plan induction model, which uses the planning space constructed by the plan generation model as the basis for explanation and prediction of operator behaviors that might arise.

The plan generation sub-model is invoked by a system event, which is a change in the status or behavior of some environmental parameter — e.g., a loss of coolant flow or an endless loop in a procedure. When a system event is observed, the plan generation model constructs restoration plans and implementation strategies that might be invoked by the operator in reaction to the event. Two general sets of ad hoc plans, each forming an indication of the possible intentions of an operator, are produced by the plan generation model.

The first is the set of alternative program pathways that might be navigated by an operator in pursuit of either program or management goals. Because a pathway is comprised of both procedural actions and the conditions required for their execution, the pathway defines an operator’s programbased options as well as the specific nature of any violation — i.e., violation of a condition, independent action, and so on — required in order to achieve a goal.

Implementation plans consider the values of the various decision variables, and are carried out within the constraints imposed by the constraining procedure. ## 5. Model behavior and evaluation

In the empirical studies described in Refs. 17,18 ,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> several teams of operators were presented with scenarios related to two specific types of plant emergency, each of which was created using a high-fidelity plant simulator mirroring an actual plant control room. One of the scenarios involved a type of incident termed an interfacing system loss of coolant accident Ž . ISLOCA . An ISLOCA is a type of loss of coolant accident Ž . LOCA that occurs when coolant escapes from a nuclear plant subsystem, primarily through a leak or a break in the piping. An ISLOCA typically is accompanied by a reactor trip, or shutdown, which in turn leads operators through a prescribed set of emergency operating procedures. An ISLOCA is a serious development within the plant, and if unresolved could lead to an inability to maintain proper temperature and pressure, and possibly to a core meltdown. Because of this, and because IS-LOCAs often are difficult to recognize, locate and correct, NPP operators are provided with a series of written procedures intended to guide them through the resolution process.

The monitoring, interpretation and prediction capabilities of the model were tested against a number of simulated emergency scenarios presented to operators. This section begins by describing both the operators’ and the model’s behavior against a particular incident in the ISLOCA scenario: i.e., a component failure that prompted operators to violate a procedure condition in order to restore an important m-goal. The model’s performance is then evaluated in the context of the ISLOCA and two additional scenarios.

## 5.1. OÕerÕiew of the ISLOCA scenario

The ISLOCA scenario is characterized by operator violation of a procedure condition. NPP procedures typically are comprised of a series of steps that include a default action to be performed if a condition is true, and perhaps an alternative action if the Ž . condition is not true termed a ‘response not ob-Ž tained’, or ‘RNO’ action . If an observed action does. not conform to the system state indicated by the condition, then the action will have violated the condition, and therefore the procedure as well. The ISLOCA scenario structured events so that operators were required to violate the condition for a procedure in order to resolve the incident. The ISLOCA itself caused a compensatory increase in the rate of charging i.e., coolant flow, to the point whereŽ . charging flow was at its maximum level. This in turn was a violation of an established m-goal, which is to maintain charging flow at some level below its maximum so that operators will have some flexibility in increasing the flow when that becomes necessary. Consequently, the operators were seeking some means of addressing the management goal violation, and therefore chose to realign flow through the BIT. This realignment essentially invoked a restoration method Ž . r-method in order to restore the violated charging flow management goal to within acceptable boundaries.

Notably, the procedure does not permit the operators to take this action. The procedural action causing realignment of flow through the BIT is associated with the RNO directive of the applicable procedure. However, plant conditions at the time required execution of the default action rather than the RNO action. The default action did not allow use of the BIT, and therefore did not, in the operators’ view, address the current goal violation. Therefore, they chose to violate the procedure by executing the RNO action, despite its lack of conformance with current conditions.

## 6. Implications for decision support in complex systems

The management of a complex system, such as an NPP, requires human operators to monitor and respond to the behavior of hundreds of parameters — coolant levels, pressures, valve positions, flow rates, and so on. Because of this, much of the empirical study of NPP operations has focused on the general cognitive abilities and limitations of operators, with an eye toward limiting the contribution of human error to critical incidents and emergencies through human factors engineering and improved ergonomics <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 2,5,20,22 . Other studies have focused more closely on the detailed cognitive processes of operators, with the intention of reducing human error through cognitive modeling and improved computer-based decision support 17–19,25 . These latter studies provide<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> an empirical and conceptual basis for this research, since they investigate the same issues of situation assessment, planning, and procedure navigation necessary for the development of an intelligent DSS in this domain.

Decision support in this type of environment must consider a who in the organization is a decisionŽ . maker and therefore in need of decision support, and Ž . b what are the types of information required for effective decision making. With respect to the first consideration, decision making in a complex system typically is distributed between the organization’s management and the operators who act on behalf of management’s goals and directives — as indicated in policy and procedure manuals. As such, decision support is required by both parties. By constructing a situation assessment and set of possible response plans in real-time, a DSS can help to guide operators through problems for which static, written procedures may be inadequate. Management, in turn, is provided a type of control by proxy, whereby organizational goals and intentions can be communicated to operators through the computational model. The model’s situation assessment and associated planning framework also provides a context within which the DSS can anticipate or predict operator actions and then explain subsequently observed actions within the same context. In real-time, on-site management could use the information about anticipated actions to communicate organizational preferences and preempt undesired actions. A post-mortem explanation of operator actions, in turn, could help management Ž .a to adjust control mechanisms as needed — i.e., to facilitate organizational learning — and b to Ž . educate operators regarding appropriate actions under observed conditions.

The types of information required for effective decision support in this domain reflect the distribution of knowledge across both the physical and human elements of the complex system. Knowledge of the human operator within the DSS is particularly important because of the critical role of the human operator in the overall generation of system behavior. The operator influences system behavior through various manual interventions, at times interrupting or modifying a causal chain of events occurring as a result of physical processes. For example, an increase in pressure in a vessel above a certain threshold might be expected to cause a cascading series of system failures — unless the operator takes a compensatory action, such as opening a relief valve. A cognitive model of the operator, including his or her goal and planning structures, allows a DSS to anticipate and understand these types of interventions, and to provide an interface that facilitates effective communication with the operator.

This notion of decision support in complex systems, which models the distributed nature of the task environment, is consistent with a more general philosophy of knowledge-based systems in support of knowledge workers KW . Wiig 24, p. 73 , forŽ . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> example, suggests that effective KBS support requires Asufficient correspondence between the concepts, associations, mental models, conceptual frameworks, objectives, and driving forces that the KW employs to perform his or her work.B A properly structured KBS combines this disparate knowledge into a repository accessible to individual decision makers, and in doing so provides a common point of reference that promotes collaboration 4, p.<sup>w</sup> 94 . This approach to decision support, while impor-<sup>x</sup> tant to KBS deployment in general, is especially important in the dynamic, distributed environment of complex systems, and is reflected in the model developed in this research.

## 7. Summary and conclusions

This research indicates that distributed knowledge in complex systems can be formalized and modeled, and used as a basis a for predicting and understand- Ž . ing interventions, and b for determining which Ž . interventions might or might not be appropriate given a situation. As noted, written procedures alone are an important source of complex system knowledge. That is, because the majority of procedure-constrained interventions are procedure-compliant, those interventions can be predicted and explained through simple extrapolation of procedural pathways and navigation through the hierarchy of procedural goals. In fact, most of the actions performed by the operators in the ISLOCA simulations fall within this definition.

Non-compliant interventions, in turn, are predicted and explained through consideration of other sources of system knowledge. As shown in the example of operator and model behavior above, the process entails modeling the impact of system events on management goals, and then assessing each of the compliant and non-compliant means of maintaining or restoring impacted management goals. Although clearly not deterministic — individual operators might choose simply to follow a procedure even when doing so jeopardizes management goals — the model of goal interactions nevertheless provides a situational context amenable to procedure deviations.

The model also provides a context for operator rationalization and argumentation in defense of chosen actions. In arguing that Adoing it on max charging isn’t the way to be goingB Ž . Fig. 2, line 3 , the operator reveals the output of an reasoning process formed from the hierarchy of goals and methods influencing the original decision to deviate from the procedure. The model in turn considers the state of the complex system within the context of a set of decision variables, and then anticipates an implementation strategy in which a decision is made and defended.

Because procedures do not determine behavior — even in the highly proceduralized domain of NPP operations — the findings described here are potentially applicable to other procedure-driven organizations and environments. In addition to the complex systems described earlier 8,15 , the model also is<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> applicable to decision making in complex social systems, such as Mergers and Acquisitions regulations 21 . Invariant aspects of the model across the <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> domains include: a the use of procedures and pro-Ž . cedure goals to understand and anticipate behavior, Ž . b the influence of system events and management goals on decisions to deviate from procedures, and Ž .c the notion of an argumentation strategy based on existing reasoning structures. In each of these respects, the same essential components of the model are used to explain and predict behavior in each domain.

In a more general sense, the theoretical model developed in this research provides a contextual understanding of process constituents and relation ships that is required for decision support. An intelligent monitoring DSS, containing knowledge of the physical plant, management goals, procedures, and operators, could provide structured, defensible guidance to operators when procedures become inadequate. An understanding of operator intentions, in the form of inferred restoration plans, would allow the DSS to provide context-sensitive advice to an operator, either when asked or voluntarily, where part of the context is knowing what the operator is trying to accomplish. The DSS ultimately would help operators to construct correct plans, in conformance with the overall goals of the organization — although not necessarily in conformance with current procedural directives.

In conclusion, our research establishes a framework for an explanatory model of organizational knowledge and behavior in environments where procedures are an important influence on system behavior. The theory embodied in the model has general implications for human–computer collaboration in complex systems. In a general sense, by exploring how and why actors sometimes feel compelled to work outside of imposed procedures, this study lays the groundwork for an improved computationally based method of procedure execution — one that is capable of engaging in a collaborative problem-solving dialogue with a human decision maker.
