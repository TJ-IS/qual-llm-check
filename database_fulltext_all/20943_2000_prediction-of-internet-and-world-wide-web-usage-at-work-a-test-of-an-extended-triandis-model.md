# Prediction of Internet and World Wide Web usage at work: a test of an extended Triandis model

Waiman Cheung <sup>a,)</sup>, Man Kit Chang <sup>b</sup>, Vincent S. Lai <sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Decision Sciences and Managerial Economics, The Chinese UniÕersity of Hong Kong, Leung Kau Kui Building, Shatin, Hong Kong, People’s Republic of China

## Abstract

The Internet<sup>r</sup>World Wide Web WWW has significantly impacted every facet of operations in organizations. AsŽ . information systems, including Decision Support Systems, are rapidly moving to the Internet platform to facilitate remote access and group cooperation, it is crucial to understand the adoption process of the Internet. This paper studies various factors affecting Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW usage in working environments. Previous studies on Internet or WWW usage have adopted the Technology Acceptance Model TAM as their research framework. As such, important factors, such as facilitatingŽ . conditions and social factors, which we consider important in explaining the use of the Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW, were left out. Therefore, a more comprehensive theoretical model is constructed for this study by modifying the Triandis social psychological model based on the results of previous studies. Factor analysis and multiple regression were used to analyze data collected from 241 questionnaires. The results support our modified model. Facilitating conditions and social factors are confirmed as the two most important factors affecting Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW usage. Other significant factors include perceived near-term consequences and perceived complexity. All new paths that we have added to the original Triandis model are found to be significant. The proposed model should provide a valuable alternative theoretical basis for technology adoption studies in the future. q 2000 Published by Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Triandis model; Internet and WWW; Technology adoption

## 1. Introduction

The Internet and World Wide Web WWW haveŽ . impacted every facet of our lives: communication, entertainment, social activities, shopping, etc. For organizations, the Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW has created a host of opportunities, ranging from support for existing work practices to business process reengineering, as well as the creation of new business possibilities. A recent American Internet User Survey 7 has shown<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> that 77% of American adults agree that online services have made them more productive at work. Moreover, Decision Support Systems, particularly group DSS, have rapidly adopted the Internet to facilitate remote access and cross countries collaborations. Companies, therefore, should be more closely examining how they can promote the use of the

Internet and the WWW so they can realize future productivity gains. The focus of this study is to investigate the factors affecting Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW usage.

A few recent studies have investigated factors affecting the use of the Internet or WWW 2,15,23 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> The theoretical basis adopted in all these studies is the popular Technology Acceptance Model TAMŽ . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> 8,9 or its extensions. Davis et al. 9 , a pioneer in using the TAM for technology adoption, dropped the subjective norm from the TAM because of its uncertain theoretical and psychometric status. Consequently, subsequent applications of the TAM, including the above-mentioned studies on Internet or WWW adoption, seldom included variables relating to the social environment. However, since the Internet and the WWW are currently much discussed topics, we believe social pressure plays an important role in explaining their use.

Another factor that we believe to be important is facilitating conditions i.e., the availability of theŽ necessary resources and supports while using the Internet . This factor has been excluded in existing. studies to explain Internet and WWW usage. In order to provide smooth access to the Internet, a large number of elements must work closely together. These elements include the internal network, connections to the external Internet, as well as a variety of Internet software, such as email clients and Web browsers. Therefore, we decided to adopt a more comprehensive social psychological model, the Triandis model 26,27 , which includes both the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> social factors and facilitating conditions constructs, to explain Internet usage in this study.

The Triandis model has been successfully used in a number of technology adoption studies 4,19,24,25 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> However, certain important relationships were neither included nor tested using this model. Nevertheless, these relationships, such as how complexity affects short- and long-term consequences and how social factors affect were significant in previous studies using other theoretical frameworks. Therefore, we attempted to incorporate these relationships into the Triandis model to form a new research framework for the investigation of factors affecting the use of the Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW at work. The results may prove insightful for organizations hoping to encourage more use of the Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW.

In the next section, we discuss our research framework based on the modified Triandis model. Then the methodology of the study is described in Section 3, followed by the results of our analysis in Section 4. Discussion and conclusions are presented in Sections 5 and 6, respectively.

## 2. Research framework: a modified Triandis model

Triandis 27 proposes a theoretical network relat-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ing attitude and behavior to many constructs, such as culture and biological factors. Fig. 1 depicts a subset of Triandis framework similar to the one adopted in Thompson et al. 24 that is relevant to this study.<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> According to the Triandis model, the probability of performing a given behavior is determined by a number of factors: 1 the habit of performing the Ž . behavior, 2 facilitating conditions, and 3 inten-Ž . Ž . tion. Habit is a behavior that is or has become automatic in a given situation. Facilitating conditions are important because, as stated by Triandis 27 ,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> even when an intention is strong and a habit well established, if some objective obstacles exist, such as geographic and resource limitations, then no behavior will occur. In his model, behavioral intention is determined by social factors, affect, and the perceived consequences of performing the behavior.

In this study, we applied the Triandis model to predict Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW usage at work. Since we were interested in the prediction of actual behavior, AintentionB was dropped from the model. Instead, we studied the direct effects of social factors, affect, and perceived consequences on current behavior. Habit was also excluded from our investigation because in a cross-sectional study measurements for habit and actual behavior are the same.

Fig. 2 shows the extended model that we used. Three aspects of the perceived consequences, namely, near-term consequences, long-term consequences, and complexity were investigated. In an attempt to extend the work of previous studies, we also investigated how the perception of complexity in using the Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW affected the perceived near-term consequences, perceived long-term consequences, and affect. Moreover, the impact of social factors on affect was also investigated. The relevant constructs in our research model are discussed in more detail below. Our hypotheses are summarized in Fig. 2 and the figure is referenced in the following discussion.

## 2.1. Affect

Many behaviors have positive consequences, but are unpleasant to perform. Thus, the first determinant of Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW use is affect. Affect is the direct emotional response to the thought of a behavior and is referred to as the Afeelings of joy, elation, or pleasure, or depression, disgust, displeasure, or hate associated by an individual with a particular actB <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 27 . Existing findings relating affect to usage in information systems were mixed. Pare and Elam´ <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 19 , studying the adoption of the personal computer Ž . Ž PC , concluded that anxiety a negative affect, their operationalization of the affect construct has a nega-. tive relationship with usage. Other studies have not found any significant relationship between affect and usage 4,24,25 . Thompson et al. 24 explained their <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> insignificant results by stating that the use of PCs may not evoke any strong emotional response. However, their measurement of the affect construct had a very low reliability Cronbach AlphaŽ . <sup>s</sup>0.61 , which may attenuate correlation with other factors. On the other hand, enjoyment, which we consider similar to affect, has been found to have a positive relationship with PCs 14 and Internet usage 23 . In addition,<sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> affect has also been found to be significant in predicting other behaviors e.g., Ref. 29 . Therefore,Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. we hypothesize that affect will have a positive impact on Internet and WWW usage.

The perceived consequences of a behavior represent another factor influencing behavior. Perceived consequences are the certainty that a behavior is having some consequences with value to the performer 27 . $$

$$

where C<sup>s</sup>the value of the perceived consequences, $P _ { \mathrm { c } _ { i } } = \mathrm { t h e }$ subjective probability that a particular consequence will follow a behavior, $V _ { \mathrm { c } _ { i } } = \mathrm { t h e }$ value of that consequence, and n<sup>s</sup>the number of consequences that a subject perceives as likely to follow a particular behavior.

This construct is similar to the construct of perceived usefulness PU in the TAM 8 . It is alsoŽ . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> consistent with the expectancy theory of motivation that states that individuals choose a behavior based on the desirability of the rewards 24 . Therefore, the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> higher the perceived value of the consequences, the higher the likelihood that a person will perform the behavior. In this study, both $P _ { \mathrm { c } _ { i } }$ and $V _ { \mathrm { c } _ { i } }$ were measured and the scores for near-term and long-term consequences were calculated using the above equation. Akin to Thompson et al. 24 , three dimensions<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> of perceived consequences, i.e., near-term consequences job fits , long-term consequences, and com- Ž . plexity, are included in our model.

## 2.3.1. Near-term consequences

Perceived near-term consequences in our research context are defined as the extent to which an individual believes that using the Internet and the WWW can enhance the performance of his<sup>r</sup>her job. The impacts are on the individual’s current job. This concept closely resembles the PU concept in the TAM. Studies have consistently found that this factor is an important determinant of intention and behavior 4,8,9,16,19,24,25 . Thus, we predict that<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> there is a positive relationship between perceived near-term consequences and Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW usage.

## 2.3.2. Long-term consequences

In addition to the near-term and more direct impacts on an existing job, knowing how to use a technology also has a longer-term impact on career development. Therefore, the perceived long-term consequences of using information technology represent another dimension of the perceived consequences investigated in this study. Long-term conse quences include the increased flexibility to change job or increased opportunities to have a more meaningful job. Past findings regarding this factor were mixed. Thompson et al. 24 found a positive rela- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tionship between long-term consequences and usage, while for the inexperienced user, Thompson et al. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> 25 found this lacking. Chau 6 , when predicting the behavioral intention to use MS Word and Excel, found long-term consequences to have a significant impact. Consistent with the Triandis model, we hypothesize that perceived long-term consequences are positively related to Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW usage.

It should be noted that the nature of perceived complexity as a consequence is quite different from perceived shortterm and long-term consequences. That is to say, the perception of the complexity of using the Internet and WWW is put under the construct of perceived consequences because it is an AinternalB factor. Perceived complexity is the opposite of perceived ease of use EOU in the TAM. Gefen and Keil 12 too have found that EOU does not affect<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> usage directly but is positively related to PU. Thus, perceived complexity should also have a negative impact on both perceived short-term and long-term consequences.

Igbaria et al. 14 , in their study of the determi-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> nants for the use of computer technology, have found that perceived EOU has a significant direct effect on perceived enjoyment. That is to say, users feel happier using a technology that is easy to use.

Thus, the impact of complexity is multifaceted. In our model, we hypothesize that perceived complexity negatively affects usage, perceived short-term and long-term consequences, and affect.

## 2.4. Facilitating conditions

Except for total volitional behaviors, people require necessary resources and support to perform a behavior. According to Triandis 27 , a person may <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> have the intention of doing something, but be unable to perform it due to a geographic barrier that prevents the act from being realized. To deal with this situation, Triandis includes a construct, facilitating conditions, to predict behavior. Facilitating conditions refer to Aobjective factors, ‘out there’ in the environment, that several judges or observers can agree make an act easy to doB ŽRef. 27 , p. 205 . In<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> . the context of our study, the facilitating conditions include hardware, software, network connections, etc., that allow individuals to access the Internet when they want to, as well as the supports provided by a company to facilitate the use of the Internet and the WWW at work.

Past findings on the relationship between facilitating conditions and usage were mixed. Thompson et al. 24 found no significant relationship and a later<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> study, Thompson et al. 25 , even found a significant<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> negative relationship between PC usage and facilitating conditions for both the inexperienced and experienced users. On the other hand, Pare and Elam 19 ´ <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> found a positive relationship between the two variables. Thompson et al. 24 , in explaining the in- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> significant relationship, suggested that this may be caused by the fact that only one dimension of the facilitating condition, namely, technical support was measured. They suggested that other aspects of facilitating conditions, such as ease of access, should also be included in the measurement. Heeding this suggestion, we have included ease of access to the Internet in our operationalization. Although using the Internet or the WWW may not be very difficult from the user’s point of view, it is more complex than facilitating PC use from the supporting personnel’s point of view. Thus, the effect of support on Internet and WWW usage may be more significant than for PC usage. Therefore, we predict that there is a positive relationship between Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW usage and facilitating conditions.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Subjects

A total of 290 questionnaires were distributed to part-time diploma and MBA students in two universities in Hong Kong. Two hundred and forty-seven questionnaires were returned. Six of the questionnaires were unfinished, thus, they were discarded from further analysis. This yielded a total of 241 usable questionnaires a net response rate of 83% .Ž . The respondents’ use of the Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW was voluntary. They came from a wide variety of industries; 15% from manufacturing, 14% from the financial sector, 6% from transportation, 11% from wholesale and retail, 4% from construction, 17% from public services, and the rest from other industries. Fifty-five percent of the respondents were male, and 70% of them held undergraduate degrees or higher. The mean age of the respondents was 32.6 with a standard deviation of 6.5 years. Around 60% of the respondents were assistant managers or departmental managers, 20% were professional staff, and 9% were technical support staff.

## 3.3. Data analysis

The first step in our analysis was to assess the psychometric properties of the measurement scales. Factor analysis was used to demonstrate the discriminant validity of the measurement scales. Items should load higher on its associated construct than on any other construct. The internal consistency and reliability of the scale were assessed using the Cronbach alpha. To test the proposed model, a series of ordinary least square multiple regressions were performed on the collected data.

## 4. Results

This section presents the statistics and analytic results of the research model and hypotheses. The validity and reliability of our instrument are assessed first.

## 4.1. Construct Õalidity and reliability of the instrument

Discriminant validity was assessed using factor analysis. The 28 items measuring the seven constructs in the research model were subjected to principal component factor analysis. Eigenvalues and Scree plot were used to determine the number of factors to be extracted. A seven-factor structure was suggested using the criteria of an eigenvalue greater than 1, and the extracted factors account for 71.63% of the total variance. A varimax rotation was also performed. The resulting factor loadings are shown in Table 1 with all factor loadings less than 0.4 suppressed. All items loaded onto the expected factors as they were originally designed. Factor loadings were all higher than 0.5 on its own factors and, therefore, each item loaded higher on its associated construct than on any other construct. As suggested by Hair et al. 13 , a factor loading higher than 0.35 <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> is considered statistically significant at an alpha level of 0.05 for a sample size of 250; thus, all the items loaded significantly on its own factor. This supported the discriminant validity of the measurement. Only one item, the third item of near-term consequences, has a cross-loading of 0.42 on the long-term consequences factor. Since it still loaded higher on its own factor, we decided to keep it.

Cronbach’s alphas were used to assess the internal consistency reliability of the three scales extracted. As shown in the last column of Table 1, the reliability coefficients ranged from 0.83 to 0.91, which was significantly higher than the acceptable level of 0.7 for this kind of study Ref. 18 , p. 265 . TheseŽ <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> . results confirm that the scales used are both valid and reliable.

## 5. Discussion

We have extended the Triandis model and, subsequently, applied it to explain the use of the Internet<sup>r</sup> WWW. According to Triandis 27 , there is evidence <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> to suggest that the relative significance of different factors on the ability to predict intention and actual behavior varies depending on the type of behavior, social situation, and person. When comparing our findings to those of previous studies that also used the Triandis model to make prediction about technology adoption, we find that different exist.

In our study, we find that facilitating conditions have a significant positive impact on Internet usage, consistent with the Triandis model. This result is quite different from previous studies that used the same model for PC usage. For instance, facilitating conditions are found to be nonsignificant in Ref. <sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> 24 , to be the least significant factor in Ref. 19 , and to have a negative impact in Ref. 25 . Signifi-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> cantly, more external support is necessary from an organization’s IS department to provide smooth access to the Internet relative to the support needed to facilitate PC use. These supports include establishing and maintaining connections to the Internet, making sure the Internet server and the gateway work together, setting up e-mail accounts and web browsers, providing multimedia viewing facilities, etc. Thus, our study finds that the facilitating conditions are the most important determinant of Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW usage.

We also find that social factors are the second most important determinant affecting the use of the Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW. In addition, we have modified the model to investigate the effect of social factors on affect and, subsequently, found a significant positive effect. This means that when the social environment encourage the use of the Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW, users feel more positive about using it.

Despite the effect of affect on Internet usage is found to be insignificant, it does not mean it is unimportant. The dynamics of how factors affect the formation of behavioral intention seems to be different from how the factors affect current actual behavior. Past studies have shown that affect does not significantly affect current behavior 4,14,24,25 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> However, it does have a significant impact on the formation of intention 9 . Moreover, applications of<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> the TRA in other behavioral domains have also shown that social norms do have a positive effect on the formation of attitude 5,22 . Therefore, social<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> factors may affect the formation of intention directly, as well as indirectly through its positive impact on affect. Further research is encouraged to study the effect of these model variables on intention.

We find that perceived near-term consequences, which measure the belief that the quality and efficiency of the users’ job will be enhanced after using the Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW, have a positive effect on Internet usage. This result is consistent with previous studies 24,25 . However, its relative importance<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> among other factors in determining usage is not. While previous studies have shown that near-term consequences are the most important determinant for PC usage 24,25 , it ranks third in our study. This<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> difference in the ranking order verifies the claim of Triandis that the weights of the various factors affecting intention and behavior differ in different contexts. Using the Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW seems to be a trendy activity right now for both work and pleasure; thus, social pressure seems to supercede near-term consequences as a determinant of Internet usage.

As mentioned before, affect does not have a significant effect on usage. Because both social factors and near-term consequences have significant positive impacts on Internet usage, the combination of social pressure and the usefulness of Internet<sup>r</sup> WWW may lead to increased use, whether the users enjoy becomes less important. This may explain why social factors are the second most important determinant of Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW usage in our study.

Conversely, perceived long-term consequences do not have a significant effect on Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW usage. Our finding is different from that of Thompson et al. 24 , but is the same as Thompson et al. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 25 found for inexperienced PC users. Long-term consequences mainly measure effect on career development. Since most of our respondents are not in the field of IS<sup>r</sup>IT, the Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW generally only plays a supportive role in their jobs. Consequently, long-term consequences become insignificant.

The complexity of Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW technology does discourage users. Complexity, which is the opposite of the concept of EOU found in other studies, is found to have a significant negative effect on Internet usage, near-term consequences, long-term consequences, and affect. Thus, complexity not only directly affects usage, it also affects usage indirectly through near-term consequences.

The results of our study further support previous findings in that the effect of perceived EOU is mediated by PU 9,12 . That is to say, the less <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> complex the IS application is, the more useful it is perceived by the user to improve the quality and efficiency of work, which, in turn, increases the usage of the IS application.

In addition, the negative impact of complexity on affect implies that users are both more interested in using the Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW and enjoy it more if it is easy to use. This result shows that the effect of complexity is multifaceted. Thus, designing an easy to use system is an important objective in all information system development. Moreover, future studies using the Triandis model should consider the relationship between different measures of perceived consequences. In particular, when complexity is involved, the mediating effect of usefulness should be considered.

There are a number of theoretical contributions and insights from the current study. Firstly, our results provide evidence to support Triandis’s claim that the relative importance of different factors in predicting behavior varies depending on different behavioral contexts. This has important implications for future technology adoption study. Take social influences as an example. Davis et al. 9 dropped <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> this construct from the TAM because the technology they studied i.e., the word processor is fairly per- Ž . sonal in nature; thus, its usage was not affected by social influences. However, as more and more applications move to the Internet and provide group supports, social influences may become a dominant factor in determining usage. Thus, further research should systematically investigate the relative contributions of different factors in determining usage for various type of information systems. This information will help practitioners better promote different kinds of information technology. Secondly, we have successfully integrated various empirical findings, such as the effect of complexity and social factors on affect, into a well-founded social psychological theory, the Triandis model. The extended model is further supported by the research results. This extended model provides a fuller picture and a better understanding of the interplay between the various determinants of system usage. This will further elucidate the technology adoption process. Finally, a number of the measurements that we developed in this study, such as social factors and affect, have demonstrated desirable psychometric properties. This can provide a useful instrument for further technology adoption study.

Our results have a number of implications for organizations, which are trying to promote the use of the Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW for job related activities. First of all, creating a supportive social atmosphere is very important. Supervisors should support and encourage their subordinates in their use of the Internet and the WWW. Besides social support, physical support is also very important. Companies should provide sufficient technical support and easy access to the Internet for their employees. Finally, to help speed the adoption of the Internet<sup>r</sup>WWW and to increase its use, companies should communicate the positive impacts of using the Internet on an employee’s future job prospects. Companies should also provide more learning opportunities for employees in the form of training courses and experience sharing. These programs are consistent with the findings of Roger 21<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> that result observability speeds up diffusion and adoption of innovation.

In general, our results show that the extended Triandis model is useful to enhance our understanding of technology adoption. It provides an alternative theoretical framework to that of the commonly used TAM and TRA, and it also enriches our research repertoire.

However, there are a few limitations in our study, which should be taken into account when interpreting the results and implications. Firstly, our respondents’ education level is on the high side. Although it has been said that Internet users tend to be highly educated, as the user base rapidly expands, future research should include subjects with various levels of educational attainment. Secondly, in the original Triandis model, social factors, affect, and perceived consequences affect actual behavior through the formation of behavioral intention; therefore, behavioral intention should be included in future studies.

The limitation of using self-reported measures of usage is that method variance may contribute in part to the correlation between two constructs. However, the effect may not be substantial and it is hard to estimate. According to Bagozzi and Yi 3 method<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> variance accounts for only moderate amount of the variance in construct measures. As Fiske and Campbell 11 suggested, <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Amethod and trait or content are highly interactive and interdependent. We may have to settle for the practice of studying trait-method unitsB. Moreover, self-reported usage measures have been used to operationalize system usage when objective measures are not available in information system research 8,16 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

## 6. Conclusions

Existing studies on Internet usage are mostly based on the Technology Acceptance Model TAM and,Ž . consequently, do not include the two constructs, facilitating conditions and social factors, which we believe are particularly important. In this paper, we have adopted and extended the Triandis model to form a new theoretical model to study Internet<sup>r</sup> WWW usage. Two hundred forty-one data samples were collected through questionnaires from part-time diploma and MBA students who represent a wide range of professions and industries. The data analysis results support the extended model.

The construct of facilitating conditions is found to be the most important factor affecting Internet and WWW usage, followed closely by social factors. Perceived near-term consequences of using the Internet and WWW also have a positive impact on usage. The results further support our extension of the original model in that 1 social factors and complex-Ž . ity significantly affect the formation of affect, and Ž . 2 complexity has a direct negative impact on Internet usage, as well as an indirect impact through its negative effect on perceived near-term consequences. The findings here provide insight on how to promote the use of the Internet and the WWW in working environments.

Compared with past studies using the Triandis model for technology adoption, our study provides evidence to support Triandis’s claim that the weightings of factors affecting intention and actual behavior change across different behavioral contexts. Furthermore, our extension to the model has been tested and has gained support. It should provide a better theoretical foundation for future studies in technology adoption.
