# Evaluation of a decision support system for credit management decisions

S. Kanungo<sup>)</sup>, S. Sharma, P.K. Jain

Department of Management Studies, Indian Institute of Technology, Delhi Hauz Khas, New Delhi 110016, India

Accepted 1 April 2000

## Abstract

This study was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of a decision support system DSS for credit management. ThisŽ . study formed a part of a larger initiative to assess the effectiveness of IT-based credit management processes at the State Bank of India SBI . Such a study was necessitated since credit appraisal has emerged to become a critical sub-function inŽ . Indian banks in view of growing incidence of non-performing assets. The DSS that we assessed was a credit appraisal system developed in Quattro Pro<sup>w</sup> at SBI. This system helps in the analysis of balance sheets, calculation of financial ratios, cash flow analysis, future projections, sensitivity analysis and risk evaluation as per SBI norms. We used a strong quasi-experimental design, called the Solomon’s four-group design, for our assessment. In our experiment, managers of SBI who attended training programs at the SBI training college, were the subjects. The experiment consisted of measurements that were taken as pre- and post-tests. An experimental intervention was applied between the pre-tests and the post-tests. The intervention, or stimulus, consisted of DSS training and use. There were four groups in our experiment. The stimulus remained constant as we took care to ensure that the course contents as well as the instructors remained the same during the course of our experiment. Two were experimental groups and two were the control groups. All four groups underwent training in credit management between the pre- and post-tests. Results from our research show that while the DSS is effective, improvements need to be made in the methodology to assess such improvements. Moreover, such assessment frameworks, while being adequate from a DSS-centric viewpoint, do not respond to the assessment of a DSS in an organizational setting. In our concluding section, we have discussed how this evaluative framework can be strengthened to initiate an activity that will allow the long-term, and possibly the only meaningful, evaluation framework for such a system. q 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. ## 1. Introduction

Our current research forms part of an initiative to assess the effectiveness of a decision support system Ž . DSS . Specifically, we are dealing with a DSS for credit appraisals in the State Bank of India SBI .Ž . SBI is the largest commercial bank in India in terms of business, infrastructure and service. It has 8800 domestic and 60 overseas branches. The bank has a history of over 200 years. Although the cumulative expenditure on information technology is Rs 129.60 crores US\$1Ž . <sup>f</sup>Rs 42.00 and 1 crore<sup>s</sup>10 million until 1994, information systems for the entire credit cycle proposal evaluation, loan advancement, loanŽ monitoring, follow-up and closure, policy formation and generation do not exist yet. Hence, we had to. assess a system of limited scope.

In this paper, we provide the background for the research, the research methodology, results and discussion. We conclude the paper by highlighting some of the major findings and their implications in terms of managerial relevance and DSS research.

Our objective was to evaluate the effectiveness of a DSS for the credit appraisal part of the credit management function. This study was necessitated since credit appraisal has emerged as a critical subfunction in Indian banks. Inadequacy of proper credit appraisal is reflected in the fact that 17.3% of the total bank advances have been declared as non-performing assets NPAs 25 . To evaluate such a DSS,Ž . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> the ideal framework would have been to benchmark decision-making processes and their effectiveness as well as the level of NPAs before the implementation and use of DSS. Following DSS use, the same measurements should have been taken at regular intervals.

## 2. Literature review

While it is expected that a DSS will improve decision quality, prior research indicates that the effect of DSS use on managerial decision performance is still uncertain. In some instances, decision performance improved, while in others, decision quality deteriorated, while in still others, there was no effect 5,29 . This is not entirely unexpected since <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> there are multiple variables, issues and contexts that interact when we set out to assess DSS use.

These factors can be categorized as DSS factors <sup>w x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 12 , task factors 7,19 and user characteristics 6,24 . An example of the DSS factor is the suggestion that DSS effectiveness and efficiency have contradicting effects on DSS use 17 . The task factor has to do<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> with the nature of tasks at hand. For instance, the impact of a DSS is enhanced when the task at hand is characterized by an increase in the number of alternatives to choose from 35 . Examples of user<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> characteristics include experiences, data preferences, intuition and effort 33 . The interaction among these<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> three factors related to DSS use also influences the effectiveness of the DSS. Prior research 3 also<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> suggests that while user factors do impact DSS success, user-situational variables are more important than individual differences. The implication for our research was that we decided to account for all the three factors — DSS, task at hand and user characteristics.

We had to rule out a long-term and elaborate procedure because the institutionalization of the DSS in SBI is still a long way off. Moreover, since the DSS covered the initial steps of the credit cycle, we opted for an evaluative framework that could provide us with more controls in all the three dimensions of DSS, task and user. At the time of initiating this research, many bank managers had expressed a need to demonstrate the utility of such a system using objective criteria. In order to develop the theoretical foundations for this research, we analyzed descriptions and case studies of similar systems 1,14,<sup>w</sup> 15,18,20,30,31 employed in other studies. We used<sup>x</sup> well-established constructs based on prior research <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 5,10,11,17,21,22,26,28,36 .

One of our areas of emphasis was measurement. Since we wanted to quantify our assessment, we used multiple criteria for evaluating the DSS. While some researchers 28 advocate the use of the ana- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> lytic hierarchy process AHP methodology for such Ž . multi-criteria evaluations, we decided to adopt experimental controls to assess the DSS on multiple criteria. This approach is supported by a valuable research base 11,21,22,26 . While we had to rule out a<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> comprehensive evaluative framework suggested by Evans and Riha 10 , we also realized that by adopt-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ing a rigorous quasi-experimental approach, we would be addressing a research gap in empirical evaluations of DSSs.

Mykytyn’s 21,22 research on users’ perception<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> of DSS training and its influence on DSS success prompted us to use an extended form of the control group. This is because our experimental intervention ended up being a combination of training in credit management and in DSS use.

The DSS that we employed was a credit appraisal system developed in ‘Quattro Pro’ at the SBI. This system is described in Appendix A. This system helps in the analysis of balance sheets, calculation of financial ratios, cash flow analysis, future projections, sensitivity analysis and risk evaluation as per SBI norms. The system provided for data entry at the clerical level and allowed inquiry and analysis at the managerial level. The subjects in our experiment are managerial users. We define a user as the person who utilizes DSS for assistance. This excludes data entry operators from the purview of ‘users’. In our evaluation of the DSS, we studied whether use of the specific DSS affected the effectiveness of credit decision-making. We also studied the relationship between perceived importance of DSS and the outcomes of using the system. We used this AwellacceptedB DSS as we wanted to avoid contaminating our results with resistance to use<sup>r</sup>change aspects. We believe, based on past research 16 , that users<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> do respond positively to meaningful systems.

The research builds on 1 the generic findingsŽ . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 5,29 , 2 gaps in the existing state of knowledge Ž . and 3 the need for an empirical assessment frame- Ž . work for DSS especially the ex-ante approach suggested by Gardner et al. 11 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

## 3. Research design

## 3.1. Solomon’s four group design

The experiment consisted of measurements that were taken as pre- and post-tests on a group of managers from SBI. An experimental intervention was applied between the pre-tests and the post-tests. The intervention, or stimulus, consisted of DSS training and use. There were four groups in our experiment. The stimulus remained constant as we took care to ensure that the course contents as well as the instructors remained the same during the course of our experiment. Groups 1 and 3 were experimental groups while groups 2 and 4 were the control groups. All four groups underwent training in credit management between the pre- and post-tests. Fig. 1 presents this research design schematically.

Group 1 was administered the stimulus and tested both pre and post. In other words, members of group 1 underwent training in credit management as well as in DSS use and subsequently followed it up with using DSS for responding to the case problem. Group 2 was tested pre and post but without any stimulus. This meant that members of group 2 underwent training in credit management but were not exposed to the DSS. Hence, they were not able to use the DSS to respond to the case problem. Group 3 was administered the experimental stimulus without a pre-test and group 4 is only post-tested. The experimental design permits four meaningful comparisons. If DSS use alone were to improve the ratings of variables used as measures for credit decision-making, we should expect the following outcomes.

The study was conducted at SBI Staff Training College at Gurgaon on four groups of officers drawn from all over India working in SBI and its associate banks.

## 3.2. Questions and hypotheses

The major research question addressed in the experiment was: Does the use of the DSS improve the quality of decision-making pertaining to credit? The subsidiary questions that make up the overall research question were:

Ž .i Will the use of DSS help organize the structure of thoughts in a manager’s mind while attempting to assess a proposal?

Ž . ii Will the use of DSS speed up the decision process?

Ž . iii Will the DSS change the focus of the manager from a subjective to objective outlook? Ž . iv Does the use of DSS change the risk rating perceptions of the manager?

Ž . v Does the DSS increase the learning of the manager?

Ž . vi Does the DSS increase perceived confidence of the manager in solving the problem? Ž . vii Does the DSS, increase satisfaction levels in solving a credit problem?

Ž . viii Does the DSS improve the credit appraisal capabilities of the manager?

All hypotheses associated with these questions are one-tailed with an expectation of improvement in the decision parameter e.g. speed, organization of Ž thoughts, managerial learning, etc. except for risk . perception for which we did not have any reason to Ž hypothesize either way ..

## 6. Conclusion

It is clear that there is persuasive evidence to support our expectation that the use of DSS improves the decision-making in many respects. Consequently, it is reasonable for us to conclude that the DSS is effective. While that may be so, the findings are not entirely satisfactory. For one, we did not find anything that was startlingly unexpected or extraordinary. In this context we have three sets of conclusions to report. The first relates to the relevance of the exercise. The second conclusion deals with enhancements to such an exercise. The last set of conclusions is concerned with extensions to such an exercise that are organizationally relevant.

As regards the contention of proving the obvious, we believe that in the world of work, the AobviousB does not necessarily work. To be more precise, while we all agree that the use of DSS leads to, or at least should lead to, better decisions and, in some longer run, in bottom-line benefits, many DSS implementations belie such expectations. In SBI itself, we have been able to identify reasons for this outcome. In a multiple case study exercise undertaken by the authors on the use of information systems in banks, it came out that many banks, including SBI, have not yet committed themselves to implementing DSSs citing the reason of lack of evidence of their benefits. A previous attempt at SBI to formalize a DSS failed since, in that instance, a personal DSS was expected to evolve into an institutional DSS. The confluence of such perceptions and beliefs has inhibited the growth of DSS in banks. Hence, the essence of our evaluation was that if we could show that this DSS works in controlled conditions, there is no reason why it should fail to deliver benefits in actual work settings, if the work environment is made conducive to DSS use. The essence of institutional evaluation will be on answering a question like Ahow did loan evaluation decisions affect the delinquency rate?B There is a significant delay between loan proposal evaluation, loan advancement and the repayment of, or the default on, a loan. Loan monitoring and follow-up activities can also intervene to influence the outcome variable inŽ this case, the delinquency rate . From such a view-. point, it appears that quasi-experiments, like the one we conducted, are appropriate for initial assessment and evaluation. Once a DSS is implemented, it is important to continuously refine and enhance such systems with the intent of improving their organizational utility. In the absence of continuous refinement, the DSS may loose its utility and may have to be retrenched or be replaced by another system.
