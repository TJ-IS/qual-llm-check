# Virtual field experiments for a digital economy: a new research methodology for exploring an information economy

Beomsoo Kim <sup>a,</sup>\*, Anitesh Barua <sup>b</sup>, Andrew B. Whinston <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Information and Decision Sciences Department, The University of Illinois at Chicago, 601 South Morgan Street MC294, Chicago, IL 60607-7124, USA

<sup>b</sup>MSIS Department CBA 5.202, The University of Texas at Austin, Austin, TX 78712-1175, USA

Accepted 6 January 2001

## Abstract

Many researchers are concerned about the appropriateness of traditional research approaches and methodologies in the analysis of a digital economy. Using the Experimental Digital Economy (EDE), a new technology infrastructure that we have developed for a digital economy, we propose a new research methodology, a virtual field experiment, which makes it feasible and effective to test research hypotheses with the desired level of experimental controls and to probe successful business strategies in a real business world. Three summaries of research on a digital economy, like the efficiency of a digital market, the effectiveness of digital markets (posted-price markets and auctions), and the impact of quality certifications, address the implications of virtual field experiments. <sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Virtual field experiments (VFE); Field experiments; Laboratory experiments; Digital economy; Digital goods; Research methodology; Experimental Digital Economy (EDE)

## 1. Introduction

Laboratory experiments, in economics, are often used to understand and analyze various types of the economy. We focus our research on a digital economy, a special economy where all goods and services traded are in digital format. As with physical products, a digital product also needs an outlet or marketing channel through which it can reach the consumer from the producer or seller. The most common marketing medium by which digital products are sold on the Internet today is through a storefront in the form of a web site. Most companies selling software, information, etc., provide an Internet shopping storefront (outlet) as a part of their web pages, allowing consumers to purchase their products directly. Unlike traditional marketing channels, the cost of establishing a storefront on the Internet is relatively minimal and, thus, one can find almost every manufacturer providing its services on the Internet. As a result, consumers are provided with a richer set of choices in terms of prices and features.

Many researchers are concerned about whether traditional research approaches and methodologies are appropriate in analyzing the digital economy. Thanks to advances in information technology and the distinctive features of this new economy, we propose a new research methodology for a digital economy, a virtual field experiment, which makes it feasible and effective to test research hypotheses with the desired level of experimental controls and to probe successful business strategies in a realistic business setting.

In traditional laboratory experiments, a researcher defines preference and value for each participant in the way that these sets of information shape the whole economy and the experiments. Subjects in a normal market experiment trade a commodity that has no intrinsic or use value. That is, subjects engage in paper transactions. The commodity is given value by the experimental rules governing the redemption values for buyers and the terms on which sellers can acquire the units they wish to sell. By allowing for the preferences and values of participants known to the researcher, it is possible to conduct more powerful tests of various hypotheses; this is not available in typical field experiments. Conversely, the result of a laboratory experiment becomes applicable if and only if the business environment meets pre-defined conditions that can be very artificial and focused.

In the virtual field experiment, participants’ preferences and utilities for each product they produce or consume are naturally determined in parallel to the real business environment. Unlike field experiments, under which it is nearly impossible for a researcher to collect this level of information, researchers using this new approach can observe or monitor participants preferences and utilities, using proper measurements and technological infrastructure over an extended period of time. That is, this new approach provides powerful instruments to a researcher, allowing hypotheses to be tested while not sacrificing parallelism to the real business world. The term design parallelism is used to indicate closeness to natural situations rather than closeness to the theories that economists have devised.

## 2. Review of experiments

This section reviews research methodologies that can be used to explore and understand the digital economy. For research on market environments and mechanisms, commonly adopted methods include theoretical modeling, laboratory experiments and field experiments that are motivated by industrial organization issues.

Despite the contrast between the relative simplicity of the laboratory experiment and the complexity of most naturally occurring markets, there is a wellestablished tradition of experimental research in the field of industrial organization (IO) or market organization. Thus, we start this section by reviewing these experimental approaches.

## 2.1. Laboratory experiments in economics

A laboratory experiment is an experiment conducted in a setting expressly prepared for the research control [22,32]. It is theoretically possible to achieve total control of effects that are due to extraneous variables. In reality, the control that is achieved is not total, but is thorough relative to field experiments conducted outside a laboratory.

Experimental economics has made significant progress by recognizing the key role of incentives and by emulating real-world conditions in the laboratory. In contrast to experiments in psychology that focus on individual perception and behavior, experiments in economics create markets to test economic theories that have been developed through the application of microeconomic theory.

A market or institution is created by specifying appropriate rules and incentives. Experimental economics deals with a system involving multiple relationships, rather than addressing a problem in isolation from the environment, within which phenomena of interest are likely to be observed. These features allow experiments in economics to create settings that are similar to their real-world analogs. Since Chamberlin’s first market experiment in 1948, experiments in market organization and competitive equilibrium seek to test theories of exchange that can be formulated in terms of the aggregate supply and demand curves of the market. These experiments are induced in the laboratory using Chamberlin’s technique of giving each buyer and seller a reservation price for each unit they demand or supply.

Similarly, most laboratory experiments in economics are based on simplified and focused experimental settings. Subjects in a normal market experiment trade a commodity that has no intrinsic or use value [34].

Subjects get cards on which they find a price or a value of a homogeneous product. The commodity is given value by the experimental rules governing the redemption values for buyers and the terms on which sellers can acquire the units they wish to sell. Buyers make money by buying units from sellers and reselling to the experimenter according to a predetermined redemption value schedule. The difference between the purchase price and redemption value is a profit that the buyer keeps. Sellers make a profit by purchasing units from the experimenter at a predetermined cost schedule and selling to the buyers at a given marketplace. Sellers, like buyers, keep profits, which are the difference between the sales prices and purchasing costs. That is, subjects engage in paper transactions with predetermined incentive mechanisms.

In this institution, experimenters observe the average market price and its convergence. These experiments have been conducted to test theories and models that are based on the assumptions of the traditional market in physical goods. In fact, most of the empirical research in the social sciences attempts to explain the phenomena of the traditional market, which trades physical goods using traditional marketing channels. Unlike traditional economic theories and models, these laboratory experiments observe and deal with real human beings. Conducting these experiments requires the researcher to make fine judgments about which differences among human beings matter for research purposes, and which differences do not matter. This simplified and parsimonious setting allows experiments to test the theorem without many complications. However, we find that the implications are too difficult to be applied in a real business environment, even though these experiments are parsimonious. Many laboratory experiments involve an oversimplification, which again leads to the concern about external validity [13,34].

In addition, these experiments in economics deal with homogeneous goods. In our experiments, we analyze the market efficiency for homogeneous goods and customized goods. Especially in the digital economy, customized goods are more readily available than in the traditional manufacturing and retailing environment. Choi et al. [4] and Gilmore and Pine [14] address the fact that information technology and flexible work processes allow suppliers to customize goods or services for individual customers at a relatively low cost. This change toward customization in the industry and the introduction of the new digital economy cause us to question whether theories and business models in the traditional market are valid in this new environment. The impact of other new features such as negligible marginal costs and quality uncertainty in the digital economy also need to be explored. Traditional theories and assumptions in economics need to be checked and tested in this new business environment because the underlying principles on which this new economy is based are different.

The main advantages of using laboratory experiments are replicability and control. Replicability refers to the measure of other researchers’ capability to reproduce the experiment and thereby verify the findings independently [7]. To a certain degree, lack of replicability is a problem of any observational inquiry that is non-experimental, such as field experiments or field studies. However, relatively inexpensive, independently conducted experiments allow replication. Control is a measure of the manipulability of experimental conditions so that observed behavior can be used to evaluate alternative theories and policies. An absence of control in natural contexts presents critical data problems in many areas of economic research [7,22]. That is, laboratory experiments allow a dramatic reduction in the number of auxiliary hypotheses involved in examining a primary hypothesis or proposition.

## 2.2. Field experiments

A field experiment is another approach that a researcher can adopt to explore a digital economy. In fact, it would seem logical to follow a laboratory study with a field experiment, i.e., a test in a restricted natural setting. On the other hand, as Davis and Holt [7] argue, field experiments are usually expensive, and rare as a consequence. For instance, Lucking-Reiley [30] claims that his research is one of the first attempts to use field experiments on market mechanisms, even if the efficiency of auctions is one of the research topics that economists have been working on for the last several decades.

Compared with laboratory experiments, which look for parsimony and design parallelism to the theoretical models, a field experiment is a study conducted in as natural a setting as possible. That is, a field experiment uses data or an information set collected from the real world or businesses where some variable is directly manipulated in an otherwise naturally occurring process. Once a location is selected, express care is taken so that its naturalness is not changed any more than is necessary to conduct the research. This method of experimental design is widely used in the fields of agriculture, physics, biology, botany, marketing research, and industrial production [12,32].

One advantage of this approach is that subjects often do not know they are involved in a research project when they participate in a field experiment. Thus, the collected data are more likely to represent the realities of the marketplace. The control of extraneous variables is almost always substantially less than with laboratory experiments. It is not possible to control all the extraneous variables in a natural business environment, since the variables are too numerous and too complex. This can reduce the costs of experimental design and administration substantially.

On the other hand, this added realism of the field environment incurs several costs. First, field experiments tend to be extremely expensive, both in terms of administration and planning costs and in terms of the real costs of disrupting normal economic activity. Second, many of the key environmental variables may not be well controlled, and these elements may change from the time or location at which one treatment is conducted to the time or location of the others. Third, this methodology works well when a researcher looks for an explanation for a trend or searches for the overall effects of a factor. However, this approach often cannot show an impact of a factor on a certain incident because the researcher often cannot isolate one factor from the others. Therefore, a trade-off exists between increases of external validity and decreases of internal validity.

In addition, field experiments on market mechanisms with naturally occurring data are extremely rare because the information held by businesses is rarely observed or often is considered confidential. Moreover, field experiments for digital products in a digital economy are almost impossible to execute, because the digital economy is not yet fully developed to its potential in the real business world. The call is for a new approach that allows researchers to test results from laboratory experiments in a more realistic environment, like the Experimental Digital Economy (EDE), for virtual field experiments.

Another stream of research methodology that can be applied to the analysis of electronic markets is computational economics, established by Kydland and Prescott [27]. By constructing and calibrating a model economy, a researcher collects a set of data which reflects the real-world economy or businesses and which is hardly available, because of the complexity of real business processes. Proper analysis of data allows him/her to understand real-world phenomena and events. Over 2 years, analogous to the approach of computational economics, Barua et al. [2] used the E-Cube system to collect a realistic dataset and to analyze the characteristics of the new market environment and business strategies for digital products. Barua et al.’s research, in which 18 companies and 200 buyers from various locations participated, simulates realistic electronic commerce marketplaces and monitors each company’s and buyer’s behavior and strategies. These attempts and experience laid the groundwork for the virtual field experiment and help us to understand the features of the digital economy. When researchers cannot collect natural data from real-world business environments, well-designed virtual field experiments allow them to test theories and find in-depth knowledge on the market structure.

By contrast, the Experimental Digital Economy (EDE) is a technological infrastructure that facilitates elaborate large-scale virtual field experiments for electronic commerce, while it provides participants with a realistic digital economy. That is, we use the digital world, EDE, which has realistic features and components, to test models and propositions of the digital economy, in which over 700 buyers and sellers trade information goods. The EDE allows us to capture the detailed features of market phenomena, as well as the simple outcomes of trading information goods, like prices, demand, supply curve, and consumer surplus.

## 2.3. Experiments in information systems and marketing

Much of the research on electronic commerce [3,18,33] as a new marketing channel focuses on estimating the customer base, developing customer profiles or demographics, determining what people are buying and how much they are spending, or identifying features of electronic commerce as a new channel. In addition, most studies and experiments pursued in marketing [21,28,31] are observing and analyzing traditional market environments, which deal with physical goods rather than information goods.

Experiments in information systems focus on issues such as measuring the effectiveness of a computer– user-interface [20], understanding individual human behavior, improving group decision-making processes, and searching for better investment strategies in financial markets. Most of these experiments follow the guidelines provided by experiments in psychology. As Lee et al. [29] suggested, experimental research in Information Systems (IS) uses singlerelationship models which do not address the richness of the underlying phenomena. Single relationships cannot adequately capture the essence of a problem of interest, and can even lead to misleading results. By contrast, realistic models are usually represented by a system of relationships, which are naturally more difficult to formulate and analyze [2]. Our foundation for the digital economy model involves a much higher level of complexity and realism than the relationships that are typically investigated in these laboratory experiments.

A concern in typical laboratory experiments is the external validity of these experiments, as Patzer [32] and Reinig et al. [36] addressed. Studies using experiments in marketing or IS suffer from the usual limitations of laboratory experiments. Because these experiments use ad hoc groups of undergraduate or graduate students with little intrinsic motivation, many researchers feel that the results or data may not be representative of the real world.

In the virtual field experiment that we propose, subjects play their roles for an extended period of time while making business decisions to maximize their own goals, participating in a more natural, realistic, and interactive environment. That is, participants are engaged in this experiment for at least one semester, in contrast to 10 or 15 minutes of participation in a typical laboratory experiment. Another concern is the incentive for the participant to perform as a consumer or seller. Incentive mechanisms, including the use of salient rewards, are designed and placed so that these participants will reveal their preferences truthfully.

To our knowledge, there is no experiment in the fields of marketing or information systems that focuses on the market mechanisms and economic structures of the digital world and the digital economy in such a virtual setting. In addition, most experiments pursued in information systems are observing and analyzing behavioral aspects of subjects rather than testing economic models and mechanisms. Our experiments, discussed in the following section, focus on testing theories and propositions in different environmental settings for a digital economy, which are comparable to the features of the real business world.

In this section, we overviewed research methodologies, like laboratory experiments, computational economics, and field experiments often adopted for the analysis of market mechanisms. These research methodologies were also contrasted with virtual field experiments. Experimental research in information systems and marketing has also been summarized to understand research trends in relevant research disciplines and to demonstrate the need for virtual field experiments. In the following section, we briefly discuss the background and the reasons behind developing this new research approach rather than adopting either laboratory experiments or field experiments.

## 3. The advent of the virtual field experiment

As in Table 1, laboratory experiments align with a highly controlled environment that is important for high internal validity. Field experiments align with a realistic or natural setting that is important for high external validity. The ideal experiment, that permits both a completely natural setting and total control to eliminate alternative explanations of the results, has not been readily available. Thanks to remarkable technological advances in recent years and the successful quest for effective research methodologies, we propose a new research approach, which can be more effective than typical laboratory or field experiments. That is, this virtual field experiment can increase reality in laboratory experiments and also elevate control in field experiments.

## 3.1. Search for realism: external validity

This new methodology guides research that takes place in a ‘‘natural setting.’’ The researcher manipulates the independent variables while trying to control the most important intervening variables. The researcher then measures the effects of independent variables on dependent variables by systematic observation of human subjects. This systematic observation of human subjects distinguishes this virtual field experiment from most field studies even if the natural setting in this experiment, one parallel to the real world, ensures high external validity for this new methodology.

Another advantage of this research approach is that it permits discovery of new market phenomena that researchers want to explore and the hypotheses they wish to test later on while running a set of experiments. That is, virtual field experiments not only allow the testing of research hypothesis on a digital economy, but also help in finding interesting applications of business strategies.

Because market behavior is sensitive both to individual preferences and to details of the structure of the institutional arrangement, the experimenter must avoid contaminating these variables with experimental procedures. That is, the appropriate set of incentives, choices, and rewards ensures successful testing of research hypotheses and facilitates the search for interesting business activities, in virtual field experiments as in traditional experiments.

## 3.2. Technological breakthrough: Experimental Digital Economy

A typical experimental infrastructure or environment, which uses chalkboards and computer systems with traditional file systems to share information for transactions and to record actions in the marketplace, has limitations in terms of scalability and tractability. That is, the traditional technical infrastructure, which supports market experiments, cannot provide the same proper set of information that needs to be collected when these experiments take place outside a laboratory. On the other hand, the EDE available at http:// ede.bus.utexas.edu, offers thorough monitoring and tracking of over several hundred participants’ activities in distributed network environments without losing the controls required for hypothesis testing (Fig. 1).

This improvement due to a technological breakthrough is comparable, if not more significant than, Smith’s [39,40] improvements on earlier competitive market experiments in economics, such as those done by Chamberlin. One of the major contributions Smith made in experimental economics is that he identified the problems of institutional settings and came up with a new setting that provides proper incentives and mechanisms to convey the purpose of the experiments. He first conjectured that the decentralized trading that occurred as students wandered around a room was not an appropriate institutional setting for testing received theories of perfect competition; instead he made information available to all participants by posting all bids, offers, and transaction prices on a blackboard. That is, these pieces of information become public information using a blackboard, so he was able to accomplish the goal of testing competitive market institutions. Using this double-auction experiment, he demonstrated that competitive markets could converge to efficient and competitive outcomes. By the same token, the technological infrastructure we developed helps us test theorems and propositions in a realistic environment with a complex set of rules and processes, as an extension of laboratory experiments, where field experiments are almost impossible to pursue.

In this marketplace, the products, as well as offers, bids, transactions, the transition of a final product from a seller to a buyer, and the interactions among participants are all in digital format. The number of participants in this type of approach usually exceeds several hundred and interactions among participants take place almost every minute. As a result, each run of an experiment generates such a large amount of data and information that the traditional institutional setting cannot handle or process it. For instance, this infrastructure for a virtual field experiment has to process huge amounts of information. That is, the dataset which an experimenter is interested in collecting and analyzing, as well as the transactions and activities, like e-mail, forums, electronic chat, and advertising on the Web, which participants want to use in a real business world, need to be promptly processed. Advances in telecommunications, like the World Wide Web, CGI, Java, HTML, and Internet technologies and object-oriented web-based database technologies, such as DB2, and Oracle, provide fundamental resources to build such an infrastructure, which can handle this large-scale, distributed and long-term experiments with customized information and data (Fig. 2).

3.3. Challenge in education: distance and active learning

Over the last few years, we also found that this virtual field experiment is a valuable educational instrument. Due to the difficulty of customizing lectures for individuals and the high costs of commuting to schools in a traditional school system, virtual universities and distance learning programs are rapidly growing and are getting more attention from both educators and the public [23,37,41]. This trend demands revolutionizing the way information is delivered in education, which is mostly done in the form of classroom lectures. Moreover, advances in information technologies at a phenomenal speed provide new means to create, collect, store, share and disseminate knowledge or information [23,38,41].

Typical distance learning programs or virtual universities that take place on the network use static, dynamic or customized web pages, forums, chat rooms, archives, and search engines. For instance, popular online learning platforms such as Blackboard from Blackboard, WebCollege from TNL, WebCT from WebCT, PearsonCMS from Pearson Distributed Learning and other off-the-shelf software systems allow teachers to share off-web information with students online; however, these platforms do not provide features that are required to carry out the learning-by-doing education in information technology-related courses. On the other hand, in this virtual field experiment setting, students taking various levels of information-technology-related courses can learn, collaborate, and teach each other voluntarily through a competitive market setting while using other resources available in a typical distance learning program. In other words, we push distance learning into a higher ground, turning it into an active distance learning through market mechanisms. In this new environment, some students can get additional information and the help necessary to do their assignments, while others who know the subjects better or are more interested in the issues explore this subject by themselves in depth and offer the results of such study in the form of digital products to those taking introductory level courses. For instance, students taking advanced-level information technology courses organize and prepare summaries on an information technology, or write up interviews with business managers, generating digital products. Along with typical resources, like books, journals, and lectures, these digital products become a new resource that students taking introductory level courses can use to do their assignments. They can browse, purchase, and consume this knowledge or ideas at their convenience.

Because participants in these experiments trade and consume real digital products, which are produced by a group of participants, participants (information consumers as well as digital goods’ producers) can enhance their knowledge on a specific field of interests. This, along with other monetary rewards, also works as an incentive for participants to involve themselves in active learning, instead of learning information-technology contents in a passive manner as in a typical educational setting. Our research done in Spring 1999 and Spring 2000 shows very positive feedback from students for this active way of learning through various marketplaces, a method complementary to traditional classroom education.

For both faculty and students, this environment opens up an additional communication channel that was not available in the past. With the arrival of the Internet to educational society, more and more faculty members use the Web as an additional means to communicate with their students and are looking for more effective ways of using the web environment in education. The technological infrastructure devised for virtual field experiments serves this function as an efficient and effective communication and teaching channel. The details of this technological infrastructure are discussed in the following section.

## 4. The environment for virtual field experiments

This section discusses how to construct a virtual field experiment environment including a technological infrastructure, procedures, and experimental control. A proper technological infrastructure is the key enabler of this type of research. Thus, we start this section with the technological infrastructure, the Experimental Digital Economy (EDE), which we developed for this virtual field experiment.

The Experimental Digital Economy, the implemented system of a digital world, creates and provides an electronic marketplace where digital products are produced and exchanged among students in telecommunications, electronic commerce, and database management courses in several universities, such as the University of Texas at Austin, George Mason University, the University of Southern California, ITESM-Mexico City, and the University of Illinois at Chicago. This technological infrastructure is developed on an IBM workstation, using DB2, CGI Perl scripts, and Java. The EDE provides the services which are required for this digital environment, such as a digital warehouse (digital inventory), virtual storefronts (outlets), accounting systems with bundling and pricing features, a virtual community (internal groupware tools), document publishing and filtering systems, customer service, auction systems, electronic banking, and security services.

Our experiment drew on students at different institutions and at different levels of expertise in order to simulate a market in which information goods were sold. Students who were sellers created ‘‘digital products’’, which provided students who were buyers with information helpful to them in their classroom assignments. In this case, the digital products were teaching aids on the subject of information technology itself, helping ‘‘buyers’’ better understand technologies on data communications, databases, and programming languages. Among the most popular items in this marketplace were brief descriptions of a technology, summaries of interviews with managers in information-technology-related businesses, and sometimes also software. ‘‘Buyers’’—often students in introductory level classes—then used such goods to help them on their assigned projects and to gain knowledge on certain technology issues.

Each seller produces digital products and sells his/ her own products. Thus, in our research we use the term ‘producer’ interchangeably with ‘seller’ or ‘supplier.’ Specifically, sellers collect data from the Internet, literature reviews, interviews with IT experts, and surveys. In turn, sellers process and customize this data to provide value-added products to buyers. This value-adding process provides incentives for the buyers to buy digital goods from the mall rather than studying or collecting information by themselves on the Internet. That is, effective customization and value-adding processes are key survival factors for the sellers. This realistic setting with its value-added chain provides us with rich information on Internet businesses and allows us to model the digital economy.

The advantages of a digital economy are (1) ease of information access, (2) interaction richness, and (3) low information and interaction costs [1,2,4]. In turn, these are the major reasons why consumers prefer the digital world to the traditional environment or market mechanisms. In this new environment, users can access relevant information dispersed throughout the Internet without traveling extensively in Webspace. That is, in this digital economy, users can access information more easily than in a traditional economy. This ease of information access is provided by organizing information using intellectual indexing schemes, good search engines, dynamic linking, open connectivity standards, and new market transaction mechanisms. Moreover, meaningful and rich interactions among the users of this environment allow users to overcome the barriers of space, time, and media/document formats in interacting with others. For instance, some of the EDE technological features, such as multimedia support, interaction management, asynchronous and synchronous communication support (forums, announcement boards, and virtual chat rooms), and dynamic linking capability, provide rich interactions among the users of the system. In addition, by combining features and capabilities in a coordinated fashion within this unique system environment (EDE), we increase the level of information access and interaction richness while reducing the user’s costs of information access and interaction.

The electronic (virtual) community setting and market information boards deliver information that participants need to know. In most economics experiments in the past, these pieces of information were written on a blackboard in the classroom or laboratory [39,40]. Using this experimental setting, Plott and Smith [35] compared various trading institutions, and they show the inefficiencies that can result from a rule that limits the ability of traders to deviate from posted prices. Sharing trading information, such as prices and quantities available and sold, on a blackboard is an efficient way of communication; however, this limits students’ participation in experiments by distance and their convenient times. To overcome these limitations, Plott, Smith and others also developed technological infrastructures, using computers and LAN technologies, where participants in computer labs with LAN use computers to share the information necessary for trading. The scale of these LAN-based laboratory experiments is still limited to the size of the facility provided by the experimenter. That is, the number of computers and network connections in each laboratory determine the scale of an experiment. Plott’s experiments, available on the Internet (http://eeps.- caltech.edu/), are replicas of these LAN-based experiments with flexibility of participation. That is, using this infrastructure, students can participate in short-run experiments at their convenient locations and times. Conversely, the EDE supports large-scale and long-run experiments in which several hundreds of participants use the system all over the world for their own benefit, using their own computers and Internet connections, unlike Plott’s experiments, which typically last for a few minutes or hours with a relatively small number of participants and with predetermined values and costs.

In order to run a large-scale and long-run experiment, an experimenter has to secure lots of computing resources and the voluntary participation of human beings for an extensive period of time. Thus, providing a realistic market environment with useful information and activities to get the attention and keep the participation of volunteers is a must rather than an option. Because the digital economy in the near future will be based on the Internet or a similar network environment, it is natural for us to use the Internet as a starting place for the virtual field experiments, rather than using one or multiple laboratory facilities. Moreover, using the Internet as well as computing resources, which have already been committed by the participants, allows us to keep the cost of providing physical facilities low, unlike the traditional laboratory environment where the experimenter has to provide all the computing resources.

With various control mechanisms in place, the EDE can accommodate experiments with high internal validity as well as experiments with high external validity. The combination of database and web-based communication technologies allows experimenters to test propositions which are greatly theoretical and require high levels of controls, and to apply these propositions tested in an abstract environment to a realistic experimental setting. In this realistic experiment, experimenters can observe complex interactions among controlled and uncontrolled variables and monitor strategic behaviors of subjects.

For experimenters, database technology provides tracking mechanisms for activities and events occurring in this virtual field environment, which is analogous to tape recording or close monitoring in field experiments. In addition, EDE uses database management systems to provide flexible data access, proper data control, efficient digital warehouses, convenient digital banking, and data security. Since database technology plays an important role in this new environment, we discuss a few more details of this technology.

Database technology (EDE uses IBM DB2 Universal Database) provides many benefits, compared with the traditional file systems that are used to keep track of data or events in most economics experiments. For instance, this database provides views and schemas that abstract a set of data from databases. The system can deal with a large number of buyers, sellers, products in various formats, and transactions. Using database management systems, we can organize datasets properly and utilize other database features, such as convenient access, security, backup, and recovery features. Using these database and system security features, experimenters can control participants’ access and limit each participant’s choices, if necessary.

Scalability of the database allows researchers to run experiments on a large scale. In addition, views and schemas on several levels help to analyze the dataset dynamically and provide valuable information to companies (sellers). These database features are very useful when the dataset is very large and experiments run for an extended period of time. For instance, views and schemas allow various types of users to share and access the data and database concurrently, while each type of users has a different set of choices and options. Experimenters can analyze a dataset, while subjects are making decisions and inform participants of the results of the analysis in order to monitor and measure the impact of these pieces of information. In return, experimenters or malls can provide market activities to subjects and help communication or information sharing among the subjects. Compared to traditional economic experiments, this is a more realistic and natural experimental environment, in the sense that businesses always monitor and observe market trends and other information when they make decisions. Experimenters may analyze the impact of this information on the sellers’ businesses, business strategies, market behavior, and consumer reactions. In addition, it is possible for researchers to come up with pricing models for this kind of information and test these models of information pricing and renting policies. This dynamic information service should be administered in a careful fashion so that up-to-date information on subjects’ actions does not distort or contaminate the dataset which each experiment is collecting and monitoring. Thus, dynamic communication propositions and other propositions can be properly tested without noise or disturbance in the dataset that others are interested in.

Database technology, specifically mirroring and parallelism, enables large-scale distributed experiments. Parallelism, which performs a database-related task, like retrieving a set of data from the database in parallel, speeds up the task. This technological feature is crucial when the scale of an experiment is very large and this helps subjects to communicate with each other and experimenters to monitor and analyze experiments. Mirroring (replication) allows experimenters to set up experimental infrastructure in various locations around the world without losing integrity while maximizing throughput of the experimental setting. For instance, EDE mirror sites in Chicago and Finland become the regional access points for users who want to buy and sell products in the EDE. The database replication technology enforces the integrity among the dataset in various locations. Thus, users will always retrieve the most up-to-date and accurate information at a faster access speed, even if users of the system access databases in various locations.

Participants use digital money (electronic cash provided by a digital bank) to trade products. Electronic cash is produced by a digital bank and is a very convenient medium for buying and selling goods. This lowers the transaction costs of trading digital goods and the inconvenience of the payment process. Based on their performance in various market settings, participants will get grades, cash prizes, award certificates, and best of all, acquire knowledge of various information-system-related technologies and gain experience in electronic commerce.

Digital market experiments begin with an experimental design, which seeks to control the environment, using the techniques of induced valuation, and to control the institution by defining the language and the rules under which subjects for each experiment will be allowed to trade digital goods and services. With these controls, we narrow the interpretation of inconsistency between predictions and observations, so that the burden of inconsistency is borne by the behavioral assumptions of the theory. When the experimental observations are consistent with a theory, we have our first evidence that the theory, which is implemented by the particular environment and institution that were used, has predictive power.

For this virtual field experiment with voluntary participants, an experimenter needs to collect participants’ demographic and other characteristics. Furthermore, it is recommended that such pieces of information be validated with respect to the target groups on which research results will be applied or used. This process will increase the explanatory power of this experiment for phenomena we want to explore in the digital economy or similar environments.

## 5. Applications of virtual field experiments

This section discusses research issues and results from virtual field experiments. First, we discuss developing research instruments for data collection in search of market efficiency and equilibrium analysis, obtaining market demand and supply curves, and deriving competitive equilibrium for each virtual market institution. Section 5.2 shows that a market institution of discriminatory auctions for digital goods lowers sellers’ profit if the customer base is equal for each market institution. However, market prices observed in both market institutions are higher than the equilibrium market price. In addition, relatively high transaction costs due to waiting time, search costs, pressure from peer consumers, and the discomfort level associated with using a relatively unfamiliar market institution discourage ordinary users from using an auction. As a result, transaction volume in an auction institution is substantially lower than in a post-price market unless this relatively new market mechanism appeals to a new group of consumers. Section 5.3 addresses the role and impact of quality certifications in a marketplace where sellers are not very well known to buyers. We found that quality certifications provided by a third party can improve the efficiency of a marketplace where the quality of products are unknown to buyers due to information asymmetry, or to a lack of a seller’s reputation.

## 5.1. Market efficiency and equilibrium analysis

Many researchers in economics show that ‘market institutions’ are the ideal means to exchange and allocate resources under the assumptions of perfect and symmetric information and perfect competition [5,42]. However, in the real world, we observe many instances in which these assumptions are not satisfied due to imperfect competition, incomplete information, transaction costs, and externalities. Likewise, we question the effectiveness of market institutions in the digital economy, i.e., whether market institutions in the digital economy permit efficient exchange and allocation of resources. In addition, we briefly discuss the approach and methodological details we have chosen. Because this is the first attempt to measure the effectiveness of a digital economy using a virtual field experiment, it is very important to address how to gather fitting information for this type of research.

One distinctive characteristic of digital products is the ease of customization due to the technological advances and infrastructure that sellers are using to produce digital products. On one hand, this gives great freedom of selection for buyers and may increase their utility and satisfaction. This also becomes a key for sellers to build a successful relationship with their customers. On the other hand, this is a big challenge for a researcher who wants to analyze market efficiency, because one assumption for market efficiency analysis is that products are homogeneous. This requires a categorization process in which products are grouped according to their shared characteristics and customers’ perceived valuation. Thus, we adopt a categorization technique based on the producers’ discretion. This categorization allows us to analyze supply and demand for each group of products. In addition, using buyer and seller surveys, we measure consumers’ demand (willingness-to-buy) and suppliers’ intention to sell (willingness-to-sell). Using these pieces of information which are rarely available in field experiments, we have managed to derive competitive market equilibrium and found that market prices for digital products are close to the competitive equilibrium. Many researchers argue that the Coase conjecture and earlier experiments showing that market prices are equal to the competitive equilibrium may not accrue in this new economy due to personalization or customization of digital products. However, we find that a competitive marketplace in a digital economy is still fairly efficient [25].

Another interesting finding from this research is that sellers with customization failed to capture the entire consumer surplus, countering the fear that sellers may extract the entire surplus from the market by using customization on the Internet. One major explanation for this phenomenon is that competition among the sellers prohibits them from charging prices according to customers’ demand for each product, since switching from one seller to another is not so difficult for the customers.

This market efficiency study leads us to in-depth research on various market mechanisms in the digital economy, including devising and understanding posted-price markets, auctions, and procurement systems. In the following subsection, we discuss another study, which compares a posted-price market with an ascending-price discriminatory auction.

## 5.2. Posted-price and discriminatory price auctions

We can compare various market mechanisms using a realistic business setting to test theories on the effectiveness of market mechanisms with a new set of environmental settings, such as digital products rather than physical products, low costs of information reproduction, ease of transfer, and network externality of information goods.

Market mechanisms, such as auctions, postedprice markets, and procurement systems, and various market rules and settings for each market may result in different market prices. For instance, sellers or mall providers can capitalize on consumers’ surpluses, using this intelligent pricing scheme if malls or sellers can understand consumers’ preferences and behavior precisely and personalize their products and services.

Using the traditional laboratory experiment approach, Davis and Williams [10] designed and ran a parallel series of double- and posted-offer auctions to test the market power. Market power is a measure defined by Tirole [42] as pricing above marginal cost. From this experiment, they observed higher market prices in the posted-offer auction than in the double auction. That is, the double auction yields higher market efficiencies than the posted offer auction [8– 11,19,24]. Further, the posted-offer auction allows much less interaction between buyers and sellers than is the case for the double auction [10]. Given that prices can rise above competitive levels in concentrated or monopolized posted-offer markets, other researchers [6,15 –17] consider mechanisms that may constrain market power.

Because posted-price markets and discriminatory price ascending auctions are the most popular type of market mechanisms on the Internet, our research centers on these two markets. We believe this research has many implications in the Internet world, where auctions have become one of the most popular market mechanisms, even if not much about these auctions has been known to businesses.

From this research, we observed that the average market prices in discriminatory-price ascending auctions are lower than the prices in posted-price markets. This is statistically confirmed with p and t statistics, and this result agrees with traditional experiments. We also observed 10% higher prices on average in postedprice markets. In explaining this result, this virtual field experiment offers additional evidence in addition to the argument from laboratory experiments. That is, traditional laboratory experiments explain this phenomenon by pointing to collusion among the bidders and the seller’s higher market power, while these experiments do not discuss the costs of participating in a different market mechanism since these given mechanisms are too abstract to study the buyers’ transaction costs. Moreover, this new approach offers a new explanation for the phenomenon through surveys done before and after shopping, monitoring voluntary participation in a marketplace. Because the cost of participating in an auction, the transaction costs and discomfort level are higher for auctions than for the posted-price market, the market prices in auctions are lower than those in posted-price markets [26].

## 5.3. Asymmetric information on product quality: a lemons problem

From the earlier experiments for market efficiency and market institution comparisons, we observed a ‘‘lemons’’ problem. In other words, substantially fewer trades were transacted than would be expected during the early period of the experiments. At this point in time, sellers were saying that it was very difficult to attract any business. Over an early few months this market does not perform its role of exchanging and allocating resources. The quality of a product is not observed by buyers, while a seller knows the quality of the product, because buyers must make purchase decisions based on very limited information about products. That is, the information set which is available to buyers is limited to the price, quantity, producer, and a brief product description prepared by each seller, even though information goods are generally considered experience goods that require the consumption of the product to understand the quality of it. In most cases, buyers purchased products after reviewing their short descriptions. In fact, surveys administered in Fall 1998, Spring 1999, and Spring 2000 show that product description is the most important factor when a buyer makes a purchase decision. For sellers, the cost of producing higherquality products is higher than producing a lowerquality product. Under this market environment, buyers have to take the risk of paying too much for mediocre products. That is, this information asymmetry often results in a winner’s curse where the winner of an auction pays too much for a product. Learning and adjustment over time are important phenomena in this situation. At a later period in the experiments, sellers who build a reputation and loyalty among pioneering consumers perform as we expected.

In this experiment, sellers’ products are reviewed by the professors and categorized in two types, high quality or regular quality. The regular-quality good is a product that can be obtained easily from other resources (Internet, magazines, class notes, etc.). High-quality goods are usually unique and customized information or software components that target the project goals effectively. Sellers produce high- or regular-quality products, where the cost of producing the high-quality product is greater than that of the regular-quality product. Buyers obtain greater value for the high-quality product, which is necessary to do their project and is not readily available elsewhere. Thus, buyers can get higher points if they include high-quality information. Thus, sellers can charge a higher price for a high-quality good than for a regularquality good.

Sellers are anonymous in order to minimize the impact of the customer loyalty on purchase decision. That is, sellers’ identities are not revealed during the whole experiment period. Buyers can preview a brief product description, but cannot trace who produced the product. For the control group, buyers make purchase decisions based on price, product description, quantity, and product format. In the treatment group, additional information on a product is available by quality certificates prepared by a third party. In the long run, a seller can build his/her reputation because of a known identity.

Laboratory experiments and theoretical models predict that sellers sell only regular-quality goods at high prices. The winner’s curse in the market prevails, if buyers have to buy at least one product. That is, buyers pay high prices for low-quality goods. Otherwise, there will be few transactions in the market.

Sellers sell both regular- and high-quality goods. However, prices in markets where there are no enforceable warranties and certifications will be relatively low, compared to the prices in markets with remedies for a potential lemon problem. In addition, we observed relatively low transactions in the market with no certification. In order to prevent this from happening, warranties or money-back guarantees are measures that sellers can exercise by themselves. These measures will cost sellers when buyers exercise them and have risks of moral hazard due to the fact that buyers still can return them although they have already consumed the product. On the other hand, certifications by a trustworthy third party can also signal quality to buyers, and sellers pay a fee for certification to the trusted party.

From this research, we found that certification is an important measure to improve market efficiency and avoid market failure especially for a market in its early stages of development. That is, both sellers and buyers can benefit from using quality certification provided by a third party or an intermediary when relationships among sellers and buyers such as trust or consumer loyalty are not fully pledged in the marketplace.

## 6. Conclusion

The selection of a best methodology for any particular research project is critical to the resulting quality and value of that project. The selection of the best research methodology should be determined within the context of research objectives.

This paper presents a virtual field experiment—an experimental research methodology for the digital economy—which is an extension of traditional experimental research in economics, and contrasts this new experiment with research approaches, like laboratory experiments and field experiments that are applicable for the analysis of the digital economy. We suggest that a virtual field experiment is a vastly effective approach, with enormous potential, for understanding and exploring a digital economy.

We also discuss a series of studies done using virtual field experiments. From the study on market efficiency, we found that outcomes in the competitive digital marketplace are close to the efficient market equilibrium as in the Coase conjecture. In addition, appropriate market mechanisms and improvisation may make the market more efficient and may lead to competitive equilibria in this new economy. In the study of market mechanisms’ comparisons, we found that customers retain higher surplus from trading products through auctions rather than through posted-price markets. We also show that the use of thirdparty quality certificates improves the effectiveness of a marketplace for digital goods. Likewise, we can use this virtual field experiment to explore various aspects of a digital economy and to probe into policy implications for real businesses.

In sum, this paper introduces a new approach, the virtual field experiment, that allows us to explore phenomena in a digital economy and test various propositions for many economic institutions at reasonable cost. This also summarizes the results from virtual field experiments that demonstrate the potential of this new research methodology.
