# A methodology for controlled empirical investigation of membership continuity and change in GDSS groups

Sathasivam Mathiyalakan

Quantitative Methods Department, School of Management, Syracuse University, Syracuse, NY 13244, USA

Received 14 September 2000; accepted 22 January 2001

## Abstract

Controlled laboratory experiments using monetary rewards tied directly to performance are detailed and illustrated, as part of a methodology for investigating how membership continuity and change affect group meeting performance, solution time, and productivity. Performance-based rewards were structured to induce subject attendance and to prompt subjects to contribute their best effort. A key element of our methodology is the ability to successfully conduct longitudinal studies. We illustrate the methodology in an example study of groups of size three and groups of size five. Experiments are carefully monitored and controlled as a means to begin development of a set of consistent and comparable investigations, of groups of differing sizes and differing levels of membership continuity and change. The initial experiments yielded results formally verifying that membership continuity produces better meeting outcomes (increased performance and productivity, and decreased solution time) while membership change produced the exact opposite (decreased performance and productivity, and increased solution time). Drawing from philosophy of science, we emphasize the importance of carefully checking and verifying in a longitudinal study even what some might suggest as ‘‘obvious.’’ <sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information systems; GDSS; Groups; GSS; Group decision making; Induced value experiments; MIS

## 1. Introduction

Vitalari [30] observed:

Longitudinal designs are seldom used in Information Systems research. . . Most importantly, longitudinal designs permit the exploration of time-dependent phenomenon such as learning, adaptation, and evolution within the research setting. For many research issues confronting the Information Systems field, longitudinal designs offer great opportunity for scientific progress. (p. 243)

Although a decade has passed since these observations were made, there remains a scarcity of longitudinal studies. Researchers such as Watson [32] have reiterated the need for a longitudinal study of groups using Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) as a means for determining the impact of this technology on group process and outcomes. DeSanctis [9] argued for the use of longitudinal groups in GDSS research as:

Researchers are thus advised whenever possible to solicit for laboratory studies intact groups, that is, groups whose members (a) have experience working together, (b) intend to work together in the future, and (c) have a vested interest in the group activities and outcomes. (p. 66)

Longitudinal laboratory studies using GDSS that have been conducted have been characterized as providing quite mixed results [5,7]. While longitudinal field studies exist, McLeod [23] urged caution in utilizing the results since the studies often had no control group and relied upon self-reports from participants. The purpose of this paper is to demonstrate a methodology that can be used to determine how group processes and outcomes change over time within a laboratory setting. In shaping our methodology, we worked to avoid three crucial difficulties with previous analyses.

(1) Lack of control—subject behavior was not fully monitored nor were controls utilized to insure that computer supported groups and control groups did not share information or the actual computer support system [24].

(2) Subject dropout—subject absenteeism and dropouts plagued earlier studies even where impacts on a course grade or on a course credit were used (in Ref. [24], a 69% dropout rate occurred for the control group), and Hollingshead [12], where serious absenteeism was reported and six subjects were noted as dropping out.

(3) Self-reported outcome measures.

In the next section, we discuss the need for longitudinal study in GDSS. We then outline the basics of our methodology and detail the specific formulation we developed to study group decision making. This is followed by a section devoted to presenting a detailed example of the application of the methodology in a longitudinal study of group decision making with and without membership continuity.

## 2. Longitudinal studies in GDSS research

McGrath [20] noted that while researchers have studied groups for over a half a century, there are limitations on inferences that can be made as much of the work focussed on the use of groups that met only once. Due to high cost, subject attrition, and publication delays, many researchers avoid the use of longitudinal investigations [30]. The use of these studies based on a single meeting to draw inferences about organizational groups may be misleading, as organizational groups differ in several key aspects from many of the laboratory groups, which met only once. There are several reasons for studying groups over time. Organizational groups are often continuing in nature, having a past and a future together. Members share a history and anticipate a future. The possibility of longterm association can influence group processes and outcomes. An organizational group receives rewards (including financial) for success. But in many cases, experimental groups do not receive financial rewards through the use of rewards in the form of course credit, prize for best performance, etc. A study of groups over time also permits the investigation of the following topics: learning, attitudes and changes in attitudes over time, effect of technology on work, use of technology, development of group cohesiveness, conflict resolution, group composition and changes therein. In Table 1, we provide a summary of six investigations incorporating longitudinal analysis of group decision making. Below, we detail the need for longitudinal investigations and assess previous research findings.

If group membership continuity or change are defining aspects of group outcomes as suggested by McGrath [20], then studies based on observation of a single meeting may yield misleading results. Chidambaram et al. [5] found that researchers have ignored the issue of group development. Results in Chidambaram et al. [6] indicated that there were significant differences in developmental patterns between manual (non-GDSS) groups and GDSS groups. Initially, the GDSS groups exhibited lower conflict management ability and cohesiveness than manual groups. But, by the end of the four meetings, this pattern had reversed, suggesting a potential benefit (enhanced conflict resolution skills) for GDSS groups. Walther [31] found that over time, GDSS groups exhibited more task-oriented behavior than manual groups. Dennis et al. [8] reported that established groups exhibited different personalities than ad hoc groups. The established groups were more likely to express critical comments and uninhibited comments and had a less even distribution of participation.

These studies indicate that over time, GDSS groups may exhibit a different pattern of behavior and task outcomes than manual groups, and group developmental patterns over time may not be the same. Thus, the results of a study taken during the formative stages of a group may not be indicative of its future, suggesting the potential value of longitudinal investigations.

Another factor that suggests the usefulness of longitudinal investigations is the GDSS technology itself and how the groups, learn, adapt, and use the technology. Without adequate training sessions, some or all members of a group may devote the experimental time to ‘‘catching up,’’ and thus may not contribute toward achievement of experimental goals. Zigurs et al. [34] noted that lack of prior exposure to electronic meeting systems (EMS) was a factor associated with a group’s refusal to use EMS. But in their study, they found that some groups developed positive attitude toward GDSS over time. Assuming that the intention to use a system is correlated with system usage, group members who have a negative opinion of a system may not use the system, and thus possibly hinder management efforts. Without an extended exposure to the system by users, their inputs would be of less value to system developers. A longitudinal study coupled with good training session(s) may signal to the management and system designers that the results of the investigation merit attention and are not result of a one-time phenomenon.

Without an analysis of a group over multiple sessions, examination of the effects of learning and system usage requirements and preferences cannot be analyzed. Novice users may have different preferences, requirements, and priorities than expert users. These preferences and requirements do change over time as the user gains proficiency in system usage. A study over time can provide valuable information to system designers on the ways of making a system more adaptable with the changing(improving) user abilities.

For ad hoc groups, cues about important unshared information may not be obvious. The successful elicitation of this unshared information can lead to productivity gains. In strict electronic GDSS groups, it is especially important to decipher the cues and elicit unshared information, as there is no face to face communication and participants rely solely on written communication. Participants should be able to ‘‘read’’ the messages for additional unshared information. As groups become established, they can use group history features and their own memory and cues to identify unshared information, and bring it to the surface, resulting in better information sharing. Familiarity with other group members may also result in groups becoming more cohesive and developing trust at an early stage in the group developmental process.

When groups meet over time, changes in group composition can occur due to retirement, work reassignment, or death. In addition to these long-term composition changes, absence from one or more meetings by one or more members may affect group dynamics. Depending on the personality and ability of the member(s), such an absence can have both positive (absence of a member who tends to distract the group meetings with his or her remarks) and negative outcomes (loss of the most capable member resulting in performance loss). McGrath [20] noted that when changes in group composition occurs, major shifts in the pattern of group activity may occur. Further, he goes on to state that: ‘‘There is really very little empirical evidence to draw on to support or refute those implications, because remarkably little research has been done on the effects of even such major changes as loss, addition, or substitution of new members. . .’’ This too suggests the need for a longitudinal investigation.

## 3. General methodology and specific implementation

Previously, we have outlined the need for longitudinal investigation in GDSS research and possible problems (attendance, lack of motivation, cost, etc.)

that may arise during such investigation. To overcome these problems, we propose the use of a methodology, which draws heavily from the induced-value experimentation literature gaining significance in economics.

Since the 1960s economists have examined human decision making behavior under market conditions. When a researcher conducts an experiment possibly using student subjects, there is a need to ensure that the subjects perform their best and give their best effort. To motivate a subject and to ensure that each subject performs at his or her best, researchers use incentives. These incentives include extra credit, course work requirement, and financial rewards. We do believe that the use of non-monetary reward mechanisms pose difficulty as there is a need to sustain motivation throughout the experiment process (in a longitudinal investigation, it may mean several days or weeks), and to ensure that the given reward structure is sufficient to motivate all the subjects (for example a student who can earn only a ‘‘C’’ grade based on prior scores may not contribute his or her best to the experiment).

Smith’s [26 – 28] pioneering efforts consistently emphasize the importance of monetary reward mechanisms to overcome possible subjective tendencies and focus subjects on the experimental task. Smith [26] states:

Control is the essence of experimental methodology, and in experimental exchange studies it is important that one be able to state that, as between two experiments, individual values (e.g., demand or supply) either do or do not differ in a specified way. Such control can be achieved by using a reward structure to induce prescribed monetary value on actions. The concept of induced valuation. . . depends upon the postulate of non-sation.

## He further goes on to state:

Given a costless choice between two alternatives, identical except that the first yields more of the reward (usually currency) than the second, the first will always be chosen (preferred) over the second, by an autonomous individual, i.e., utility is a monotone increasing function of the monetary reward, U(M), $\mathrm { U } ^ { \prime } > 0$

Smith also stressed the construction of experiments that satisfy parallelism, arguing:

The laboratory becomes a place where real people earn real money for making real decisions about abstract claims that are just as real as a share of General Motors. (26: 276)

We do believe that rewards such as a flat fee for participation or the reward to the best performer(s) does not adequately address the need to sustain a subject’s motivation. Thus, we advocate the use of a reward scheme, which has as its key features both a flat fee for experimental participation/show-up and a performance-based fee. Details of different reward structures and their use in different experimental settings are given in Refs. [13,14,26 –28,33].

Hoffman et al. [11] provide significant procedural detail on experimental economics that will not be repeated here. Our adaptation required a network communication process for the groups, an integrated decision support system (DSS), and a longitudinal approach, where subjects committed to and did complete a full set of experiments.

Marsden and Pingry [17] argued that information is neither a free good that should always be provided nor a precious commodity that will necessarily enhance decision making. The type and detail of information to access will tend to differ from one decision setting to another. Information is a resource that has a cost and a potential return in contributing to enhanced decision making and performance. As Bergeron [3,4] noted, organizations have indeed begun to implement usagebased charge back systems in an attempt to control resource utilization. Our analysis recognizes that resources (information) cost money and should be used only if justified by enhanced performance. As detailed below, our experimental decision-makers can access a variety of information (e.g., evaluations of a firm’s management or of the economy) and can engage in what-if analysis using an embedded decision support system. But each information resource accessed costs money, and can either yield a positive or a negative net impact on subject reward.

In summary, we use an incentive system that provides financial rewards for both performance and experimental participation. As detailed below, rewards were set such that it was sufficiently greater than the average subject’s opportunity cost. We cannot guarantee success through the use of induced value experiments as other factors such as task, system design, experimental length, and number of replications may have a role in determining success. The success of this approach in experimental economics and in DSS research [10,18,19,29] leads us to believe that this approach will also be useful in our setting.

## 3.1. Incentive structure

In the experiments, subjects performed tasks typical for bank commercial loan officers. They had to decide whether or not to make a loan and, if choosing to make a loan, the amount of the loan. The amount approved could range from zero (no loan) up to the amount requested by an applicant. Two important points incorporated in our experimentation carefully follow current practice in economic laboratory experimentation: (1) monetary rewards that provide incentive at every point in a session, and (2) reward sufficiency levels set at least at \$12–15/h for subjects performing in the middle of the expected performance range. The reward structure consisted of a participation fee set at \$7 per meeting and a performancebased fee set at a maximum of \$12 per meeting. Group members received compensation based upon lending amounts and defaults. Our data recording procedures kept track of all individual loan recommendations to their group, allowing us to track the quality of individual recommendations as well as group loan decision outcomes. The best performance involved granting the largest amount of money requested without incurring a default. Subjects earned positive rewards as a percentage of repaid loans after deducting for all system usage (information) costs. To ensure sustained motivation, the performance-based fee was divided into: (1) a reward for individual effort computed at every decision period, (2) reward for group effort computed at the end of every decision period, and (3) a performance-based reward that was a function of overall performance with the requirement that there was at least the preservation of capital. Default determination was a realization of a random process set as a function of information on the applicant’s current and projected balance sheet, management quality, sales forecasts, and economic outlook as it impacts the applicant firm. If a default occurred, a penalty was imposed. As suggested by the bank loan officer consultant, the penalty was set equal to 25% of the amount loaned. The lending officer indicated that this was consistent with net costs after collateral and loan security devices were taken into account.

## 3.2. Subjects

The selection of subjects is crucial to the experimental process. Some of the issues that should be considered are: generalizability of the laboratory findings to the real world, motivation, familiarity of the subjects to the task & computer software, and member ability. As the researchers worked in a university environment, we had an abundance of student subjects. Ashton and Kramer [2] supported the use of student subjects as surrogates for real world decision-makers, noting that both sets possess information processing characteristics and biases that are similar. However, the use of student subjects alone may pose a restriction on the generalizability of the findings. To overcome such a concern, we used six commercial lending officers with several years of work experience to benchmark the performance of the student subjects.

A possible concern with the use of commercial lending officers is their lack of motivation. As described above and detailed in the next sub-section, we use monetary rewards to motivate the subjects. Given the restriction on monetary resources, we could not provide financial incentives to the commercial lending officers. We however used the concept of ‘‘negative inducement.’’ All the commercial lending officers who participated in our experiments were subordinates of the senior bank official who helped us with the rule set development. They were given time off from their daily duties to participate in the experiments. We knew that these lending officers were motivated as they knew that their performance would be an issue when the authors discussed the project and its findings with the senior bank official.

A concern when using student subjects is the possible variation in member ability. To overcome this, we conducted the experiments in two stages. In the first stage, subjects acted as individual decisionmakers and we used their performance to select the subjects for the second stage, in which each subject acted as member of a group(results reported in this study). In the first stage, the subject pool consisted of both students and lending officers from a nearby bank. They were volunteers; notices and announcements were used to recruit the subjects. A total of 40 students participated in the first stage of our experiments. Of these, there were 19 undergraduates, one student was studying toward a master’s degree, and the rest (20) were either working toward a doctoral degree or had recently completed a PhD. There were 24 business major students.

While the use of student subjects is consistent with several previous longitudinal studies [5,6,24,34], we went a step further to check the reliability of our experimental analyses. In addition to the student participants, six commercial lending officers from a large Kentucky bank also participated in this investigation. As noted earlier, the use of the bank officers was to determine whether there are any significant differences in performance and perception between student subjects and the bank officers. In the individual subject experimental settings, we tested bank loan officers’ experimental performance compared to student subject performance and found no significant difference [15]. In the event of non-significant differences, we suggest that our findings may have greater generalizability than had we used only student subjects.

To control for member ability, we selected the top 16 available performers from a corresponding set of individual experimental sessions, recruited to participate in group experiments. The invitation for participation was made on the basis of performance at the individual experimental sessions. Such a procedure was an attempt to control member ability and we used those subjects whose performance did not vary significantly from that of the bank personnel. The use of 16 members was based on budgetary constraints (i.e., a larger sample size would have been preferred but the availability of funds, which we used as a motivational tool, dictated the sample size). Finally, we ran an experimental session with commercial lending officers to again check for significant variation in performance. We found none.

Zigurs et al. [34] had noted that lack of prior exposure to electronic meeting systems (EMS) was a factor associated with a group’s refusal to use EMS. With this in mind, the use of subjects with prior exposure to the task and to individual DSS software helped us to have a pool of subjects who were knowledgeable in both the task and the software. The networked group software we developed retained individual DSS task analysis features and incorporated EMS capabilities. We also had a training session to ensure that the subjects learnt the mechanics of the experiments but not the decision rules.

The two group sizes, three members and five members, followed previous studies and laboratory research [7]. Four sessions were conducted for each group, with two experiments in a session. Each experiment consisted of seven loan problems. The 16 subjects were divided into four groups, two groups with three members each and two groups with five members each. Each subject agreed to and did complete eight full multi-period experiments. Group membership changes occurred at the end of the second session. Comparisons relating to membership continuing (group history) involved sessions I and II and sessions III and IV. Comparisons relating to membership change involved comparison of outcomes from sessions II and III.

## 3.4. Software and procedures

We designed and developed LAN software relying heavily on input from a local bank officer and from a banking professor colleague. Shneiderman [25] had noted that for novice users, crowded screens are more difficult to examine. To limit such problems, we developed, tested, and modified prototype versions over several months. Comments and suggestions of numerous students and bank personnel volunteer subjects were used to iteratively develop improved interface formats. Emphasis was placed on information presentation and clarity, ease of interface use, communication processes, and subject feedback through summaries following each experimental period. The communication processes were anonymous (no identification of message sender). Different calibrations of our software, including randomization of key elements, were used to prevent passing of any useful information across subject groups or subject sessions. One experiment in a session used our basic system (no what-if capability) while the other used our more advanced system (what-if capabilities available at the individual mode).

Our system provides both quantitative and qualitative support. Subjects could pay to access information on each of these elements. The quantitative support is provided through a mechanism for performing what-if analysis. The system is capable of presenting financial performance statistics on the applicant firm if a loan of a particular amount is granted. When provided with what-if capabilities, subjects could also check the forecasted balance sheet and projected likelihood of loan repayment for any dollar amount between \$1 and the loan amount requested. The system can provide three different types of qualitative information on an applicant firm: a management capability rating (high, good, marginal, and bad), a rating on the status the applicant firm enjoys with the bank (good, average, fair, and new), and an economic outlook rating (good, average, and bad). The last of these provides a rating on the economy as it relates to the loan applicant firm, (i.e., the rating may be very good for a particular firm in a specific industry but fair or bad for a firm in another industry).

In the first phase of an experimental period, subjects can use stand-alone DSS to investigate loan applicants and requested loan amounts. They can seek ratings on management, loan applicant status, and economic outlook. When what-if capabilities are available, they can input various alternative loan amounts and receive projected balance sheet figures and loan repayment likelihood. Such flexibility is a key ingredient in the formulation and intended future uses of our methodology.

After all participants have decided on the amount to recommend to the group or if the time allotted for individual analysis has elapsed, a new interface screen (see Fig. 2) is distributed to subjects for participation in the group decision making stage (the individual recommendation distributed to all group members is set to zero if a subject does not make a decision within the allotted time).

Fig. 2 shows the screen at the start of the group decision making stage. The mechanism for navigating through the process is given in the form of specific keys as depicted in rectangle C of Fig. 2. The time remaining (180 s maximum) is given in rectangle D of the figure. The different recommendations of the various users at the individual stage as well as the possible alternatives are given in rectangle F. Each member in a group is identified by a number, such as user 1, which is randomly assigned and not tied to location of the subject in the laboratory. In Fig. 2, the values in rectangle F indicate that user 1’s individual recommendation is \$500, user 2’s is \$400 and user 3’s is \$880. Rectangle F also indicates that the loan amounts that can be granted by the group are restricted to values of zero, even amounts in hundreds up to \$800, and \$880 (the requested amounted). The rationale for limiting this choice or loan options is to provide group members with a clear choice set, from which a single decision value can be selected within the allocated time.

Fig. 3 shows the screen at a point during the group decision making. It shows the comments of various users (see rectangle G of the figure). To send a message, a user types in the message and presses the return key, which triggers electronic sharing of the message to all group members. To activate the period memory, a user presses the tab key. When this key is pressed, a previous message (such as ‘‘shall we give 880’’ from user 3) is shown. By pressing the tab key repeatedly, a user can review previous comments. Group members may ‘‘vote’’ by pressing the F4 function key and entering one of the alternative loan amounts. Such voting may be triggered by individual choice or by the group appearing to be converging on a specific amount. We set the program up to be capable of using either a majority decision rule or unanimity decision rule. A separate program (which we termed the Monitor) receives the separate messages from each of the different members in a group, determines whether a decision has been made (i.e., the assigned decision rule has been satisfied), and transmits the group decision to all the different work stations. If voting does not yield a decision, that is when the members are unable to agree among themselves, the monitor transmits the requested loan amount as the group decision. The use of the requested loan amount (actually the maximum of possible values) in these cases increases the possibility of a loan default. Thus, there is a risk associated with non-compliance. If time elapses without an agreement, the monitor also transmits the requested loan amount as the group decision. In fact, for the illustration experiments discussed below, the process seems to have worked well since no ‘‘non-decisions’’ were recorded, that is, groups reached a loan decision in each case.

When the monitor transmits a group decision loan amount (or the requested amount if no decision is reached), the monitor triggers the display of the period summary. The summary provides information on both individual and group performance in the period, including: loan amount recommended by individuals, loan amount recommended by group, repayment outcome, and net earnings on loan transactions, which include the individual and group components indicated earlier. The loan outcome (repayment or default) is determined as a function of the quality of management, economic outlook, customer history, and a random component. The information that can be purchased by subjects represents information signals relating to the likelihood of default. Thus, if an applicant has good management, good economic outlook, good customer status, and a strong balance sheet, it is still possible for a loan default to occur.

This outcome, however, is less likely than for a loan applicant with less favorable information signals. Each signal accessed by a subject results in a cost, an information charge. Performance outcomes relate to net outcomes or loan earnings minus information signal costs. The summary screen was displayed for 20 s, after which a new period was initiated by the monitor. The process was repeated until the scheduled set of loan periods had been completed.

To avoid subject fatigue, sessions were limited to 90 min. This allowed us to complete two experiments (seven loan task occurrences each) within a session. Subjects arriving at a session were randomly assigned to a network node (computer). Privacy shields and strategic disbursement of subjects in the lab were used to avoid sight of any other subject screens. When all subjects had arrived, the facilitator read from a carefully prepared script to provide all groups with the same set of instructions. The mechanics of subject participation and system procedures and the details of the subject reward mechanism structure were emphasized. Subjects then participated in a practice experiment that included five decision periods. The practice experiment used a stand-alone environment with simulated group discussion, so individuals could proceed at their own pace with five decision periods. To ensure that subjects learned only the mechanics of using the system, the practice sessions used whitenoise information signals with ‘‘group communications’’ pre-programmed into training session versions.

Using groups in longitudinal laboratory experimentation poses several difficulties to researchers. First, there is a need to ensure that subjects sustain their motivation over the experimentation period. Secondly, there is a need to thwart unplanned absences from meetings. Finally, there is a need to ensure that individuals contribute their knowledge to group discussion. As noted above, to overcome these difficulties our experimental methodology draws heavily from the induced value approach set out in Smith [26–28], using continuing incremental performancebased monetary rewards to achieve continued subject attendance and to draw out subjects’ best efforts. Our purpose was to provide mechanisms for analyzing and comparing group performance on decision making tasks when there is membership continuity and when there is membership change.

To demonstrate the application of our methodology, we began by studying groups of size three and of size five. As detailed below, we found that membership continuity produced better meeting outcomes measured in increased performance, productivity, and diminished solution time. Membership change produced the opposite, resulting in decreased performance, productivity, and increased solution time. The direction of results was consistent in every case, with somewhat greater statistical significance for groups of size three than for groups of size five. Using the induced value approach with a set of monetary rewards that were constructed to average approximately \$12 – 15/h (compared to \$4– 6/h for typical student labor jobs), we were able to complete the full set of experiments without the subject dropout or absenteeism that had plagued other longitudinal studies [7,12,24].

## 4. Research hypotheses

## 4.1. Membership continuity

Drawing from the MIS literature discussed earlier, we formulated the following three hypotheses relating to membership continuity:

H1. When group members remain intact, groups perform better over time.

H2. When group members remain intact, groups reach decisions more rapidly; that is, solution time decreases over time.

H3. When group members remain intact, groups become more productive; that is, productivity (performance/solution time) increases over time.

Clearly, acceptance of both H1 and H2 should lead to acceptance of H3, but it is possible to accept H3 while rejecting one of the first two hypotheses.

## 4.2. Membership change

McGrath [21] noted that variation in group composition is more the norm than the exception. Yet, researchers have, for the most part, ignored these variations. In the non-electronic meeting environment, Ziller et al. [35] examined creativity of groups with a history of success or failure, followed by alteration in group composition. The authors found that groups undergoing membership change were more creative than those not undergoing such changes. In an electronic meeting system (EMS) environment, Arrow and McGrath [1] and McGrath et al. [22] investigated membership changes for differing groups, some using and some not using computer support. The investigators intentionally altered some group composition in weeks 11 and 12 of their study. Other membership change occurrences were not planned by the researchers. In weeks 7 to 9, eight groups had a temporary member since the assigned task required three people and, due to subject absenteeism, these groups did not have the required forum.

The researchers found that whether the altered group composition resulted from a temporary member or from a continuing imposed change, the groups with change performed better than groups without change on tasks reported as dealing with reflecting about groups’ internal processes. No such effect was found for other tasks. Further, evidence suggested higher cohesiveness among groups with changes. Groups with a new member reported that they spent more time on a task rather than on conflict resolution.

McGrath’s [20] TIP theory identified three functions of group activity: (1) production, (2) well-being, and 93) member support. These functions represent contributions to the organization, to the group itself, and to the group’s members. Hollingshead et al. [12] noted that when a group undergoes change, there may be a need to spend relatively more time in solving technical issues and dealing with conflict prior to initiation of analysis and task completion. In TIP terminology, this implies that well-being and member support functions may dominate the production function of the group.

Drawing from TIP, we formulated the following three hypotheses relating to situations with membership change:

H4. When group composition changes, initially there will be a decline in group performance (production).

Acceptance of both H4 and H5 should lead to the expectation of acceptance of H6. H6 may also be accepted under certain conditions in which either H4 or H5 is rejected.

## 6. Discussion and conclusions

## 6.1. Summary of results

Using our controlled laboratory experimental methodology, we investigated the effects of membership continuity and change on various group meeting outcomes such as performance, solution time, and productivity. Results of our tests of six hypotheses are summarized in Table 11.

## 6.2. Discussion

We have presented and illustrated a methodology for use in analysis of groups over time. In organizations, groups often have a history and a future. Managers are commonly faced with decision choices concerning group membership continuity or change. What we have demonstrated is a methodology for determining the impact of change or the maintenance of continuity.

The illustration we provided here relates to one problem domain and used a specific subject set. Other investigations [16] have provided evidence that student subject performance in induced-value, direct monetary reward experiments mirrors that of business decision makers in the same settings. But results to date are of a limited nature. As we seek to address the generalizability of results, we must do so from both the decision domain and the subject pool. To date, the indications are positive but very tentative. What we do have is a methodology that enables us to proceed on these investigations.

We advocate the use of longitudinal studies to examine effects of membership continuity and change. Longitudinal studies utilize greater resources, effort, and time by the researcher(s), but we argue they are necessary to study group decision making. In addition, it is important to utilize careful monitoring to ensure that no knowledge of matters that may affect the validity of studies is passed between different subject groups. There is also a need to electronically monitor activities in order to collect precise observations of individual and group actions, and determine values for individual and group performance metrics. Finally, there is a need to ensure that subjects sustain motivation over the period and that there is no unplanned attrition. The methodology set out here is directed at satisfying these requirements—to carefully control and monitor, to avoid subject dropout and absenteeism, and to directly measure rather than rely on self-reported outcomes. The experimental analyses presented to illustrate the methodology demonstrate the process and yield initial results, relating to group performance with and without membership continuity. While we find these results interesting, we stress the longer-term potential for our methodology. It is a mechanism that can help us structure and complete thorough analyses of group performance across problem domains, group size differences, and variations or continuity in group membership.
