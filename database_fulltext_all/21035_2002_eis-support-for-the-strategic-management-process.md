# EIS support for the strategic management process

Sanjay K. Singh <sup>a,</sup>\*, Hugh J. Watson <sup>b</sup>, Richard T. Watson <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Business, Department of Management, University of Alabama at Birmingham, Birmingham, AL 35294, USA <sup>b</sup>Department of Management Information Systems, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602, USA

Received 1 August 2000; received in revised form 1 March 2001; accepted 1 June 2001

## Abstract

The success of an executive information system (EIS) depends upon many factors, including providing support for the strategic management process (SMP). The SMP is comprised of five phases: organizational objectives, environmental scanning, strategy formulation, strategy implementation, and strategic control. The study empirically investigated (1) the support of EISs for the phases of the SMP and (2) the relationship between the amount of support provided and EIS success. Data were collected from EIS professionals and executive users in 51 organizations. Descriptive statistics were calculated and correlation analysis was used to test the hypothesized relationships. It was found that the success of an EIS is related to its support for operational objectives and strategy implementation. The findings can be understood by considering the conditions that surround the development of an EIS and revisiting the relationship between management activities and information requirements. <sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Executive information systems; Strategic management process; Environmental scanning; Strategy formulation; Strategy implementation; Strategic control; Implementation succes

## 1. Introduction

Executive information systems (EISs) first appeared on the decision-support scene in the mid-1980s [55]. These systems were designed to ‘‘provide executives with easy access to internal and external information that is relevant to their critical success factors’’ [66]. Throughout the 1990s, EISs evolved with changes in technology and approaches to organizational planning and control. EISs now typically access much of their data from data marts and/or data warehouses. These data stores make it easier to access clean, consistent, integrated data. Advances in EIS software have increased the quantity and quality of information provided to users. Most EIS software uses a Web browser for the user interface, which provides easy access to data on a company’s intranet and from the Internet. OLAP (Online Analytical Processing) capabilities allow users to perform multidimensional analyses by ‘‘slicing and dicing’’ the data. Data mining capabilities can provide insights on relationships that are hidden in the data. The balanced scorecard concept has influenced EIS design and content. It highlights the importance of monitoring financial, the customer, internal operations, and innovation and learning measures [34,67].

Over the years, much has been learned about the keys to EIS success. A frequently discussed key is that an EIS should provide support for organizational objectives [50,53]. For example, it might support downsizing efforts [4], make the organization more responsive to changing market conditions [46,48], or contribute to a total quality management program [46].

An important executive activity is to be actively engaged in the strategic management process (SMP) in order to take actions that lead to the accomplish ment of organizational objectives [60]. This process requires information that is not always readily available [15]. A lack of relevant information to support strategic management, arguably the most important task of senior executives, can have a deleterious effect on organizational performance. An EIS has the potential to provide information that supports the SMP [10,27,64,65]. Such a system can improve the efficiency and effectiveness of executives who use it [62].

The authors have been engaged in an on-going program of research on EIS. Through research, conversations with EIS managers, and seeing many systems, several impressions have emerged. Successful systems usually support the accomplishment of business objectives. The scope of the support provided, however, is surprisingly narrow, with most of the information serving well-defined needs and drawing from internal databases. Much less is done to support the parts of the SMP that are less structured or require data that are external or non-machine resident.

Even though there is a logical appeal to the recommendation that an organization’s EIS should support the SMP, there has been only limited research of how, and to what extent, EISs support the various phases of the SMP, and more importantly, what is the relationship between providing support and the success of an EIS. This situation motivated our study, which generated interesting and important findings. These findings are interpreted in the context of the realities of how EISs are developed and today’s turbulent business environment with resulting changes in executives’ job responsibilities and information requirements.

## 2. EIS and the strategic management process

We begin with a discussion of the strategic management process and how it might be supported by an EIS, and measures of implementation success.

## 2.1. The strategic management process

Strategic management is the essence of an executive’s job [2,11,56]. The process deals with ‘‘. . .the fundamental organization renewal and growth, with the development of the strategies, structures, and systems necessary to achieve such renewal and growth, and with the organizational systems needed to effectively manage the strategy formulation and implementation process’’ [30].

The SMP is complex and unstructured [43]. It requires inputs from various stakeholders of the organization and a large commitment of resources [20,21,60]. Executives need relevant information to successfully execute it, and some of this information is potentially provided by an EIS [5,10,41,73].

The strategic management literature provides a variety of SMP models, which are similar in most ways (see Fig. 1). They prescribe how an organization’s objectives, policies, procedures, and actions can be unified into a cohesive whole [2,49,60]. Strategies are developed and implemented to translate objectives into pre-determined results. The process is iterative and continues throughout the lifetime of the organization [43]. The metamorphosis of objectives into results is realized through the effective execution of the SMP.

## 2.2. Organizational objectives and EIS

The SMP begins with the formulation of organizational objectives [31]. Organizational objectives can be either strategic, administrative, or operational. Strategic objectives provide the overall direction for the organization; administrative objectives specify the necessary organizational infrastructure to accomplish the strategic objectives; and operational objectives identify specific work tasks that must be performed. Metrics should be developed to measure progress on accomplishing the objectives.

An EIS is not used in setting organizational objectives; rather, it is more helpful in monitoring whether objectives are being met and supplying information that assists personnel in meeting the objectives. This use of an EIS more directly supports the other phases of the SMP.

## 2.3. Environmental scanning and EIS

The analysis of environmental information is crucial for executives to mold the organization’s future [12,15]. Information is needed about customers, suppliers, competitors, new technologies, the economy, labor markets, the international scene, demographic trends, and political developments [2,61]. Executives rely most heavily on external sources for this information (e.g., trade journals, friends in the industry), but internal sources are also used (e.g., VPs/managers in marketing). When it comes to strategic environmental information, executives have a limited number of sources which they constantly consult [18].

An EIS has the potential to support environmental scanning. Watson et al. [71] found that 56% of the firms’ EIS provided access to news services, 46% to stock market information, and 34% to trade/industry data. Since the study, the technology for accessing external databases (e.g., Web browsers) has improved considerably, thus improving the ability to support environmental scanning. Specially prepared environmental information, such as competitive analyses can also be made available in an EIS. Also, the softer kinds of information that are exchanged in conversations can be at least partially captured in an EIS, such as through electronic discussion applications [70].

## 2.4. Strategy formulation and EIS

The next phase involves the development, evaluation, and selection of strategies. Alternative strategies are formulated based on matching the strengths and weaknesses of the organization with the opportunities and threats facing it, which is popularly known as SWOT analysis [2]. The different strategies are then evaluated on various financial and non-financial criteria and the best strategies are selected for implementation [2]. This part of the SMP is analytical in nature.

The information provided by an EIS can be used in formulating the plans, programs, and ideas that comprise a firm’s strategies. For example, the strategies should be set with the organization’s critical success factors in mind, and an EIS often contains CSFrelated information [56]. An EIS can be used to collect and consolidate various plans, programs, and ideas from organizational members; either through email or specially designed applications. Analytical capabilities can be included to perform OLAP, ‘‘what if’’ analysis, trend analysis, forecasting, and budgeting on what has been proposed. An EIS can be used to facilitate the communication and negotiation of alternative plans and programs.

## 2.5. Strategy implementation and EIS

Once strategies are formulated, they must be implemented. The activities associated with implementation help sustain commitment to the objectives of the organization and the achievement of results [2]. Line managers are typically responsible for managing the implementation, including the political and social aspects of change [49]. Two important activities in this phase hinge on the fact that: (1) because managers must communicate the actions that individuals and organizational units need to implement the strategies, they must convey job descriptions, policies and procedures, and the appropriate code of conduct; and (2) they must identify the right people with the right skills and training for the job [42]. This phase requires assigning task responsibilities, coordinating the efforts of different specialists in the organization, and establishing an organizational structure to facilitate the flow of work throughout the organization [36].

There are a number of ways that an EIS can support the strategy implementation phase. Project plans can be included, with information about budgets, schedules, and who is responsible for performing various activities. Communications and coordination of work activities can be improved through integrated e-mail, electronic discussion applications, and workflow applications such as those associated with Lotus Notes [28]. Applications can be included that support the budgetary process. Human resource applications can allow managers to search the personnel database on the basis of name, organizational unit, job title, and job skills in order to identify candidates for filling open positions [66].

## 2.6. Strategic control and EIS

The final phase of the SMP is the formalization and institutionalization of a measurement and control process. The need for control, as Andrews [2,p,106] states ‘‘. . .is rooted in the central facts of organization itself. The inevitable consequence of divided activity is the emergence of sub-strategies, which are at least slightly deflected from the true course by the needs of individuals and the concepts and procedures of specialized groups, each with its own quasi-professional precepts and ideal.’’ A well-defined strategic control system can improve the probability that the organizational strategies are implemented despite the chaotic, unpredictable, and dynamic nature of an organization’s environment [8,19].

This phase of SMP is operational in nature. Managers compare individual and organizational performance against past results, goals, and benchmarks to make appropriate adjustments in strategies [42,44]. Based on the variance of actual and planned results, current strategies are either modified or discarded.

A well-designed EIS can support strategic control activities in a variety of ways. A staple of virtually all EISs are screens that compare actual performance against budgets, goals, standards, and benchmarks. Equally common are drilldown capabilities that allow users to view underlying detail data, typically in search of specific problems, such as sales regions that are below quota. Annotations (i.e., comments) to these screens can provide understanding and interpretation to numerical data. For example, a comment may indicate why the problem occurred, what steps are being taken to correct it, when the problem will be solved, and what steps are being taken to insure that it will not occur again. Some systems include text-based applications that track progress on the accomplishment of specific objectives. For any objective monitored, there may be a description of the objective, who is responsible for it, when it is to be completed, and comments about its status. Screens for strategic control help focus organizational attention on those things that need to be done well. This truth is reflected in the saying, ‘‘That which gets watched, gets done.’’

## 2.7. Measures of EIS implementation success

The implementation term is sometimes used to refer to the last stage of the system analysis and design process. For our purposes, however, the meaning of the term is much broader in scope. It is a process that ‘‘. . .begins with the very first idea for a system and changes it will bring. Implementation terminates when the system has been successfully integrated with the operations of the organization’’ [38]. This perspective is consistent with the scope of the SMP.

Measuring the implementation success of an EIS, as with other systems, is a complex and difficult process [53]. Researchers have proposed various measurement methods [24,25,38]. One possibility is to conduct a benefit/cost analysis. Even though most firms are able to evaluate costs, the benefits of the system are more intangible and difficult to quantify [7,32,57].

The success of a system might also be judged by the degree of goal attainment of the organization [13]. Kettinger et al. [35] attempted to link the financial success of an organization with its adoption and implementation of different types of information systems. However, it is difficult to make a connection between the utilization of systems and organizational success, because of the complexity and number of intervening variables [51]. The relationship between an organization’s structural components (e.g., information systems) and measures of financial success (e.g., ROI, ROA) is tenuous at best [47,54,57]. Due to the problems involved in direct measurement, researchers typically rely on surrogate measures.

In this study, the constructs of implementation success developed by Lucas et al. [39] were used. In their view, successful implementation is reflected by the acceptance of the system by users, the usage of the system, the impact of the system on user performance, and users’ satisfaction with the system. Usage of the system, user satisfaction, and impact on the system on individual performance are also components of DeLone and McLean’s [14] model for measuring IS success. The constructs are also consistent with Seddon’s [58] recommendation that measures of IS success should be selected with the type of system and the stakeholders in mind. The four constructs chosen for this study measure change and improvement in decision-making processes.

Acceptance of a system is a proclivity to use it. Executives are typically in a position where they can reject an EIS either by explicitly saying that they do not want it or implicitly by choosing to not use it. In other words, executives have a choice whereas operational personnel may be required to use a system. Without acceptance there is no EIS support for decision-making processes.

Use of a system implies that a change in decisionmaking behavior has occurred. System usage has been employed by many researchers as a surrogate measure of system success [6,9,16,17,22,52].

Measures of acceptance and use should be considered concurrently because one may accept a system conceptually but find it difficult or impossible to use. It is also noted that usage by itself is an inadequate measure because given the choice between an unsatisfactory system and no system, users may use the unsatisfactory system [23].

The post-adoption evaluation of a system can be operationalized by measuring the impact on user performance and user satisfaction with the system.

When evaluating an EIS, a desirable measure would be the difference in performance or the effectiveness based on the results obtained from decisions made using the system as compared to decisions that would have been made without the system.

Venkatraman and Ramanujam [63] developed an instrument to measure the success of strategic systems as reflected by the improvements in capabilities of the planning process and key planning objectives. Based on a review of the strategy literature, the authors developed a two dimensional model of strategic system success. The first dimension represents the capability of the system to support the overall strategic management process. The second dimension indicates the degree of fulfillment of the key organizational objectives. The authors identified 12 key factors to support these two dimensions which can be used to measure the impact of an EIS on the performance of executive users.

Another widely used measure of success is user satisfaction [73]. If the user perceives the system as providing valuable information not available elsewhere, the system designer can be relatively confident of the satisfactoriness of the information system for that user [33]. Therefore, user satisfaction with an information system is a useful evaluation measure [40].

## 3. The study

In order to investigate EIS support for the SMP, a study was conducted, in which two research questions were explored.

RQ1: To what extent do EISs support the SMP? RQ2: Are higher levels of support related to greater perceived success?

To answer the first research question, descriptive statistics were computed for the support provided for the various phases of the SMP. Statistics were also calculated for the perceived success of the systems. This allowed the second research question to be explored by testing the following hypotheses.

H1. The success of an EIS is related to its support for organizational objectives.

H2. The success of an EIS is related to its support of the environmental analysis phase of the strategic management process.

H3. The success of an EIS is related to its support of the strategy formulation phase of the strategic management process.

H4. The success of an EIS is related to its support of the strategy implementation phase of the strategic management process.

H5. The success of an EIS is related to its support of the strategic control phase of the strategic management process.

The independent variables are support for organizational objectives, environmental scanning, strategy formulation, strategy implementation, and strategic control. The dependent variable is EIS success. A positive relationship was anticipated for all the hypotheses (i.e., the higher the level the support for the SMP or any of its phases, the more successful the EIS will be in the organization).

## 3.2. Data collection

A mailing containing a cover letter, questionnaire A (one copy), and questionnaire B (two copies) was mailed to EIS professionals at 315 firms listed in the authors’ database of companies known or likely to have an EIS. Created from a variety of sources, the database is believed to provide a representative sample of firms with an EIS and has been used in previous studies [67,69,71].

The cover letter requested the recipient to complete questionnaire A and forward the two copies of questionnaire B to two executive EIS users within the organization. Collecting data from executives is a difficult proposition. Keeping this in mind, two copies of questionnaire B were sent, with the hope of getting back at least one completed questionnaire. In cases where both copies of questionnaire B were returned, the completed instrument from the user having the higher organizational ranking was used. However, if both users were of the same rank, then responses from the executive with the higher EIS usage was used.

The initial and a follow-up mailing resulted in data being collected from 51 firms in which both the EIS professional and an executive user had responded.<sup>2</sup> The data analysis was performed on this sample of firms.

## 5. Discussion

While EISs have the potential to provide strong support for all phases of the SMP, the study findings reveal that significantly greater support is given for operational objectives and the strategy implementation phase. There are several possible reasons for this.

## 5.1. The conditions that surround EIS development

An EIS is often built under the following conditions: executives with limited time to spend with analysts and who have difficulty fully articulating their information needs, developers who have little experience in developing systems for executives, and pressures to roll out the initial version of the system quickly [67,68,70].

The conventional wisdom is that an EIS needs to be developed quickly while executive interest is high. Interest, support, and confidence in the project is likely to wane if it takes a long time to develop the system. Watson et al. [71] found that the average time required to develop the initial version of an EIS is 4.9 months. This is not long to do all that is requiredassemble the EIS development team, determine information requirements, select the software, put any new hardware and communications capabilities in place, prepare the data, design the screens, test the system, and train users.

Adding to the difficulty is that the executive time needed to define information requirements is a scarce resource, and even when it is available, executives are not good at identifying what information they need. One study found that the amount of face-to-face contact between EIS developers and users is 1 h or less per week in the majority of firms [72]. An EIS typically provides information that is broader in scope with more soft information than other systems [70]. Executives are not used to computer-based systems delivering some of this information and may not think about requesting it. These and other reasons explain why EIS developers rank ‘‘getting executives to specify what they want’’ as their number one concern in developing an EIS [59].

For many EIS developers, this is their first experience with an application of this type. Their previous contact with executives and understanding of the nature of executive work are typically limited. Consequently, they do not begin the project with a good understanding of what information to include in the EIS.

The conditions just described help explain why the EISs in this study provide the greatest support for operational objectives and the strategy implementation phase. Faced with time pressures and difficulties in getting a comprehensive set of information requirements, EIS developers focus much of their efforts on information that they believe is useful to executives and can be relatively easily and quickly delivered-EIS versions of existing reporting systems. Executives and executive assistants can be asked what reports are used, what information is most important for them, how the information can be enhanced, how it should be displayed, and how frequently it should be updated [67]. Because most existing reporting systems support operational objectives and management control, it is not surprising that an EIS that draws upon them would also provide the same kind of support, at least in the short run, and probably with long-run effects.

## 5.2. Revisiting the relationship between management activity and information requirements

On reflection it is not surprising that the focus of an EIS is on operational objectives and strategy implementation. Three major changes have created the circumstances where senior managers have to focus more on the short-term and less on the longer-term.

First, in a rapidly changing, global economy, longterm stability has evaporated. Corporations need to focus on what is happening in the immediate-term to detect changes of strategic consequences. Microsoft, literally days after releasing Windows 95 as its strategic weapon to control personal computing, had to redirect significantly its strategic thrust to cope with the rapidly emerging threat of Netscape. How can corporations think long-term when companies such as Netscape in less than a year move from incorporation to strategic threat? A highly turbulent environment puts the stress on managers to make quick decisions and make them happen. As a result, consulting firms have been doing fewer strategy development and more implementation assignments [29]. There is no time and little data for strategic analysis. Unless firms can implement rapidly and successfully, their future is threatened.

Second, downsizing, outsourcing, and hierarchical flattening have decimated layers of management and analyst positions. Consequently, senior managers have had the scope of their responsibility broaden to embrace more of the operational and management control domains. Their span of control has moved both vertically, with responsibility for more employees, and horizontally, with responsibility for a greater range of decisions. Furthermore, they are taking on these responsibilities in an environment where there is less staff to perform specialized analyses.

Finally, the technology for monitoring operational objectives has developed substantially over recent years. The graphical user interface, data marts and warehouses, OLAP tools, local area networks, and rapid application development software have supported the deployment of EISs. Ad hoc filtering, reporting, and highly selective analysis of large volumes of operational data are quite feasible. A decade ago, such systems were very expensive and limited.

As a result of these changes, it is probably now time to revisit the foundational work on the relationship between managerial level and information requirements [3,26]. These authors relate management activity to information requirements (see Table 14). The implicit suggestion of their work is that senior managers have considerable need for external, aggregated, future-oriented, information (the right side of Table 14) and have less need for internal, detailed, current information (the left side).

We see senior managers’ information needs as a three-layer onion, with an inner core of operational information, surrounded by a layer of management control and strategic planning information (see Fig. 2). Our interpretation is the onion of the 1970s was rather small with relatively thick layers for the middle and outer layers. The onion was small because there was less need for information in the less competitive environment of the 1970s, and even in situations when more information was required, the technology was not available to provide it. The inner core was small because there was no information technology to support rapid ad hoc reporting. The outer layers were thick because the stability of the industrial era rewarded longer-term planning.

As we enter the new millennium, our perception is that the onion is larger and the outer layers relatively thinner. For the reasons we outlined above (global competition and downsizing), today’s senior managers need more information (the larger onion) and focus more on operational data (the larger core), and information technology makes it possible to create this large core. We also need to recognize that elements of this onion are continually changing because of the dynamism of the environment and the flexibility of information technology. The contents of the onion change as circumstances alter information needs. The differences we note between 1970 and today may not be permanent. Once we move through the discontinuity created by the transition from an industrial to information society, the environment may become more stable and long-term planning may regain its relevance. Meanwhile, the evidence of this study suggests that managers require short-term operational data if they are to keep abreast of rapid economic change.

## 6. Further EIS evolution

This study found that some phases of the SMP process are better supported by EISs than others. This is subject to change, however, as is support for the phases in general, because of technological advances. For example, since when the data were collected, the Web browser has become more common as the user interface of choice for EISs. This makes it much easier to perform environmental scanning. Through the Internet, users can easily access, for example, competitor, economic, and political information. Data warehouses are becoming more common and are an important data source for EISs. They support the strategy formulation phase by extracting/integrating data from multiple source systems and providing information about an organization’s CSFs. They also support the strategy implementation phase by consolidating data across business units, providing detailed financial/operational data, and supporting budgeting processes. OLAP capabilities support the strategic control phase by making it easier to drilldown to detailed data, compare actual to planned/budgeted amounts, and better understand variances in results. Over time, we can expect more EIS support for the SMP process, and the possibility of greater EIS success as a result.
