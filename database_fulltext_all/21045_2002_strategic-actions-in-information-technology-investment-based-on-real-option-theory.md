# Strategic actions in information technology investment based on real option theory

Yong Jin Kim <sup>a,1</sup>, G. Lawrence Sanders <sup>b,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup>State University of New York at Buffalo, 325N Jacobs Management Center, Buffalo, NY 14260, USA <sup>b</sup>State University of New York at Buffalo, 310A Jacobs Management Center, Buffalo, NY 14260, USA

Received 1 February 2001; received in revised form 1 May 2001; accepted 1 July 2001

## Abstract

The rapid growth of information technology (IT) investments has imposed pressure on management to take into account risks and payoffs promised by the investment in their decision-making. Comprehensive but easily understandable methodologies are needed to solve the complicated evaluation problems resulting from the complexity of new technologies. This paper develops a framework of strategic actions based on real option theory. The paper identifies the basic components of IT values and strategic actions and provides the basis for valuing IT investment in terms of economic and real option value. It also provides IT managers with an easy-to-understand framework to assist in evaluating and justifying IT investments. <sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information technology (IT) investment; Strategic action; Real option theory; Interaction effect; Competitor reaction

The technological environment we live within is something to be manipulated and influenced, but never again something to control. There are no real experts, only people who understand their own little pieces of the puzzle. The big picture is a mystery to us, and the big news is that nobody knows [28].

## 1. Introduction

The rapid growth of information technology (IT) investments has imposed pressure on management to take into account the risks and payoffs in their investment decision-making. At the same time, they have been confronted with conflicting information regarding the outcome of IT investments. Some researchers and professionals argue that IT will be a key resource for sustainable competitive advantage, or at least complement the creation of distinctive advantages [3,13 –15,27,45]. On the other hand, others suggest that the productivity gains from IT investments are neutral or negative [49,50].

Managers facing the evaluation problems of IT investment have used various methods from simple computational formula to very complex techniques that weave both quantitative and qualitative analysis together. Among them, quantitative methods are common in evaluating new technology, but these types of methods that are often used in capital investment analysis do not necessarily capture the entire impact of new technology adoption [55]. This difficulty is linked to the intangible characteristics of the benefit promised by IT, such as improved communication and control, enhanced capabilities, and competitive advantages [54]. Moreover, the complexity of new technologies such as flexible manufacturing systems and computer-aided engineering and design prevents management from getting a complete picture of the firm’s IT portfolio.

Recently, metrics such as the economic value added method and the real option valuation have been recommended by information systems (IS) researchers as a way to understand and facilitate investment decision [4,32,59]. These methods focus on the economic value and real option value of IT at the time of investment justification and provide information on the perceived impacts of the IT investment. Although real option theory is a very promising approach for valuating IT investment, as some researchers contend (cf. Refs. [4,32]), the lack of knowledge about real options prevents managers from utilizing this salient method to valuate their investment. According to Busby and Pitts [11], real options frequently occur in the managerial decision-making process and managers recognize the importance of securing flexibility in capital investments. However, most managers still use the traditional formalized methods, such as NPV and ROI, because they are skeptical of new analytical tools and they have difficulty in renouncing ongoing investments. Moreover, few firms have used methodological procedures for thinking of investment flexibility [4,11,32] because flexibility is often dealt with informally and in an ad hoc way in firms.

This paper proposes a comprehensive but simple framework to provide managers with strategic actions that incorporate the concepts of real option valuation. In essence, it provides managers with a simple methodology, without complex mathematical calculations, for understanding the underlying risks and opportunities of an IT investment.

The contributions of this paper are twofold. First, it identifies the basic components of IT values and strategic actions by which an IT investment justification can be made, thereby providing the basis for valuing IT investment in terms of economic and real option value. Secondly, the paper provides managers with insight into the complexity of evaluating and justifying IT investments.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The next section discusses previous literature on quantitative analyses for capital investment. This is followed by a section that addresses the relationship between IT investment and real option theory. Section 4 then proposes strategic actions for IT investment in the context of real option theory and several examples for each strategic action. In Section 5, the paper discusses the dynamics of strategic actions based on the concept of path dependency and Markov chain. Finally, a set of recommendations are presented for future research.

## 2. Previous literature on quantitative analyses

Cost-benefit analysis dominates the justification and assessment of capital investments and it is frequently used as the criterion for investment decisionmaking or capital budgeting. These methods involve return of investment [9,35,40] and economic value added approach [39,58].

Return of investment analyses use various valuation metrics such as return on investment, return on equity, return on total assets and discounted cash flow analysis [9]. Although useful, each of these approaches has shortcomings. Companies using return on investment, equity and/or total assets are likely to underestimate the true profitability for a new IT investment and overestimate the profitability for old ones. This is primarily because these methods employ the book value for assets and inflated values for revenues [9]. At the firm level, the value of a firm is expressed as the net-present-value of its future cash flows. Robust discounting approaches include cash, timing, and risk factors [35]. Discounted cash flow approaches have a sound theoretical foundation in the measurement of economic value. However, the timing of the cash flows can distort the evaluation of a project and firm performance. Moreover, the project level net present value tends to focus on short-term financial results, not on qualitative issues such as quality improvement or flexibility enhancement. This can result in eliminating major technological breakthroughs because the benefits from new IT investments are invisible and would not be captured by the traditional methods [31] and eventually, these innovations do not pass the net present value test [26].

Recently, Scarso [53] has suggested the term ‘Expanded Net Present Value’ (ENPV) as a criterion for capital investment. ENPV incorporates the traditional net present value of expected cash flows, the value of operating options, and interaction effects. In this formulation, the operating option represents a set of options available to management if a project is accepted and the interaction effect refers to synergy and inter-project dependence. While ENPV takes into account flexibility and interaction effects, it still ignores competitor reaction to an investment.

A relatively new valuation method is the Economic Value Added (EVAk) approach developed by Stewart III [39,58,60]. This approach is used at the firm and firm component level to measure value and evaluate performance. Well-known companies, such as Eli Lilly and Coca-Cola, have deployed EVA and believe that EVA has changed their corporate culture and rallied management teams to look at businesses differently [38]. In the context of IT investments, this approach can provide managers with the information about the relationship between IT investments and the bottom line. Although comprehensive, this method also focuses on short-term monetary benefits.

As discussed above, the traditional approaches have failed to address important issues such as IS impact on strategic direction and less tangible IT benefits of improved information capability for management decision-making, more timely product delivery and product quality (cf. Ref. [52]). The approaches also ignore a very important facet of technology investment, namely ‘flexibility’ [11,16,22,34,37,42,53]. Most investments are largely irreversible and therefore become sunk costs because they are firm or industry specific [42]. Making irreversible investment decisions in uncertain situations is, obviously, risky [16]. To reduce risk, decision-makers must be allowed to change their investment decisions as new information in terms of prices, costs, and other market conditions becomes available. This is the core concept of flexibility in capital investment, and it allows firms to use real option valuation for IT investments. For example, companies have outsourced strategic planning for IT investment from professional consulting firms and developed the target systems by outsourcing systems integration resources. These phenomena illustrate that companies implicitly recognize the importance of flexibility in IT investments. When managers are confronted with risks resulting from a large IT investment, they implicitly recognize the consulting projects as an option to proceed with the project or abandon it. In the same vein, a pilot project in the implementation stage can be considered as a real option.

The real option valuation differs from the financial option in terms of priceability and tradability of the underlying asset [37]. With financial options, the underlying asset of an option is priced and known, and the asset is tradable [8]. However, in technology investment situations, the price of an underlying asset is rarely known, and the tradability of the underlying asset is limited [37]. The real option theory has been applied to information systems investment research by several researchers (e.g., [4,18,32]). They contend that IT investment has option-like characteristics particularly in decisions related to subsequent investments.

## 3. IT investment and real option theory

In the context of organizations, the role of information technology is to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of an organization by reducing the bounded rationality of decision-making [2]. Thus, information technology as a business enabler is driving changes not only within organizations but also throughout the value chain [6,36,44,46]. Once IT is deployed for supporting each value activity, it tends to transform the way value activities are performed and the nature of linkages among them [44]. In other words, IT investments enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of an organization by influencing performance factors of value activities directly and/or indirectly. This process eventually impacts the bottom line of both current financial statements and future performance.

Accordingly, when deciding to invest in a new information technology, a company has to take into account at least two values: performance under the current condition and target performance based on future strategies. Taudes et al. [59] define the value of an IT investment as consisting of economic value and option value. In this paper, we define economic value as the direct value resulting from cost reduction via the IT investment. This value depends upon the problem-solving capability of that investment. It can be captured in terms of the changes in current and short-term financial performance estimation. We also define real option value as future value resulting from the strategic fit between the investment and external and internal components [27].

## 3.1. Real options in IT investment

There are several strategic purposes for IT investment that can override a negative expectation of economic value. Sometimes, even though the expected economic value is below zero, companies decide willingly to invest in a certain technology to capture the opportunities the investment endows (e.g., investment in infrastructure as in Benaroch and Kauffman [4]). The reason decision-makers pursue this route is because these investments may involve other options such as growth opportunities. These types of real options can be largely classified into growth, postponement, and abandonment options [16,53]. Growth options include scaling up, switching up, or scoping up an investment and give the opportunity to exploit a prior investment in a sequential investment. Postponement options involve deferring an investment in order to obtain new and better information about prices, costs, and other market conditions. Abandonment options, which include scaling down, switching down, or scoping down an investment, allow managers to exit from an IT investment before they become too involved. Among these options, postponement and growth options are considered the most important by managers, while the abandonment option is considered less important [11].

This typology is not exactly the same as the option pricing model. The option pricing model focuses on deferring decision-making in order to obtain a larger expected value than a now-or-never type investment [4]. This typology, however, elaborates the type of options embedded in the management decision process even though the expected value of each option may not be readily computed.

## 3.2. Factors determining real option value

According to the Black –Scholes option-pricing model [8], the value of a financial option is determined by five factors including the exercise price of an option, the time to exercise date, the current price of the asset, the variance per period of rate of return on asset, and the risk-free rate of interest. Among these factors, the variance per period of rate of return plays a critical role in valuing options. While this factor addresses the uncertainties of return on investment, it does not manifest the underlying reasons related to the variance in the rate of return. In this paper, we define the underlying factors of the variance as interaction effects on current assets and competitor reactions among various internal and external factors.

A new IT investment may interact with current assets or investments whether the interaction effect is negative or positive [22]. When it is positive, it may provide the firm with synergy, while in case of negative effects, it may hurt the firm’s competitive advantage. This problem may be caused by ‘‘action persistence or undue persistence’’ [24]. If the investment reinforces the interaction effect between new and current investments, it can create new technological paths [23]. However, when undue persistence takes place, it drains a firm’s valuable resources and makes it difficult to exercise any real options, except the abandonment option.

In addition, a new IT investment may force competitors to think about counter investments to compete with the investment. These competitor reactions or counter investments made by competitors can affect the revenue base and cost structure of a firm in the long term, and eventually impact the justifiability of the technology investment decision [22] and the economic value of an investment [43].

As such, interaction effects on current assets and competitor reactions affect the variation in the return on the IT investment and in turn, the variation is the primary mover of the real option value. Hence, the value of a real option can be expressed in terms of interaction effects and competitor reactions.

We assume the value of an IT investment is comprised of EV and Option value [59] and the value of the real option is some function of the interaction effects and competitor reactions. Accordingly, we conjecture that there are no logical leaps in using the two factors as dimensions to derive strategic actions in the context of IT investments.

## 4. Strategic actions in IT investment decision making

Fig. 1 illustrates the model that delineates the relationship between interaction effects and competitor reactions in a real option decision. This section elaborates on these dimensions and discusses their role in real option decision-making.

## 4.1. Interaction effects and competitor reactions

In this section, we discuss strategic actions that are decided in terms of the two dimensions causing variance of return on investment. As discussed in previous sections, we define the three real options as growth, postponement, and abandonment. The growth option includes scaling up, scoping up, and switching up. Switching up has some characteristics related to the other two options. Switching up inevitably involves two options: growth and abandonment option. In other words, when a company decides to switch up its investment, it may give up previous investments and take a different growth path based on the capabilities derived from the previous investments. Switching up may be understood in terms of a differentiation strategy that enables the company to avoid strong competitor reactions and also to gain the benefit of the IT investment. In this paper, we divide the growth option into two categories: switching up and scaling up/scoping up (see Fig. 1).

In this framework, when both interaction effects and competitor reactions are low, a firm can just wait for the arrival of new information such as price, cost, and other market conditions to leverage its prior investment in the lower competitive condition (postponement). The new information may allow the firm to decide whether or not it continues to invest in an IT project. If the cost of that investment can be significantly lowered or the revenue from the investment can be increased considerably, then the company may proceed with the subsequent investment with confidence.

When interaction effects appear to be strong, but competitor reaction is low, a company may scale up and/or scope up the IT investment project. For example, consider the situation where a company builds a web site to communicate with its stakeholders, but industry competitors do not recognize the value of the Internet as a communication medium. Suppose the company in question has previous experience about networking. The company, as a first mover, may build without any pressure from competitors a web site for efficient communication and therefore lower its communications costs significantly. This success may then encourage the firm to invest in electronic commerce in order to capitalize on the current Web site (scaling up or scoping up).

However, when the competitor reactions are strong but interaction effects are low, a firm may scale down and/or scope down, or even give up a subsequent IT investment to capitalize on other IT investments. Suppose that most companies have their own web sites for product sales and their revenues from these sites decrease over time due to pressure from the competition. Moreover, they may have conflicts with their retailers who ask them not to sell their products on the Web. To cope with this environment, regardless of the amount of previous investments, a company may scope and scale down its site to the extent that it can keep up with the market needs without increasing investments in electronic commerce (abandonment).

Finally, given a strong competitor reaction, even though the interaction effects are expected to be high, a firm may be forced to change its future growth path or the direction in terms of IT investment. In this context, the firm tends to seek other opportunities that were not considered in the beginning stages of the IT investment. This action can be referred to as switching up an IT project. For example, during the Web site building project, the firm may determine that selling products on the Web is so difficult that it cannot catch up with the competition. However, at the same time, the firm recognizes that it may capture a particular market segment that provides incumbents with tools for competing, if it utilizes its specific knowledge accumulated from the previous investment and develops a specific service. The firm may then invest in the service or software development and establish competitive advantages in that area. This switching-up strategy is related to business growth patterns even though the new business is not a physical extension of its existing business.

## 4.2. Interaction effects on current assets

As discussed in the last section, every IT investment has its own purpose such as improving service quality, reducing time, and increasing speed. A certain IT investment may give a firm the opportunity to improve firm performance when it complements the company’s existing assets. On the other hand, it may destroy the firms’ competitive advantage when it does not fit into the company’s technological infrastructure. As Garrity and Sanders [21] contend, fit among ‘‘structure, task, people, and technology’’ (cf. Ref. [33]) is the key factor in technology acceptance. A focus only on the task and information technology may ignore the quality of work life of the individual, thereby resulting in deteriorating organizational performance. Recent economic evidence has also shown that firms which invest more in IT and new decentralized work practices earn nearly 5% higher returns on the investment than firms which are low on IT investment and low on new decentralized work practices [10]. Hence, if the interaction effect of an IT investment on existing investments is positive, the investment brings positive feedback effects such as positive return to scale and network externalities to a company by complementing and leveraging existing technologies of the firm [1].

Sometimes, the positive interaction effect of an IT investment inadvertently arises and managers do not recognize it. That is, positive interaction effects more likely arise when a company establishes its technological path [23] and invests in IT at each strategic node toward its strategic goal. In this context, all subsequent investments follow the prior investments and this allows firms to effectively invest in technologies at later stages. Strategic actions are not always the same, as new paths draw on prior investments.

This is the so-called ‘path dependency’ phenomenon [1]. In essence, without prior investment, a company cannot afford to plunge into the next strategic node. The implication is that investment interactions lead to synergy. Moreover, at a certain node, real options allow a firm to exercise the investment option (a subsequent investment) only if an outcome is favorable [37,42].

For example, consider the proliferation of Sun Microsystems’ Java in the Internet environment. Java, alleged as platform independent programming language, was originally created to facilitate the communication among consumer electronics without using heavy and unreliable programming languages. Java is a reflection of the company’s vision of decentralized and heterogeneous computer systems and is based on Sun’s well-developed programming knowledge and strong technical foundation. With the evolution in electronic commerce, the application of Java to the Internet allows Sun Microsystems to establish competitive advantage and create new revenue streams.

## 4.3. Competitor reactions

In contrast to interaction effects, competitor reactions may generate multiple and conflicting cues about the merit of any course of action [22], thereby preventing a firm from obtaining the expected outcome from a certain IT investment. This phenomenon can be addressed by the ‘‘productivity paradox’’ [10]. This paradox reflects the fact that, while IT investment and firms’ productivity have increased greatly, firms’ profits have not been improved commensurately. That is, even though IT investment is imperative to the survival of a firm, it does not necessarily increase firms’ profits because almost all companies have invested in information technology to cope with the competition.

The benefits that a competitor enjoys as a first mover or a dominant player may be interpreted as stock dividends. A stock dividend is the underlying asset of the stock option. The higher a company’s dividend rate, the lower the value of its stock options [25]. Likewise, higher competitors’ revenues from related IT investment will lead to lower values for an IT investment because competitors have already exploited a large portion of benefits. Even though an IT investment project is expected to produce high yields, if potential competitor reactions are also strong, the merit of the investment will be significantly reduced.

This competitive pressure is considered the main reason that companies change their strategic paths, thereby resulting in different strategic actions in new IT investments. For example, one of the authors worked as a consultant for a company that specializes in the transportation business. To cope with competition from companies involved in shipper-carrier marketplace on the Web, the company developed its own Web site. However, the company found a large number of carriers preparing to enter the e-marketplace if it pursued this strategy over the long term. Eventually, the company turned to an application service provider to service its customer base and develop its Internet presence.

## 4.4. Examples

In this section, we will discuss several examples to explain how strategic IT investment actions are related to real option decision making.

## 4.4.1. Scaling up and/or scoping up

The action of scaling up and scoping up takes place when interaction effects are high but competitor reaction is low. Two examples, 3M [57] and Amazon [29], illustrate how the positive interaction effects of an IT investment on existing capabilities and the competitor reaction affect the justifiability of a subsequent IT investment. 3M is well known for innovative products such as Scotch tape and Post-it Notes. The company’s first investments into EC were strictly business-tobusiness electronic data interchange (EDI). 3M introduced EDI to its organization more than 10 years ago as a means of eliminating paperwork, reducing labor through automation of reports, and speeding purchase order processing. It has taken EDI to the next level and used it to check order status as well as to conduct electronic fund transfers, facilitate vendor-managed inventory and send advance ship notices with more than 2000 US-based trading partners.

As communications technologies have evolved over the last few years, 3M has gradually expanded its definition of electronic commerce to embrace these changes. Eventually, the portfolio of electronic commerce initiatives at 3M included electronic catalogs, direct sales and customer service via the Web, as well as a fax-on-demand service. These series of actions are based on the knowledge and experience gained from EDI investment and focus primarily on the process and performance improvement of the supply chain.

Online book retailing giant Amazon is pursuing a growth option or scaling up as it sells books, videos, toys and home improvement tools, as well as electronic goods. Amazon acquired a 5% stake in Audible, a company that delivers audio versions of books and newspapers online. This contract gives Amazon’s 16 plus million customers access to more than 20,000 hours of high-quality digital audio content from newspapers, magazines and best-selling authors available at Audible. This alliance certainly has the potential to provide synergy effects to both companies.

## 4.4.2. Switching up

The action of switching up takes place when both competitor reactions and interaction effects are high. The Perfumania case [30] explains the relationship between IT investment decision-making and high competitor reaction and interaction effects of an IT investment. Perfumania, which has annual sales of about \$180 million, originally pursued an Internet discount perfumery chain, but recently announced a dramatic shift to becoming a holding company for ecommerce ventures. The company developed tremendous online marketing savvy and Internet knowledge from its customers and experiences on the Web. Perfumania is essentially abandoning the online retailing business, taking on the name of E Com Ventures, and concentrating on the consulting and venture capital industry.

This shift in its strategy has two primary benefits. The first is that Perfumania is able to leverage its online retailing experience and avoid the increased pressure from online rivals such as Beauty.com and Drugstore.com. They have held a large share of the beauty market. By capitalizing on the experience its management acquired during the development and spin-off of Perfumania.com and PostAcard.com, the company developed e-business knowledge and technological expertise for constructing a Web portal for venture capital.

## 4.4.3. Postponement

The postponement action in an IT investment decision-making happens when both competitor reactions and interaction effects are low. This is illustrated in the case of Britannica [48]. When the venerable Encyclopedia Britannica announced it would no longer sell through retailers but would offer the contents of its 32-volume set free on the Internet, reaction among principal rivals such as Grolier and Encarta was to wait and see how well Britannica would fare. Grolier and Encarta knew that an investment in the Internet may provide them benefits, but they were not sure about the certainty of success.

Grolier Publishing, which publishes three encyclopedias to different age groups and has a sales force to sell its products online and to schools and libraries, decided to continue to derive revenues from subscriptions from schools and libraries. Encarta Encyclopedia, which is the leading seller at the retail level with a 43% market share, does not put its online set on a free basis. There are many complex questions in this example, such as the uncertainty of Britannica’ success in the online look-it-up market, the benefits of actual book publishers from Britannica’s defection, and the possible responses of the other online reference publishers to their old rival’s new strategy. This uncertainty could be the primary reason that companies embark on the postponement strategy.

## 4.4.4. Abandonment

The abandonment action, which has not gained much attention from managers [20], happens when interaction effects are low but competitor reactions are strong. According to Ewusi-Mensah and Przasnyski [20], IS project abandonment is a complex multidimensional issue including such factors as cost overruns and schedule slippages, technological inadequacies, and behavioral, political, or organizational issues. Recent examples from the online toy retail industry [51] show that abandonment occurs in situations where there are significant competitor reactions.

Toysmart.com, a privately held Internet toy retailer that is majority controlled by Walt Disney, ceased operations because it faced fierce competitions from very strong players in the online toy market. Two weeks before Toysmart announced its withdrawal, Red Rocket, an online retailer of educational toys owned by media giant Viacom, ceased operations, citing difficulties in sustaining its business. One week after Red Rocket’s announcement, Toysmart and

KBkids.com announced employee departures and layoffs. It is clear that entry into this hyper-competitive online market will require deep pockets for survival and above-average internal efficiency to cope with the competition.

## 5. Dynamics of the strategic actions in IT investment

In the previous section, the paper defined four strategic actions of IT investment at a point of time in terms of interaction effects and competitor reactions. The strategic actions will evolve over time as interaction effects and competitor reactions unfold.

Increasing returns and diminishing costs and accumulation of technological capabilities lead to different paths [7,17,41,56]. That is, ‘‘what a firm can hope to do technologically in the future is primarily constrained by what it has been capable of doing in the past [19].’’ The factors influencing such path dependencies are the accumulation of experience, network externalities, economies of scale, informational increasing returns, and technological inter-relatedness [47]. The recency effect is critical in strategic options as decision-makers tend to refer to the conditions of the last period to decide what kind of strategic actions to take in the present period. This is a natural situation for employing a Markov process to understand the dynamics of making strategic investment decisions via real option theory (cf. Ref. [41]).

In this model, strategic actions are assumed as the state space, and each strategic action has its own transition probability (see Fig. 2). As discussed above, the transition of each strategic action to the next state depends primarily on the strategic action a company took in the last period. For example, if scaling up or switching up was selected in the last period, scaling up and switching up are likely to be selected in the current period. ## 6. Conclusion

This paper has developed a framework of strategic actions that can be used to evaluate IT investments based on the real option concept. This method may lead to an objective and better-structured decision process, because it incorporates the linkage among economic value, real option value, and IT investments. In addition, it simplifies the real option model to a two-factor model (interaction effects and competitor reactions) to focus on the basic factors influencing the variance of return on investment. Using this framework, IT managers can understand the strategic impacts of an IT investment and then evaluate IT investments. The framework can also be used by companies to set up a well-structured portfolio strategy for future IT investments. This will in turn allow companies to harvest higher and more consistent returns and to reduce the risk embedded in IT investments. For example, most firms have updated their information systems to deal with Y2K problem and in turn, have developed several options with the latest networking technologies that may allow them to aggressively pursue an e-business strategy. It should also be noted that the real option strategy can be applied incorrectly. For example, Amazon may have been too aggressive in pursuing its diversification strategy, only to lose their strategic focus and brand integrity. This is in part because they have been very concerned with competitor reactions and did not examine the interaction effects of their investments.

At the industry level, firms may try to proactively change the dynamics of the competitor reaction dimension. Strategic alliances for developing industry standards are a good example. Such standardization may enhance compatibility and increase value. It could also reduce the technology risk faced by firms in the industry and reduce switching costs of users [56]. In this case, strategic alliances and participation in the development of industry standards can be viewed as a real option.

At the societal level, the real option framework can provide governments with an evaluation tool for technological investments. In essence, the real options approach can be used by countries in technological planning. For example, during the global economic crisis, the Korean Ministry of Information and Communication increased investments in IT infrastructure in some areas and also supported IT venture companies in the development of businesses centered around creative products and services. This policy has been very successful in creating national wealth [12]. The Japan Ministry of Economy, Trade, and Industry (http://www.meti.go.jp) and the US Department of Commerce (http://www.ecommerce.gov/) provide technological information regarding industry trends and analytical insights to support profit and nonprofit organizations. Although this investment in infrastructure is not in the form of direct monetary support, it may be considered as a real option for the support of nationwide knowledge management systems.

The real options approach has several minor limitations that are similar to the problems encountered in applying the option theory to practice. In the Black– Scholes option-pricing model, the time to exercisedate and variance per period of rate of return on the asset must be estimated. While the estimation of the time to exercise-date is relatively straightforward, obtaining a reliable estimator of the variance is very difficult [59]. Furthermore, in order to apply the optionpricing model to the IT investment valuation, specific assumptions regarding risk must be made [5]. IT projects are accompanied by both technical risk and organizational risk and they may not be priced by financial markets [59]. This paper has tried to avoid the problems in risk incorporation and variance estimation by directly using the root causes of variability such as the interaction effects of an IT investment and competitor reactions. However, timing is still an issue. This paper relegates the timing decision to managers, as it should, without addressing any specific factors to be used for deciding the exercise time.

This paper has focused on two root causes of variance, namely interaction effects and competitor reactions. These causes can be used to take actions, but technical risks (technological obsolescence and compatibility) are areas for future study. We are confident that through this framework, IT managers can understand what factors influence the value of their IT investment and what strategic actions they can take.
