# Decision-making under time pressure with different information sources and performance-based financial incentives—Part 2

James R. Marsden <sup>a,</sup>\*<sup>,1</sup>, Ramakrishnan Pakath <sup>b,2</sup>, Kustim Wibowo <sup>b,2,3</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Shenkman Chair in e-Business, Operations and Information Management Department, School of Business U 41 1M, University of Connecticut, Storrs, CT 06269-1041, USA

<sup>b</sup>Decision Science and Information Systems, School of Management, C.M. Gatton College of Business and Economics, University of Kentucky, Lexington, KY 40506-0034, USA

Received 1 August 2000; received in revised form 1 June 2001; accepted 1 August 200

## Abstract

In Part 2, we examine the viability of the new symbolic language that we described in Part 1, in a specific setting. Using an abstract classification task that involves decision-making under time pressure, we study multiple measures of subject performance at this task using the new language vis-a\`-vis written and spoken English. Initial experimental results suggest that, despite its relative novelty, the proposed language is at least as effective as the more traditional communication modes in the specific setting examined, while succinctly conveying what must be conveyed. <sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Decision-making; Time pressure; Symbolic language; Multimedia systems; Mobile computing; Ex-ante DSS evaluation; Induced value theory

## 1. Introduction

In Part 1 [3], we provided the necessary background for this work and also described the construction and refinement of our experimental platform and an accompanying subject training software suite. Here we expand on the description of the platform, detail our experimental design, and present our results. In conjunction with three task complexity levels and a reward – penalty structure, specific time parameter settings enable us to operationalize the concepts of explicit and implicit time pressures. We describe this parameter choice process in Section 2. In Section 3, we review the goal of our exploratory research effort, describe our experimental design, and provide a rationale for both. Section 4 describes the construction of the reward/penalty mechanism. Section 5 sets forth our experimental findings. Section 6 provides concluding thoughts.

## 2. Determining critical control parameter settings

In order to determine three critical time bounds, namely, the Standard Rule Learning Time (SRLT),

Standard Help Time (SHT), and Standard Decision-Making Time (SDMT), we used student volunteers. These volunteers participated in a training session prior to using the platform. The lab conditions and general environment were maintained as intended for the real experiment. The only exception was that when determining SRLT and SDMT, we disabled the system help facility (which we re-enabled when determining the SHT). For determining these two time bounds, each participant, while encouraged to be as efficient as possible, was allowed as much time as needed to study each rule set and to make each decision. From our own prior experience with the platform, we expected it would take a participant between 45 and 60 min to complete the training session and 4 min or less to study a three-rule rule set and make a decision. We used a randomization procedure to assign rule sets and six experiments to each volunteer, two from each mode, such that no two adjacent experiments were from the same mode. Thus, each volunteer spent no more than 1.5 h on this pre-test.

Volunteers proceed through the six experiments assigned to them as follows. Suppose the first assignment is a Text-Level 2-Slower experiment. A set of three rules fitting these characteristics is randomly generated and offered for study. Once a volunteer is ready to proceed, a sample fitting one of the three rules is randomly generated and offered for scrutiny. Whenever the volunteer confirms a final decision, the next experiment (say, an Image-Level 1-Slower experiment) is similarly generated. This procedure continues until the volunteer has completed all six assigned experiments. The system records the time taken to study each rule set, make each decision, and the decision quality (i.e., correctness). We used these measurements to determine the SRLT and SDMT values.

The rule learning time data (in seconds) gathered was organized by mode, speed, and complexity level (see Table 1). As one proceeds from Level 1 to Level 2 to Level 3, the mean rule-learning times generally increase regardless of mode and speed. The only exception occurs with the Audio-Slower information source where the mean learning time for Level 2 is appreciably larger than that for Level 3. However, this may well be an aberration given the very high variance of this set of observations.

We wished to make sure that whatever SRLT was chosen, it was adequate to comfortably facilitate rule learning for all three modes, speeds, and complexity levels. Picking a standard time of 2 min results in substantial portions of the observations being excluded (i.e., exceeding the 2-min bound). For instance, one third of each of the < Text, Slower > and < Audio, Slower> cases would be excluded. Moving up to 3 min leaves 28% of the < Audio, Slower> observations excluded. However, we chose 3 min as the SRLT as this choice would include approximately 95% of all rule learning times recorded. An SRLT of 3 min would likely enable a substantial number of subjects to review each rule set at least once in the more complex Level 3 setting and more than once in the simplest Level 1 case.

Observe that the decision time data, when viewed from the perspective of the three time windows mentioned (i.e., V 2 s, >2 and V 5 s, >5 s), has a layout with the bulk of the values in all three cases (correct, incorrect, and overall) falling in the middle window. The proportion of incorrect decisions falling in the >5 s window is larger than those in the V 2 s window whereas just the opposite is true for the correct decisions. Finally, note that about 46% of all decisions (and 48% of all correct decisions) were made in 3 s or less, without system help. These observations lead us to the final structure of our reward/penalty matrix (discussed in Section 4) and our choice of an SDMT value (discussed next).

As noted earlier, the built-in help facility was deactivated for the sessions used to gather data on rule learning times and decision-making times. In our actual experiments, the choice to use the help facility was part of the process to be analyzed: rewards for correct decisions are smaller and penalties for incorrect decisions are higher with the usage of the help feature.

In a separate pre-test of the help facility, five students were asked to invoke and use the help facility for all three modes and complexity levels. Each participant was asked to execute the steps of the help routine one time for two sample problems drawn from each mode-complexity combination (only one speed setting, Normal, was used since the speed setting did not relate to the help facility time). The subjects were encouraged to adopt a pace that was comfortable to them. Thus, each participant yielded 18 ‘‘help time’’ values for a total of 90 values for all five participants.

Table 3 contains the help time data (in seconds) thus generated. In general, for all three modes, the help times increase with increasing complexity. About 82% of the help times are V 39 s. With 39 s as the choice for SHT, we could allow a SDMT of 39 + 3 s = 42 s (with system help), given that 48% of the correct decisions made without help were made within 3 s. It was our belief that, in reality, a subject would probably need just a second or two following help invocation to click and confirm a bin choice. Thus, we expected that, in reality, subjects would have as much as 40 to 41 s available for help facility usage with a 42-s SDMT. Therefore, about 85% to 90% of the observed help-time values would most likely be covered with this choice for the SDMT value.

In sum, we set the SRLT at 180 s, the SDMT at 42 s, and the SHT at 39 s (with the SHT being part of the SDMT). We next discuss how we used these time bounds in the experimental design for our exploratory study.

## 3. The goal of the exploratory study and the study design

Our broad research goal was assessing whether our new, symbolic language held potential for adequately conveying abstract notions. Utilizing a hypothetical task, this exploratory study addressed the following question: In our experimental setting, is there any evidence that the proposed symbolic language (Image) is at least as good as the conventional Audio and Text alternatives? With the novelty of our symbolic language pitted against the familiarity of written and spoken English, there was a built in bias against the former. This follows a standard approach used in the philosophy of science, where the burden of proof is placed against a new approach. As we discussed in Ref. [3], examining this issue is particularly relevant to the design of support devices that offer relatively limited displays and memory.

Given the flexibility of the platform, there were many ways in which we could conduct our preliminary investigations. With three modes, three speeds, and three complexity levels, the platform allows us the flexibility of examining 27 mode–complexity– speed combinations. Given the exploratory nature of this study, and the presence of budgetary, temporal, and subject availability constraints (discussed below), we concentrated on investigating specific combinations that also necessitated the re-use of subjects in different experimental settings. With this approach, care must be taken to avoid carryover effects impacting the analyses. Carryover effects occur when the sequence in which an individual operates in the settings impacts the results. To mitigate such problems, we proceeded as follows.

First, we chose to examine the following three of the more-interesting mode sequences—Text, Image, and Audio (TIA); Audio, Image, and Text (AIT); and Image, Text, and Audio (ITA). Text and Audio are identical in content except for the fact that Text is displayed and Audio is orally conveyed. Both of these modes make use of English, a language for which we required fluency of all our subjects. Image, to the best of our knowledge, was not a ‘‘language’’ familiar to any of our subjects. In TIA and AIT, we have placed Image between the relatively more familiar Text and Audio, with Text preceding Audio in TIA and viceversa in AIT. In ITA, a subject begins with the relatively unfamiliar Image.

Secondly, with regard to complexity levels and speeds, we chose the following fixed sequences for all three mode arrangements. A subject would move from a set of Level 1 experiments to a set of (more complex) Level 2 experiments and, finally, to a set of Level 3 experiments. At each level, a subject would progress from a set of Normal speed experiments to a set of Slower speed experiments and then to a set of Faster speed experiments.

Different groups of subjects would be used in each of the three chosen mode sequences, allowing us to statistically test for the presence of carryover effects resulting from mode arrangements, if any. Given this general picture, we elaborate on the three constraining factors: budgetary, subject-related, and temporal—that lead to our specific experimental design.

## 3.1. The influence of budgetary constraints

Following the induced value approach of Smith [6– 8], we sought to establish a performance-based financial reward scheme, in addition to a fixed participation fee, with the goal of driving each subject to strive for the best possible performance that he/she could offer. A total budget of \$2000.00 provided for \$1750.00 as the maximum available for subject payments.

A subject assigned to a given mode sequence would participate at approximately the same time on three consecutive days, earning a show-up fee of \$5.00 per day (a typical amount). However, a subject who missed any one of the three sessions would forfeit all money earned—both rewards and show-up fees. Thus, we budgeted for a minimum of \$15.00 per participant. This incentive was intended to make it attractive for an individual to participate in the experiment even if he/ she thought they might earn nothing in terms of performance-based payoff. A key goal was to avoid subject dropout, a problem that has plagued other experimental work (such as Ref. [5]).

Following conventional wisdom, we also decided to limit lab sessions to 2 h each. To aid subjects in viewing time spent in a session as being worthwhile, we structured the payoffs (see Section 4) such that a subject could earn as much as four times the prevailing hourly minimum wage (\$4.25) during a session. This required that we budget 3<sub>\*</sub>4<sub>\*</sub> \$4.25=\$51.00 per subject for three sessions. With an available subject payment budget of \$1750.00 and a \$100.00 ‘‘safety factor,’’ the total number of subjects possible is \$1650.00/ \$51.00 = 32. This translates to $3 2 / 3 \approx 1 0$ subjects per sequence for a total of about 30 subjects. (As detailed below, our use of 27 subjects resulted in payments totaling \$1516.25, averaging \$56.15 per subject.)

## 3.2. The influence of subject-related constraints

Another hurdle that we faced was acquiring enough qualified, willing, and as homogenous a group of subjects as we could find. As one step towards ensuring this, we decided to focus solely on the undergraduate population of the University of Kentucky in selecting participants. Undergraduates form a substantially larger chunk of the student group in comparison with the masters and doctoral student populations. Further, although it is customary in the MIS literature to use MBA students, we felt that this was unnecessary given our adoption of the ex-ante evaluation methodology (hypothetical system with a hypothetical task) and our careful incorporation of Smith’s induced value approach.

For consistency, each subject was exposed to the Training Tool Set prior to beginning the experiments. This 45 to 60 min training session was to be conducted prior to the first experimental session and was to be an added, unpaid time commitment on the part of each participant that restricted our participant pool.

Fatigue was a concern in limiting experimental session length to 2 h, which is in line with conventional wisdom in this arena. Once a session began, a subject was required to complete the entire session without taking a break. While we did not physically restrain anybody from taking a break, each experimental session itself was designed without any planned breaks between experiments (as induced pressure was part of the design). This meant that classification rule sets and associated samples would automatically continue being generated and an absentee subject risked loosing some portion of the accruable rewards. As such, none of our subjects actually took any breaks.

## 3.3. The influence of time constraints

We now consider the impact on problem replications of a 2-h maximum time bound for each experimental session. Recall from prior discussion that the SRLT and SDMT were set at 180 and 42 s, respectively. Following the display of a rule set (pertaining to a particular complexity level–speed combination for a given mode), a certain number of sample objects are offered to a participant for classification. We have a total of nine complexity level – speed combinations. This means that, within a 2-h session, we had approximately 800 s available per combination.

We utilized the 800 s in the following manner. With a limit of three rules per rule set, the SRLT would not become prohibitively large for the more complex Level 2 and Level 3 rules. We also settled on two samples per rule set. Allowing three rule set displays, each followed by the presentation of two sample objects for classification, would result in consuming a total of $3 * ( 1 8 0 + 4 2 + 4 2 ) = 7 9 2$ s, in the worst case. The remaining 800–792 = 8 s of slack time would allow the system some time for its rule set and sample generation activities. With this in mind, we created the final experimental design matrix shown in Table 4. The table shows six experiments in each cell (i.e., complexity level – speed combination) for a given mode, for a total of 54 experiments in a given session.

The worst-case time scenarios for the three modes, which also account for the actual sample display times for the various samples, are shown in Table 5. Note, from the very last column, that it takes no more than about 2 h for a subject to complete a session using any of the three modes. In reality, as discussed in the following section, subjects used help very infrequently. Thus, they took much less than 2 h to complete each session. This reinforces our belief that the actual average payoff of about \$56.00 dollars for three sessions (or about \$19.00 per session), should have been a strong motivator for our subjects to perform their best.

## 3.4. Obtaining and assigning subjects to treatments

Significant effort was put into attracting volunteer subjects. Despite these efforts, to reach the 27 subjects required the inclusion of nine subjects who had participated in the SRLT- and SDMT-related pre-tests discussed earlier.<sup>4</sup> Subjects were randomly assigned to the treatment sequences necessary to yield nine participants in each. The number of subjects from various academic majors was as follows: Marketing—2, Management—2, Economics—1, Finance—6, Accounting —1, Decision Science and Information Systems—2, Architecture—1, Forestry—2, Pharmacy—1, English—1, Mechanical Engineering—1, Chemical Engineering—1, Electrical Engineering—2, Marketing and Finance—1, Accounting and Advertising—1, Marketing and Management—1, and Marketing and Finance—1.

Each subject was first asked to appear for a training session to insure that all subjects were familiar with the environment, decision problem setting, and experimental procedures. Before beginning the session, the subjects were asked to read and sign a General Information Sheet that described the terms and conditions of their participation, the reward/penalty structure, and such. Subjects participated in the first session of their sequence on the same day, following a 15-min break. Thereafter, they were asked to show up at approximately the same time the next day and the day after to participate in the next two sessions. Thus, each session was separated by about 24 h. Prior to beginning the first session, and after training, each subject was administered a short questionnaire (see Appendix A) that attempts to elicit their pre-experimental perceptions about the various modes, speeds, and complexities. We use the data gathered to conduct a comparative analysis of actual versus expected performance.

## 5. Analyses of experimental findings

We collected experimental data on eight specific measures for all subjects within each of several predefined categories of experiments, such as, ‘‘all Text-Level 1-Normal experiments’’ (see Section 5.2 for a complete listing of categories). The measures used were:

(1) Average Earnings (AE): average of the monetary rewards earned (cents);

(2) Decision Accuracy Rate (DAR): percentage of correct decisions;

(3) Average Decision Time (ADT): average of the decision-making times (in seconds);

(4) Average Rule-learning Time (ART): average duration (in seconds) spent (presumably) studying and absorbing the rules in a rule set;

(5) Average Number of Sample Reviews (ANS): average number of times that one or more of a sample object’s views were reviewed by a subject before making and confirming a final decision;

(6) Average Number of Cancels (ANC): average number of times that a subject changed his/her mind about a bin choice before confirming a final decision;

(7) Average Number of Help Uses (ANH): average number of times help system is accessed before confirming a final decision; and

(8) Average Number of Rule Reviews (ANR): average number of rule set invocations before confirming a final decision.

The eight measures may be grouped as follows: (a) AE, DAR, ADT and ART may be viewed as ‘‘timeand-money’’ measures that are primary measures of subject performance; and (b) ANS, ANC, ANH, and ANR may be viewed as measures of ‘‘activity’’ during the learning and decision-making phases.

Given the very conservative estimates provided by the theorem, we could expect all category values of interest to a particular preference ordering analysis to be contained within three contiguous intervals of the type we have defined.

## 5.3.2. Discussion

In our experiments, subjects recorded the best performance with Image in terms of rule learning time and effort, decision quality, and subject earnings (i.e., ART, ANR, DAR, and AE). Subjects seem to have grasped visually displayed rules using imagery quicker and more accurately than rules expressed using sound as evidenced by the relatively higher ART and ANR and relatively lower DAR and AE ranks for Audio. Textually expressed rules caused subjects to spend about as much time and effort in learning as spoken rules. However, unlike Audio, learning seems to have been more successful with Text as its DAR and AE are nearly on par with those for Image.

Our findings parallel recent-past and current trends observed in computer-to-human communication where pure-text interfaces have given way to iconic interfaces and audio is used only in very specialized settings. However, the superior results with Image in our experiments are intriguing considering that, unlike Audio and Text, the vocabulary used in Image was not something that our subjects would have been exposed to in prior computer interaction and other settings. Thus, while prior, learned preferences for textual and aural interactions with a computer might help explain the relative preferences observed for Text and Audio in our experiments, we believe no scope for such bias exists with Image. Our imagery is markedly different from icons used in modern computing and other environments (e.g., road signs) in that it has no known real-world analogies. Ease-of-comprehension of an icon’s intent is a paramount focus in these environments. Our Image mode was quite arcane, given the abstract context, with succinctness and accuracy of representation being the paramount focus. Yet, Image did as well as it did in our study.

We also found it intriguing that, despite seemingly absorbing rules relatively rapidly with Image, subjects tended to spend the most time (ADT) and effort (ANS) on making decisions with this mode. The ANS for Image, notably, is nearly twice the average of 1.9204 for the three modes. We venture one possible explanation for this phenomenon.

In Text and Audio, information is presented word by word, with pauses between words. For example, suppose we wish to present the information, ‘‘View two has three pins.’’ Depending on the speed setting (i.e., Slower, Normal, or Faster), given their sequential, character/word-at-a-time presentation approach, this takes between 2 and 4 s with Text and Audio. The complete statement is only ‘‘available’’ at the end of this interval. Image, however, conveys all of the information almost instantly, in graphical form, as follows:

![](/api/attachments/5XS6J7M5/fulltext/images/74053dc6e40c6bf00390047b578700807fb9a580cc5df9c46c464f7844b85cc4.jpg)

However, to make sure that the flow rate for this mode is commensurate with that for the corresponding Text and Audio renderings, the image continues to be displayed for between 2 and 4 s, depending on the speed setting. Thus, with Image, a subject may observe the complete view-related information almost instantly and keep absorbing it until the display time is over. Possibly, the more ‘‘integrated’’ nature of the displayed information, and the added exposure to it, both play a role in enabling our subjects to more easily comprehend/memorize what is displayed and, hence, the smaller ART, ANR, and ANH values with Image. At the same time, given its novelty, it is possible that our subjects had trouble recalling rules and sample features in Image, thus accounting for its larger ADT and ANS values. Likewise, it is also possible that our subjects found the task of mapping/ correlating the sample information with the previously acquired rule information more time consuming with Image. This, too, could have contributed to the larger ADT values.

It also appears that the mapping/short-term recall difficulties mentioned earlier, coupled with the structuring of the reward mechanism, has resulted in the values for ANS being higher than those for ANH. Recall that, once exposed to a sample, a subject has three choices: (a) make a decision (perhaps after some delay time), (b) review sample information (before making a decision), (c) use recalled sample information to seek system help (before making a decision), and (d) use approaches (b) and (c) together. Rewards are time and accuracy based with decisions made with system help being less valuable than those made without such help. While only seven subjects reviewed sample information and only eight used the system help facility, the total number of sample reviews by the seven subjects is 84, whereas the total number of system help invocations is 50. Further, one subject alone utilized the help facility a total of 37 times.

Also, observe that the relationship between the ANS and ANH rankings is just the opposite for Audio. Subjects seem to have relied more heavily on the help facility and placed more confidence in or, more likely, blindly accepted the system’s recommendation as evidenced by the smallest ANC rank for Audio.

We next examine mode preferences at various speeds and complexity levels to see if the overall picture just discussed persists within these categories or if there are any differences.

## 5.3.3. Speed-based mode preferences

Table 9 summarizes the experimental outcomes for various speed levels. A comparison of this table with Table 8 reveals that there indeed are a few categorywise differences vis-a\`-vis the aggregate picture found in the latter.

5.3.3.1. Normal speed. Consider Normal speed first. Focusing on the time-and-money measures, Image continues (as in Table 8) to be dominant in terms of rule learning time and nearly so in decision accuracy, being only marginally inferior to Audio. While it remains the worst in terms of decisionmaking time, it is appreciably worse than Text in this regard. Consequently, unlike in Table 8 where Image dominated overall in terms of the primary measures, we see that it is appreciably worse than Audio when the information source operates at Normal speed.

As in Table 8, Text ties with Audio as more expensive than Image in rule learning time. Now, however, it is nearly as desirable as Audio with respect to decision time. While it posted the worst accuracy rates, earnings were nearly on par with that for Audio and appreciably better than that for Image. In terms of these four measures, Audio was the overall best performer at Normal speed with Text and Image following, a marked departure from the overall trend observed in Table 8.

Consider the activity-related measures next. The measure rankings are identical to that in Table 8 and the preference orderings nearly so with the only difference being that Text is marginally better than Image with regard to indecisiveness (ANC). As such, the rank sums are identical to that in Table 8 suggesting no overall differences between the three modes at Normal speed.

It appears that, at Normal speed, subjects retained the sample information better with Audio than with Image and fruitfully exploited system help to make quick, correct, albeit less lucrative, decisions. With both Text and Image, subjects tended to not want to use help. This resulted in relatively greater levels of indecision, relatively larger decision-making times for Image, and relatively poorer decision accuracy levels for Text. It appears, however, that the errors with Text were somewhat offset by the reliance on system help with Audio resulting in commensurate earnings with these two modes. In the case of Image, despite the lack of reliance on system help, the very large decision times and the marginally-worse-than-Audio accuracies, resulted in it being tied for last place with Text, overall.

In sum, Audio outperforms both Image and Text in terms of results, but ties with them in terms of effort, when the information source operates at Normal speed.

5.3.3.2. Slower speed. At Slower speed, the primary preference orderings reveal only marginal deviations from those in Table 8, and hence the primary rankings (and rank sums) in the two tables are identical. Thus, Image is preferred to Text, which is preferred to Audio.

Likewise, the secondary preference orderings are only marginally different in three out of four cases and, therefore, the rankings for these three cases coincide in the two tables. The only exception is ANC, where Image reveals the least indecisiveness, with Text and Audio being commensurate. Consequently, the rank sums for the secondary measures show Image as being the most preferred, followed by Text and Audio in that order (whereas, no such preference could be discerned in Table 8).

The picture that emerges is that both in terms of effort and results, Image outperforms Text and Audio when the information source operates at Slower speed.

5.3.3.3. Faster speed. The findings for Faster speed parallel those for Slower. Once again, the preference orderings for nearly all measures coincide with those in Table 8. The only exception is with ANC where Audio and Image are both preferable to Text. Consequently, the rank sums for the primary measures suggest that Image is the most preferred followed by Text and Audio, while those for the secondary measures reveal Image as the most desirable with Text and Audio being tied for second place.

As such, as with Slower speed, Image dominates both Text and Audio at Faster speed, in terms of both results and effort.

## 5.3.4. Discussion

Going into these experiments, our a priori belief was that at all three speeds Image would dominate followed by Text and Audio, respectively. While our findings at the Slower and Faster speeds are in line with expectations, we found that we were mistaken about how well Audio would do at Normal speed (and how poorly Image would).

We believe that the results observed are quite likely due to the manner in which we created the three speed classes within each mode. Recall, from Ref. [3], that we created the Normal-Audio recordings first. All other mode-speed combinations were derived from these combinations. Perhaps it ought not to come as a surprise, then, that Audio did as well as it did at Normal speed. In other words, while we held Normal speed constant across all three modes for experimental control purposes, what is ‘‘normal’’ speed for speech appears not to be so for delivering written or imageintensive messages. The same observation would apply to our Faster and Slower speed classes.

There are two other important points we wish to make. First, although in these experiments Image did not do as well as Audio overall at Normal speed, Image is in the same league as Audio in terms of decision accuracy while being appreciably better in terms of preparatory effort. It is also noteworthy that whatever earnings were obtained with Image, were obtained with appreciably less reliance on system help than with Audio.

Second, in reality, it is possible that the information that originates at a particular speed (say, Normal) ultimately arrives at its destination at an aberrant pace (e.g., Faster or Slower). This is especially true when vast distances separate source and sink or the intervening communication mechanism is unreliable (as is generally true of the Internet). What our experiments reveal is that in the presence of deviations from normality (which is essentially what we did when we began with an Audio-Normal source and created all of the remaining sources), Image is the preferred communication mode.

## 5.3.5. Complexity level-based mode preferences

Table 10 summarizes the experimental results for the three complexity levels. A comparison of this table with Table 8 reveals a somewhat larger number of category-wise differences than we observed when comparing Tables 8 and 9.

5.3.5.1. Complexity Level 1. At Level 1, the primary measure preference orderings and rankings coincide almost entirely with those in Table 8. The only exception is the preference ordering for DAR, which is marginally different. Consequently, the Image rank sums for the primary measures at Level 1 are identical to the overall rank sums in Table 8. Likewise, the orderings and rankings for three of the four activity measures are commensurate with the corresponding entries in Table 8, barring a few nonconsequential preference differences. The exception here is the ANH measure. At Level 1, Audio records the fewest number of system help requests whereas overall (i.e., in Table 8) it logged the most. Consequently, Audio emerges as the best mode overall in terms of the secondary measures at Level 1 although we could discern no such dominance in Table 8.

In sum, with a Level 1 source, Image is the most preferred in terms of results whereas it ties with Text as being appreciably more demanding than Audio in terms of effort.

5.3.5.2. Complexity Level 2. More marked deviations from the overall results in Table 8 appear at Level 2. Only two of the four primary measure rankings tie with those found in Table 8. While Image ranks high in terms of accuracy with a Level 2 source, it proves to be the worst in terms of decision time and nearly so, as a consequence, in terms of earnings. The net impact is that Image trades places with Text to become the second most desirable mode in terms of the primary measures, whereas it was the most desirable at Level 1.

The activity-related measures, however, are more in line with those found in Table 8. The only exception is the decisiveness-related measure, ANC, where we see that Image ties with Audio as being superior to Text. Consequently, Image registers a slight preference over Audio and Text at Level 2, whereas all three modes appeared commensurate in Table 8.

Thus, with a Level 2 source, Image emerges as the most desirable in terms of effort but is second best in terms of results.

5.3.5.3. Complexity Level 3. Once again, just two of the four primary measures in Tables 8 and 10 coincide albeit with slight preference ordering differences. Image records the best ranks for both decision accuracy and earnings while continuing to hold on to its number one rank for rule learning time. Consequently, Image records its best overall primary score ever at Level 3. As with Level 2, the activity measures coincide in three of four measures. In the lone exception, ANC, Image dominates. Consequently, Image also emerges as the best overall performer in terms of the secondary measures.

In conclusion, Image dominates in terms of both results and effort with a Level 3 source.

## 5.3.6. Discussion

Our pre-experimental expectation was that we would find Image dominating at all three complexity levels followed by Text and Audio, in that order. Our reasons were based on the old adage, ‘‘A picture is worth a thousand words,’’ although in our case, our pictures are closer to abstract art than portraits.

The trends analyses findings both supports and refutes this viewpoint, depending on perspective. Considering the primary and secondary measures separately, Image is the most preferred in terms of results at Level 1 and Level 3 and effort at Level 2 and Level 3. If one were to combine both classes of measures (by summing the respective rank sums at each level), Image is no worse than the other modes at any of the three levels and its standing seems to improve with complexity (the respective combined rank sums are 15.5, 15.0, and 12.0 for the three levels). Specifically, Image ties with Audio as being the most preferred at Level 1, ties with Text as being most desirable at Level 2, and is by far the most superior at Level 3.

What surprised us, however, was the relative performance of Text across the three levels. While Audio registers combined rank sums of 15.5, 18.0, and 18.0, Text resulted in a somewhat baffling 17.0, 15.0 and 18.0. Thus, while the Audio and Image performances across levels are more consistent with our expectations, it appears that Text performance is more sensitive to changes in complexity and in a counterintuitive way. What is discernible, though, is that given a scenario with varying complexity levels, performance with Image would likely be appreciably better overall than with either Text or Audio.

It intrigues us that, even in our abstract imagery, ‘‘cues,’’ such as those obviously incorporated in icons, are perhaps unintentionally available. Also, there perhaps is a level of complexity at which Image becomes an impediment to human comprehension. This is an issue that calls for further investigations.

## 6. Concluding remarks

In Section 5, we examined a single source element, Mode, for trends, both in isolation and in the context of the remaining two elements—Speed and Complexity. (We repeated the analyses for these two source elements although we are not reporting our findings here). We also used the trends analysis rankings to draw aggregate inferences about all 27 information sources in terms of the four primary measures, as follows.

Consider the source, < Text, Level 1, Slower>, as an example. We have primary measure rank sums for each of the elements in this source vector in the context of the remaining two elements. Using these, we computed average rank sums, called element scores, for each element in the vector. We then averaged the three element scores to obtain an overall score for this source. In this example, Text had a rank sum of 8 at Level 1 and a rank sum of 8, again, at Slower speed. Thus the element score for Text in this vector is (8 + 8)/2 = 8. Proceeding similarly, we obtained the element score for Level 1=(4.5 + 4)/2 = 4.25, and that for Slower = (6 + 8.5)/2 = 7.25. Thus the overall score for the source, < Text, Level 1, Slower>, is (8 + 4.25 + 7.25)/3 = 19.5/ 3 = 6.50.

Proceeding as above, we obtained scores for each of the 27 sources used in this experiment. Table 11 shows the resultant descriptive statistics for the 27 sources organized by mode.

The best possible score is (4 + 4 + 4)/3 = 4. Notice from the spans (ranges) of the scores, that Audio has the tightest span and the smallest variance, followed by Text and Image, in that sequence. However, from the perspective of the mean, the median, the minimum, and the maximum scores, Image is the most attractive. If one were to order the information sources from the most desirable (the one with the smallest score) to the least, and sum the ranks for all sources using a particular mode, the rank sums are 128 for Text, 106 for Image, and 144 for Audio (with score sums of 72.50, 68.75, and 74.75, respectively). Again, both sets of statistics reiterate the preference orderings that we discerned from the overall analysis of the three modes in Table 8—i.e., Image>Text >Audio.

The intent of our exploratory analyses was to establish whether the abstract, symbolic language that we call Image is worthy of further, more-refined scrutiny. Our study suggests that this is indeed the case. Alternately, given the platform’s capabilities and flexibility, our findings may also be used as guides to design subsequent experiments. For instance, we noticed that when we ordered the information sources within each mode on the basis of their scores, all of the Level 1 sources in each mode appeared at one end of this spectrum whereas the Level 2 and Level 3 sources were somewhat intermingled. Similarly, if one computes the rank sums/score sums for each complexity, the figures are 45/60.76, 154/76.49, and 179/78.76, respectively. This suggests that our subjects perceived a significant ‘‘leap’’ in complexity when moving from Level 1 to Level 2 but not from Level 2 to Level 3. Such insights suggest that our subjects found Level 2 and Level 3 settings somewhat commensurate in difficulty and we should substitute one of these with some other, more complex, level in future experiments.

We would like to conclude our analyses by contrasting pre-experimental perceptions with experimental findings. We had administered a short questionnaire (Appendix A) to each subject immediately following their training session and before their participation in the actual experiments. Subjects were asked for their perceptions on two of our four primary measures— accuracy levels and decision-making times—with respect to the three modes, speeds, and complexity levels. In each case, they were asked to rank each element on a scale of 1 to 3, where 1 denotes ‘‘most preferred’’ and 3, ‘‘least.’’ Subjects were allowed to offer tied ranks. Subjects viewed Level 2 as being easier to deal with than Level 3, but our analyses suggests that they only did marginally better at Level 2 with Level 1 performance matching expectations. Likewise, subjects felt that they would do better at Normal experiments than at Faster. Our analyses suggest that performance at the two speed classes was commensurate with performance at Slower matching pre-experimental perception. They felt that accuracy would be the highest with Text. Overall, Image is marginally better than Text. They felt that their slowest decisions would be with Audio whereas, overall, the fastest decision-making speeds were recorded with Audio. They felt that Image would take the least decisionmaking time. Our findings are that it took the most.

We now summarize the work presented here. We began with a detailed description of the development of our experimental goals and design, the tuning of the platform to enable implementation of the design, and our experimental process. This was followed by analyses of the gathered data that suggest that the proposed visual language, and others like it, merit further scrutiny. One such study [1] has been completed and the results will be reported in separate papers.
