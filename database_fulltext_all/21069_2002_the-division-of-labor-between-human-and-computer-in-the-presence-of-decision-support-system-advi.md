# The division of labor between human and computer in the presence of decision support system advice

Donald R. Jones <sup>a,</sup>\*, Darrell Brown <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Information Systems and Quantitative Sciences, College of Business Administration,

Texas Tech University, Lubbock, TX 79409-2101, USA

<sup>b</sup>School of Business Administration, Portland State University, Portland, OR, USA

Accepted 1 October 2001

## Abstract

Prior research suggests that decision support system (DSS) provide model advice and display non-modeled information for decision makers [4,13]. We investigate whether decision makers (1) delegate the processing of the modeled information to the model, (2) cognitively process the non-modeled information, and (3) decide based on the model’s advice adjusted for the nonmodeled information. Experimentally, decision makers were no more likely to execute normative strategies when they had requisite knowledge for the strategy than when they did not have the requisite knowledge. We observed alternative processing, including ignoring the advice altogether, and evaluating the advice. Our findings suggest that DSS builders must encourage decision strategies that capitalize on the relative strengths of human and computer in using those features. <sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Decision strategies; Decision model reliance; DSS development; Human/computer interaction

## 1. Introduction

When a human decision maker is offered advice by a decision support system (DSS), how does the decision maker divide labor between human and machine in processing available information? Research in psychology and information systems has documented the relative strengths and weaknesses of human judgment and intuition compared to mechanical processing of information and the results are clear: formulas, algorithms, and decision rules are almost always superior to humans when processing the same information as humans, but humans can recognize when such mechanical advice should be adjusted because of additional information or extenuating circumstances (see Ref. [17] for a review). Hoch and Schkade [13] recommended a psychological approach to DSS that supports these relative strengths and compensates for these relative weaknesses of human and machine by providing model advice but also displaying potentially informative non-modeled information to the decision maker.

The question addressed by the current research is whether or not decision makers offered DSS model advice and a set of information, some modeled and some non-modeled, form strategies that capitalize on these relative strengths and weaknesses. That is, do decision makers construct ‘‘divide-and-conquer’’ strategies in which they delegate to the DSS the processing of information available to the DSS model while focusing their human effort on the information not modeled by the DSS to make adjustments to the model advice?

To address this question, we conducted a computerized process-tracing experiment. In choosing a university, participants had available DSS advice that recommended the highest-ranking university based on a linear model of a subset of the available information. Participants also had access to other non-modeled information that either supported or contradicted the model advice. Participants in a model-revealed condition had enough information to execute the divideand-conquer strategy while those in a model-hidden condition and a no-advice control condition could not execute the divide-and-conquer strategy. This experimental design enabled us to detect whether decision makers, given the requisite conditions, would use the divide-and-conquer strategy to a greater extent than decision makers not given the requisite conditions. Computerized process data also enabled us to observe alternatives to the divide-and-conquer strategy.

In the remainder of the paper, we describe the divide-and-conquer strategy as well as other possible alternative decision approaches. We then explain our experimental method, describe our results and provide our conclusions.

## 2. The divide-and-conquer strategy and alternatives

## 2.1. Advice and non-modeled information

Given an array of information and a judgment or prediction task, most research has concluded that mathematical combination of the information is superior to human intuition or judgment [17]. Extensive early research in the contest of ‘‘heads’’ versus ‘‘formulas’’ concluded that heads almost never win [19]. Particularly persuasive in favor of formulas were findings by Goldberg [11] that a linear composite of personality scales outperformed the best from 13 clinical psychologists, and findings that the particular weights chosen for linear models had little effect on the models’ ability to outperform human judges [9]. The advantage of formulas and algorithms is their ability to quickly perform consistent and accurate computations, while human intuition or judgmental combination of available data suffers from inconsistency and imprecision [17].

In these comparisons of human and model, the unaided human suffers from the limitations of bounded rationality, which results from the small amount of information that can be processed by the human brain at a time and the slow speed with which the information is processed [20,25]. In multiattribute choice tasks (similar to the task of our experiment), increasing the information set to only a fairly modest size makes the choice problem sufficiently complex to stimulate decision makers to shift from compensatory strategies (which allow high values of one attribute to compensate for low values of another attribute) to non-compensatory strategies (less accurate but easier strategies) such as elimination-by-aspect.<sup>1</sup> In contrast, mathematical approaches such as linear models embody compensatory strategies, and can be applied to much larger data sets so long as the data are properly structured (see Ref. [21] for discussion and extensive evidence concerning compensatory and non-compensatory decision strategies).

An advantage of human judgment over model computation is the human understanding of when the model advice should be adjusted or overridden. Meehl [18] observed that a model predicting Professor X had a 0.90 probability of going to a movie on a particular night would not hold if Professor X had just broken his leg. Thus, a model user could have other information, not incorporated in the model, that should lead to an adjustment of the model advice [18]. Other research has found that decision makers are influenced by informative non-modeled information when it is available [4,13,30].

## 2.2. The divide-and-conquer strategy and alternatives

DSS designers should take advantage of the aforementioned psychological findings and create DSS that provide model advice as well as display of informative non-modeled information [13]. The decision strategy that takes maximum advantage of the relative strengths and weaknesses of model advice and non-modeled information is the divide-and-conquer strategy. The divide-and-conquer strategy is characterized by three elements: (1) the decision maker delegates processing of the shared information to the DSS model (accepting the model’s advice and spending little or no effort processing the modeled information), (2) the decision maker attends carefully to the non-modeled information (spending effort processing the non-modeled information to assess its information signal), and, (3) the decision maker expresses a final decision that consists of the model advice adjusted for the nonmodeled information signal.

The first element takes advantage of the model’s strength (and compensates for the human limitation), the second element takes advantage of the human strength (and compensates for the model’s limitation), while the third element makes certain that the first two elements are both entered into the judgment. Thus, for DSS that provide both model advice and displays of non-modeled information, the divide-and-conquer strategy efficiently and effectively capitalizes on these features. If the relative strengths of model and human are valid, then the strategy should be more accurate than other strategies that fail to take advantage of these relative strengths. The strategy also fares well in its effort requirements, since little or no effort is expended processing the modeled information, only the nonmodeled information. Thus, to the extent that strategies are selected based on their potential effort/accuracy tradeoffs [3,6,23,24], then the divide-and-conquer strategy may often be seen as favorable compared its alternatives. Other research has shown that DSS users are sensitive to anticipated effort in their choice of strategies [27– 29].

## 2.3. Alternatives to the divide-and-conquer strategy

What are the alternatives to the divide-and-conquer strategy? The first element of the strategy is the decision maker’s delegation of the processing of modeled cues to the model, by conditionally accepting its advice. Two alternatives to delegating to the model advice are ignoring the advice and duplicating the processing of modeled information. If the advice is not ignored and processing of model information is duplicated, then a likely objective is to evaluate the advice.

There is some evidence that decision makers may frequently ignore model advice. Boatsman et al. [5] reported widespread occurrence of auditors ignoring the advice of DSS in evaluating an audited company for management fraud and choosing audit procedures. Since the auditors’ responses were binary (fraud/no fraud), however, the authors could not detect whether the auditors ignored the advice altogether or gave it insufficient weight to reach a mental threshold for changing their preliminary judgments. In another study, decision makers given time series sales data and historic data of sales promotions either ignored or gave little weight to a statistical forecast of the time series data, while allowing the sales promotion data to have too much influence [12]. It is not known from these studies whether the decision makers really ignored the advice or merely gave it low weight. Obviously, if model advice is ignored altogether, it can have no influence on the decision.

There is also reason to believe that decision makers might duplicate the processing of model advice in the presence of modeled and non-modeled information. When the model and human process the same information (i.e., when there is no non-modeled information present), decision makers often disagree with model advice. Arkes et al. [1] found decision makers deviating from advice unless they were sternly warned of the folly of doing so. Other studies also found deviation from advice [2,7,8,10,15,22]. One study demonstrated that decision makers worked backward to modify the inputs of a decision rule to obtain the advice they wanted [16]. While these studies did not include non-modeled information, the finding that decision makers processed modeled information and deviated from a model’s advice indicates that decision makers might be reluctant to delegate to a model’s advice in a divide-and-conquer strategy. Instead, decision makers appear willing to duplicate a model’s labor of processing modeled information.

Given bounded rationality, if humans expend effort duplicating processing of modeled information, it may reduce the amount of effort they are willing to spend on the non-modeled information. That is, for a given level of effort, if decision makers were to delegate processing of modeled information to the model, they would have more potential effort to expend on the non-modeled advice than if they also duplicated processing of the modeled information. Thus, if human decision makers are faced with processing all available information (rather than just the non-modeled information) then they may resort to strategies that are less compensatory (and less accurate) than if they had limited themselves to processing only the non-modeled information. In such case, the decision maker’s response to the non-modeled information signal might be inconsistently formed or missing altogether.

In summary, we have identified a decision strategy (divide-and-conquer) for taking advantage of DSS that provide model advice and also display informative non-modeled information that could affect the decision. The alternatives to this strategy include ignoring the advice and duplicating processing of modeled information. If the advice is ignored, it can have no influence on the decision. If the decision maker expends energy processing modeled information, the effort available for processing the non-modeled information may be reduced, potentially muting the signal from the non-modeled information. The next section summarizes an experiment designed to detect whether decision makers execute the divide-and-conquer strategy or its alternatives.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Task and experimental design

The task was to choose a university to recommend to a hypothetical high school senior from a computerized information display such as the one shown in Fig. 1. This task was adapted from prior studies [26] and was presented using software<sup>2</sup> that displayed a single item of information when the participant used the computer mouse to click on the item’s location. The software displayed only one item of information at a time. The software recorded each participant’s data acquisition pattern, including sequence and duration, and provided a means of making inferences regarding the participant’s decision strategies. This methodology is discussed at length [14,21]. In conditions where DSS advice was offered, the advised alternative was made available for participants to access using the same mouse technique as with other data items. The computer screen provided a position for entering the participant’s choice.

The definition and possible range of each attribute was made available to the participants on a printed page (see Fig. 2). Participants were instructed to choose a university for a hypothetical prospective student as described in Fig. 3. The intent was to encourage participants to consider all attributes as having approximately equal importance while leaving the participants some degree of latitude to apply their own judgment. We also wanted to make the task difficult enough to deter the use of compensatory strategies [21].<sup>3</sup> This was to help ensure that model advice might be seen by participants as having some potential benefit.

The basic experimental design was a 3  2 factorial, with three levels of DSS advice (advice with model revealed, advice with model hidden, control group with no advice) and two sequences of non-modeled attribute signal manipulations (three supporting trials, one neutral trial, three contradicting trials, or three contradicting trials, one neutral trial, three supporting trials). All participants did seven experimental trials, with a neutral trial always occurring in trial 4. All participants were given practice using the mouse and an additional neutral practice trial (identical for all participants) to choose a university prior to beginning the seven experimental trials.

The DSS advice was the result of a linear model computation that gave equal weight (over their respective ranges) to four attributes: percent of faculty with PhDs, student/faculty ratio, student quality, and school size. This left two additional attributes available to the decision maker but not included in the decision aid model, total cost and distance from home. ## 3.2. Participants and procedure

The participants were 135 upper-division undergraduate business students from two large urban universities. Participants were randomly assigned to condition. Forty-two percent were female and the median age was 27 years. All participants received course credit for their participation in the experiment. After signing consent forms, participants were instructed to begin interacting with the computer system. In all experimental conditions, the system provided participants with several screens that presented a short tutorial about choice tasks and explained methods for making choices. The methods given were presented as examples and included an explanation of linear models, decision rules, and simple heuristics. Including this tutorial had several purposes: to give participants in the model revealed condition sufficient background to understand the linear model description, to give participants in the model hidden condition an idea of the range of possible methods that might be used to create DSS advice, and to help ensure that the participants in the unaided control group had background information about decision models equivalent to the other groups. As participants worked through this tutorial on the computer, they were required to acknowledge their understanding of each screen before going on.

After completing this tutorial, participants familiarized themselves with the data collection procedure (by selecting an automobile) including the use of the mouse and the entry of the chosen alternative. Then the software instructed participants to read the written materials that included the attribute definitions and the decision scenario, to prepare them for the experimental trials. The participants returned to the computer system displaying a screen informing them that experimental trials were about to begin. Then the participants performed a neutral practice trial followed by the seven experimental trials reported on in this paper. Finally, participants provided their personal weighting of the six attributes, responded to several Likert-scaled questions about their impressions of the decision tasks and the DSS advice (for conditions with advice available), and completed a demographic questionnaire.

## 3.3. Measures

The software recorded all data acquisitions, including acquisition of the advice, and the final choice entered for each trial. From these, we constructed several measures. We computed the proportion of times each participant made choices that agreed with the model advice, the proportion of time spent looking at data for the non-modeled attributes, and the proportion of time spent looking at the recommended alternative. We also used process data to identify trials in which the model advice was ignored.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Ignoring the advice

The divide-and-conquer strategy requires that the decision maker delegate processing modeled information by accepting (at least conditionally) the model’s advice. This delegation cannot occur if the decision maker ignores the model’s advice. Thus, in our search for decision makers who executed the divide-andconquer strategy, we partitioned out those who routinely ignored the advice. To accomplish this, we analyzed the process data from each of 630 trials (90 participants with DSS advice available multiplied by seven experimental trials per participant) to search for clear cases of ignoring the advice. In 19.0% of these trials, the advice was never acquired by clicking the mouse to open the advice cell on the computer screen, and so we coded these trials as ignoring the advice. In another 16.3% of these trails, the decision maker obtained advice that disagreed with the final choice, but receiving this advice led to no further data acquisitions (i.e., the ‘‘disconfirming’’ advice was first acquired as the final data acquisition in the trial). In these trials, we assume the advice must have had no substantive influence, otherwise the decision maker would have obtained additional information while considering the advice. We coded these trials as ignoring the advice. When these two categories were combined, 35.3% of trials were coded as ignoring the advice.

In another 55.2% of trials acquiring the advice was followed by further data acquisitions, meaning that the advice was in mind before the choice process was completed, implying that the advice likely had some level of influence. In the remaining 9.5% of trials in advice conditions, the first acquisition of the advice was the final data acquisition and the decision maker’s choice agreed with the advice. In this situation, we could not tell whether the participant used the advice to confirm the decision or whether the advice was ignored (but coincidentally agreed with the participant’s choice). We did not code these as ignoring the advice.

We investigated to see if the inclination to ignore the advice was related to experimental condition. We conducted analysis of variance (ANOVA) on the frequency (out of seven trials) that a participant ignored the advice. This 2  2 ANOVA included factors for model revelation (revealed, hidden), nonmodeled attribute sequence (supporting/contradicting, contradicting/supporting), and their interaction. This ANOVA model was not significant ( F(3, 89) = 0.46, p = 0.707). Thus, there is no indication that the choice to ignore the advice was determined by experimental condition.

By analysis of our post-experimental questionnaire and process data, we found that the decision to ignore the advice was driven by the consistency of the participants’ own judgment policies. We measured consistency of a participant’s judgment policy by computing the correlation between the self-reported relative weights of the six attributes (from the postexperimental questionnaire) and the relative amount of time spent looking at data for those attributes.<sup>4</sup> Then we regressed this measure on the number of trials (out of seven) the advice was ignored. This regression was significant (t = 3.52, p = 0.001).

From these analyses, we concluded that the decision to ignore the advice was not related to our experimental conditions but was related to individual characteristics of participants, which were randomly assigned to treatment. For subsequent analysis, we divided participants in the advice conditions into users and non-users of advice. Participants we coded as ignoring the advice in a majority of trials were classified as non-users. All others were classified as users. Summary measures for users with model revealed, users with model hidden, non-users, and the control group are shown in Table 1.

Table 1 shows the percent of data acquired (i.e., cells opened at least once out of 36 possible), the number of acquisitions (mouse clicks to open a cell on the screen other than advice) and average elapsed time. We performed ANOVA and appropriate post hoc comparisons on these measures and these analyses are also reported in Table 1. Percent of data acquired did not differ among the four groups. The users with model revealed, users with model hidden, and the control group did not differ on the number of acquisitions or the average elapsed time. Non-users, however, did differ from the control group and users with model revealed on number of acquisitions and elapsed time. This is further indication of the direct manner in which non-users processed the data. They apparently had a plan and stuck to it.

## 4.2. Lack of tendency to divide-and-conquer

Table 2 summarizes our tests to see if users with model revealed (for whom the divide-and-conquer strategy was possible) had any more tendencies toward using the divide-and-conquer strategy than users with model hidden (for whom the divide-and-conquer strategy was not possible). First, we investigated the proportion to time spent looking at non-modeled attributes. A tendency toward the divide-and-conquer strategy would be associated with a higher proportion of time being spent on non-modeled attributes. This is because in the divide-and-conquer strategy decision makers would spend relatively little time looking at the modeled attributes (because the processing of these attributes would be delegated to the model) but would spend relatively more time carefully processing the non-modeled attributes. The results in Table 2, however, show no difference between users with model revealed and users with model hidden in this measure, in either the supporting or contradicting non-modeled attribute trials.

As another test for the divide-and-conquer strategy, we looked at the proportion of trials where the final choice agreed with the advice. In a divide-and-conquer strategy, choices made in supporting non-modeled attribute trials should often agree with the advice because of the tendency to conditionally accept the advice followed by careful consideration of supporting (non-modeled) information. In contrast, choices made in contradicting non-modeled attribute trials should often disagree with the advice because of the careful consideration of the contradicting non-modeled information. These effects should be stronger with the divide-and-conquer strategy than with other less compensatory strategies, which would be prone to add noise to both the advice and the non-modeled attribute signal. Comparison of users with model revealed to users with model hidden indicated neither of these effects.

We considered the possibility that decision makers whose personal weightings of the factors were substantially different from the equal-weighting of the model might have been unwilling to delegate to the model. We computed the variance of participants’ selfreported weightings of the modeled attributes and excluded all participants with variance greater than 0.005. Those remaining (16 users with model revealed and 16 users with model hidden) had self-reported weights for the modeled attributes that were approximately equal, making there self-reported weights virtually equivalent to the model. The results for this analysis are also reported in Table 2. We found no differences in these groups’ willingness to use the model. We concluded that the reluctance to use divideand-conquer strategies was not due to individual weighting schemes differing from the equally weighted linear model used to create the advice.

## 4.3. Evidence of evaluating the advice

One alternative to delegating to the advice is to expend effort evaluating the advice. We tested to see if users exhibited any tendency toward evaluating the advice. For this analysis, reported in Table 3, we compared the proportion of time spent looking at the recommended alternative for users with model revealed, users with model hidden, non-users, and the control group. We conducted separate ANOVAs for this measure in the supporting non-modeled attribute trials and in the contradicting non-modeled attribute trials. Both ANOVAs were significant, and post hoc analysis of both ANOVAs (shown in Table 3) indicated that model users (whether the model was revealed or not) spent a larger proportion of their time examining data for the recommended alternative than either non-users or the no-advice control group. These results indicate significant evaluation of the recommended alternative when it was used. The tendency to evaluate the model’s advice was the same whether the model was revealed or not.

## 4.4. Influence of advice and non-modeled attributes

The participants did not appear to use the divideand-conquer strategy, but did they use strategies that allowed for influence of the model advice and the non-modeled information? Table 4 shows the results of our tests to investigate this question. We conducted ANOVAs on proportion of trials that agreed with advice to compare users versus non-users, and users versus the control condition. These ANOVAs were significant; users agreed more frequently with the advice than non-users or the control group, whether the non-modeled attributes were supporting or contradicting. Thus, the model advice had positive influence on its users.

To see if the non-modeled attribute signal had influence, we conducted paired difference tests of the proportion of trials in agreement with the advice in supporting versus contradicting non-modeled attribute trials, for each user group. As shown in Table 4, these tests were all significant, with the supporting non-modeled attributes having greater levels of agreement than contradicting non-modeled attributes. We concluded that both the advice and the non-modeled attribute signal influenced the choices. Thus, while a divide-and-conquer strategy might have led to greater response to both the advice and the non-modeled attribute signal, these sources of information did have some influence.

## 5. Discussion

If decision makers were to apportion the division of labor between human and computer based on the relative strengths and weaknesses of each, DSS users offered model advice and non-modeled information would adopt divide-and-conquer strategies in which the processing of modeled information was delegated to the model and the human decision maker would focus on the non-modeled information. The human cognitive limitations of bounded rationality imply that alternative strategies to divide-and-conquer that process both modeled and non-modeled information must either be more difficult than divide and conquer (because they require additional cognitive processing of the modeled information that would not be necessary if that processing were delegated to the machine) to achieve the same level of accuracy, or degrade to less accurate non-compensatory strategies for a given level of effort (because additional cognitive effort spent processing the modeled information would reduce the available effort that could be applied in processing the non-modeled information). Thus, the divide-and-conquer strategy offers a relatively easy and accurate means of processing the decision task information.

In spite of these potential advantages, our experiment detected no more tendency toward using the strategy when it was feasible (because the requisite knowledge for the strategy was available to decision makers) than when it was not feasible (because the requisite knowledge for the strategy was not available to decision makers). Instead, we observed widespread instances of ignoring the model advice and expending cognitive effort to evaluate the model advice.

Decision makers who ignored the advice were able to construct strategies that consistently reflected their own preferences; their relative attention to attributes was positively associated with the weights they expressed for those attributes. Other studies have documented low levels of influence of advice [5,12]. Those studies were unable to distinguish whether the decision makers actually ignored the advice or merely gave it low weight. With our process data, which told whether and when decision makers acquired the model advice, we were able to observe widespread instances of ignoring the advice. Of course, for model advice to have any influence it must be at least considered. If DSS builders want model advice to be used, they have to find ways to make sure the advice is acquired and entered into the cognitive process. The ploys a DSS designer might use to affect whether the decision maker uses model advice probably differ from the ploys the DSS designer would use to affect how much weight is afforded the advice. Thus, our observation of widespread ignoring of model advice was an important finding of this research.

Among users of model advice, the other alternative to the divide-and-conquer strategy was to spend effort evaluating the model advice. Strategies that have the feature of evaluating the advice have the advantage of allowing the model to have at least some influence. Thus, users were more likely to agree with advice (in the presence of both supporting and contradicting non-modeled information) than non-users or those given no advice. Users (like non-users and those receiving no advice) were also somewhat influenced by the non-modeled information. Thus, evaluating the advice consisted of an evaluation based on both the modeled and non-modeled information. The evaluation of non-modeled information may have been somewhat muted, however, given that users given contradicting non-modeled information still agreed with the advice more frequently than non-users or those receiving no advice. This finding highlights the possibility that users of model advice may process the non-modeled information in a manner that insufficiently attends to or inaccurately combines the information (i.e., processes non-modeled information using non-compensatory decision strategies). Thus, a model could be too persuasive, if its advice drew cognitive information processing away from careful consideration of non-modeled information.

Our research is subject to some limitations that deserve comment. First, it is possible that our information display made the modeled and non-modeled distinction insufficiently salient to suggest the divideand-conquer strategy to the participants. While this might have been true in certain cases, our data collection method was designed to capture any tendency toward model-revealed participants using the strategy. Since the identity of modeled and non-modeled attributes were displayed in the model-revealed condition at every trial, as well as on a screen just prior to starting the trials, it is unlikely that such a high percentage of participants would be unaware as to make no measurable difference possible between model-revealed and model-hidden participants. Of course, in addition to explaining the model on each screen we could have also divided the screen into sections for modeled and non-modeled information, or otherwise highlighted this distinction. We think such artifice would have flown in the face of external validity, however, because decision makers often acquire data from a variety of sources that may have nothing to do model development. They need to able to distinguish modeled from non-modeled information.

Second, it is possible that our equally weighted linear model did not agree with the priorities of the participants, and this disagreement would have motivated them to evaluate the advice before relying on it. When we identified those whose personal weights of the modeled attributes were approximately equally weighted, we obtained the same results as with the larger set of participants. We concluded that the tendency to evaluate advice (rather than delegate to advice) was not driven by decision makers having different priorities than those reflected in the model. More likely they viewed the model as imperfect (because it only modeled a subset of attributes) whose advice should be evaluated in light of all available attributes. This approach would have been available to users with model-hidden, as well as model revealed, and could explain why those with model hidden and model revealed produced virtually identical measures.

Our research indicates that decision makers do not always take to the divide-and-conquer strategy in the normal course of events. Given the potential benefits of dividing labor between human and computer in accordance with their relative strengths and weakness, DSS designers and researchers should accept the challenge of learning how to encourage the divideand-conquer strategy.
