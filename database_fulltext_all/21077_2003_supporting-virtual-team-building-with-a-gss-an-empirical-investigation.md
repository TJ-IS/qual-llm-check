# Supporting virtual team-building with a GSS: an empirical investigation

Wayne W. Huang <sup>a,</sup>\*, Kwok-Kee Wei <sup>b</sup>, Richard T. Watson <sup>c</sup>, Bernard C.Y. Tan <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of MIS, College of Business, Ohio University, Athens, OH 45701, USA <sup>b</sup>Department of Information Systems, National University of Singapore, 3 Science Drive 2, Singapore 117543, Singapore <sup>c</sup>Department of Management Information Systems, University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602-6273, USA

Accepted 30 November 2001

## Abstract

As virtual teams become more and more common in organizations, supporting virtual teams will be an important research topic for group support systems (GSS) scholars. This research examines how a GSS may be used to support virtual teambuilding. The results reveal that a GSS with an embedded goal-setting structure helped virtual teams to foster better team cohesion, better team commitment, and better collaboration climate than a GSS without the goal-setting structure. Virtual teams using a GSS with an embedded goal-setting structure also had better perceived decision quality and generated more decision alternatives than virtual teams using a GSS without the goal-setting structure. Implications of these findings are discussed. <sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Group support systems; Virtual teams; Team-building; Goal-setting

## 1. Introduction

Some prior studies have reported that GSS can enhance teamwork by helping teams to attain better decision quality, generate more ideas and decision alternatives, and achieve more even influence distribution [2,3,9]. A closer examination of GSS research literature indicates that: (1) most prior GSS research is conducted in a face-to-face setting rather than a dispersed setting (i.e., a virtual team setting) [12]); (2) most prior GSS research examines how a GSS may be used to support teamwork rather than team-building [12]; and (3) goal-congruence between individual and team goals has been an important unanswered question in GSS research [3].

This study explores the use of a GSS to support virtual team-building by enhancing goal-congruence. The independent variables are communication setting (face-to-face versus virtual) and goal-setting structure (presence versus absence of such a structure). The goalsetting structure is a team interaction process through which team members disclose and define individual and team goals, exchange ideas and align both types of goals, and reach a consensus on team goals that are shared by all team members (see Section 3.1 for details). The focus of this study is to test whether a GSS embedded with this goal-setting structure (GSS with GS) can support virtual team-building better than a GSS without this goal-setting structure (GSS without GS).

The dependent variables are: team cohesion [29], team commitment [20], collaboration climate [20], perceived decision quality [13], and number of decision alternatives generated [7]. The first three dependent variables reflect the process of virtual teambuilding. The last two dependent variables reflect the outcome of virtual team-building.

## 2. Research hypotheses

Research hypotheses are formulated to test whether the goal setting structure can enhance virtual teambuilding and thereby improve virtual teamwork. Since supporting physical (i.e., face-to-face) team-building is not the focus of this study, research hypotheses will be formulated only for the virtual communication setting (comparing GSS with GS and GSS without GS).

Team cohesion refers to the mutual attraction of team members and the closeness that team members feel towards each other [29]. The goal-setting structure allows team members to align individual goals with team goals. This alignment can contribute towards more cohesive teamwork [8], and decrease goal conflicts among team members [31]. Conflict, in general, correlates negatively with cohesion [7]. Therefore, by reducing conflict, team cohesion can be increased. Further, shared team goals can bind team members together [30], and help them to feel close towards each other [1,18,28].

H1. GSS with GS will result in better team cohesion in a virtual communication setting than GSS without GS.

Team commitment refers to the team spirit, a sense of loyalty and dedication to a team [20]. The goal-setting structure may increase team commitment in several ways. First, shared team goals can result in greater involvement of team members in team activities [21]. With greater effort and involvement, the commitment of team members is likely to be increased. Second, prior research indicates that shared team goals can foster the goal commitment of team members [21,10], and this is likely to increase team commitment [30]. Third, the goal setting structure can enhance team cohesion (H1). Members of a cohesive team are generally more willing to remain in the team [4,11]. As a result, team members are more likely to be committed to the team.

H2. GSS with GS will result in better team commitment in a virtual communication setting than GSS without GS.

Collaborative climate refers to the degree to which a team can work well together [20]. The goal-setting structure may enhance the collaborative climate of a team in two ways. First, the purpose of teamwork is essentially to attain team [20]. When team goals are fully shared by team members, they are more likely to collaborate so as to achieve team goals. Second, the goal-setting structure encourages discussion and demonstrates to team members the benefits of their efforts. This may help to develop team norms that encourage team members to assist each other [5].

H3. GSS with GS will result in better collaborative climate in a virtual communication setting than GSS without GS.

The decision-making task used in this study has no correct answers (see Section 3.2 for details). Hence, perceived decision quality and number of decision alternatives generated are used as surrogate measures to assess the output of teamwork. Perceived decision quality is the perception of team members regarding the final tea [13]. The goal setting structure can enhance team cohesion (H1). More cohesive teams generally arrive at better decisions [11]. In addition, teams with shared goals tend to arrive at decisions that are in line with expectations of team members [27]. Hence, the goal setting structure can perceived decision quality.

H4. GSS with GS will result in better perceived decision quality in a virtual communication setting than GSS without GS.

The number of decision alternatives generated is the total number of unique alternatives generated by a team [7]. Shared team goals can motivate team members to focus their attention on their task and direct their efforts at proposing more decision alternatives for completing their task [21]. As a result, a greater number of decision alternatives may be generated.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Research design

This research adopts a 2  2 factorial design (see Table 1). Communication setting (face-to-face versus virtual) and goal-setting structure (presence versus absence) are the two independent variables. The goalsetting structure is a team interaction procedure that encourages team members to align their individual goals and then generate shared team goals. Team members have to disclose and define their individual goals and then agree on a set of team goals based on the individual goals disclosed [30]. This involves an exchange of justification for individual and team goals (a process of aligning individual and team goals) [8] and a ranking of proposed team goals so as to reach a consensus on final team goals that are generally shared and accepted by team members.

In the virtual communication setting, team members could neither see nor talk to each other. They could only communicate electronically (see Fig. 1) via a Macintosh version of the SAMM groupware. In the GSS research literature, the face-to-face communication setting had normally been used as a benchmark. Hence, this setting (see Fig. 2) was included in this study.

Subjects consisted of 240 sophomore undergraduates in a big university who were taking a core information systems course. The team size was five, as in many prior studies [15,22]. Approximately half of all the subjects were male and half were female. Subjects were randomly assigned to the four experimental treatments (see Table 1). To motivate subjects to participate seriously, they were given course credits for their participation. Subjects were also told that the team that was judged to be the best in each of the four treatments would be awarded US\$150 [6].

## 3.2. Task

The task was a business problem pertaining to geographic diversification, adapted from [7]. It required teams to choose suitable country (or countries) for business diversification efforts of a large multi-national corporation. The task description provided background information (e.g., political turmoil, racial discrimination, macro-economic factors, and other social factors) about a list of candidate countries that needed to be considered before teams could make their decision on business diversification. No correct answer existed for this task. Team members, roleplaying as board directors in this corporation, were asked to make decisions based on their own preferences and frames of references. Given that different team members were likely to have different preferences, this task could amplify differences in team behaviors arising from the presence or absence of shared team goals [7]. Since there were no correct answers for this task, perceived decision quality and number of decision alternatives generated were used as surrogate measures to assess the output of teamwork.

## 3.3. Procedure

Based on Chidambaram [6] and our own pilot study, the maximum time allowed for each meeting was 150 min. All teams in the four treatments went through the following steps, adapted from Chidambaram [6].

(a) This step took 45 min. Teams assigned to treatments with the goal-setting were asked to define and generate shared team goals. Teams assigned to treatments without the goal-setting structure were asked to perform a filler task that involved discussing the pollution problem faced by the world. The purpose of this filler task was to ensure that teams in all treatments had the same amount of time to interact prior to the actual task [14].

(b) Teams were asked to work on the actual task (i.e., the geographic diversification problem. They generated decision alternatives, discussed their decision alternatives, and chose their preferred alternative(s) through a ranking process.

(c) Teams members individually completed a postmeeting questionnaire. They then attended a short post-meeting debriefing.

## 4. Research results

## 4.1. Manipulation check and measure reliability

A manipulation check for the goal-setting structure was conducted based on the responses of subjects to six questions measuring team goals (see Appendix A). Teams that used the goal-setting structure reported a significantly stronger extent of shared team goals than teams that did not used the goal-setting structure (t = 34.31, p < 0.01). The manipulation of the goal-setting structure appeared to be successful.

Cronbach’s alpha was used to assess the reliability of questions measuring the constructs (see Appendix A). Cronbach’s alpha for team cohesion, team commitment, collaborative climate, and perceived decision quality were 0.88, 0.90, 0.89, and 0.89, respectively. Hence, these four constructs satisfied Nunnally’s [26] criteria for reliability.

## 4.2. Research findings

Descriptive statistics for all dependent variables are presented in Table 2. The results of ANOVA tests are shown in Table 3. A significance level of 0.05 was used for all tests. The hypotheses were tested using Tukey – Kramer’s honestly significant difference (HSD). This test is a normal alpha-level test if the sample sizes are the same but is conservative if the sample sizes are different. There is a significant difference if the absolute difference of the means subtract the least significant difference yields a positive value [19]. This value is denoted TK in this study. The ANOVA tests also revealed some significant interactions that were not hypothesized. First, virtual teams with the goal-setting structure reported better team cohesion (TK = 1.93), better team commitment (TK = 1.54), better collaborative climate (TK = 1.16), better perceived decision quality (TK = 1.81), and generated more decision alternatives (TK = 5.71) compared to face-to-face teams without the goal-setting structure. Second, with the goal-setting structure, virtual teams and face-to-face teams did not differ in terms of team commitment (TK = - 0.21) and collaborative climate (TK = - 0.19). However, with the goal-setting structure, virtual teams reported better team cohesion (TK = 0.18), better perceived decision quality (TK = 0.07), and generated more decision alternatives (TK = 5.71) than face-to-face teams.

## 5. Discussion and implications

The research results yielded two useful findings. First, GSS with the goal setting structure could support virtual team-building better than a standard GSS (without the goal setting structure) in terms of team cohesion, team commitment, and collaborative climate. This better support for virtual team-building improved the output of teamwork in the form of better perceived decision quality and more decision alternatives generated. Second, in the GSS research literature, face-toface teams had commonly been used as a benchmark condition [12,16,17]. GSS with the goal setting structure was found to better support team-building than this benchmark condition in terms of team cohesion, team commitment, and collaborative climate. This better support for team-building also improved the output of teamwork in the form of better perceived decision quality and more decision alternatives generated.

Team cohesion has been inadequately studied in GSS research. One notable exception is a longitudinal research [7]. This study reported that initially, GSS use decreased team cohesion in virtual teams. But as time went on, team cohesion could be gradually increased by GSS use. This result revealed the effect of time on team cohesion. Our research was not based on a longitudinal study. Team norms pertaining to shared team goals appeared to develop quickly and were adhered to by team members, as in Hinsz [14]. Consequently, it was found that GSS with the goal-setting structure helped to increase team cohesion even at a very early stage of teamwork.

Just like the brainstorming tool that has often been considered an inherent part of a GSS, the goal-setting structure in this study can be considered an integral part of a GSS. In this respect, this research demonstrates that after embedding suitable structures into a GSS (e.g., the goal setting structure), GSS users may be able to reap some benefits of GSS use quickly (e.g., virtual team-building). This may improve GSS adoption in organizations.

Briggs et al. [3] speculate that team productivity may be reduced if individual goals and team goals are incompatible. Our research results generally support this speculation. Treatments that have compatible team goals and individual goals tend to yield better-perceived decision quality and have more decision alternatives generated. In the past, the effects of GSS and goal-congruence on team-building has been an unanswered research question [3]. Results of this study answer this research question and contribute to our accumulated knowledge on GSS.

Further, it would be interesting to study whether the positive effects of the goal-setting structure (evident in the results of this study) may be moderated by team size [22], task type [24], and reward system [23]. The answer seems affirmative based on the goal-setting literature. However, this literature comprised almost exclusively of studies conducted in a face-to-face setting (without GSS support). Since team behavior in a face-to-face setting and a virtual setting may be different, future GSS studies can examine this issue.

One limitation of this laboratory study is its relatively low external validity. For example, the student teams used in this study were ad hoc teams with neither a history nor a future. Hence, their behavior may be different from that of actual teams in organizations. Thus, attempts to generalize the research findings to actual organizational teams must be done cautiously.

Another limitation of this research is the measurement of the outcome of teamwork using perceived decision quality. This perceptual measure had been used because the task had no correct answer. However, the use of a perceptual (rather than an objective) measure may weaken the conclusion that the GSS with the goal setting structure can enhance the outcome of virtual teamwork. This weakness was partly compensated for by another objective measurement of the outcome of teamwork (number of decision alternatives generated). Nevertheless, future studies can employ tasks with objective measurement of decision quality to verify the results reported in this research.

Virtual teamwork is becoming common and important to many organizations [25]. Effective support for virtual team-building and teamwork is a relatively new GSS research topic. This study represents an initial exploration into this research issue. It yielded some encouraging results about how GSS may be used to support virtual team-building and teamwork.
