# Online support for commerce processes by web retailers<sup>\$</sup>

Amit Basu \*, Steve Muylle

Cox School of Business, Southern Methodist University, Dallas, TX 75275, USA

Accepted 31 January 2002

## Abstract

This paper examines the extent to which companies in various industries are using the World Wide Web and its associated technologies to conduct retail business. Using the framework of an Electronic Commerce Architecture (ECA), a variety of commercial Web sites are analyzed to determine which commerce processes are being supported online in each industry and each type of industry. The results of this study provide useful insight not only for researchers on the state of Web technology adoption and electronic commerce practices in industry, but also for companies seeking to derive competitive advantage through electronic commerce.

## 1. Introduction

Electronic Commerce (EC) has been widely heralded in the popular business press for providing new ways to create business value and driving strong economic progress. Indeed, the explosive growth of the World Wide Web on the Internet has given rise to a promising locus of commerce. According to International Data Corporation [14], the number of Web users worldwide at the end of 1998 amounted to 92 million, and is predicted to reach 320 million by the end of 2002. The number of Internet hosts climbed from 5.8 million in January 1995 to an astonishing 56.2 million in July 1999. During the same period the number of commercial hosts (.com) shot up from 1.3 million to 18.8 million [17]. Furthermore, the Boston Consulting Group [5] estimated consumer spending for 1998 to be \$9.66 billion, which more than tripled from 1997.

Notwithstanding these impressive and promising figures and growth-rates, some key challenges in successfully instituting EC initiatives still need to be resolved. CommerceNet [7,8] outlines three major barriers and inhibitors to the continued and widespread adoption of Internet commerce. These are consumers’ and companies’ incorrect perceptions about Internet capabilities and the resulting need for education of the broader public, current technology limitations (e.g., need for robust applications or services, standards, and interoperability), and lack of effective marketing and business models. A number of initiatives have been launched to educate the market, especially by technology solution providers, and much effort has been put into the development of interoperable standards and applications (e.g., the

Object Management Group’s Common Object Request Broker Architecture (CORBA)). However, a comprehensive framework that encompasses relevant marketing and business models is still needed.

This study is an attempt to address the expressed need for effective marketing and business models by studying online support for commerce processes using a framework for EC use by firms to investigate the actual state of adoption of EC. By looking at the business from a process perspective, we draw on recent developments in management science that explicitly recognize information technology as a very powerful enabler of business innovation and success. Focusing on firms that are technology users, rather than technology solution providers, the following three research issues are addressed: (1) What is the extent of support for commerce processes and sub-processes in EC? (2) Are there significant differences in support for each of these commerce processes across various types of industries? and (3) What are the patterns of correlations for the support of the different commerce processes across and within industries?

In order to provide an answer to these questions the Electronic Commerce Architecture (ECA) proposed by Basu and Muylle [3] is presented in the next section. First, the rationale for adopting a process view of the business is outlined and a distinction is made between commerce processes and internal business processes. Then, the ECA is described, which is useful for studying the adoption of EC as it highlights the different levels of commerce processes involved in EC. Section 3 describes the empirical study that forms the basis for this paper, including the ECA-based measures, data collection method and data analysis techniques. The research results are outlined in Section 4, and Section 5 concludes the paper with a discussion of the study and directions for further research.

## 2. An architecture for electronic commerce

An important step in the direction of understanding business processes in online marketplaces is the development of an architecture for EC. Such an architecture would allow market participants to identify the major issues involved in engaging in EC, and, at the same time, provides a framework for studying the adoption and diffusion of EC. The Electronic Commerce Architecture (ECA) that we use is organized around flexible business processes, rather than traditional business functions. Before describing the architecture itself in this section, we first relate recent developments in the field of business process design and redesign.

## 2.1. Process view of the business

The Internet offers tremendous opportunities to radically alter the way business is done. However, in order to identify and exploit such opportunities, companies engaged in EC need to understand their current operations. A particularly useful way for studying the relationship between Internet technology and the way business is carried out is adopting a process view of the business [9,12]. A process view acknowledges the natural partnership of information technology and business process design [10] and calls for moving from functional perspectives to looking at the fundamental process of the business from a customerdriven, cross-functional perspective [11].

In definitional terms, business processes can be seen as ‘‘a set of logically related tasks performed to achieve a defined business outcome’’ [10, p.12] where a process is ‘‘any activity or group of activities that take an input, add value to it, and provide an output to an internal or external customer’’ [13, p.9]. Clearly, the customer, being the recipient of the defined business outcome, plays a central role in the process view of business [9,10]. In this regard, a useful distinction can be made between processes that involve direct contact with external customers and back-room processes on which the customer-facing processes are dependent [9]. Customer-facing or commerce processes are those processes that entail direct interaction with the external customer and involve functions such as marketing, sales, and customer service, while backroom processes are those processes that support commerce processes and involve functions such as purchasing, manufacturing, finance, and other internal business functions. It should be noted that commerce processes and business processes are highly intertwined as online support for front-end customer – firm interactions affects the structuring and execution of back-end business processes. For instance, offering overnight delivery of products sold online requires firms to construct logistics processes to support the commerce process.

## 2.2. Electronic commerce architecture

A starting point for the architecture used in this study is the eCo model developed by CommerceNet [18]. The main objective of this extensible objectoriented architecture is to promote the interoperation and reuse of applications and services for EC, based on a multi-layered collection of network, commerce, business, and market services. From a managerial perspective, Basu and Muylle [3] proposed a multi-layered Electronic Commerce Architecture (ECA), based on the technically inspired eCo framework. The ECA is intended to facilitate strategic decisions by various types of companies engaging in Electronic Commerce. As such, technology users, technology providers, as well as intermediaries, can use the ECA framework to examine their roles and business models in the EC environment. The ECA consists of three levels of services: network services, commerce services, and content services. Network services are services that support the network (e.g., the Internet) through which EC is implemented. Commerce services relate to commerce processes in EC (see Table 1). The commerce processes proposed in the ECA are based on a generalizable model of key exchange processes developed by Kambil and van Heck [15]. Content services are services that are specific to a particular industry or domain (e.g., packages for real estate applications).

By applying a generalizable model of key exchange processes [15] to the technically oriented eCo framework, the ECA is well suited to serve as a technically and managerially sound foundation for studying EC adoption. Moreover, given the huge potential of the Internet to capture value-adding information on customers’ preferences and idiosyncrasies at each commerce process level, the ECA also provides a lens through which information gathering and customization opportunities and strategies can be evaluated.

In summary, the ECA allows companies to identify the major issues involved in engaging in EC by examining which roles and business models they can adopt at the level of content, commerce, and network services. For instance, the ECA can initiate the strategic decision of a company in the news industry to go beyond providing basic content services and start accommodating advanced transactional commerce services by making the shift from simply putting standardized news reports online, backed up by advertising revenues, to the provision of an interactive, personalized journal, paid for by the reader. As such, the ECA assists the company in expanding its role in EC (along the levels of the ECA) and guides the company in understanding which commerce processes should be created and pursued. Furthermore, the ECA serves as a helpful tool for exploiting customization and personalization opportunities.

In other words, the ECA can help a company address two important questions. First, ‘‘What are the specific processes and features that I should support online, in order to exploit electronic commerce?’’ and second, ‘‘What is the relative importance of the different processes and features for my particular company and industry?’’ It should be noted that a company’s motivations for supporting the different commerce processes outlined in the ECA could be guided by either the existence of a direct relationship between support for these processes and business performance, or be driven by competitive pressure to be consistent with competitors in its industry segment. For instance, the above news industry company could base its decision to offer innovative, personalized, online news services on the beliefs that this will enhance customer satisfaction and increase profits; alternatively, this company could merely be imitating the EC initiatives of progressive competitors or new entrants who threaten the sustainability of the company’s competitive position in the online marketplace. While the ECA provides a framework for examining the first issue, this paper focuses primarily on its use to analyze the competitive landscape on various industry-specific dimensions.

## 3. Research method

The results presented in this paper are based upon an empirical analysis of commercial Web sites in different industries. The Web sites were analyzed in terms of the Electronic Commerce Architecture developed by Basu and Muylle [3]. As commerce processes involve the interface between the business and its customers, they can readily be observed and analyzed from an external point of view. Conversely, internal business processes are often transparent to the customer. In order to study the relationships between commerce processes and underlying business processes, a subsequent stage of this research project will involve a survey of Web site managers in the same firms, analyzing the firms’ internal perception of support for the ECA processes and relationship to business success. In this section, the data collection method, the measurement instruments, and data analysis techniques used for the Web site analysis are described.

## 3.1. Data collection

The site analysis was structured using a comprehensive checklist based on the ECA. This score sheet consists of 96 checkbox items relating to the commerce processes in EC. The unit of analysis was a company’s Web site, which was scored in terms of support for specific commerce processes. For instance, for the payment process, one of the questions was whether or not the Web site supports electronic wallets (see Section 3.2). Binary response formats were used in order to avoid subjectivity influencing the results.

## 3.2. Measures

In order to summarize the vast amount of information generated by collecting data on the 96 checkbox items, meaningful aggregate variables were constructed for each commerce process. These variables all range between 0 and 1 as they represent the mean of selected binary questions pertaining to particular sub-processes in EC. The basic commerce processes in EC are derived from averaging over the related subprocesses. The aggregate variables are defined in Table 2 (a detailed specification of these measures is available in Ref. [4]).

Search. The extent of support for searching the seller online combines items relating to Web portal query results, banner ads, and World Wide Web location (URL: http://www.companyname.com), while the extent of support for searching products online pertains to internal search engines, search forms, and search supporting e-mail functions. Search related support services include site-maps, downloadable product catalogs, and alternative offline ways to support search (e.g., 1-800 number). Search customization embraces personalization of the Web site and customized product design/bundling.

Valuation. The extent to which price information is displayed refers to listed prices for products and shipment, as well as taxes. The price determination mechanisms pertain to product –price mix negotiation, haggling, discounts, frequency marketing programs, matching prices to competitors’ prices, and alternative ways to obtain prices (e.g., via e-mail). Valuation customization focuses on ways to tailor prices to individual buyer’s levels of price sensitivity (e.g., personalized discounts).

Authentication. Authentication involves two dimensions, identity (of buyer and seller) and quality. In the case of the seller, the latter involves the quality of both the organization (its credibility and quality of business practices) and the quality of the product or service itself. In the case of the buyer, the relevant issue is the credibility and integrity of the buyer (e.g., the likelihood that the buyer will pay for his purchase). In evaluating a Web site, support for identity authentication includes use of digital certificates and secure protocols such as SSL, while authentication of quality is exemplified by the use of trusted third parties (e.g., Better Business Bureau Online, Truste), as well as references to external press releases. Product quality authentication functions entail descriptive product information, customer testimonials, virtual communities, standards on quality, expert opinions, trials, and warranty policies. Authentication of the buyer embraces digital certificates, registration at the Web site, e-mail authentication, and offline mechanisms. Customization of product authentication pertains to guiding buyers to particular parts of discussions in virtual communities that address specific products, or even product attributes.

Payment. The extent of online payment support embraces e-wallets, shopping cart mechanisms, online credit card payments, smart card systems, e-cash, and payment processes through trusted third parties (e.g., First Virtual). Offline payment support refers to check, cash (e.g., on delivery), wire, and offline credit card payments. The extent of support for customization addresses personalization of payment options, differentiated payment programs, installment/lay-away plans, and electronic wallets.

Logistics. Core logistics support includes offline delivery by seller or agent, identification of nearest point of sale, provision of basic order status services as well as online order tracking functionalities. The extent of support for online logistics is measured by the availability of online delivery and the provision of multiple-source sites, i.e., information on the nearest server to download from. The extent of support for customizing logistics refers to order consolidation and multiple delivery options. For logistics, we identified two overall logistics processes. The extent of support for offline logistics combines the extent of core logistics support with the extent of support for customizing logistics, whereas the extent of support for digital products logistics also includes extent of support for online logistics.

In addition to constructing measures for the basic commerce processes in EC, a measure tapping the extent of services for supporting the customer after the transaction was defined. This customer support measure involves support on product use, repair, and disposal, dispute resolution services, service contracts, virtual communities, and feedback options.

Also, a measure of the degree to which overall customization is supported was constructed. This overall customization measure summarizes the separate customization sub-processes as identified in Table 2.

## 3.3. The sample

Each site in the sample group was visited and analyzed by one or more graduate students in an electronic commerce course at a leading US University in early 1999. A total of 141 Web sites across 10 industries that provide consumer retail services were examined (see Fig. 1 for a description of the sample). The decision to focus on the business-to-consumer segment of EC was driven by the fact that this segment was the predominant and more advanced segment of EC at the time of the study. It is important to note that the Electronic Commerce Architecture can be used to analyze adoption trends in the business-tobusiness segment of EC as well.

The choice of Web sites and industries was primarily guided by concerns for generalizability of the results. The choice of industries was made to obtain variations along two major dimensions, product type and product cost. Given the natural affinity of the EC environment for digital products, a distinction was made between industries offering physical products and industries offering digital products. In contrast to physical products, digital products can be digitized and delivered via electronic networks. Digital products not only include information and entertainment products (e.g., newspapers, books, music recordings, television programs, software, computer games), but also embrace symbols, tokens and concepts (e.g., flight reservations, sport events tickets, financial instruments such as securities), as well as services (e.g., car insurance, welfare payments) [6]. In this study, the air travel, the gaming, the music-CD, and the news industry are considered digital product industries, whereas the automotive parts, the book, the consumer electronics (which includes computers), the gift service, the home entertainment, and the retail clothing industry are viewed as physical product industries. It should be noted that this classification scheme deviates from the above definition of digital products. Rather than adhering to this theoretical definition, the classification used in this study is based on the current feasibility and acceptance of delivering digital products in digital format in various industries. For instance, as the delivery of music over the Internet is rampant (e.g., through the MP3 standard, the Real Audio standard, etc.) the music-CD industry is considered to be a digital product industry. Conversely, given that, at the time of the study, no book company was delivering books in digital format, the book industry was viewed as a physical product industry even though, theoretically, books can be delivered via the Internet.

As to product cost, a distinction was made between inexpensive and expensive products. As consumers are more involved with expensive purchases than with the buying of low-cost products [1], it is of interest to investigate whether there are profound differences in support for online commerce processes across companies selling low- versus high-cost products. In this study, the book, the gaming, the music-CD, the home entertainment, and the news industry are considered low-cost product industries (average sales price less than \$50), whereas the air travel, the automotive parts, the consumer electronics, the gift service, and the retail clothing industry are considered high-cost product industries (average sales price greater than or equal to \$50).

Finally, it should be noted that the goal of the Web site analysis study was to examine the extent to which companies adopting EC were supporting the different commerce processes, and was not to assess the extent of EC adoption across each industry as a whole. Thus, the sample was designed to be representative of those companies that already had an online presence in each industry, rather than the whole industry.

## 3.4. Data analysis techniques

The extent of support for commerce processes in EC was analyzed using descriptive statistics of central tendency and dispersion. Pearson or non-parametric Kendall Tau-b correlation coefficients were used to study the relationships between the commerce processes in EC. The analysis of the differences across types of industries was based on independent samples: t-tests, Mann – Whitney U-tests, or Kruskal – Wallis one-way ANOVAs. In order to statistically account for multiple testing the Bonferroni inequality for developing joint confidence intervals with a specified family confidence coefficient was relied on. Given the conservative nature of the Bonferroni procedure, the family confidence coefficient was specified at 90% [16].

## 4.1. Extent of support for commerce processes in EC

A key finding of this research study is that companies engaging in EC predominantly rely on a few, basic functionalities to support their online businesses. Indeed, companies entering the online marketplace dedicate most of their efforts to facilitate the product search process, display price information, and support the offline logistics process. This can be seen in Table 3, which shows that the searching the products online (61%), the display of price information (58%), the offline logistics (46%, combining core logistics and logistics customization), and the search-related support (44%) processes enjoy the highest levels of support. Conversely, support for other search and valuation sub-processes, as well as support for authentication and payment processes, is remarkably low. Overall, companies do not heavily promote their online presence by buying keywords in Web portals or displaying banner ads, virtually ignore price determination mechanisms such as price matching and product – price mix negotiation, rely mostly on credit card payments for online transactions, and pay little attention to authentication issues. Even when the analysis is limited to the Web sites that support online sales (see Table 4), support for EC is again predominantly limited to the same basic (sub) processes. Indeed, when looking at the mean extent of support for commerce processes among the 67.4% of companies that provide online sales, the highest levels of mean support are observed for the display of price information (76%), the searching products online (69%), the offline logistics (63%), and the searchrelated support (50%) processes. A likely explanation for this finding is that companies engaging in EC are merely extending existing business functionalities online. By focusing on the automation of a limited set of rudimentary business functionalities, companies can easily manage their online businesses and achieve potential cost savings. However, companies comfortable with the status quo and confining EC to the automation or mechanization of existing business functionalities are potentially missing much of the commercial opportunities offered by the Internet. Another striking observation, in line with the previous finding, is that most of the surveyed companies have done little to exploit the unique customization and personalization capabilities of the Internet. Apart from support for customizing logistics, and to a lesser extent support for payment customization, all other customization sub-processes are only weakly supported. This is of particular concern given that the ability to create and foster personalized customer relationships is one of the key factors differentiating the Internet from traditional marketing environments, where such customization is much harder to accomplish. Clearly, if companies want to unleash these revolutionary new capabilities of the Internet, they should strive to exploit information on customers’ personal preferences and idiosyncrasies at each of the commerce process levels, so that long-term, trusting, personal relationships with valued customers can be built and a sustainable competitive advantage can be achieved.

Similarly, most of the surveyed sites limited customer support to visitor or buyer feedback. Indeed, by providing more extensive support on product use, repair, and disposal, dispute resolution services, virtual communities, and service contracts, companies can assist the customer in the post-purchase stage and, hence, dramatically enhance customer satisfaction. Moreover, companies can leverage the information gathered at the customer support level to further strengthen customer relationships (e.g., by offering customized service contracts) and possibly pushing potentially interesting complementary products or services.

Another surprising finding is that the adoption of online logistics appears to be very slow. That is, Web retailers in digital product industries do not extensively use the Internet to deliver their products (see the low level of the mean extent of support for the online logistics process (29%) in Table 3). This can be explained by heavy reliance on physical containers that ‘‘imprison’’ the digital product. For instance, many Web retailers in the gaming industry still sell their games on CD-ROM (e.g., Electronic Arts http:// www.ea.com)), while some air-travel companies send tickets through regular mail (e.g., http://www.americanairlines.com). Furthermore, most companies that do support online logistics also support offline logistics. Examples of this hybrid approach are Music Boulevard http://www.musicboulevard.com), which offers encoded music online delivery as well as traditional CDs, and the Wall Street Journal, which offers both its paper edition as its customized online journal http://www.wsj.com). Clearly, all of the above-mentioned digital products do not necessarily need a physical presence. Releasing these digital products from their physical containers eliminates the need for expensive, time consuming, offline delivery and offers huge opportunities for customization, discriminatory pricing, and cost-effective online logistics.

## 4.2. Overall pattern of correlations between commerce processes in EC

Consistent with intuition, most companies that extensively support online sales were found to provide stronger support for the valuation, payment, and offline logistics processes. Indeed, the observed correlations between extent of support for valuation, payment, and offline logistics are high, as can be seen in Table 5, while the extent of correlation between these three processes and support for online sales respectively amounts to 0.74 ( P < 0.0001), 0.75 ( P<0.0001), and 0.72 ( P<0.0001). However, it is important to note that as support for online sales increases, customer support is only weakly extended (r=0.33, P<0.0001). Again, as suggested in Section 4.1, companies could benefit from going beyond the provision of rudimentary feedback forms and redefining the customer support process in function of the new capabilities offered by the Internet.

Similarly, at the customization level, there is a lot of room for innovation. As the extent of support for online sales increases all efforts seem to be devoted to setting up customized payment and logistics arrangements, whereas search, authentication, and valuation customization opportunities are largely left untouched. Indeed, looking at the correlations, we observe that the extent of support for online sales is highly correlated with support for payment customization (r=0.59, P<0.0001) and logistics customization (r = 0.72, P < 0.0001), whereas no significant correlations are found for search and authentication customization, and only a weak correlation is observed for valuation customization (r=0.25, P<0.0001). As to the valuation customization process, it is also important to note that virtually no Web retailer uses the power of the Internet to offer personalized discounts or other ways of tailoring prices to individual customer’s levels of price sensitivity.

Another important observation is that Web retailers appear to mainly target pre-informed customers and rely on a marketing communication strategy in which awareness of the Web site is assumed and predominantly built offline. As support for online sales increases, companies seem to limit support for the search process to the provision of more extensive search functionalities within their Web site and neglect online awareness building processes. Indeed, the significant moderate correlations between search and support for valuation, payment, offline logistics, and online sales are driven by the extent of searching the products online sub-process.

Surprisingly, the overall authentication process does not correlate with any of the other basic commerce processes in EC. This is of particular concern given that the authentication process plays an especially important role in EC, due to the absence of physical authentication that is commonplace in traditional marketplaces. It should be noted, however, that, even though the broad spectrum of authentication functionalities is not supported, there is awareness of product quality authentication. Indeed, this subprocess is moderately correlated with all of the basic commerce processes in EC. A possible explanation for this finding is that offline established brand equity and trust may diminish the need for authentication and limit authentication issues to assuring the quality of new or improved products and services. Furthermore, new technologies (e.g., digital certificates) and procedures (e.g., endorsement by a trusted third party) that are available to establish Web site and buyer authentication may still be poorly understood by Web retailers and customers alike, driving Web retailers to rely on familiar, offline authentication strategies. It is also of interest to note that, although a high correlation may be expected between support for authentication and support for payment due to the use of SSL at the payment level to authenticate the identity of the seller, no significant correlation was found as a result of the lack of support for authentication of the quality of the Web site.

In summary, Web retailers are not fully exploiting the potential synergies among support for the various commerce and customization processes in EC. Our study indicates that Web retailers still largely focus on basic search, valuation, payment, and logistics functionalities. The only types of customization that have moderate levels of support are logistics customization (49%) and to a lesser extent, payment customization (23%). Thus, Web retailers are still not exploiting EC by simultaneously providing support for the various commerce processes and capturing information on customers’ preferences and idiosyncrasies at each commerce process level, which would enable them to provide more value to the customer and achieve higher performance levels.

## 4.3.1. Digital product versus physical product industries: extent of support for commerce processes in EC

Since all commerce processes can be supported online for digital product industries, especially the online logistics process, one would expect to observe that companies in these industries are more extensively using the Internet to sell their products and services than companies in physical product industries. Our study confirms this prior expectation with support for online walk-in based and/or subscription-based sales at 79.6% in digital product industries, as opposed to 59.8% in physical product industries ( P=0.0055). Table 6 details the actual frequencies for both types of online sales support. Likewise, the observed differences in terms of support for commerce processes in EC between digital and physical product industries are consistent with intuition. As the extent of support for online sales is significantly stronger in digital product industries, we would expect to see stronger support for commerce processes in these industries. Indeed, our study shows that companies selling digital products provide significantly more support for the search, authentication, payment, and customer support processes than industries selling physical products (see the mean extent of support for these processes in Table 7). Although no significant difference was observed for the valuation process, this can readily be explained by the overly narrow focus of both kinds of industries on posting listed prices for their products.

## 4.3.3. Low-cost product versus high-cost product industries: extent of support for commerce processes in EC

Another useful dimension for specialization of the analysis is in terms of whether a Web site sells expensive or inexpensive products. A remarkable finding is that companies selling low-cost products are more progressive and provide various options for overall authentication, authentication of the buyer, authentication of the product, and authentication customization, while companies offering high-cost products are more conservative, providing only fixed authentication mechanisms and concentrating on support for offline logistics processes (see Table 10 for a comparison of the mean extent of support for these processes). While the higher levels of authentication support in low-cost product industries are promising, it should be noted that the extent of support for authentication is still low for both types of industries. This suggests that for companies engaging in EC, and especially for companies offering high-cost products, most of the authentication is achieved unrelated to the Internet.

Not surprisingly, Web retailers offering low-cost products provide more extensive support for searching their company online. Indeed, because consumers of low-cost products typically have low involvement and possibly do a lot of brand switching, a Web retailer needs to put extra effort in generating traffic to its Web site to get consumers of most low-cost products to visit or revisit the company’s Web site. On the other hand, sellers of high-cost products offer more extensive search-related support services, and provide stronger support for price determination mechanisms. This is also in line with prior expectations since expensive purchases typically involve more buyer deliberation and are more likely to involve product – price mix negotiation.

An interesting finding is that Web retailers of highcost products provide more extensive support for online payment. This may indicate a growing awareness among Web retailers that buyers of expensive products and services are willing to settle payments online, now that secure, easy-to-use online payment instruments are available. It may also be due to the fact that prevalent online payment mechanisms such as credit cards are more attractive for larger payment sums, due to the associated transaction costs.

Although the small sample sizes contribute to the lack of significant findings, no clear patterns of correlations between the commerce processes in EC emerged. Given the absence of clear patterns of correlations between commerce processes in EC for every individual industry, it may be argued that support for commerce processes in EC is not consistent within industries and is still confined to a few early movers who more fully embrace the potential of the Internet and EC than industries as a whole do.

## 5. Conclusion and directions for future research

This paper examines the extent to which companies in various industries are using the World Wide Web and its associated technologies to conduct retail business. Using the framework of an Electronic Commerce Architecture (ECA), a variety of commercial Web sites are analyzed to determine which commerce processes are being supported online in each industry and each type of industry. Our findings indicate that adoption of Web technology and electronic commerce practices is still quite primitive in many industries, even those in which early movers have made significant advances. Many Web retailers seem to be confining EC to the automation of a narrow set of existing business functionalities. They also appear to mainly target pre-informed customers and rely on a marketing communication strategy in which Web site awareness, brand equity and trust are predominantly established offline. Little is done to leverage information on customers’ personal preferences and idiosyncrasies at all of the commerce process levels. Also, most companies in digital product industries are largely forgoing the benefits of online delivery and customized product design/bundling.

As discussed in Basu and Muylle [3], the ECA is useful both for identifying the scope of a firm’s EC adoption level, as well as for determining potential trajectories for expanding this scope. In this paper, we have shown how it also serves as a useful basis for surveying the competitive landscape. We are in the process of expanding our external assessment with critical internal assessment as well, by using the ECA to develop a survey instrument for Web site managers.

It is our belief that the internal view provided by such a study will serve as a valuable complement to the current work, and provide insight into not just support of commerce processes online, but also support of internal business processes.

In addition to assisting managers and strategic planners in engaging in EC, this study also raises two important questions regarding the successful exploitation of EC. First, must a company uniformly pursue online support for all commerce processes in order to compete successfully in the EC environment, or are there particular combinations of processes that are key determinants of success for each industry, company type, target market, etc.? Second, what are the implications of pursuing fully fledged EC for organizational structure and management systems? As commerce processes cut across existing vertical business functions and departments, and customization and personalization require adaptability to constantly changing customer demands, companies engaging in EC need to rethink organizational structures with the capabilities of Internet technology in mind in order to abundantly exploit this promising new locus of commerce.
