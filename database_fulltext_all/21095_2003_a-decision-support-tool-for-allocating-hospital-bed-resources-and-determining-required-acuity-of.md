# A decision support tool for allocating hospital bed resources and determining required acuity of care

Steven Walczak <sup>a,</sup>\*, Walter E. Pofahl <sup>b</sup>, Ronald J. Scorpio <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>College of Business and Administration, University of Colorado at Denver, Campus Box 165, PO Box 173364, Denver, CO 80217-3364, USA <sup>b</sup>Department of Surgery, Brody School of Medicine, East Carolina University, Greenville, NC 27835, USA <sup>c</sup>Spartanburg Regional Healthcare System, 101 East Wood Street, Spartanburg, SC 29303, USA

Accepted 28 February 2002

## Abstract

Limitations in health care funding require physicians and hospitals to find effective ways to utilize resources. Neural networks provide a method for predicting resource utilization of costly resources used for prolonged periods of time. Injury severity knowledge is used to determine the acuity of care required for each patient and length of stay is used to determine duration of inpatient hospitalization. Neural networks perform well on these medical domain problems, predicting total length of stay within 3 days for pediatric trauma (population mean and S.D. 4.37 F 45.12) and within 4 days for acute pancreatitis patients (7.75 F 79.19).

<sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Neural networks; Hospitalization; Length of stay; Trauma; Pancreatitis; Acuity of care; Fuzzy ARTMAP; Backpropagation

## 1. Introduction

Hospitals are faced with limited resources including beds to hold admitted patients. This resource constraint is particularly important in specialized areas of the hospital, such as intensive care (ICU) and intermediate care units, since the number of beds available is a fraction of the number of floor beds, where the intensity of care is less. Cost-effective patient management is critically dependent on accurate assessment of individual patient outcome and resource utilization [3]. Evaluating length of stay (LOS) information is a challenging task [21], but is essential for the operational success of a hospital. Intensive care resources in particular are often limited and pose scheduling problems for hospital staff and administrators [18]. Predicting LOS is difficult and is often only done retrospectively. There are different components of a patient’s LOS. Lengths of stay may be evaluated for ICUs, step down units, and floor units individually, and may also be evaluated as an aggregation of all areas to provide a total LOS. Existing research in this area looks at predicting or analyzing the LOS for patients in a specific hospital area (e.g., ICU) [3,7,10,11,18]. The research presented in this article will initially be concerned with ICU LOS, but will ultimately address predicting the LOS for a patient at all levels of care in the hospital and providing a total LOS.

A patient’s LOS is correlated with that patient’s injury or illness severity [9], that is patients who are more critically ill normally require greater lengths of stay within hospitals to return to a functional level. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that a single decision support model will be able to estimate both of these values for patients with a given set of input variables. Establishing a patient’s injury severity may require extensive and invasive testing of the patient.

Earlier identification of injury severity, and consequently LOS, would enable better resource planning and ultimately improve patient care by the hospital. The likely correlation between injury severity and LOS indicates that if characteristic variables of injury severity can be identified, they can also be used to model LOS. Since the relationship between the characteristic variables (e.g., age, type of injury, history of heart disease, . . .) and injury severity (and consequently LOS) is unknown, a nonparametric modeling technique is desired. Additionally, the modeling of injury severity or LOS appears to be a categorization problem. Prediction of the acuity of care required by a patient (or injury/illness severity) naturally separates the population of patients into several distinct categories (discussed in more detail in Sections 3.3 and 4). Those patients that have critical injuries and must be treated in an ICU unit are members of one category of patient and patients with more moderate injuries and only requiring care in a standard floor unit are members of another category. Neural networks are an ideal tool for creating nonparametric categorization models.

The research in this article utilizes a supervised learning neural network approach to create a decision support tool that accurately predicts the LOS for two distinct patient populations: (1) pediatric trauma patients and (2) patients with acute pancreatitis. Patients with acute pancreatitis are initially treated in a hospital’s emergency room (ER), but are not considered trauma patients. Additionally, the neural network models will define the acuity of care needed (or injury severity) for the pediatric trauma patients. Severity measures were not available for the acute pancreatitis patients, however, domain experts have communicated with the research team that illness severity is normally categorized as a direct function of the LOS (i.e., S = f(LOS) + e).

Data for the pediatric trauma patients are taken from the National Pediatric Trauma Registry, which currently contains over 8000 records of patients that have been treated at a participating hospital’s emergency room, while data for the patients with acute pancreatitis are obtained from patient records at a single hospital. The research will attempt to confirm the efficacy of using neural networks with information that is available within the first 10 min (without the use of invasive tests) of the patient’s arrival at an emergency room to accurately predict a patient’s LOS and the severity of the patient’s injuries.

## 2. Background and significance

Neural network applications in medicine have been primarily limited to laboratory applications (e.g., test evaluation) or medical imaging applications [6,14]. Buchman et al. [3] reports that neural networks in patient assessment domains typically outperform standard statistical modeling techniques. Recently, a few applications of neural networks, described in the ensuing paragraphs, have been made in the area of patient planning and medical resource allocation for patient care.

Previous studies that examine the use of neural networks for predicting patient outcomes have been limited to only predicting the occurrence of mortality [5,7,8,12]. Most of these neural network predictors of mortality are specific to a particular type of injury, such as rib fracture [5], burns [7], or postcoronary care [12]. The research presented in this article significantly extends this type of neural network research by categorizing those patients who will not die into multiple distinct groups: four categories for trauma patients and three categories for acute pancreatitis (medical) patients. While acute pancreatitis is a specific illness, pediatric trauma encompasses a wide variety (different types) of injury.

A few attempts have been made to use neural networks to predict a patient’s hospital or ICU LOS. These attempts all have significant drawbacks. Most neural network LOS prediction models only predict a broad category for the LOS [3,7,8,11], such as less than 7 days or greater than 7 days, thus, breaking the patient LOS problem into a two-group classification problem. Unfortunately, the two-group approach does not provide hospital administrators with enough information to adequately plan for hospital resource allocation. Furthermore, all existing neural network attempts to predict patient LOS work only for a specific type of injury. Additionally, the only neural network attempt to predict the precise number of days for the LOS [13] as well as other neural networks that utilize the broad two-group classification of patient LOS [11,18] required extensive testing and preevaluation of the patient to acquire the input values used in the neural network model. The methodology used for the research presented in this article and discussed below, uses values that are readily available within the first 10 min of a patient’s presentation to an emergency room. Furthermore, the developed neural network systems predict the actual number of days (as opposed to a weekly or other LOS categorization value) for all patients who are treated in an emergency room. This approach is more generalized than previous approaches and supplies a more accurate determination of both injury severity and LOS with readily available data at a much earlier time in the patient’s treatment.

All of the referenced neural network applications have been developed using the neural network training technique of backpropagation. While backpropagation is a well-known and widely used technique [1,12], many other neural network training methodologies exist and may be more appropriate for medical domain applications. The research discussed in this article uses various supervised learning neural network training methods, including backpropagation, to determine the best possible modeling paradigm for the medical domain problems of predicting a patient’s LOS and the patient’s severity of injury or illness.

## 3. Neural network models for LOS prediction

In this section, the methodology used to design and implement the respective neural network models is discussed. The neural network design methodology follows the guidelines for neural network implementation made by Walczak and Cerpa [20].

## 3.1. Independent variable selection

The first step in the development of any neural network problem solution is to identify the independent variables [20]. A possible reason why neural network applications in medical domains have lagged behind nonmedical domains is the difficulty in determining which clinical variables are relevant [3]. The National Pediatric Trauma Registry provides 371 data values concerning the complete care of all pediatric patients that arrive at the emergency room of a participating hospital. Many of the data values are eliminated immediately because they are not available within 10 min of the patient’s arrival. The remaining values are screened using experts in the domain to determine all variables that could in any way contribute to a prediction of injury severity or LOS.

The pediatric trauma domain experts identified nineteen variables as potential characteristic variables. Six of these variables are condensed into three, because of partial overlap of information, to reduce noise in the neural network model introduced from correlated variables [17]. An example of variable information overlap is a variable representing if CPR was given during transport and another variable representing if CPR is administered in the ER, which is condensed into a single variable indicating that CPR was initiated on or before arrival in the hospital’s ER. Two of the variables (type of injury (e.g., blunt, penetrating, crushing, other) and mechanism of injury (e.g., motor vehicle accident, sports related, animal related) are categorical and required 3 and 16 neural network variables, respectively, to encode the desired information. Finally, two of the remaining variables are rejected due to statistical insignificance in the data distribution (not enough examples to train and test), such as the application of MAST trousers which occurred in only 4 out of the 8840 patients. Resulting in a 31-value input vector to the neural network, representing 14 independent variables. Due to the possible commercialization of the described neural network methodology, the full variable lists for all reported neural networks will not be described except in general terms (researchers and other medical informatics developers may contact the primary author for details on the specific domain variables selected).

The qualitative variables for the type of injury (three input nodes representing four possible values) indicate the breadth of conditions evaluated by neural network for predicting the LOS and acuity of care for pediatric trauma patients. When all three variables have a value of zero, this indicates the ‘‘other’’ value from the original data set, which is complex and includes: abrasions, lacerations, burns, cardiac or pulmonary failure, and any other condition treated as a trauma case in the ER. Thus, greater than eight distinct categories of injury are being evaluated by the single neural network.

Separate neural network models are developed for the pediatric trauma and the acute pancreatitis patients, due to the potential qualitative differences between pediatric trauma patients and adult pancreatitis patients and to maximize the predictive capabilities of the neural network models. Thus, two distinct knowledge acquisition phases are used to identify the relevant independent variables for each neural network model. While there exist some similarities between the two models (e.g., age, heart rate, and systolic blood pressure), there are explicit differences also such as the use of various blood test values available for the chronic illness patients that are normally not obtained for pediatric trauma patients. The common theme in both models is that only values that are typically available in the first 10 min of a patient’s treatment in an ER are used to predict severity and LOS.

The input values to the neural networks for predicting the LOS of pancreatitis patients are determined from a collection of 146 data values recorded for acute pancreatitis patients at a specific hospital. Again, domain experts are used to determine appropriate variables: patient information values that are available at the time of admission to the hospital or shortly thereafter (e.g., age, gender, heart rate, temperature, hemoglobin, hematocrit, and various other blood analysis results), as inputs to the network. All of the acute pancreatitis patients seen at the hospital that provided the patient data, had been monitored by a physician for a period of time prior to their admittance into the hospital and, hence, the blood analysis values were readily available from the patient’s records. If new blood analysis values (hematocrit, hemoglobin, etc.) are required, then the use of the blood analysis input values delays the potential time of use for the neural network from the first 10 min until 1 – 2 h after ER admission.

Two different input sets, one with 44 elements and one with 22 input elements are used for training the described neural networks. The input vector reduction is accomplished by removing input values that contained a lot of noise (i.e., missing values; which caused the elimination of training and test samples in the 44 input data sets). The reduction of required data elements increases the number of data samples that contained all of the required data. The validation test set went from 57 cases for the 44-input variable model to 100 cases for the 22-input variable model, thus, increasing the statistical significance of the research results. While historical patient data may contain missing or noisy data elements, once a data element is identified as necessary most hospitals are extremely reliable in procuring the required data. Hence, the proactive use of the described neural networks will realistically never be faced with missing input data elements.

## 3.2. Training (learning) algorithm

Backpropagation, radial basis function, and fuzzy ARTMAP are the training methods used to optimize the neural networks. The backpropagation neural network learning algorithm is commonly used [1,12] and has been proven to be a reliable general classification tool [8,22]. Backpropagation trained neural networks have demonstrated superior performance over other types of supervised training neural networks for specific problems [19]. However, the backpropagation technique has been criticized for failure to extrapolate and sometimes getting trapped in local minima (instead of a global minima) [2].

Radial basis function (RBF) trained neural networks are selected as an alternative training paradigm due to their superior performance over backpropagation trained neural networks when extrapolation from the training population is required [2]. Fuzzy ART-MAP trained neural networks are also implemented to evaluate the performance of adaptive resonance theory learning paradigms and because of their reliable performance when operating in noisy or fuzzy domains [4]. Noisy input should not be a factor once the neural network systems are adopted. However, the fuzziness of data input values (approximations or rounding errors) and the need for extrapolation are unknown and result in the selection of the three different training methods to optimize the performance of the resulting neural network models. Readers interested in the mathematical details of the neural network learning algorithms are referred to Refs. [2,4,8,12,17,19,22].

## 3.3. Neural network hidden-layer architectures

The input vector is determined through the selection and representation of the independent variables by the domain experts. Recall that all of the pediatric trauma neural networks will utilize the same input vector and the pancreatitis neural networks will all use a common input vector, but one that is different from the pediatric trauma networks. The output vector for all of the neural networks is composed of the predicted LOS values for: ICU/PICU, step-down or other intermediate care unit, floor unit, and total LOS. Additionally, the output vector contains the categorization of the patient into one of the distinct severity of injury or illness classifications (that indicate required acuity of care) appropriate to the specific medical domain problem.

A concern in neural network research and application is the overfitting or underfitting of the training data set by the neural network model [2]. Underfitting and overfitting both produce poor generalization performance. Typically, the quantity of hidden nodes in the neural network’s architecture is the primary control on the fit between the training data set and the neural network model. To control and minimize the performance problems associated with underfitting and overfitting the data, numerous hidden node architectures are tested.

Each backpropagation trained neural network decision support model (pediatric trauma and pancreatitis) is implemented with both one and two hidden layer architectures. The quantity of nodes in each layer is varied from 5N to 2N, where N is the size of the preceding layer in nodes (i.e., the input vector size for the first hidden layer), using a step-size of 5. Additional nodes were not added to create a new architecture if the neural network’s performance dropped off after initially increasing, which is an indication of overfitting [2]. This resulted in over 100 backpropagation-trained neural network architectures being tested for each of the medical domain problems.

The RBF neural network requires a hidden prototype layer that uses unsupervised learning to learn categorizations of the input vector values and then uses a nearest neighbor approximation to align new input values with the determined categories [19]. The implementation used for the research reported in this article utilizes an optional hidden layer that occurs after the prototype layer to enable supervised learning between the categories created with unsupervised learning. Again, various architectures are implemented using the same protocol that implemented the various backpropagation models, but this time requiring that both the prototype and optional hidden layer are present. This protocol still produces just over 100 different architectures for each medical domain problem.

Fuzzy ARTMAP neural networks implement two ART I neural networks, each of which has the same size as the input vector and, hence, is predetermined. These two ART I neural networks are combined using a hidden layer designated as the F2 layer [4,19]. Multiple F2 architectures are implemented utilizing a protocol similar to the RBF and backpropagation protocols, with F2 nodes varying from 5N to 2N with a step-size of 5, where N is the size of the input vector (as opposed to the two ART neural networks layer that is double the size of the input vector).

Since not every possible architecture is implemented, the results in the next section should be viewed as the minimum achievable performance by a neural network model (and not as an optimum). With well over 200 neural network architectures implemented for each of the two medical domain problems of pediatric trauma and acute pancreatitis, only those networks that produced the best generalization results for a particular training paradigm are reported in this article.

## 4. Results

Separate neural network models are developed and implemented for each of the two medical domain problems, as described in the previous section. Recall that the pediatric trauma model covers numerous types of trauma categorizing the patient into one of five categories (four of which are presented due to the lack of data in the fifth category) and the acute pancreatitis model places patients into three unique categories. Each model is presented in turn.

## 4.1. Pediatric trauma neural network models

As mentioned earlier, the Registry contains data on over 8000 pediatric trauma cases. To simulate a prospective proactive use of the neural network, the data is divided into training and test sets based upon date. A validation of the simulation is performed by randomly selecting 3000 trauma cases from the database regardless of date. The randomized trial had nearly identical (with a mean difference of less than 0.1 days for LOS predictions) results to the proactive simulation. Since the proactive simulation is more realistic with respect to actual implementation and use, only the proactive simulation results are reported. All pediatric trauma patient records recording injuries occurring prior to June 30, 1996, approximately 4200 examples, are in the training set, while the remaining 3881 cases form the test set. Results for the backpropagation neural network predicting LOS values for ICU, step-down, floor, and total LOS for the pediatric trauma domain problem are displayed in Table 1. The minimum and maximum stay predictions for the networks along with the mean absolute difference (MAD, also called the RMSE) between the predictions and the actual LOS is shown. Calculations of the p-values are performed using a t-test between the predicted LOS made by each neural network and the actual LOS of each patient.

While all three neural network training methods categorize patient mortality with 80 – 88% accuracy and 100% sensitivity, the presence of these patients decreases the accuracy of the LOS predictions. This is due to the fact that the majority of the critically ill patients who suffer mortality, die within the first 36 h, while those that survive have extended hospital stays due to the severity of their injuries and injury related artifacts (e.g., surgical complications or infection). The neural network models have patients that die in the hospital removed from the training set, to improve the accuracy of the neural network’s LOS predictions on out-of-sample data. Those patients that do suffer mortality are still represented in the out-of-sample test set for each neural network architecture and training paradigm.

The single hidden layer backpropagation trained neural network outperforms all of the other neural network models when predicting LOS for the various hospital units a patient will visit and in total, with the exception of the fuzzy ARTMAP predictions for stepdown unit LOS. It is also interesting to note that the single hidden layer backpropagation trained neural network model is able to predict the LOS of 52% of all patients to within 1 day.

Evaluation of the various neural networks’ performances on categorizing pediatric trauma patient’s injury severity (and consequent acuity of care) is more difficult. Unfortunately, there is only one example of a low severity patient in the test set, so this case is not shown. The values in Table 2 are prediction accuracy, that is the number of correctly predicted injury severity cases for each category divided by the actual number of cases treated in that category. The distribution of injury severity, with respect to discharge from the ER, in the test set population is shown at the bottom of Table 2, but for purposes of evaluating the LOS results: 43% spent time in PICU, just over 8% spent time in a step-down or other intermediate care unit, and approximately 99% spent time on a floor unit), with the remainder being either low or mortality patients. This indicates that even though a pediatric trauma patient is initially admitted to the PICU, the patient may also spend time on an intermediate care unit and will probably spend time on a floor unit.

Assuming that moderate patients, those discharged to a floor bed, have an innately higher recuperation rate than those classified as critical (discharged directly to an operating room or to an ICU bed) or severe, then the better performance of the fuzzy ARTMAP on these categories of patients provides the hospital with better patient care planning and ultimately with better patient recovery. Another concern in evaluating the neural network models performance is the validity of the historic patient records. Economically, the cost of care for a patient in a floor unit bed is less than patients in a PICU bed (due to continuous monitoring and more advanced care stratagems). It may be possible that patients treated as moderate severity and admitted to a floor unit bed, may have had a shorter overall LOS if they had first been stabilized in a PICU or intermediate care unit as predicted by the neural network models.

The aggregate prediction accuracy across severity categories may be calculated by multiplying each categorization accuracy (given in the table) by the percentage of patients historically classified in that category (bottom row of the table). This yields an aggregate prediction accuracy of 66.7% for the single hidden layer backpropagation trained model and a 44.3% aggregate prediction accuracy for the fuzzy ARTMAP trained model (with values in between these two for the other models).

The higher aggregate performance of the single hidden layer backpropagation neural network model is achieved at the expense of not identifying some of the more critically injured/ill patients. The zero percent accuracy for intermediate care (severe) patients in the backpropagation trained models is likely due to the small percentage of actual patients categorized as belonging to this severity group. Since the backpropagation neural networks utilize a single, although very complex, model to categorize the severity and predict the LOS for all patients, the backpropagation models maximize their effectiveness by concentrating first on the moderate severity patients and then on the critical severity patients. The fuzzy ARTMAP on the other hand, uses a pattern grouping (unsupervised learning) approach, which first recognizes each of the independent categories to be evaluated, then uses supervised learning on its F2 layer to maximize the categorization performance of the neural network model. Thus, the fuzzy ARTMAP model accurately categorizes patients in all severity groups (at the price of lower overall performance), while the backpropagation model has only succeeded in learning to categorize patient trauma severity into one of the two most prevalent groups (comprising 94% of the population). Adjusting the quantities of different severity groups in the training sets (to be more evenly distributed) may enable the backpropagation and RBF trained neural network models to more accurately predict these small groups [23] and is a topic for future research.

Why do two different neural network training methods produce the best performance for the two related medical domain problems of predicting the LOS and categorizing the injury severity? If the two problems are in fact synonymous, then the same neural network training algorithm and architecture should produce the best results for both problems.

However, as indicated above, backpropagation trained neural networks produce better results than fuzzy ARTMAP trained neural networks for LOS predictions, while fuzzy ARTMAP outperforms backpropagation for categorization of injury severity with respect to classification of more critical trauma severity categories.

The differences between the two different neural networks heuristically indicate that although correlated, the LOS and injury severity medical domain problems are unique types of problems. The fuzzy ARTMAP trained neural networks perform the best on injury severity because this problem is more of a traditional unsupervised pattern recognition problem, separating the population into distinct categories that already have traditional heuristic methods such as the Glasgow Comma Score. However, the backpropagation trained neural networks perform the best on LOS predictions because this type of problem may best be represented using a more algorithmic approach (e.g., a complex sigmoid equation) instead of a standard pattern classification approach. The results from the presented research indicate that the two medical domain problems, with respect to pediatric trauma patients, must be modeled using different types of models and that a using a single modeling approach for predicting both LOS and injury severity may produce inferior results.

The 22-input variable neural network model consistently outperformed the 44 variable model with respect to the mean absolute difference in LOS, and so only the 22-input variable model results are discussed. Since this prediction problem uses more than two classifications, specificity is defined as the accurate segmentation of the pancreatitis patient population into three categories of hospital stay: short (stays less than or equal to 1 week), medium (more than 1 week, but less than or equal to 2 weeks), and long (greater than 2 weeks). A single hidden layer backpropagation network produces an overall (aggregated) illness severity specificity of 72%. This means that the neural network model can accurately predict the severity of the acute pancreatitis problem for 72% of acute pancreatitis patients within 10 min of arrival at the emergency room, placing them correctly into short, medium, and long recovery periods.

The LOS specificity for the pancreatitis domain is similar to the injury severity rating from the pediatric trauma domain, with the exception that a closeness criterion may be established for the LOS specificity values (whereas a patient is either admitted to ICU or is not admitted). For the single hidden layer backpropagation neural network, if a 1-day overlap is permitted between the LOS categories, then the medium stay category accuracy increases to 42%. Furthermore, if a 2-day overlap is used between categories, then the short stay accuracy increases to 90% and the medium stay accuracy increases to 75%, with no change in the long stay accuracy (producing an aggregated accuracy of 82.44%). This indicates that when the backpropagation trained neural network model misses the illness severity categorization, it is usually very close (within 2 days) of the correct categorization.

The mean difference is greater for the pancreatitis neural networks than it is for the pediatric trauma neural networks. This is a reasonable result given the fact that the pediatric trauma patient test population has an average LOS of 4.37 days with a standard deviation (r) of 6.7 days, while the pancreatitis test patient population has an average length of stay of 7.75 days with a r of 8.9 days. Furthermore, the patient demographics for each neural network model population are significantly different: trauma versus medical etiology, mean age of 8 years versus mean age of 43.5 years, and the presence of other com pounding factors (medical history of older patients).

The fuzzy ARTMAP neural network produces the minimum difference, but has difficulty in recognizing patients that have a LOS of greater than 1 week. Almost 30% of the acute pancreatitis patients have a LOS of greater than 1 week. Again, a combination of two different types of neural networks appears to be warranted for the pancreatitis LOS domain. The fuzzy ARTMAP neural network should serve as the primary LOS predictor due to its minimal absolute average difference in days from the actual LOS, but should be supplemented with the single hidden layer backpropagation neural network to identify those patients that are likely to require extended care.

While the same two types of neural networks are recommended for the pancreatitis LOS prediction problem as for the pediatric trauma LOS prediction problem (Backpropagation and fuzzy ARTMAP), the reason for using each type of neural network has been reversed. The reason for the reversal of the neural network training methods performance on the two different problems may be due to the difference between medical etiology versus trauma and is the subject of future investigation. The finding that predicting the precise LOS versus categorizing the illness severity is consistent with the general findings from the pediatric trauma neural networks and again indicates the need for two distinct types of models.

LOS for 12% of the 100 pancreatitis patients and predicts within 2 days the LOS for 35% of the pancreatitis patients. When used in combination with the single hidden layer backpropagation neural network, the accuracy of the perfect LOS predictions increases from 12% to 17% of all patients.

## 5. Neural network evaluation of LOS heuristics

The results presented in the previous section lend strong support for validation of the research goal: that a patient’s LOS and severity of injury or illness may be accurately predicted by a neural network model using only information that is available within 10 min of a patient’s arrival at a hospital’s emergency room. Additionally, a very interesting corollary result is obtained from the acute pancreatitis neural network decision support models. In addition to removing input variables that contained noise or missing values, the 22-input value set also removed a RANSON score value. The RANSON score value is a heuristic method currently used by physicians to predict outcomes for patients with pancreatitis [15,16] including LOS. The neural networks utilizing the 22-input value vector consistently outperformed the 44-input value networks, indicating that the RANSON score may not be the best possible predictor of pancreatitis LOS.

Following the discovery that a neural network, which did not use the current medical heuristic value for estimating a pancreatitis patient’s LOS, outperformed neural network models that did incorporate the RANSON score heuristic, it was noted that the pediatric trauma neural network models also did not use the current medical values used for estimating injury severity, either the Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) or the Pediatric Trauma Score (PTS). The GCS and PTS were intentionally removed due to the knowledge acquisition performed with medical experts. The domain experts indicated that intubation, to maintain a viable airway for the pediatric patient, is an important domain variable. However, the presence of an endotracheal tube (intubation is the insertion of this tube), necessarily eliminates a GCS or PTS value from the patient’s record as a verbal response cannot be evaluated. Again, the fact that the neural network model had a MAD of less than 2 days (for PICU, or less than 3 days for total LOS) for the LOS of all 3881 pediatric trauma patients indicates that a better mechanism for patient evaluation is now available.

A further examination of the effect of a RANSON score value on the nonparametric nonlinear models produced by the neural networks is conducted by adding the RANSON score into the 22-input variable set and by removing just the RANSON score value from the 44-input variable set. Each new neural network undergoes the same rigorous testing as the original models, including use of both single and double hidden layer models with numerous different hidden node architectures.

First, the best of the neural network models produced by the addition of the RANSON score to the original 22-input variable set, increased the MAD marginally and decreased the illness severity specificity by 5% to 67% overall. Finally, the best of the neural network models produced by deletion of the RANSON score value from the 44-input variable set increased the illness severity specificity from 71% to 73%. Removal of the abbreviated RANSON score value from the 44 and 23 variable neural network models improves the illness severity categorization by 2% and 5%, respectively. Furthermore, the inclusion of the abbreviated RANSON score necessarily delays the application of the neural networks categorization from 10 min to several hours, as this is the time required to calculate an abbreviated RANSON score (the full RANSON score, not used in this research, requires 48 h of time to calculate). Future research will investigate the causal prediction effects of the abbreviated RANSON, full RANSON, and Apache II scores, all of which are used to determine pancreatitis recovery. The current results indicate that the RANSON score does not add any significant knowledge to the neural networks’ models for predicting LOS and illness severity. The current popular medical heuristic method of using the RAN-SON score for evaluating pancreatitis patient outcome can be replaced by a neural network decision support tool that provides more accurate illness severity assessment in a much shorter period of time.

## 6. Summary

The research reported in this article has shown that neural network systems are capable of providing significant planning and patient care information with a relative paucity of diagnostic knowledge. All knowledge used as input for the neural networks implemented and tested in the presented research is available within the first 10 min of a patient’s arrival at the hospital for the pediatric trauma patients and within the first 2 h after arrival for the acute pancreatitis patients (the optimal 22-input neural network model for pancreatitis did use only data available in the first 10 min also). For pediatric trauma patients who had an average LOS of 4.37 days (ranging from 0 to 146 days), a backpropagation neural network predicted within 1 day the LOS for 52% of the patients. For the patients suffering from acute pancreatitis who had an average LOS of almost 8 days (ranging from 1 to 58 days), a fuzzy ARTMAP neural network was able to predict within 1 day the LOS for over 35% of the patients.

6.1. Advantages provided by the neural network models

Knowledge concerning the acuity of care required by patients (indicated by the trauma or illness severity categorization produced by the neural network) can assist physicians and hospital administrators in determining the ability of the hospital to meet a new patient’s care needs. As an example, in January 1998 severe ice storms hit the northeast United States and southeastern Canada [24]. Montreal, Canada suffered severe power outages and subsequent trauma patients produced by the hazards of power outages (Ref. [24] reports that 46 people died as a result of the storm). During this time of power grid failure, Montreal’s hospitals needed to direct all but emergency cases to be seen at other locations. The described neural network decision support system, would have enabled physicians to diagnose the severity of injuries with a relative paucity of information, most of which was available prior to arrival at the hospital.

During times of bed shortages on specific floors or units of a hospital, new patient care is impacted by the resource shortages. Those patients that are categorized as noncritical, may be able to be rerouted and consequently receive care in a more timely manner prior to entering the hospital’s ER.

Other than planning for bed resource utilization, the accurate categorization of patients into trauma and illness severity categories may also serve to identify more severe medical problems for a patient than those initially identified upon entry into an ER. A physician or ER nurse could use the severity categorization produced by the neural network model to indicate further evaluation and testing of a particular patient. Future research is required to monitor the early evaluation of patients via the neural network models against existing medical practices and more comprehensive testing, to determine if the neural network models are capable of identifying unexpected medical conditions that may worsen a patient’s recovery time.

## 6.2. Insights into medical modeling of ER patients

Another finding of this research is that a twonetwork system combination of a single hidden layer backpropagation neural network and a fuzzy ART-MAP neural network system performs better than just a single neural network system for predicting both a patient’s injury severity and LOS. The combined neural network model increased the perfect LOS predictions for acute pancreatitis patients by 5%. Although the findings indicate that this type of increase in predictive power may be achieved by the pediatric trauma neural network models, future research is still required to investigate methods for combining the two neural network architectures into a single model. The finding that different types of neural network models have better performance for the problems of LOS prediction and trauma/illness severity categorization indicates that although correlated, the medical problems of predicting LOS and injury/illness severity require distinct types of models. In the combined neural network system, a primary neural network is used to indicate the LOS for patients, while the secondary neural network serves as a signal to identify patients with more severe injuries or illnesses. This combination of neural network systems forms a decision support tool that effectively enables physicians and hospital administrators to determine the requirement and allocation of critical hospital resources.

The fact that the type of neural network which operates best on predicting injury severity or LOS is switched between the pediatric trauma and acute pancreatitis domains reveals an underlying difference in the medical states of these two types of patients. A preliminary suggestion has been made that the type of problem (i.e., more algorithmic or pattern-oriented) may affect the performance of different types of neural network learning techniques. Future research is needed to determine why specific (and different) types of neural networks operate better on different aspects of the LOS/injury severity problem.

The research results presented for the pediatric trauma domain are generated using in-sample training data from numerous hospitals subscribing to the National Pediatric Trauma Registry. It may be possible to further increase the accuracy and sensitivity of the implemented neural networks by limiting the training samples to a more localized region with similar patient demographics. Future research will investigate the differences caused by local versus regional versus national data sources for modeling medical domains with neural networks. The inverse problem exists for the acute pancreatitis domain, which is evaluated using only local patient knowledge.

Those patients that are identified as requiring an extended LOS will continue to accumulate knowledge concerning their condition throughout their stay. While the described neural network methodology provides an initial and immediate assessment of the patient’s LOS and injury/illness severity, it should be possible to refine the LOS estimates as time progresses. Additional diagnostic information that is acquired may be used by a series of neural networks, each of which uses knowledge currently available and refines the predictions of previous neural networks. Future research will investigate the use of staged neural network models to constantly revise the LOS prognosis for patients.

Neural networks have been shown to provide an accurate and reliable means for estimating the LOS for patients suffering different emergency problems (trauma or acute pancreatitis). Usage of neural network systems by hospitals to predict patient LOS enables better resource allocation and resource planning in a more timely manner.
