# New tools for the determination of e-commerce inhibitors

Roger Debreceny\*, Martin Putterill, Lai-Lai Tung, A. Lee Gilbert

Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University, Nanyang Avenue, Singapore 639798, Singapore

## Abstract

Decisions on electronic commerce (EC) participation are of importance not only to the firms involved but also increasingly for national economic progress. Such decisions involve simultaneous analysis of the intra- and inter-organizational systems, adoption of new business models and often the wholesale restructuring of existing business models. Firms’ ability to progress in the EC domain is dependent in part on the inhibitors that restrict their forward movement. The complexity of these relationships combined with the relative immaturity of EC warrant careful selection of analytical tools for the analysis of EC participation and investment decisions. This research into EC inhibitor appraisal is conducted in Singapore, an appropriate environment for this purpose being both an island state and an active IT participant on the international stage. An integrated suite of research techniques is presented, combining a survey, focus group discussions with group decision support systems, and computerassisted qualitative analysis of the Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS)-facilitated focus groups (Computer-Assisted Qualitative Data Analysis, CAQDAS). The paper evaluates the results and methodology within a framework of grounded theory, and in the process of building a foundation for further research, presents a rich and far-reaching set of conjectures about the EC inhibitor process. <sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Electronic commerce; Barriers; Singapore; Group decision support systems; Grounded theory; Focus groups; Qualitative research

## 1. Introduction

In this early stage of electronic commerce (EC) innovation, it is important to understand the nature of EC inhibitors, i.e. the forces that moderate adoption of electronic commerce (EC) by firms. Inhibitors have the potential to impact strategy and public policy of enterprises and nations in an increasingly dynamic networked international economy. EC investment has features in common with other business decisions, for example, alignment of information technology (IT) and firm strategy [47,48]. Yet even cursory observation suggests that for business managers in particular, EC-related decisions are more challenging than earlier generations of IT investments. Complexity in the decision-making environment has escalated because the new climate frequently requires decisions that simultaneously focus on (1) integration of internal systems; (2) development of inter-organizational systems; (3) adoption of new IT-enabled business models and (4) wholesale restructuring of existing business models.

In many countries, the development of electronic commerce is seen as important to national interest, a development to be fostered by explicit EC policies. Governments are progressively viewing adoption of electronic commerce as an important determinant of future national economic growth. Similarly, regional and global groupings such as APEC, ASEAN, OECD,

UNCTAD and WTO, clearly believe EC to be a matter for close consideration [2,3,61,82].

These governmental and non-governmental instrumentalities can be seen playing a fostering role at the national level, and collaborating to maintain the Internet as a global, public network. Government, broadly defined, has in most countries assumed a significant role by creating appropriate legal and fiscal frameworks that aim to reduce commercial uncertainty in global EC in issues such as security, skill development and cross-border EC-fostered trade in both physical and virtual goods. The EC environment is categorized by rapid change in intra- and inter-organizational relationships; significantly lowered transaction costs, and interplay between the firm and national and international policy settings to a degree not previously seen in earlier iterations of IT innovation.

In this fluid initial stage, the study of a subtle and complex issue such as inhibitors to the adoption of EC at the level of the firm requires considerable care and the use of appropriate research tools. This paper reports on a project that tackled the problem of EC research method by using an integrated, multi-stage and multi-instrument investigation of inhibitors to the adoption of electronic commerce. The research approach described below combined a survey, focus group discussions with group decision support systems, and computer-assisted qualitative analysis of the GDSS-facilitated focus groups (CAQDAS). We refer to this research approach as the Inhibitor Determination Methodology (IDM). Within a framework of grounded theory [26,27,69], this IDM approach generated a rich and far-reaching set of conjectures about the EC inhibitor process.

The study was carried out in Singapore, an appropriate research environment being a small country with vigorous services, manufacturing and entrepot sectors, and an active IT participant on the international stage. Over the last decade and a half, the Singapore government has actively pursued information technology as a promoter and catalyst of economic growth [12,52,81]. The government has instigated a range of IT programs including significant skills development at all levels; an active change agenda to create an EC-friendly legal environment, and removal of barriers to entry in a variety of ECrelated markets, most notably in telecommunications. A national EC plan was promulgated in 1998.

In the final analysis, it is firms, and not governments, that make decisions to adopt electronic commerce. While a proactive government can foster change, crucially it is decision-makers in firms that make the difficult EC investment and business process engineering decisions. Accordingly, notwithstanding the role of government described above, in designing the research approach, a specifically firm-level methodology has been chosen, one that has the capacity to deeply engage in the search for explanations of a complex process.

The main focus of this paper is on the selection, use and appraisal of EC research tools suited to firmlevel inquiry. Particular goals are to (1) analyse alternative research techniques to capture and analyse inhibitors to the adoption of EC, (2) describe a suite of research techniques appropriate for mapping the EC decision-space and inhibitors to the adoption of EC at the level of the firm, and (3) outlines the application of this methodology to Singapore.

The paper proceeds as follows: Section 2 examines research techniques relevant to this inquiry, and rationale for choice of methods. The context in which the study was conducted, the stages in the research program and the results of each of the stages is described in Section 3. The appraisal and validation of this method of EC inhibitor investigation is presented in Section 4. The final section draws the threads of this research together and sets an agenda for future work.

## 2. New challenges, new tools, new insights

At this ‘initiation’ stage [66] of a widening diffusion of EC, a high level of uncertainty about critical elements seriously hampers investment decision-taking. In essence, outcomes are, or are perceived to be, unpredictable. There are simply no clear markers for firms to follow as they weigh up strategic choices about participation in electronic commerce. In some settings, the problem is compounded by a lack of clarity on government policy and infrastructure development, and the special considerations that pertain to investments in an integrating, pervasive medium such as EC. If those were not enough, firm-level decisionmakers are also required to grapple with all the considerations of global business and its demanding emphasis on high financial performance.

The elements and processes that constitute ‘uncertainty’ in a firm-level EC investment context can be labeled conveniently as ‘inhibitors’. These are ‘‘real or imagined impediments that restricts or prevents adoption of EC’’ [10]. Although EC innovation raises many critical research issues, this paper is specifically concerned with the search for a clear understanding and ranking of the components of this ‘inhibitor’ term in current EC investment planning and appraisal. This section pursues a strong methodological orientation, being concerned with the choice of research methods to explore and explain inhibitors within an EC decision setting. It (a) identifies reasons why researchers are having to confront the distinctive characteristics of the ‘new EC landscape’, (b) outlines choice of research epistemology, (c) describes the chosen research methodology suite, the Inhibitor Determination Methodology (IDM), and (d) sets out the distinctive features of the research.

## 2.1. New EC landscape

An important research question to consider is how firms will react to the many challenges wrought by EC. The literature on adoption of IT has a number of references to the need in the past to identify barriers or inhibitors that prevent corporations from moving forward in adopting IT for value generation [31,41,42,48]. As previously noted, EC not only presents a new landscape in IT investment but identification of inhibitors to EC is more difficult and subtle than for other forms of IT. Identifying inhibitors to the adoption of EC is becoming a matter for concern for international public policy organizations [2,3,61,82].

Flowing from this are several methodological considerations including identification of an appropriate unit of analysis for the identification of inhibitors, and the selection of tools to support an inquiry into the direction of firm-level EC decision-making. In addition to establishing a foundation for further research and model construction, the additional interest is in finding an answer to the seeming contradiction, at least in the case of Singapore, between national policy and firm-level outcomes.

As the discussion above has intimated, the approach in this paper rests on three legs. Firstly, we will argue that the unit of analysis should be undertaken principally, though by no means exclusively, at the level of the firm. Secondly, there should be explicit recognition of external factors, such as the role of national and international policy making or the impact of alliances upon business models. This focus is very unlike previous theories of IT adoption, even those explaining adoption of inter-organizational IT systems such as EDI [4,37,63]. Thirdly, to avoid over-simplification of the complex issues present in this immature EC environment, traditional research methods such as large-scale surveys will be avoided in favor of a tailored approach. In essence, the goal of this research is to understand the responses and motivations of firmlevel decision-makers to EC inhibitors.

## 2.2. Choice of research epistemology

The question then is which research methodology, or set of methodologies, is appropriate for explaining the adoption of EC? For more than a decade there has been an ongoing debate on appropriate epistemology for the information systems discipline [24,25,38,44,77]. The principal research paradigm is positivist. While increasing in frequency, interpretive research is only employed in a relatively few studies. Case studies, broadly defined, are used in less than a third (28%) of studies published in the major IS journals that employ data [20]. Case studies have made an important contribution to knowledge and theory building in a variety of areas including electronic meeting systems [29]; decision support systems [71]; the design of interorganizational systems [8] and systems implementation [78]. While some see the choice of research method as solely an epistemological issue, others see the choice of method as a solely technical matter (‘‘which technique will be fit the theoretical domain being researched and the nature of the data collection environment.’’) [6].

As an alternative to a positivist approach, the use of qualitative research may be appropriate in EC research. Examples include a variety of techniques such as the long interview [53], focus groups [43,54,68] or case studies [19,30,83]. The strength of the qualitative approach lies in a capacity to explore human subject motivation and actions within a research study frame of reference, thereby exposing the richness of the data. Qualitative research is designed to allow the researcher to make forward movement in the development of theory. Conversely, quantitative research is designed to confirm pre-existing theories [6]

The distinction between quantitative and qualitative research is, as Fitzgerald and Howcroft [22] remind us, somewhat vacuous. There is a role for both quantitative or ‘‘hard’’ positivist and qualitative ‘‘soft’’ interpretive research. Positivist and quantitative research is appropriate when there are strong theoretical underpinnings and when the nature of theoretical constructs can be clearly and unambiguously stated, a priori [6].

The meeting of quantitative and qualitative research can be within a single research study, as argued by Gable [24] who calls for multi-method research approaches, or by following a pluralist strategy as defined by Fitzgerald and Howcroft [22]. Early examples of such multi-method study were from Mantei and Teorey [49] and Kaplan and Duchon [39]. Equally, these research domains can be combined within a research stream. The studies of EDI adoption by Iacovou et al. [37] initially employed a small number of structured interviews to flesh out a theoretical framework. Subsequently a large-scale study was employed to test the theoretical framework described in the earlier stage [9].

## 2.3. Inhibitor Determination Methodology (IDM)

The approach taken in this study has features in common with the Benbasat study described in the previous sub-section [9,37], but with notable differences. The development of theory in the essentially more complex and unstable environment of EC requires sustained analysis involving a wide range of actors. The IDM multi-stage and multi-method research strategy followed in this study appears in Fig. 1. While a variety of other qualitative research techniques have been employed in IS research [51,57], including ‘‘Revealed Causal Mapping’’ [58], ‘‘repertory grids’’ [36] and content analysis [1,62], the whole research approach followed here, was informed by Grounded Theory [5,26,69]. This is consistent with our belief that Grounded Theory most accurately describes the adjustment process that ensued as at successive stages of the research we came to a better understanding of the implications of the data for theory construction.

## 2.4. Distinctive features of the research

There are several distinctive features of the research. As can be seen from Fig. 1, the research method was multi-stage and multi-method. First, we made extensive use of focus groups to search for and inspect EC inhibitors. Second, the most intensive analysis of the issues was conducted within GDSSfacilitated focus groups. Third, we employed Computer-Assisted Qualitative Data Analysis (CAQDAS) to understand the directions taken within the computer-facilitated focus groups and to draw out theoretical conclusions. We describe each of these in turn.

Focus groups were used to drill down into the catalog of EC inhibitors so as to tease out some of the factors that were important inhibitors to the adoption of EC that might not be obvious from straightforward observation of the catalog (see Phases 1A and 1B in Fig. 1). As noted above, there has been increasing interest in the use of qualitative research techniques in information systems research. The focus group technique is well established in communications studies, sociology and marketing but not as well established in knowledge domains such as information systems or strategy. The technique has been used in only a handful of studies within the information systems domain, e.g. Refs. [45,49,67]. Yet, the focus group provides the ability for rapid and interactive concentration on key issues at play in the research domain [54,56,68]. The focus group produces ‘‘a very rich body of data expressed in the respondents’ own words and context. There is a minimum of artificiality of response, unlike survey questionnaires that ask for [pre-defined] responses associated with their answers. Thus, responses have a certain ecological validity not found in traditional survey research’’ [10,68].

A core element of the focus group technique is the level of active interaction between participants [43]. Ideas can be rapidly explored, addressed and fleshed out. The high contextual validity, richness of data and flexibility would seem to make the focus group particularly appropriate in the exploration of EC inhibitors.

Another important element in this study was the use of Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) supported focus groups (see Phase 2A in Fig. 1). These focus groups undertook sustained and in-depth analysis of the inhibitors identified in the earlier stages of the research. The members of the GDSS-enhanced focus groups used both ranking and brainstorming tools. Meaning was extracted directly from the rankings and indirectly via detailed analysis from the brainstorming interactions, a matter to which we will return later in this section.

GDSS was used to assist further cataloguing of the key inhibitors and to gain a better understand of the interrelationships between the inhibitors, thereby contributing to theory building. This application is an example of the widening and maturing role of GDSS particularly as an inter-linking research device. In must be noted that as in several other IS knowledge domains, GDSS studies started with exploratory studies and matured from this early ‘‘design science’’ [50] to experimental research.

Much of the GDSS research has been in ‘face-toface same place/same time’ settings. In the 1980s, many studies were conducted in such environments to look at the potential of adding IT to improve the productivity of groups [16]. In the earlier part of this phase, researchers focused on the value-added of the technology in helping to improve group processes and outcomes, as well as to compare that with group processes without the application of technology. The early laboratory experiments were conducted mainly in university settings, which arguably had low levels of external validity. Factors studied fell under the main categories of independent, process and outcome variables [23] along with intervening variables, which are often specific to the experimental methods employed. Results of comparing environments with and without GDSS technologies have been mixed [16]. Most of these studies focused on the analysis of ‘‘derived’’ outcomes (dependent variables) such as perceptions, with examples including satisfaction with processes and outcomes. Examples of performance measure derived outcomes are unique ideas, decision time and assessed performance. Independent variables commonly found in one or more of categories, notably technology, included process structure, group, task or context [23].

For the bulk of these studies, the content of the comments made by participants was not analyzed. GDSS sessions made little or no contribution to developing knowledge on the underlying theoretical constructs discussed in the session. Many measures did not rely on what was keyed-in by participants. Rather, the level of performance was assessed on taskdescriptive properties such as time taken for mission completion; quality of the answers generated (typically by participant self-grading), or some kind of a content analysis based on a preset category or categories. Past GDSS research has generally made no attempt to extract comments for finer-grained interpretation. Nor have there been attempts to understand what is being said in sessions beyond preset categorization. Gopal and Prasad [28] point out that ‘‘GDSS use may result in reactions from its users that are difficult to capture using conventional methodological assumptions (positivist approach), thereby helping explain why past results have not been consistent.’’ The positivist realism approach ‘‘sets a premium on pre-specified theoretical models, experimental research methods, and the identification of a parsimonious set of variables that encapsulates the (generalizable) essence of the GDSS experience’’ [28].

In the latter part of the 1980s and in the early 1990s, there was a call to move GDSS research from the confines of the laboratory to research that is conducted in the field as well as for a closer examination of process issues in GDSS studies. In moving GDSS studies into the field, Fjermestad and Hiltz [23] highlighted case studies, field studies and action research as three main research methods used. Studies of GDSS used in organizations, such as those conducted by Nunamaker et al. [60] and Dennis [13] looked at variables such as satisfaction and anonymity. Other studies covered productivity issues such as efficiency and performance, and looked at perceptions such as satisfaction with the process. Results of the studies in the field were generally positive, with a 91.5% success rate [23]. They were differentiated from the experimental research by the fact that ‘‘many field studies involved large groups of managers and professionals performing complex tasks over several days using GSS that included an active process facilitator’’ [16].

Contrasting with this, laboratory studies usually involve smaller groups, many of which involve student participants with no prior history of team interaction, performing task limited in complexity over one session. While these studies looked at GSS use in the field, again, few studies looked at the interpretation of what is said in the GSS sessions themselves. It is ironic that while the purpose of GSS use is often to be able to bring back ‘‘content’’ to the organization out of a pool of comments, either in the form of a series of ‘‘good’’ ideas, feedback on a conflicting topic or some indication of consensus, few studies have reported on the process of consolidation of these comments. Even fewer studies have discussed ways to process or create themes out of the whole range of comments. In short, no attempt is made to consolidate this material into a useful and manageable form that can be digested by people both within and outside of the group (see Ref. [35] for an example of an exception).

Hilmer and Dennis [35] argued that GDSS group processes often do not promote individual information processing and integration of information with respect to new information generated during the session. Results of their experimental study confirmed the positive effects of framing the information through categorizing and filtering to raise attention to important information and help reduce information overload for information process and integration. Moreover, they have highlighted the important issue that such framing can be based on the decision criteria, or it can allow group members to form their own categories. They note that ‘‘[a]dditional research is needed to investigate other means of framing information to group members and their effects on attention, integration, and decision quality’’. We argue that appropriate framing of such information is not only important during the group process itself, but also after the group process in order to understand and communicate what is going on during the session to others. There is a need for studies looking at framing [35] or forming themes from the content of discussion in order to interpret them and to assist in theory building. These studies should be constructed within an interpretive paradigm, and will provide an alternative fruitful thread of research as well as triangulation of research [74].

The call for focus on process yielded studies that focused on the ‘‘process’’ box between the input and output box in an input – process– output model. Notably, several studies have looked at processes such as group information use and exchange [14,15,17,34] and group conflict processes [64,75,76]. While attention is on investigation of a variety of group processes, the epistemological approach remains mainly positivist and focused on outcomes.

According to Trauth and Jessup [74], the recent IS literature gives evidence of an interpretive movement in IS research in general (the latest phase). Methods such as grounded theory, ethnography and hermeneutics are approaches in the interpretive paradigm. For instance, an alternative epistemological lens offered by Gopal and Prasad [28] is to use qualitative research methods and a symbolic ‘interactionist’ basis to look at GDSS processes. An interpretative stance is taken to looking at each process as opposed to a positivist approach. The study reported in this paper also follows an interpretative stance. Trauth and Jessup [74] argued that the deeper probing into the subtleties of context, and the fact that other studies had used these approaches successfully, makes the interpretive approach a plausible alternative research approach for understanding GSS discourse.

A special feature of this study was the use of Computer-Assisted Qualitative Research Analysis (CAQDAS) to extract meaning from the GDSS-focus group dialog [21]. The CAQDAS software tool used was NVivo [65]. The protocols generated in the GDSS sessions were moved directly to NVivo, thereby eliminating most of the pitfalls described by Easton et al. [18]. A process of closed coding then took place. The protocols were coded against two dimensions— one dimension based upon the issues that were brainstormed in the GDSS sessions, and the other against a mapping of the appropriate theory. Details including results of the application of the EC inhibitor research approach in Singapore are set out in the next section.

## 3. Application of the Inhibitor Determination Methodology (IDM)

In the discussion that follows, a description is given of the inquiry pursued into EC inhibitors in Singapore. We describe and analyze the context of the study, the flow of the research and set out the results from each phase of the research. Arguably innovative, the application of the Inhibitor Determination Methodology (IDM) allows us to search well below the surface at the level of the firm, for EC inhibitors and related processes.

## 3.1. Context

The research was conducted in Singapore. The Singapore government has, over the last two decades, seen information technology as an important element in national economic development [81]. Over this period there has been significant investment in IT skills building and infrastructure development. National programs have been constructed in areas such as trade facilitation [59], libraries [7,33] and K12 education [80]. Singapore has also been an early and sustained adopter of the Internet [73] and the development of widely dispersed broadband services under the ‘‘Singapore One’’ brand name is well known internationally (e.g. Ref. [72]). As a consequence of these efforts, Singapore has been identified as one of a small number of countries with IT achievements well in excess of size of natural resources [11].

A special feature of this research location is the apparent ambivalence of firms in Singapore towards EC involvement. Notwithstanding the efforts of the Singapore Government to create an environment conducive to the pervasive use of EC, policy makers and official clearly believe that the momentum towards wider commercial usage of EC has not been as rapid as deemed necessary for long-term economic advantage [46]. Singapore, then, provides a singularly appropriate setting in which to explore the adoption of EC by firms and, to invert the image, of the inhibitors that firms perceive as blocking the way towards adopting EC.

## 3.2. Flow of the research

Read from left to right, Table 1 outlines the order in which the research was undertaken. Phase 1A: a poll of selected IT opinion leaders resulting in ranked potential inhibitors. Phase 1B: focus groups of EC practitioners and decision-makers ranked and analyzed the key inhibitors identified in Phase 1A. Phase 2A: GDSS-facilitated groups of EC practitioners and decision-makers ranked the factors associated with each prime inhibitor identified in Phase 1. Phase 2B: the same GDSS groups commented through synchronous topic discussion on the highly ranked factors identified in Phase 2A. The selection of the number and background of participants in each stage of the research aimed to ensure that further analysis to support theoretical conclusions would be possible. The research sequence set out in Table 1 is further elaborated below.

The aim of this process was to select a core of important inhibitors to further explore in the succeeding phase. In Phase 1B, four focus groups comprised of managers in firms that have or were contemplating EC activity, and a smaller number of advisers from ITrelated firms and public sector policy makers, analyzed the refined catalog of inhibitors created in the previous phase. Membership of the four focus groups was determined primarily by core EC interests, including business-to-consumer (two groups), business-to-business (one group) and policy formation (one group). The focus groups were invited to dig below the surface of the rather bald list of inhibitors generated previously. The consideration given each issue had a strong temporal foundation. The focus group participants were asked to identify when the issue under consideration would no longer be an inhibitor. Discussion in the focus groups concentrated on those issues where there was significant dispersion in views and/or where most participants viewed the inhibitor being resolved in the medium term. If an issue was to be resolved quickly or conversely in the long term, the issue was less worthy of consideration than one that could be resolved in a time period that left room for firm-level decisions or policy-making.

## 3.4. Identification of Pilot Classification Elements

From the rankings given to the inhibitors by the opinion leaders in Phase 1A and from the attention given to the inhibitors by the focus group participants in Phase 1B, a set of ‘‘substantive issues’’ was defined, shown as the five highest-ranked inhibitors in Table 2. Extensive semi-verbatim field notes were taken during the focus group sessions. Transcription of these notes generated a more detailed set of concepts that provided flesh on the skeleton of the substantive issues. These more detailed characteristics of the substantive issues are referred to as ‘‘Pilot Classification Elements’’ (PCEs). There were a total of 43 PCEs spread across the substantive issues (see Ref. [10] for detailed elaboration of the PCEs). After analysis of the transcript, the inhibitors ‘‘erosion of current advantages’’ and ‘‘investment in current model’’ were found to be analogous and combined. This combined inhibitor called ‘‘erosion of current advantages’’, was substituted for the inhibitor ‘‘existing technological inhibitors.’’ There was a good deal of dispersion in views on the combined inhibitor and it seemed clear that this combined inhibitor was likely to be more important in a future-oriented analysis than would the issue of a technology-based inhibitor.

3.5. GDSS rankings of Pilot Classification Elements (PCEs)

The catalog of the substantive issues and Pilot Classification Elements (PCEs) derived in Phase 1 was moved forward for more detailed analysis in Phase 2. This stage in the research drew upon senior ECommerce practitioners and decision-makers from the public and private sectors in a GDSS-facilitated group interview [70]. In Phase 2A, participants ranked the PCE for each of the inhibitors, using the GDSS ranking tool. Details of the method, participants and output from this sub-phase are summarized in column (3) of Table 1. PCEs were analyzed for overall ranking and divergence of support. ## 3.6. Grounded theory analysis

In Phase 2B, the topic commenter tool was used in a Delphi-like brainstorming session, to closely examine the top ranked PCEs for each of the substantive issues identified in Phase 2A. The protocols arising from Phase 2B were analyzed using the NVivo Computer Assisted Qualitative Data Analysis Software (CAQDAS) [65]. Details of the method, participants and output from this sub-phase are summarized in column (4) of Table 1.

A total of 448 insight statements were generated in the three GDSS-facilitated sessions. These insight statements provided the foundation for theory building. Analysis of data within a grounded theory framework is designed to inform, reform and to extend existing theories. Coding is a very important component in the process of conducting such research [21,69]. In order to assist with the coding of the protocols arising from the GDSS brainstorming sessions, the researchers constructed a Theoretical Coding Element Framework (TCEF). The TCEF used as a starting point but then significantly extends the Kraemer – Dedrick Model (KDM). The complete TCEF is shown in Fig. 3.

The CAQDAS software used in this study, NVivo [65], provides the opportunity to code passages across multiple dimensions and to explore a variety of interrelationships within the data set. At any time, the researcher can retrieve the passages within the protocols that have met the given search criteria. NVivo is further characterized by the capacity to output relationship matrices in a form compatible to that recommended by Miles and Huberman [55]. As Miles and Huberman point out, such a relationship matrix invites reflection and further analysis. Table 3 shows the TCEF/PCEs relationship matrix. The number of coded document passages totals 751. ## 3.6.1. MindState

The revealed attitude of actors towards EC is an important element in digging below the surface and understanding the EC process [79]. Recall that an important part of the coding process was to assess the MindState of each protocol, where it was clear that an attitude was being expressed. The MindState coding scale ranged from ‘‘Awe/LostMinus’’ to ‘‘Challenge/ ChallengePlus’’. A response in the former category implies that the respondent is not only simply in awe but is overwhelmed by the scale of the EC challenge. A response in the ‘‘ChallengePlus’’ category indicates that for a particular issue a respondent is motivated to both seek an EC solution and move forward to implementation. Viewing Table 6, from the TCEF perspective, the distribution of assigned responses shows approximately 40% to be ‘‘ChallengeMinus’’ and 41% to be ‘‘ChallengeNeutral.’’ The overwhelming preponderance of ChallengeMinus and ChallengeNeutral underscores the tentative attitudes towards to EC on the part of the panelists.

On the question of cross-border order facilitation, there were an approximately equal number of comments that were coded as ChallengeMinus and ChallengeNeutral, with none being coded as ChallengePositive. Participants raised three primary areas of concern. First, the issue was raised of an appropriate inter-governmental framework for the cross-border transfer of goods and services. Participants were generally quite negative about the current status of this framework, pointing directly to increased costs and lowered potential for EC in the region as a result of inadequate bilateral and multilateral arrangements. For example, one participant noted that ‘‘[t]he blurred borders online require a re-examination of rules that govern cross-border business—current rules provide either too many loopholes or are too vague and open to misinterpretation.’’ A second matter closely related to the previous issue is the need to standardize terms and conditions of EC trading in the region. Thirdly, there was a feeling that the combination of logistics, finance, taxation and costs were disincentives to the penetration of EC.

## 4. Effectiveness of method and approach

Preceding sections of this paper have provided detailed information and results of the search in Singapore for insights into the nature of EC inhibitors. This study of inhibitors, in part a study of EC itself, proved to be composed of more delicate relationships than had been expected. Examples include the relationships between skills development, formal training and education, and choice of business model and the ability of the firm to migrate from one technology to another. Aspects of public policy, such as security and infrastructure, were found to be much less important to firmlevel decision-makers than the literature might have predicted.

From this background to the study, there arises the logical evaluation requirement applicable to any research method at the time of introduction, to establish the extent to which it is ‘fit for purpose’. A commonsense viewpoint is that this appraisal has two components: (a) an assessment of the method as used, and (b) a ‘test of time’ validation proposal to cover qualities of the method not assessable after a single iteration.

In the case of IDM, one aspect of the test of ‘fitness’ is the extent to which its use has been able to address the immediate purpose of the research. These relate to the identification of inhibitors and related EC processes and to government policy concerns about rate of EC adoption. The evidence presented suggests that IDM as applied in the Singapore setting, has been effective in meeting these primary aims. What firms currently regard as inhibitors as well as decision-maker attitudes to EC-related matters is clear, while the research has been able to begin the construction of tentative causal process relationships. The IDM has a capacity to provide a foundation to address several pressing intermediate research objectives. These encompass the tasks of constructing an EC datum point in Asia, completing an EC domain ‘map’ with principal causal linkages, and keeping track of dynamic changes in the ranking of inhibitors including responses to government policy settings.

A second aspect of the test of ‘fitness’ of IDM is the extent to which it responds to the research setting. In EC, the setting is global involving a wide constituency of firms, government, institutions and individuals in a complex changing mixture of traditional, electronic, and hybrid business models. By undertaking the study in Singapore, we were able to test IDM in a setting that combines all the global EC factors with an Asian regional parameters. The integration and flexibility of the various phases of the IDM provide effective tailoring to this complex setting.

In summary, it has been shown that across a number of EC inhibitor purpose and setting dimensions IDM has effectively met substantive appraisal tests. That is not the full story, as there are ‘test of time’ components of this EC inhibitor research yet to be validated by further investigation and replication. The aim of early replication is to screen the component procedures to identify conceptual or methodological weak links not identified in the peer review process. Thereafter, with greater confidence in IDM as a reliable and repeatable method, the way opens for longitudinal studies of EC inhibitors and related processes in single settings, and through cross-cross country evaluations and case studies. With the motivation of establishing whether there is a common underlying set of EC causes, or several variants, researchers might be encouraged to build additional exploration and modeling tools onto IDM. The choice of these additional features would be dictated by ECrelated factors such as size, complexity, culture, policy or some other determinants of innovation. A case in point would be the use of a causal modeling approach such as LISREL [32,40]. This is an important tool to confirm theories about latent variables, and explore the implications of changing parameters, an essential requirement when dealing with a dynamic setting such as EC.

This study is subject to a number of limitations. Addressing a new research field highlighted the need for several new terms, and the consequent problem of taxonomy. The choices of terms and constructs need to be reviewed and if necessary reformulated as an essential step towards the broader goal of understanding the full ambit of EC macro- and micro-level processes. Further, as EC is a difficult and uncertain firm-level decision-making environment requiring an integrated research approach, it cannot be said with certainty that the size and composition of the various panels was apt.

This paper has described the introduction, application and evaluation of IDM, a method tailored to the task of identifying inhibitors to electronic commerce. It was argued that an approach other than a traditional positivist research posture was mandated given the complex nature of the environment at this early stage of EC development. Tests undertaken to evaluate the application of IDM were generally supportive but the scale of the so-called ‘test of time’ research tasks requires that the methodology be hardened by repeated applications and reformulation.
