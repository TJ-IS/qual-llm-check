# Technology push, legislation pull? E-government in the European Union

Gerhard Strejcek <sup>a,1</sup>, Michael Theil <sup>b,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup>University of Vienna, Department of Public and Administrative Law, Juridicum, Schottenbastei 10, A-1010 Vienna, Austria <sup>b</sup>University of Economics and Business Administration Vienna, Institute of Risk Management and Insurance, Augasse 2– 6, A-1090 Vienna, Austria

## Abstract

E-government is new on the European agenda. Member states have announced plans to a more open, accessible and transparent administration by using the latest in information technology. Yet, the current situation is far from that. Hardly coordinated projects and the notorious individualism of the member states probably describe the state of affairs best. The paper assesses the status quo of e-government in the European Union and explains the current problems by missing coordination in legislature. <sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: E-government; Public administration; Information and services; Legislation; European Union

## 1. Introduction

The European Summits at Lisbon (March 2000) and Feira (June 2000) put a European initiative for egovernment on the agenda. It consists of four main tasks: firstly, the development of Internet-based services to improve access to public information and services; secondly, the improvement of transparency of public administration by using the Internet; thirdly, full exploitation of information technology within public administration; fourthly, establishing e-procurement [3].

However, as often, there is a wide gap between the announcement of such an ambitious project and its realisation. Therefore, this paper sets out to contrast the plans for future e-government with the current state of affairs in the European Union. To this end, we will first assess the situation in each of the European Union member states, focusing on the organisational body entrusted with e-government projects and on the trigger events for this development. In fact, both differ considerably between the countries. We conjecture that these differences may explain differences in aims, procedures, and status quo of the individual states. In the next section, we discuss the current main issues that the member countries target with their national programmes.

Broadly speaking, any initiative that is intended to have effect in all countries of the European Union requires an appropriate legal background. Without this common ground, the projects are likely to lead to individual solutions that fall short of serving the envisaged ‘‘European perspective.’’ Drawing from the case of Austrian telecommunication law as an example for many, we describe the problems that arise from privatising formerly state-run organisations without jeopardising the citizens’ supply or healthy competition in the field. The last section describes the problems with—and without—a centralised decision and the impact on European Union issues.

## 2. The state of the art in the European Union

## 2.1. Overview

The report by Chatrie and Wraight [1] provides a good summary of the activities of governments in the European Union concerning e-government and related issues. This synopsis, however, suffers the major drawback that it misses the opportunity for comparative analysis.

A second problem lies in the fact that Chatrie and Wraight [1] rely on self-reports provided by the respective member states. Quite generally, such selfprojection is often suspected to stress strengths and merits and play down weaknesses.

Nevertheless, it is uncontested that this report is the most comprehensive in portraying all 15 European

Union member states in this matter. The issue of selfreporting is certainly problematic if one expects an account based on ‘‘objective’’ standards. On the other hand, the method employed by Chatrie and Wraight [1] gives the opportunity to gain insight in what the particular member countries they feel important and interesting.

In the following we will offer an overview to the current state of affairs first and discuss the details of what triggered development in the past and which issues seem most important in the nearest future subsequently. To serve this purpose, we will consider the report by Chatrie and Wraight [1] as well as additional material, in particular the related Action Plan [4] and the report prepared for the Stockholm council [3].

Table 1 provides a comprehensive summary of the main issues for all 15 member states (first column) of the European Union: The second column gives the respective years in which the individual states started governmental initiatives to promote what is referred to as the ‘‘Information Society.’’ The ISI index stated next makes clear what is generally understood by this popular term. It comprises a number of variables describing computer infrastructure (for instance: PCs installed per capita, per household, per student, and so forth), information infrastructure (for instance: cable subscribers per capita, cellular phone ownership per capita, and so forth), Internet infrastructure (primarily Internet usage at home, for business purposes, and in education), and social infrastructure (civil liberties, newspaper readership, press freedom, secondary and tertiary school enrolment). The Information Society is, thus, best viewed as a complex social formation having particular characteristics in terms of technical infrastructure, legal provisions, as well as political and economic development. In the remaining columns are described the administrative authority in charge of the implementation of the initiative, the event or situation that initially triggered this process, and the main fields of action envisaged for the nearest future.

## 2.2. Authorities

The responsibility for the initiatives to promote the Information Society generally lies with highest-ranking officials or members of the government. In most cases, the head of government himself, one or several ministers are the appropriate authority. Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, and the United Kingdom, by contrast, rely on committees particularly appointed in this assignment. Because of its federal system, the responsibilities are split between central and local authorities in Belgium.

This distribution of responsibilities reflects the importance this issue is awarded by the European Union: The European Commission has declared interchange of data between administrations and the provision of information to the citizens top priority [9].

## 2.3. Trigger events

In general, the dramatic developments in the telecommunication sector seem to have aroused awareness of the potential usefulness of these new technologies in particular as well as of the leading role of governments as customers and providers.

Depending on the level of development of the telecommunication sector, there were two basic approaches: In some member states (for instance, the Scandinavian countries, the Netherlands, Austria and the United Kingdom), the widespread availability of telecommunication services for private and business purposes made public use a logical and obvious supplement. A different pattern may be observed in those states that felt their telecommunications market or infrastructure having fallen behind the others. In this spirit, in countries like Greece, Ireland, Italy, Portugal, and Spain, the government decided to stimulate progress to catch up by creating a favourable legal environment (mostly regarding privatisation and governmental control) and stepping up markets through governmental procurement power.

Germany and, at least in part, France saw the problem of unemployment as the leading reason to engage in this initiative. The rationale of this position is that new technologies may create new and lasting high-quality jobs. The difference in focus is mainly attributed to domestic policy, where the problem of unemployment was of pivotal importance in recent election campaigns.

In Belgium, finally, the development seems to be driven mainly by local interest of the two main sections of the population that considered electronic communication as an instrument to express their positions more effectively.

## 2.4. Current main issues

## 2.4.1. Administration

Because of differences in current development, member countries naturally see different main areas for further development in the near future. One issue that is of top priority for most of the member states is to bring public administration to a further stage. One of the most ambitious projects in this respect, coined ‘‘Digital Denmark,’’ serves as a model for other countries [6]. As a consequence, providing information via electronic media is probably the most advanced service to date. The European Union itself is a good example in providing digital access to documents of her institutions, to ‘‘frequently asked questions’’ information, and to statistical data. Similar programmes have been launched in other countries, also outside the European Union, for instance in the United States [10].

A second yet more difficult issue is to put citizens into the position to fully communicate with public authorities, including that they can file applications, make payments, and more [4]. However, a number of problems are still to be solved, for instance electronic signatures and other security concerns. What seems feasible in the near future is that citizens and enterprises may keep track of their applications digitally [6]. Some states have implemented services like electronic land register and electronic commerce register. The customer front end of digital services is also being improved, while some countries, for instance, Spain, take the approach to reconsider administrative procedures completely, in order to streamline administration, instead of simply putting the present administrative tasks online.

The rising pressure on administrative costs both inside and outside the public sector is one of the main drivers and a major obstacle at the same time. On the one hand, the public requires administration to become more cost efficient; on the other hand, the transition to digitally run processes involves investments that in turn require additional capital [8]. In this matter, learning from a more developed yet much simpler field may prove fruitful: Concerning the reform of the hospital sector, Pettersen [12] shows that institutional reality changes only slowly. At the same time, there is often (too) rapid action on the political level, for instance, generating ideas, but politicians often hesitate when it comes to supply appropriate funding. Therefore, the vision of the European Council concerning ‘‘e-government,’’ ‘‘Information Society,’’ or whatever slogan is used, may be quite far ahead of being put into practice. This is mirrored by the current action plans, where often little is said about concrete steps to implement strategies.

## 2.4.2. Education

Education is another area of development for the majority of member states. This issue involves training of IT professionals on the one hand as well as computer-based education in schools and at universities on the other. Some countries also recognise the need for advice and support of their citizens in order to facilitate their contact with new technologies. Finland, a leading country concerning the Information Society, has termed this issue ‘‘prevention of alienation’’ [4]. Governments also strive to reduce regional disparities concerning education. Some countries initiate programs to assist the disabled to get on the Internet. To be successful in their new role within a more effective and more transparent administration, former government officials, now ‘‘public managers,’’ also need to develop new skills [13].

## 2.4.3. Infrastructure

Infrastructure is another high-priority subject, although for different reasons: For the higher-developed countries in the European Union, infrastructure generally means to bring electronic communication closer to the citizens, for instance, to promote home computers with Internet access or to provide public connections to electronic services. For another part of the member countries, the improvement of infrastructure often implies supply with communication network, privatisation in the field of telecommunication, and the like [4]. While privatisation and competition may work in densely populated areas, some countries, for instance, Finland and Sweden, see regional disparities growing and take countermeasures. Tax incentives for individuals are intended to spread Internet access over households.

## 2.4.4. Economy

While there is general agreement concerning the importance of these issues, some countries prefer to emphasise particular problems. Consistent with their policy related to unemployment, France and Germany take a bearing on the creation of new jobs in the telecommunication sector or by using this technology, for instance to promote teleworking. Many countries hope that additional training requirements will have an impact on employment in this sector. In addition, the Internet is heavily used for the purposes of job placement programs.

The particular situation in Belgium, where the initiative is supported on a regional and community level on the first place, makes clear that Information Society projects are attractive, not least because they make considerable funds available.

Electronic business, as a broad term for a variety of commercial uses of telecommunication, is found on the agenda of Austria, Belgium, and the United Kingdom. Apart from encouraging enterprises to start related activities, the creation of additional workplaces is obviously hoped for as a side effect [4].

To avoid negative effects of this kind of business, Germany also gave priority to consumer protection. In view of major security concerns concerning Internet services, Sweden put this issue on his agenda [4]. There seems to be good reason for prudence: In a Delphi study, Coutorie [5] finds that digital communication is expected to be the top target for future criminal acts. Even today, credit-card fraud is one of the major issues in Internet crime.

## 2.4.5. Legal problems

Related to these problems is the fact that the widespread use of telecommunication services and applications generally creates a number of additional legal questions. Austria, Finland, Italy, and Luxembourg explicitly put related actions on their list of measures for the next future [4].

The importance of a suitable legal environment is hardly underestimated: The abovementioned examples concerning crime, consumer protection, and digital signature along with issues of democratic legitimisation and accountability require new provisions prior to technical implementation.

## 3. Central issues of telecommunication law—the case of Austria

Until the year 1993 the Austrian telecommunication law was determined by the Telecommunication Act 1949 (Fernmeldegesetz 1949). In this case the state held a monopoly in the field of telecommunication, administered through the post and telegraph administration (Post-und Telegraphenverwaltung). Joining the European Economic Area necessitated the adjustment of community law’s requirement before European Union membership already. Thus, the Telecommunication Act 1993 (Fernmeldegesetz 1993) brought about a separation of administrative and operational functions as well as the redesigning of legal relations to private customers from public to private law.

A further step was taken with putting into effect the Telecommunication Act 1997 (Telekommunikationsgesetz 1997). While transforming the relevant European Union directives, it constituted a legal frame for the liberalised telecommunication market from 1998 onwards. At the same time, the structure of competent authorities was fundamentally reorganised. The supreme telecommunication authority is the Federal Ministry of Transport, followed by regional telecommunication authorities. Above all, the new law brought about the creation of separate regulatory authorities. This is the Telekom-Control-Kommission, a collegial authority that is not subject to instructions.

Since their decisions concern civil rights, it was necessary to install a tribunal in the sense of Art. 6 ECHR. Moreover, the Rundfunk-und Telekom-Regulierungs is another authority that likewise supervises regulation and also serves as executive branch of the Telekom-Control-Kommission. Van As and Tillema [16] describe similar overlapping and, thus, confusing responsibilities in the Dutch telecommunications market.

These authorities aim at creating and safeguarding equal access to telecommunication market, thus ensuring well going competition. ‘‘Asymmetric regulation’’ is entitled to impose stricter obligations upon former monopolists that still dominate the market than upon others. In a neighbouring field, digital pay television, Kaitatzi-Whitlock [7] reports similar difficulties. This result, together with the analysis put forward by Scott [15], suggests that these problems are ubiquitous in the European Union.

The first stage of electronic government solutions has been realized in the late nineties. The official website run by the Austrian government basically enables Internet users to gather information on legal procedures and download forms.

The second phase has been finally implemented by the end of the year 2001: interactive communication from businesses to administrative authorities via the Internet required several legal provisions by the federal parliament. The problem of a secure identification of the user could be solved by chip cards, user codes and by the entitlement of the authorities to request more information from citizens using the Internet.

In the third phase that should be finalized by the year 2010 the range of official communication with the Austrian authorities will include procedures at civil courts and procedures for projects that affect the environment or a large number of citizens, for instance the construction of a power station or similar.

## 4. European Union legal procedures—a handicap?

In the most recent history the e-Commerce directive and the regulation of telecom law in several directives of the European Council can also be seen as a success of community legislation. Nevertheless, the age of electronic enlightenment in the field of public administration has not come yet. Real and effective e-government requires an EC-law foundation to establish basic rights of the citizens to communicate via e-media with the authorities (what is called $^ { 6 6 } \mathrm { C } \ 2$ $\mathbf { G } ^ { \ast }$ in the newspeak of e-government). The intra- and intergovernmental communication via Internet $( ^ { 6 6 } \mathrm { ~ G ~ } 2$ $\mathbf { G } ^ { \ast } )$ that may be set forth without rules or shall be regulated by treaties between European Union member states cannot be regarded as real and effective egovernment.

But beyond Feira, Lisbon, and Gothenburg it is a toss whether these fundamental rights shall be integrated into the EC law. The European Union has not even managed to get ready for the integration of the middle and east European candidate countries. The constitutional step of the European Union reform that should have been taken by the Nice treaty has turned out to be a very slow and not even finished step because of the negative Irish referendum. Scepticism towards the EC regulation has emerged in France and Germany because of the British and continental BSE crisis and the agricultural problems within the European Union. The southern member states, Spain, Portugal and Greece, are threatening to block the enlargement and the joining of new member states if the status quo ante in the sector of regional policy and the financial tasks of the cohesion funds supporting the southern regions will not be continued.

But there are even more economically and legally caused obstacles in the way to a European e-government directive. The United Kingdom and Denmark and their Scandinavian allies would not even join the Euromarket that makes the implementation of e-payment even more difficult. A restrictive interpretation of the secret ballot by the constitutional court makes it impossible to establish e-voting in Austria. The European convention of human rights (ECHR) establishes in its Article 8 the fundamental right of privacy that can be in conflict with the use of private data by the electronic administration. The enforcement of administrative decrees based upon e-government processes can also cause a legal conflict between the ECHR and the national rules. The deliverance of decisions of administrative authorities requires, therefore, the prior consent of the citizen that is a serious obstacle on the way to e-government.

Finally, one of the main constitutional problems of implementing e-government solutions all over the European Union is the lack of a legal basis in the

EC treaty and in the secondary community laws. There does not exist any article that entitles the EC authorities to interfere into the public administration regulations of the member states. The legal situation is comparable to a federal union such as the United States or Switzerland where the competence for setting the rules of the administrative process does often remain with the federal states. An even more complicated situation with regard to the EC law is to be found in the federal states within the European Union (Austria, Belgium, and Germany), where also the Provincial diets have the constitutional right of legislation [11]. In Austria and in Germany Provincial laws in the field of public management and administration that provide for the cooperation of the federal authorities in their execution and in the enforcement the consent of the federal government must be obtained for that cooperation. As a matter of fact, in the era of budgetary restrictions the federal governments will not support egovernment reform steps that require huge costs of technical improvement in the federal offices.

Even though there is no genuine competence of the EU for lawgiving in the field of e-government, several efforts have been made by the Commission to implement E-Europe in the EC treaties. Commissioner Bangemann of Sweden, former secretary of state, is the spiritus rector of the so-called Bangemann Paper of electronic government goals. More lately, the Commission has published a green book of electronic government solutions that should be realized on the basis of the national constitutions [2]. Beside this paper and the green book that might be seen as soft law incentives bilateral and national treaties between EU member states are providing electronic government measures such as the exchange of data in the field of interstate cooperation in penal law and law enforcement. Anyway, most of the legal sources include only so-called G 2 G measures. The possibilities of the interaction of businesses and individuals with administrative authorities still remain largely unsupported by the Commission. Generally speaking, European initiatives stop at providing rules for Internet access and consumer rights, leaving it to the local governments to put electronic government into concrete terms.

As there is no legislative push by the European Union itself the member states try to enter into the egovernment era more slowly and more carefully as the technical development would allow. The result is an uneconomic side by side process of traditional and new public management. Real e-government that should set to be on top of the to-do list in the reform of public administration remains a field of task forces and of technical scientists in the European Union. In Austria, the right-wing government has braked the forthcoming of electronic revolution in public life down. There is no support of university departments and economic initiatives as well as of lawyers and legal scientist that deal with the unbidden topic of economic and legal problems in the field of e-government. The reform of public administration is only seen as a cost and constitutional reform task. Some politicians are of the opinion that the Austrian public administration is still on top of the world, although it has not changed so far since the fall of the Austro–Hungarian monarchy in 1918. The result of such self-projection is that the authorities and governments of many European Union member states block unauthorised proposals and entrepreneurial contributions to a reform of the public administration by the means of real e-government. As other examples such as the telecom and railway liberation directives prove, the solution can only be seen in a legislative push by the European Union council based on a draft of the commission itself.

## 5. Conclusions and outlook

The present article shows that the current projects concerning e-government in individual member countries of the European Union are difficult to sell off as a harmonised approach. Instead, the picture is colourful, but somewhat uncoordinated. We conjecture that this problem mainly arises from the fact that the initiative is essentially left to the individual member states.

Our survey shows that problems already start at the very beginning. The impetus to engage in electronic government projects has some very different origins: Some countries have a lot of catching up to do in terms of infrastructure. A second group is much further developed, seeking to apply the existing infrastructure for particular administrative purposes. These purposes are similar in each of these states, but only in an abstract sense. Of course, the first step is to make information available on the Internet; the second is to further shorten the distance to the government office by allowing documents to be downloaded. The interaction between businesses and individuals on the one hand and administrative authorities on the other, however, requires a lot more. Austria gives an excellent example what problems arise in ensuring security of communication and how long it takes to produce results.

We have recently witnessed similar problems concerning the privatisation of telecommunication throughout the European Union. While it is often regarded as unpopular to delegate power to central institutions, this approach is perhaps more fruitful if a common effort is intended. For instance, the member states now try to organise security and privacy measures individually, which may perhaps lead to quick solutions in some countries, but certainly not to a harmonised result.

Considering the four main tasks that have been formulated for e-government in the European Union, we can see that with facilitated access to public information and services and the improved transparency of administration, the first two goals are about the be reached by all countries in the near future. Full communication with administration, however, is still in its infancy, with some countries having taken first legislative steps to lay down rules for digital signatures. And lastly, a common effort for electronic public procurement is unforeseeable. Although individual solutions seem feasible, they will most likely lead to quite heterogeneous situations within the Union. Such a result, coined ‘‘Europe of two speeds,’’ is very unpopular in the European Union and has been contested in many contexts in the past.

Critics of the European Union are likely to condemn the present patch-up job as the outcome of just another rhetoric in the European Union [14], for an overview. The problem with such impressions from the European Union’s effort has recently been demonstrated in Ireland. If citizens have the impression that much is said on European Summits but few is actually done, they again lose confidence, which is not affluent even today.
