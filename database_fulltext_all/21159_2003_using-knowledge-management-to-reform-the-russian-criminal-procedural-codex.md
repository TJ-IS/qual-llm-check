# Using knowledge management to reform the Russian Criminal Procedural Codex

William K. McHenry

Department of Management, College of Business Administration, University of Akron, 259 South Broadway, 353 CBA, Akron, OH 44325-4801, USA

## Abstract

The SenseViewer Knowledge Management System (KMS) helps users retrieve and understand information fragments and their attributes by linking them to underlying arguments within their topic realm and exposing their context within source documents. This paper examines SenseViewer, its relationship to rapid knowledge construction, and its use to support the drafting and passage of a new Criminal Procedural Codex by the Russian DUMA. It considers what set of KM functions and tools facilitate the legislative process and dissemination of knowledge to the populace. SenseViewer illustrates a new generation of web-based e-government KMS.

Keywords: Russia; DUMA; Electronic government; Knowledge management; IBIS; SenseViewer

## 1. Introduction

Drafting and passage of legislation—making the rules of the game in society—stand as central concerns of democratic governments. This process is knowledge-intensive, involving written artifacts of previous cycles of the legislative process, popular and academic written sources and hearings, discussions, debates, contributions by knowledgeable experts, etc. Supporting the internal legislative process should involve applying knowledge management concepts and tools, including creating an appropriate repository of materials (for codification and retrieval) and a set of tools for stimulating and supporting knowledge creation and use [33]. Technical support for knowledge management must go beyond ‘‘collaborative tools such as Lotus Notes, argumentation systems such as Issue-Based Information Systems (IBIS) and knowledge-based techniques’’ to encompass ‘‘complex support tools. . .e.g., for ordering and retrieval: indexing, categorising, semantic correspondences, definition of hyper-structures, fuzzy retrieval, case based search, etc.’’ [27, p. 276].

But a vision of electronic government (EG) involves not only the improvement of processes for legislators and their staffs, but increased communication with and participation by citizens [27]. The WWW now makes it possible to comprehensively address two core EG goals: providing ‘‘access to [the] administration’s information and knowledge to citizens and authorities,’’ and more specifically, ‘‘[m]aking laws, judicial decrees and administrative processes transparent to employees of authorities and, above all, to the citizens’’ [55, i]. It ‘‘implies a fundamental change in knowledge distribution for an activity,’’ shifting knowledge that was possessed ‘‘nearly exclusively by [the] authority’s internal members. . .to. . .citizens and business partners’’ [56, p. 341]. Indeed, ‘‘[i]n order for legislation to be effective it needs to be accessible for all constituents and transparent since that will give citizens the possibilities to verify the rationale behind it and behave concordant to it’’ [52, p. 126].

However, existing governmental websites vary widely in the degree to which they provide access to legislative materials. The Cyberspace Policy Research Group (http://www.cyprg.arizona.edu) has tracked such sites since 1996 and reports that there are now over 2000 sites in over 120 countries [25]. Managers of these websites have concentrated more on making information available than making it convenient to access [18]. About 37% of the countries ‘‘provided reports, research, laws, and regulations in easily readable format on screen’’ on all sites, and 60% of the countries provided this on four-fifths of their sites. However, only 8% of countries included ‘‘a searchable index for archived newsletters, laws, regulations, and requirements’’ on all sites, and about half the countries provided this on less than 30% of their sites [17]. Taylor and Burt [49, p. 62] show that ‘‘parliaments, as expressed through their Web sites, are locked into a parliamentary model of democracy that prevents a more informative, expressive, political, knowledge-developing and activist approach to relationships with citizens.’’

Furthermore, the problems encountered with full text indexing and searches are well known and remain fairly intractable. Attempts to find all relevant sources about an issue (high recall), which may be legally necessary when drafting legislation, result in the retrieval of large numbers of irrelevant texts as well (low precision) [21, 44]. Furthermore, ‘‘explicit knowledge relevant for a specific [legislative] workflow task or decision is normally spread over many different kinds of documents, forms, legislative texts, etc.. . .[L]inks and relationships between documents are often not explicitly represented in software systems for information retrieval and access’’ [1, p. 48]. Higher level knowledge structures are needed to provide semantically based retrieval.

During the Fall 2000 and Spring 2001 legislative sessions in Russia, the SenseViewer knowledge management system was used to support writing and passage of a specific bill by the Legislative Committee of the Russian DUMA. This bill (see Section 2.2) is part of a set of judicial reforms that will overhaul the Russian judicial system from top to bottom, including new rules about judges, prosecutors, trials, lawyers, juries, defendants, evidence, and so forth [37]. These reforms are seen as essential for reducing widespread corruption and providing the basis for dependable business relationships and the civil society as a whole [57].

This paper examines the ways in which the Sense-Viewer system fulfills the needs for the necessary complex legislation support tool. Section 2 examines antecedents of the system, and Section 3 looks at its design and connections to relevant knowledge management approaches. Section 4 presents some results of using the system. Section 5 discusses these findings and considers what role knowledge management may play in supporting the legislative process, both for legislators and the public as a whole. A short conclusion follows in Section 6.

## 2. Background and methodology

## 2.1. Development of SenseViewer at Russian Word/Cogitum

Work on what would eventually become Sense-Viewer was started in 1998 at the Russian Word software firm in Moscow (http://www.rword.ru) and its sister firm Cogitum in Reston, VA (http://www. cogitum.com). The initial idea was to store texts in a database, and assign attributes to them in such a way that it would become possible to see underlying patterns, sifting out redundant, stale, inaccurate, or biased information. This seemed to be a particularly pressing problem in Russia, where so much of the media perspective was slanted by ownership [4,22]. Categorizing the texts using semantic concepts could help expose ‘‘deep knowledge’’ about events and their underlying relationships. Ultimately, this led to an XML-based architecture based on a structurization of the knowledge elements and arguments related to a topic. This structurization uses frames to represents topics, slots in those frames to represent positions, and slot values to point to fragments of text on a local or Web-based server. As the software was developed, several experiments were conducted in various knowledge realms, including knowledge bases about elections in Krasnoyarsk, reasons for the rise and fall of the NASDAQ, and the similarities and differences in the characteristics of various computer models.

Emphasis on this underlying semantic structurization helps the user to expose what is valuable to keep and what can be discarded in pursuing an accurate view of affairs or a solution to a difficult problem. Perhaps it is no accident that SenseViewer arose in post-Communist Russia, where the dialectic method of Hegel was considered central to logical analysis. SenseViewer represents a ‘‘Hegelian inquiry system’ that is ‘‘based on a synthesis of multiple completely antithetical representations that are characterized by intense conflict because of the contrary underlying assumptions. Knowledge management systems based upon the Hegelian inquiry systems would facilitate multiple and contradictory interpretations of the focal information’’ [30] (see also Ref. [16]). This leads to the healthy search for alternatives: ‘‘It is the contestability of knowledge and truth that leads to innovation and creativity. Where ‘knowledge’ is uncontested and its ‘truth’ accepted then the necessity to search for alternatives will fade from view’’ [20].

The two key elements of SenseViewer both have to do with exposing context: (1) making context visible by recording certain attributes about it and (2) linking the information to structured argumentation. Context is what allows information, when assimilated, to be transformed into actionable knowledge [34]. The fundamental emphasis on the role of the user recognizes that organizational memory is not about passively retrieving things from a bin, but about the ‘‘active reconstruction’’ of knowledge that is needed (and interpreted) at a particular time, space, and setting [5].

Thus, the goal of SenseViewer is to help users to make sense out of disparate, fragmentary, biased, redundant, narrow, and/or conflicting pieces of information by linking them to the underlying arguments within the realm or topic with which they deal. It is indicative that the current version of SenseViewer does not contain a search engine (although plans to add this feature are being discussed). SenseViewer relies on the principle that ‘‘only variety can destroy variety’’; i.e., issues and positions are broad enough, and links to fragments open enough, to encompass the various specific terms in which they may be represented [11].

## 2.2. The DUMA and the UPK legislation

In Fall 2000, the DUMA Legislation Committee (LC) gave its approval for a project spearheaded by Russian Word/Cogitum to apply its SenseViewer technology to the issues and information surrounding the proposed changes in the Criminal Procedural Codex (Ugolovno-Protsessual’niy Kodeks, or UPK). Russian Word also developed the overall website for the LC and together these were launched for the general public on May 25, 2001 [41]. SenseViewer was used to create the UPK ‘‘information–analytical system’’ in Russian at http://www.duma.gov.ru/comlaw/sense/frames.htm (hereafter called the DUMA UPK System).

Since the March 2000 election of Vladimir Putin as President of Russia, and the relative political stability this brought, the Russian DUMA has worked at a furious pace. In the Spring 2001 session, it considered 474 bills, of which 158 survived all three readings; 93 of these were signed into law by Putin [48]. With 300 or more pending legislative drafts in Spring 2001, the LC was one of the busiest DUMA committees. Its purview encompassed changes in a wide range of extant Soviet-era laws, including laws on arbitration, alternative military service, and the civil procedural codex.

Given the heavy workload of the committee, the proposed reforms in the UPK provided an outstanding test case for the first public use of SenseViewer. Containing over 500 separate articles, this bill dealt with meaty and contentious core issues of the judicial system, such as measures for detainment and arrest, lawyer access, bail, extradition, pretrial hearings, and the rights of prosecutors and judges. As just one example of the difficulties of still using the 1960s UPK, the Chief Justice of the Russian Supreme Court cited the absence of sufficient procedures for pretrial release, resulting in vast overcrowding of pretrial holding areas (236,000 were being held in places for 143,000 as of February 2001) [28]. The bill passed the second reading on June 21, 2001, and engendered considerable controversy. As Radio Free Europe/ Radio Liberty reported: ‘‘The new code is significantly more liberal than the Soviet-era code it replaces, but human rights groups have noted that it still has many illiberal provisions. For example, it stipulates that the accused must prove his innocence rather than be presumed innocent’’ [39]. On November 22, 2001, it was returned to second-reading status so that a package of amendments proposed by the Kremlin could be added, engendering additional controversy. The amendments were approved and it then passed the third and final reading by a vote of 289–93 [36]. It was passed by the Federation Council on December 5, 2001, and signed into law by Putin on December 18, 2001 [37].

## 2.3. Methodology of this study

The methodology adopted for this paper is qualitative in nature, taking an interpretivist stance [24]. The author adopted an approach that falls in the gray area between participant observation and action research. In some cases, open-ended interviews were conducted in a more traditional information gathering mode, and in some cases, the author directly participated in developing concepts for the further development and use of the system. The author engaged in conversations with the chief architect of the system starting in Spring 2000, which led to a field visit in Moscow in July 2001. He worked side-by-side with and interviewed Russian Word/Cogitum system developers and content developers and interviewed a key member of the DUMA LC staff. The action research approach permits the researcher to gather deep information [3] and enter into the mind world of the subjects of study [26].

The SenseViewer system was developed to address a specific application area. The developers are now interested in a broader view of related research in order to see how it might support broader knowledge management tasks. Specific directions are already in the works, and therefore this study may be seen as representing the results of the diagnosis phase of an action research project [3]. This research contributes more broadly to understanding how knowledge management may facilitate e-government and how e-government information dissemination systems may be designed in the future. In addition to interviews, the results of using SenseViewer for the DUMA UPK are examined both from the viewpoint of the artifact itself (the knowledge base as built) and from the viewpoint of Web logs. Summaries of internal DUMA Web use statistics were provided, along with complete logs of external use, for the entire period from April to December 2001.

## 3. SenseViewer architecture and relationship to similar approaches

## 3.1. Architecture

The starting point for a SenseViewer knowledge base is a structurization of the issue under consideration. Fig. 1 shows the organization of frames and slots. The topic/problem under consideration occupies a single metaframe, and the slots in that frame represent the issues or questions of interest about that topic/problem. These link to their own frames. Each frame has an unlimited number of slots, each slot representing an issue, position, question, etc. about the topic of the frame. These slots then point towards text fragments that illuminate one aspect of the issue. Specific arguments may therefore be represented both as slot names and as slot values corresponding to specific text fragments. Text fragments are located within HTML documents that reside either on the local server or on any server anywhere on an Intranet or on the WWW. The frames are represented by XML, and X-Pointer and X-Link are used to capture the location of the fragments in the source documents. These documents do not need to be altered in any way for the system to make use of them, and different markups for the same documents can be supported simultaneously.

Each frame may be assigned a set of attributes, so that it is possible to define different frame types depending on what is needed. Separate sets of attributes may also be defined for slot values and for text fragments. Current conventions dictate that attributes contain just one value, but the ‘‘text’’ type will permit anything to be entered. No explicit provision is made for one –many relationships.

The full implementation of SenseViewer requires a downloaded plug-in for IE 5.0 and above. The developers created the SenseViewer SenseEditor, which allows content managers to easily create the needed hierarchical structures and associate text fragments with them by highlighting them in the retrieved Webbased documents. The result is a set of files for each of the major objects in the system: the frames, the texts (if moved to the local server), the cross-references, and an additional type of text attribute, defined at the level of an entire text.

For publication on the Web, the SenseViewer translates the XML files on the local version to standard HTML and JavaScript. These files can then be stored on any server and accessed with standard tools. They can be hosted anywhere, because no DBMS or other server-side software is required beyond a standard Web server. This avoids the need for the plug-in and promotes wide compatibility, but prevents any changes in the underlying structures from being made in the published version. In addition, the entire-text-level attribute feature is not available on the Web version, so some of the attribute types used for it (such as ‘‘document source’’) have ‘‘migrated’ for use as attributes of text fragments. (However, there are attributes affixed to entire documents on the DUMA LC site in the section headed Dokumenty (Documents), so Russian Word/Cogitum did make use of this part of their approach elsewhere on the site.)

When the user drills down to a specific fragment and clicks on it, the entire document from which it came appears in the right hand window with the fragment highlighted. The user may then browse the document to understand its context. The contents of the ‘‘Document References’’ window on the lower left are updated to reflect all the other places that document is used within the structurization. This gives a strong sense of the overall importance of the document and its contents, while providing additional context for its current use. These ‘‘Document References’’ are generated as separate HTML files (one per document) when the SenseEditor translates the XML files for the Web.

Fig. 2 shows a small portion of the DUMA UPK structurization (translated into English). At circle A are concrete examples of the name of a slot in the metaframe (‘‘Application of detainment measures’’), its value (‘‘Detainment measure choice bases’’) that points to a frame of the same name, slots in that frame (‘‘Goals,’’ ‘‘Social–economic bases,’’ etc.), and values of these slots that comprise specific text fragments. At circle B is one such text fragment. The attributes assigned to this frame slot value (text fragment) are indicated at circle C. On the right hand side of the screen is the full text from which the fragment came (circle D), so that the user sees not only the highlighted fragment but can browse its context. On the lower left hand corner is the ‘‘Document References’’ window, which shows all the other places in the structurization that fragments from this document have been used (circle E).

Thus, as the DUMA UPK System now exists on the website, it can be used primarily as a means to semantically browse the issues of concern and related texts about this specific piece of legislation.

## 3.2. Relationship to similar approaches

Writing legislation is a particular process within a particular context and no software systems have been created to support all its elements. However, there has been a very large amount of research about collaborative work, a field that contains elements from hypertext and hypermedia, Computer-Supported Collaborative Work (CSCW), Group Decision-Support Systems (GDSS), Computer-Supported Collaborative Argumentation (CSCA), organizational memory, development of ontologies, and knowledge management (KM). What these approaches have in common is capturing knowledge either as a direct product or as a by-product of their use. Selvin and Buckingham Shum [45] call this ‘‘rapid knowledge construction (RKC).’’ RKC projects are quite varied with respect to the number of people and teams involved, timeframes, the extent to which the goals are well-known or exploratory, and whether the emphasis is on communication and/or the production of a body of content for subsequent use. They continue:

An RKC approach should be flexible enough to accommodate goals anywhere along these continuums, and to move back and forth between emphases as needed. The knowledge captured and stored in an RKC repository should be available for reuse by new project teams or efforts, in such a way that individual knowledge elements can be easily discovered, accessed, and recombined for new purposes—in other words, recontextualized. . .RKC. . .is exploring one of the most difficult parts of the KM technology design space—representations and user interfaces which are ‘lightweight’ enough to enable situated, realtime capture, but which also sustain reuse—two goals which are often in tension with each other [45, p. 2,emphases are in the original].

## 3.2.1. IBIS, structurization, and the SenseViewer knowledge base

As Buckingham Shum [5] points out, Horst Rittle developed the concept of ‘‘wicked problems’’ in the early 1970s, along with the concept of Issue-Based

Information Systems (IBIS) to address them [15]. Wicked problems, in a nutshell, have strong political dimensions because of the involvement of numerous stakeholders with different perspectives, do not have clear solutions, and require iteration in trying to solve them [16]. Numerous variations of IBIS have been developed, including a formal language for expressing arguments [5]. Real-world applications of IBIS have been centered around planning and design and software engineering [6,10], but have not been widespread [23]. Legislative policy decisions have similar scope to those for planning and design and have a ‘‘wicked’’ nature: they are highly controversial, they take place in a context of diverging interests, and therefore ‘‘social communication’’ is required to resolve them [23, p. 163].

The goal of IBIS was not primarily to relate argument positions to documentary evidence supporting them, but to generate the arguments themselves through a highly structured discourse [23]. One of the first computer-based implementations, gIBIS, was the result of adapting IBIS for use with hypertext [14,15]. Initially, the purpose of this system was asynchronous development of argumentation maps. gIBIS was commercialized as QuestMap, which included features to link maps to reference and notes nodes. The JANUS system also combined hypertext for argumentation (recording design decisions) and links to CAD drawings about which the discourse took place [19]. Some users found these features to be more useful as a sort of local ‘‘file tracking system’’ (they did not link to external content as SenseViewer does), while others developed a way of using the system for real-time meetings with subsequent asynchronous interaction with the argumentation map [13].

This second QuestMap use is at the heart of Compendium, an RKC methodology and set of tools for graphically capturing argumentation as it unfolds in a group. Compendium helps organizations establish their organizational memory relating to how complex decisions were justified and made [45]. Compendium represents the most widely used IBIS-based system and has been applied in governmental as well as business settings [46].

The generic components of IBIS are issues, positions, arguments, and references [8], which are obviously directly related to the process of developing legislation (the way these relate to SenseViewer frames is shown in Fig. 1). Frequently encountered categories in SenseViewer are ‘‘arguments for’’ and ‘‘arguments against.’’ In the practical use of Quest-Map, the basic terminology was changed to ‘‘question, idea, pro, and con’’ [13]. SenseViewer does not explicitly support rigid IBIS link types such as ‘‘Responds To’’ or ‘‘Objects-To’’ (see Ref. [15]), but is sufficiently flexible that as long as the arguments are structured hierarchically, specific elements of IBIS can be incorporated. Within the family of systems that were created following after IBIS, SenseViewer’s structurization most resembles that within the Procedural Hierarchy of Issues (PHI) approach [40].

By not requiring the strict typing of argumentation elements, SenseViewer avoids problems encountered in IBIS implementations, where the overhead of forcing users to break thoughts into too specific subunits was significant [14] (see also Ref. [47]). Research about Compendium found that forcing users to unbundle argument elements worked best for truly wicked problems that did not have a lot of structure to begin with [5]. Legislative issues, proposals, and drafts rarely start from scratch.

SenseViewer is intended for asynchronous use within a single group—it is not currently implemented for use on the Web in a multiuser environment. The SenseViewer approach is more in keeping with some of the early results from building large hypertext systems.

First, people use knowledge structures to organize and categorize content. Second, over the course of a task people develop knowledge structuring schemes; these schemes evolve through negotiation and use. In practice, the two activities aren’t that easy to pull apart, but they each suggest a different set of use situations and user requirements.. . .The kind of knowledge structuring that we’ve observed is semi-synchronous rather than the tightly-coordinated synchronous collaboration that takes place in meetings. Much of the interaction among collaborators takes place through actions on the knowledge structures as well as through meta-comments attached to the knowledge structure [31, pp. 263 – 264].

SenseViewer supports the structurization process by providing a shared artifact around which discussion can take place. Early researchers of hypertext systems found that two levels of discussion emerged beyond content: discussion about the content, and procedural discussions about how to use the system [14]. SenseViewer does not try to capture the large amount of interplay that takes place as the content builders interact with subject experts and clients to develop the needed structurization. It does not include explicit places to record meta-comments about the structures and processes for building them. Content creators may record their rationale for choosing various alternatives in attribute fields, but this would be awkward. Not requiring such meta-information may avoid some of the problems associated with explicitly indicating who is advocating a particular argument thread [5], while making it more difficult to resume work and incorporate new content builders in the future. QuestMap users found that having everything in one place as a reminder of the thinking patterns involved in the decision was in fact quite useful [13].

A SenseViewer structurization is strongly related to the idea of an ontology. Abecker and Mentzas [1] point out that ‘‘ontology-based information systems acquire from the community of system users the commonly agreed upon structures (concepts and definitions, relationships, constraints, axioms) which logically organise a given domain of expertise or area of work. A formal representation of these generally accepted domain knowledge structures, the ontology. . ., is the basis for a homogeneous, conceptbased (instead of keyword-based) content description of knowledge. . .’’ [1, p. 51]. Selvin and Buckingham Shum [45] also call the results of using Compendium to be an informal ontology.

While the architecture of SenseViewer permits users to define and organize all these domain knowledge structures, in practice it is oriented towards issues. Users do not need to develop a new thesaurus or use an existing one in order to use SenseViewer, and the typical links needed for a thesaurus would be hard to emulate. SenseViewer does not support the development of an arbitrary hypertext structure.

## 3.2.2. Active use of e-government knowledge bases

Referring again to Fig. 3, it is now clear that SenseViewer addresses only the activities in boxes 1, 2, 5, and 6. Other e-government projects are adding more formalisms in order to explore greater degrees of automation in support of the legislative process. These efforts recognize that the mix in knowledge worker tasks is shifting more from procedural features to knowledge-based features [5, p. 3].

Unlike the relatively informal SenseViewer structurization, the POWER project of the Dutch Tax and Customs Administration uses UML and some extensions to create formalized conceptual models of legislation [51,52]. POWER came from the use of expert systems in administrative units at the operational level that had to apply already written laws [51]. In devising these systems, it became clear that the scarce expert knowledge that was being used to understand legislation was also applicable to creating legislation. (A more formal IBIS model has been combined with fuzzy logic in ways that resemble POWER [8].)

POWER attempts to detect such anomalies as incompleteness, discrepancy, circularity, redundancy, and occurrences of redundant synonyms, ambiguous homonyms, and vagueness. Detecting the problems early can allow for redrafting, eliminating problems later. In modeling the proposed Dutch Income Tax Law, 150 anomalies were found by POWER. Linking actual decisions and use of the law in the field back to specific provisions as modeled in UML may make it possible to do impact analysis on future changes in the law [51]. The POWER project thus envisions a level of formalization and automation that goes well beyond what can currently be supported by SenseViewer.

As in SenseViewer, the emphasis of POWER is on the output of the deliberative process, i.e., the UML model. SenseViewer exposes the underlying thinking of the developers in a different way, because the connected fragments show how the arguments have been supported. POWER can refer explicitly to laws, legal rulings, cases, etc. to substantiate the expert rules. The developers claim that this enhances knowability and transparency of the legislation [52]. In this case, however, one has to use the derived knowledge-based system (expert system) in order to see this substantiation. Furthermore, POWER puts the resolution of disparate points of view in the hands of developers at the point of creating the knowledge bases, whereas SenseViewer exposes contradictions for contemplation and resolution in a Hegelian fashion by users [30].

The DE<sup>´</sup> COR project is another e-government effort that uses case-based reasoning techniques to try to deliver knowledge to decision-makers in the context of a specific workflow [1]. SenseViewer assumes that decision-makers will consult SenseViewer as necessary, and does not involve the overhead of defining process steps and their correspondences to needed knowledge elements.

The structurization produced by SenseViewer has a stronger resemblance to the hierarchical question and answer architecture of Answer Garden [2]. If needed answers are not found, Answer Garden tries to link to available experts to supply answers, which are then added to the system [2]. Answer Garden users did not like to wade through large amounts of information to find things they needed and appreciated knowing quickly when information was absent [2]. In Sense-Viewer, slot values without fragments serve this purpose.

Answer Garden transforms a static knowledge base into a system that could be enhanced by specific, new contributions. One could also imagine adding discussion boards to SenseViewer so that users could add their ideas and commentary. This could also facilitate the dialogue among the initial developers of the structurization. The Compendium project has experimented with using the D3E standard to add forums at various parts in knowledge maps. An example of a discussion of a public policy issue (the Dearing Report) that uses D3E may be found at http://d3e.open.ac.uk/Dearing/. In this case, the structurization of the issues was imported from the outline of the report document itself, but it is not hard to imagine instrumenting a Sense-Viewer knowledge base in this fashion, particularly given the importance of dialogue for transferring knowledge [53]. Buckingham Shum mentions Hyper-News, which marks elements of discussion threads according to whether or not they support an argument [5]. Numerous less-structured forums or bulletin-board systems now exist on the Web.

Yet another link between SenseViewer and related research comes in the use of SGML and XML as standards for writing parliamentary documents in the European Union. The Finnish parliament has defined 21 standard document types using SGML since 1997. Efforts to incorporate authoring tools so that documents can be created directly in these formats is on-going. A consolidated legal database is to be available starting in 2003 [43]. This project ‘‘has radically improved the accessibility of information and thus supports democracy and transparency of governance’’ [43].

The process described in the analysis of documents for structurization using SGML in the Finnish Parliament is remarkably similar to the process of creating a SenseViewer KB. A collection of existing printed documents is studied, along with any existing rules and laws about how they must be created. Domain experts and document users are also involved in the process, which eventually results in the creation of a suitable document standard [42].

From a practical point of view, developers of SenseViewer knowledge bases would benefit by using the structure of proposed legislation in this format as a starting point for a SenseViewer structurization. Finding and referencing fragments would be simplified. Using these standards across all legislative committees would permit linkages across SenseViewer knowledge bases as well. Currently discussions are going on with the Russian DUMA about emulating the Finnish experience.

Thus, SenseViewer is well-positioned to facilitate major portions of the legislative process depicted in Fig. 3. Other tools and methodologies within the sphere of rapid knowledge construction and intelligent systems address related pieces of the process. No one system currently supports every important aspect of creating, passing, and using legislation.

## 4. Building and using the DUMA UPK System

Evaluating the potential for the subsequent use of SenseViewer in a wide variety of applications rests not only on seeing it from the perspective of related systems that have been developed over the past two decades, but also on understanding the process and results from its use in a major application.

## 4.1. Creating the DUMA UPK System

In September 2000, members of the staff of the DUMA LC saw a demo of SenseViewer and expressed an interest in using it internally to support handling the complexities of the UPK legislation. The deputy head of the committee had written her dissertation on using formalisms to aid drafting legislation, and so was quite sympathetic to the concepts embodied in SenseViewer. At the same time, the LC could offer no material support to Russian Word/ Cogitum, who chose to go ahead with the project as a means of demonstrating the technology on a complicated, meaty, real-world problem.

After receiving the initial set of documents from the DUMA LC, several rounds of talks were held in which frames were proposed and feedback from the DUMA LC staff was received. An initial set of frames was created and an initial set of documents marked up. This process took about 1 month. The initial system was presented to the DUMA LC staff, who found it to be exciting and gave a new set of documents to Russian Word/Cogitum for addition to the system. Several more rounds took place, ending with a demonstration at the DUMA and the system’s acceptance in early April 2001. Although the initial expectation was to keep it for internal use only, when the DUMA staff saw the first demonstration in December 2000, they started thinking it should be available to the public as a whole, which transpired as noted on May 25 [41].

The use of QuestMap was most successful with a relatively small group and someone acting as the ‘‘view keeper’’ [13]. A similar approach was adopted in the DUMA UPK project, where the content team at Russian Word/Cogitum comprised a leader and two others, who then interacted with one main oversight person on the DUMA LC.

The goal of the content editors was not to research all available sources in whatever format (hardcopy, on the WWW, etc.), but was to use the set of materials provided by the DUMA LC. Scanning and sometimes heavy editing into HTML format was necessary for some of the documents, which became available to a wide audience for the first time. Late in the project, some materials from the popular press were added (there are just 17 of these fragments). Partly because comprehensive research was not needed, members of the Russian Word/Cogitum team felt that the amount of work needed to create the knowledge base was not excessive. In the early database experience with text fragments (Section 2.1), they faced the vexing problem of needing to cut and paste or type in texts into the database in order to use them, leading to the decision to link to content on the Web. While a similar research group in the 1980s was able to create a database by typing in about 40,000 text fragments [29], other research groups working on insertion of texts in hypertext nodes found scanning and typing in to be prohibitive (e.g., [32, 45]).

Provision of texts by the DUMA LC, without periodically revisiting available sources, makes the knowledge base a static snapshot at a given point in time [8]. It eliminates maintenance work but increases the potential of omitting important information. For example, a substantive independent analysis of the new UPK by Pashin [35] appeared after the UPK System’s release on the DUMA Intranet, and was not included. If one were to broaden the scope of the system to include links to actual uses of the UPK in practice, automated or semiautomated means might be necessary to attach new fragments to the existing structurization [9].

## 4.2. The system as created

As of its state on June 22, 2001, The DUMA UPK System included a total of 47 different topic frames. Table 1 shows a selection of the most and least used topic aspects (recall that clicking on a topic aspect brings the user to the most specific arguments and the text fragments associated with them). The top two aspects, ‘‘Arguments for’’ and ‘‘Arguments against,’’ appeared in about 70% of frames. There were 65 topic aspects, of which about 80% were used in less than seven frames and 40% were only used once.

There are two explanations for this seeming inconsistency. On the one hand, the freedom that content developers have in choosing topics, positions, and aspects allows them to eliminate aspects that make no sense under certain headings, while adding special aspects for topics where it makes sense. For example, the aspect ‘‘Sanctions for violating terms of release under bail’’ appeared only under a frame devoted to bail. On the other hand, one may argue that this freedom results in a certain amount of inconsistency as well, which the content developers for the DUMA UPK System acknowledge. For example, the topic aspect ‘‘Shortcomings’’ was used just once; one can easily imagine that it could be used everywhere. One would also think that ‘‘arguments for’’ and ‘‘arguments against’’ would appear everywhere, giving the Hegelian orientation of the system. Furthermore, there is a lot of leeway in choosing slot value names and names of linked fragments, leading to different levels of arguments appearing at different points in the hierarchy.

SenseViewer’s content editor software (SenseEditor) does not enforce conventions. Users can make a frame and copy it to a template, reusing it for other frames. When a copy is modified, the original is not. This implementation avoided complexity that caused problems with QuestMap. In QuestMap hypertext nodes were implemented such that making a copy of a node at a different place in the map and then modifying it actually modified the original node as well (the second node was a pointer to the first; this property is known as ‘‘transclusive hyperlinking’’ [45]). Even experienced users had a hard time understanding this concept [13]. However, as the DUMA UPK content editors discovered the need for certain aspects under later topics, they could have added these back to the earlier created frames. Given QuestMap’s experience, it may be better to implement a wizard to assist users to be consistent, rather than forcing them to do so.

Another explanation for these inconsistencies relates to the textual material available for the fragments. The provided texts themselves suggested what topic aspects were needed, and in some cases where there were no associated text fragments, empty topic aspects were removed. In other cases, they were left as empty. The system’s designers actually wanted the empty aspects to remain, because they may show that evidence about an important part of an issue is currently missing. Thus, there is considerable tension between freedom and consistency in use of topic aspects.

How were these documents used? There were 82 source documents, some of which were broken into several subfiles to minimize loading times on the right-hand side of the screen. Eight hundred and forty-seven fragments from these documents were referenced by the 415 topic aspects at the lowest level of the hierarchy, for an average of about two fragments per topic aspect. In fact, 151 (36.4%) instances of topic aspects had no fragments attached at all, thus supporting the developers’ concept of showing areas where evidence about argument aspects was missing. Forty-three topic aspects always had attached fragments whenever they were used. Sixteen topic aspects had instances of use both with and without attached fragments. These tended to be rather inclusive categories, such as ‘‘Alternative variants,’’ ‘‘Arguments against,’’ ‘‘Arguments for,’’ ‘‘Foreign experience,’’ ‘‘Goals,’’ and ‘‘Initial situation.’’ Most surprisingly, there were six topic aspects that never had attached fragments. One wonders if fragments were later deleted or if they were created in anticipation of finding fragments that never materialized. The category ‘‘Merits’’ could have been used widely, but probably was covered by ‘‘Arguments for.’’ Four other categories—‘‘Opponents,’’ ‘‘Supporters,’’ ‘‘Consequences of application,’’ and ‘‘Economic basis for reducing consequences’’—could touch upon subjects that were ultimately deemed too controversial for an official system of the DUMA LC, or could indicate that the kinds of materials used did not include such information.

The types of documents and the distribution of fragments used from them is shown in the first column of Fig. 4. The proposed and existing codices account for only 19% of the references, which supports the claim that this system goes well beyond simply providing a table of contents to the legislation. Together ‘‘comparative materials from conference’’ and ‘‘hearings/transcripts’’ comprise 62% of the references. The former refers to a conference sponsored by the DUMA LC at which a wide range of specialists from around the world commented on the proposals. Together with the hearings and transcripts, these materials represent a wide range of viewpoints about the legislation.

The final type of information supplied by content developers was contained in the attributes, which could be associated with frames as a whole, with slot values, and with text fragments. Four of 45 frames had one attribute assigned to them at the frame level.

Ninety percent of slot values had no attributes assigned and another 6% had only one. Nine attribute types were used. About 8% were given the attribute of ‘‘Who Supports.’’ About 3% received the attribute ‘‘Importance,’’ which took the values ‘‘important’’ or ‘‘especially important.’’ About 1% was given the attribute ‘‘Type of Document.’’ Either it was not considered particularly important to assign attributes to all slot values or this was a prohibitive amount of work.

More attributes were assigned to text fragments. About 30% of fragments had one attribute assigned, 34% had two, and 31% had three. Six different attribute types were used. ‘‘Type of Document’’ was assigned to about 47% of the fragments, while ‘‘Document Status’’ was indicated on about 32%, and ‘‘Source/Author’’ was indicated for about 16%. Content developers thought that authorship would be abundantly clear for the rest. Since the documents often comprised testimony or opinions of many authorities, the ‘‘Source/Author’’ could vary widely for fragments from the same document, giving a more accurate picture than a typical bibliographical entry. Since the system was developed to support a single piece of legislation at a given point of time, it was not deemed necessary to have any date attributes affixed to texts. Nor was there an indication of who chose the fragment and linked it to the knowledge base. It may be that the presence of filtering methods that make use of the attributes could provide increased incentives for their further use.

## 4.3. The DUMA UPK System in use

## 4.3.1. Use within the DUMA LC and the DUMA

Evidence of the reaction of users to the system is largely anecdotal. No formal focus group was held, but the system was presented in April 2001 to about 20 –30 potential users at the DUMA in a small hall near the large legislative chambers. It was also shown to about 40–50 people at a specialist conference about the UPK legislation itself, where there was also positive feedback. The deputy director of the DUMA LC staff was very enthusiastic, and reported that he received numerous inquires about the system, particularly from representatives of stakeholder groups such as judges, prosecutors, and lawyers. The main drafter of the UPK legislation on the committee stated that she needed a personal tool to help keep track of all the arguments and opinions surrounding the legislation, and was quite happy with the DUMA UPK System.

Data from the DUMA Intranet Web logs show modest, but increasing usage of the UPK System by internal users in April to June 2001. The UPK System homepage went from the 22nd most requested area of the overall DUMA LC Intranet site in April to the 3rd most requested area in June. From July through September (including the traditional August vacation period), use was minimal, which increased again in each of the 3 months of October, November, and December. Perhaps the most telling statistic is that the average time per visit was about 2.5 minutes, but as this applies to the DUMA LC Site as a whole, it may not indicate limited use of the UPK System. The server received about 4 hours of total use for the DUMA LC website in June 2001, a level to be exceeded during our period of study only in October 2001.

The number of clicks on frames almost doubled from May to June. Sorting the frames visited in April–June reveals that for the top four most-clicked metaframe slot values, the frequency of clicks corresponds quite closely to their menu order on the metaframe. Once the next-level menu of frames appears, users clicked almost exclusively on the first or second topic. Overall, 88% of the clicks in April– June were on the top five (of 15) menu items. Additional clicks on frames from July–December did not change this pattern, as 76% of clicks overall were from the first five menu items in order. It is hard not to draw the conclusion that the users were browsing the system to see what was there, testing out a sample entry from the first few menu items. During the entire April –December period, 60% of the frames were never accessed by internal DUMA users.

While the users seemed to click on the early menus more, the documents they used (Fig. 4, second column) showed a more deliberate distribution. DUMA users made more use of Hearings and Transcripts and Other Materials than a random selection would have predicted, while they made far less use of the Proposed UPK, and somewhat less use of Materials from the Conference (in which they probably participated). A plausible explanation is that the DUMA LC staff and deputies needed the most help in accessing who said what at the hearings, were intrigued by new (Other) materials, and needed less help in remembering the details of the proposed changes themselves. DUMA users accessed about one-third of the available texts throughout the project.

One can infer only so much from the volume and distribution of accesses. Most valuable is the testimony of one of the participants in the project: ‘‘[R]epresentatives from the Ministry of Internal Affairs and General Prosecutor Office changed some of their points of view after amendments proposed by these ministries were published as a part of the SenseViewer content that allowed them to see the full discussion in a structured manner’’ [38]. Together with the enthusiastic responses reported from the DUMA LC staff members above, it seems clear that use of the system was perceived to have made a difference in the legislation that was ultimately passed.

## 4.3.2. Use by the general public

The DUMA UPK System was launched on May 25, 2001, and received minimal publicity coverage thereafter. The link from the press release [41] and the ComputerWorld Russia short item [12] only accounted for nine visits to the website in June 2001. Most traffic came from the larger DUMA site, with a small amount from search engines.

The DUMA Web logs for Internet access record external use of the site, as most DUMA members do not have direct Internet access themselves. Visits to the overall DUMA site tripled from May to June 2001, dropped off over the next few months, and eventually doubled again in December 2001. On average, the number of external visits was about 11 times more than internal visits. From May to December, about 12% of the visits to the website were to the DUMA UPK System rising to 15% in June and 19% in December. The average time per visit was on average 1.5 times longer, and the total use of the server in June 2001 was about 84 hours, again a value that was never exceeded in later months.

The distribution of clicks on frames for external users showed a somewhat similar pattern to that of internal users. Forty-six percent of the clicks were on frames from the first or second menu choices. Of the top 80% of frames by number of clicks, about threequarters of these were on the top five menu choices. One has to reach the 13th ranking frame to find any clicked on subtopic value that was not in the first or second position. Nevertheless, external users did visit all the frames at least once, with the mode number of frames visited at six.

External users explored more of the content than did internal users, accessing fully three-fourths of the available texts. They made far less use of Comparative Materials from the Conference, and far more use of the Proposed UPK (Fig. 4, third column). It is plausible to believe that external users needed more basic information about the UPK compared to internal users. In 83% of the visits by unique IP addresses, which may or may not be considered different users, the ‘‘IP address’’ viewed the site only once and looked only at frames, not retrieving any text. About 5% only looked at frames, but came back more than once. The other 12% looked at texts, with 8.4% coming once and 3.5% coming more than once. Considering each unique IP address to be a different user is a problematical assumption, but if we do, we find very little repeat use of any documents, especially over more than one access date.

## 5. Discussion

The DUMA UPK System does not yet meet the vision for ‘‘complex [e-government] support tools’’ [27], but does include the central elements needed to support multiple points of view, which is at the heart of knowledge management for the inquiring organization of the future [16]. SenseViewer supports multiple markups for the same external documents, which is a key advantage in a legislative environment where numerous political parties, administrative bodies, legislative committees, and other stakeholders all have varying interests in the same documents.

One enticing option to enhance and complete Sense-Viewer is to provide semiautomated process support through the addition of intelligence, as in the POWER, DE<sup>´</sup> COR, and similar approaches examined in Section 3.2.2. However, Buckingham Shum argues that ‘‘knowledge management technologies should formalize only knowledge which is stable and sanctioned. . .[I]n domains where consensus is unclear, formalization should wait until the daily practices and routines of the organization—some of which may be too complex to predict in advance—reveal the important, stable patterns that are most in need of support’’ [5, p. 25]. Trying to formalize processes that span drafting legislation to its use, as in boxes 3 and 7 of Fig. 3, may be forever beyond reach. SenseViewer does not try to automate organizational routines and allows for conflicting, preconsensus viewpoints to be exposed.

Rather than trying to tie SenseViewer in to automation of Parliamentary processes, developers should concentrate on tools that will make it possible to create and maintain SenseViewer knowledge bases over the long term. In its current state, SenseViewer makes little provision for change control across various iterations of the knowledge base and content developers. Representations of time are largely absent. Conventions for using the system are ad hoc, by agreement among team members. As the DUMA UPK System illustrates, consistency can become a problem for a large knowledge base. Little support exists for restructuring the knowledge base when significant, new information is found.

There are already features that are available in the plug-in version of SenseViewer that do not appear in the version published to the WWW for general use, and it may be necessary to retain this division so that the publicly available system does not become too complex, while adding features to increase internal usability. Some other possibilities including following the lead of Compendium to add more robust, possibly real-time RKC support for the development of the structurization; adding tools to help check and enforce consistency rules and to analyze usage patterns; and adding knowledge based techniques to aid in making structurizations and in finding and selecting appropriate fragments [11]. Once the structurization is set and fragments found which demonstrate suitable vocabularies for certain issues, it may be possible to design queries that could be used periodically to find additional content.

For external users, it could also be useful to add a search engine and more sophisticated ways of viewing subsets of the information based on topics and attributes; the ability to incorporate forum-like commentaries; and increased structure through the use of linkages with XML standards for Parliamentary documents. Additional tools related to students, scholars, and other users would include adding full bibliographical references for source materials in an easily adaptable format; adding multimedia capabilities, especially for audio and video clips; and adding personal bookmarks and personalization tools so users could remember what they had read and where they needed to go in a manner conducive to adoption and use.

Some of these directions may potentially conflict with one another. While the prospects of these technical means are all enticing, it would be unwise to neglect the ultimate goal of knowledge management—bringing to the users the information they need in such a way that they can use it (i.e., knowledge as something that must be used to be assimilated as ‘‘justified true belief’’ [53]). Such assimilation often requires some sort of dialogue [53]. SenseViewer currently involves a dialogue among a select set of legislative drafters and content developers, who see it only as an educational tool for the public.

Furthermore, one can argue that democracy is best served when government is most transparent, but this may only be the case when the end result is dialogue and eventual consensus. Knowledge artifacts can be viewed as ‘‘boundary objects,’’ i.e., means by which groups can visualize the boundaries in views between and among them [45]. SenseViewer exposes the viewpoint of the group that has created a knowledge base with it, but does not permit its participatory evolution that would serve as a means of helping groups to bridge the differences in their views. Choosing what to represent using SenseViewer ultimately means choosing what to leave out, which could also disenfranchise and alienate parts of the population [5], although the consistent adoption of a dialectical Hegelian viewpoint and use of categories such as ‘‘arguments for’’ and ‘‘arguments against’’ can guard against this.

Fuller participation by outside groups would more directly address the ‘‘sense-making’’ functions that are represented in the very name of this system. Thus, it would be transformed from a somewhat passive means of knowledge dissemination to a fuller knowledge management system that could have the effect of increasing democratic participation and ultimately, social cohesion. However, adding public forums might complicate the process of settling on an appropriate structurization or dilute the power of presenting the most informative, relevant, and expert fragments rather than everything about a subject.

As it stands now, the DUMA UPK System has a relatively small number of expert fragments per issue. Users are not burdened with wading through large amounts of irrelevant information. The relatively small use of the attribute features in the DUMA UPK System is consistent with this, but raises an interesting question about how much context needs to be provided to transform information into useful knowledge. Buckingham Shum calls this the ‘‘context paradox’’; it could be that additional context is always needed to interpret the context of the context, and so forth. Furthermore, when the use of a group memory system goes beyond the small circle of users that created it, there may be a need to add more context to make explicit those things that the group takes for granted as ‘‘known’’ [5]. The content developers and the DUMA LC staff felt comfortable with the relatively small number of attributes provided, but this may not be the case for the broader audience of the public. It becomes difficult to predict, however, what context is needed, and the labor needed to add it can skyrocket. Trying to make the system all things to all people can also backfire. It seems clear that this paradox may arise within any project to bring knowledge management to a broad public audience.

The Web logs for internal DUMA use do not represent heavy usage. The history of RKC systems suggests that even persuading the majority staff of the DUMA LC (let alone of other DUMA committees) to incorporate SenseViewer as a central part of their work habits will be quite difficult [13]. Persuading people to make the effort to insert formalizations into their work has not been easy [47] (it has been described as akin to filing [50]). Structuring argumentation requires highly developed skills and experience [50]. Experience with IBIS systems indicates that their use cannot be relegated to less competence staff members [23]. The first company set up to market QuestMap in the early 1990s went out of business due to lack of sales [13].

Perhaps because of the paucity of information made available to Russian citizens throughout most of the 20th century, the fact that some legislators have now chosen to provide such an extensive system on the Web should not be a surprise. The DUMA LC, however, falls under the general bureaucracy and security constraints of the DUMA as a whole, and since even getting little changes made on the server (such as replacing a missing file) sometimes involve multiple levels of approval, it will probably be a struggle to get approval for the widespread use of SenseViewer. An atmosphere of secrecy still pervades the Russian government (so much so that regional and local governments often have trouble tracking the legislative process). Persuading decision-makers to expose the true rationale for decisions will be difficult [50], and direct sponsorship by the DUMA carries a significant risk of implicit or explicit censorship. As the Russian DUMA matures, it will have to overcome the commonly held belief that legislators are primarily interested in writing laws that help themselves and their friends to get richer.

And it is not surprising that the level of use of the DUMA UPK site by external users seems to be low. It appears that extensive training in argumentation systems is needed before users can assimilate a tool like QuestMap [13], although this may apply more to developers than users [23]. The complexity of the SenseViewer system raises the prospect that few ordinary citizens will be able to grasp the concepts involved in using SenseViewer. Indeed, Russian Word/Cogitum has received some feedback that users did not understand what was in the right-hand panel, since the first thing that comes up is an extensive help message explaining the whole system and its use.

But an alternative explanation is that the public was simply waiting for more certainty. The general public now needs to learn a lot more about the new UPK, because they will be bound by its provisions as of July 2002. Indeed, the use of the DUMA LC site reached its highest mark in December 2001, after the bill passed its third reading and was headed for certain enactment. More publicity about the site is planned by the DUMA LC now that the bill has been signed into law, and the full text of the new code has already been added to the website [38]. The DUMA UPK System may enhance the trust that Russian citizens have in government.

## 6. Conclusion

If there is any activity that needs an organizational memory, it is the legislative process. Legislation often addresses wicked problems. It is highly structured, providing the starting point for a semantic structurization that goes beyond traditional information retrieval techniques. Legislation is drafted and revised across many years, tested and interpreted numerous times in courts by police forces, prosecutors, and by numerous other stakeholders. It must be understood by changing sets of legislators and executives. Although judges in Russia (as in most of Western Europe more generally) look at the ‘‘plain meaning’’ of the law and are not bound to try to interpret what the legislators thought they were saying, having a detailed record as represented by the DUMA UPK System will be quite useful for legislators, lawyers, judges, scholars, and others [7].

SenseViewer can encompass a complete picture of the legislation. It incorporates a Hegelian viewpoint in which conflicting viewpoints and the contexts in which they are held and developed may be compared and contrasted. The DUMA UPK System has demonstrated that SenseViewer can be used on a largescale project spanning many months and many different types of users. For the DUMA LC staff, it has been a convenient way to access information. Both through its use by the LC and by external stakeholders, the DUMA UPK System has contributed to the passage of sweeping reforms in the Russian Criminal Procedural Codex. The DUMA UPK System may represent one of the most robust Parliamentary websites now in existence.

Comparisons with other systems show which parts of the legislative procedure SenseViewer does and does not support. SenseViewer emphasizes reuse of knowledge rather than capture, although its ease of use as a web-based system does facilitate rapid knowledge construction, as the speedy implementation of the DUMA UPK System illustrates. Nevertheless, the labor needed to supply all the necessary context may be prohibitive. The experience of using SenseViewer, including the difficulty of getting widespread use, is consistent with the experience of many of its antecedents, including QuestMap, gIBIS, Compendium, and Answer Garden. Comparisons with more active systems that embody expert systems, case-based reasoning, intelligent agents, and interactive discussions show several directions in which SenseViewer could be expanded to cover more of the legislative process.

It is possible that use of SenseViewer will be institutionalized in the DUMA LC or the DUMA as a whole? The fact that the DUMA LC did not finance the development of the system means that its stake in its future is less certain. However, the knowledge base for each law may represent a stable, sanctioned collection of knowledge that will be of great benefit to a wide audience of users. An institution such as the DUMA could be uniquely positioned to make the investment in tools of this nature, if it has the political will to do so. The best course for Russian Word/Cogitum could be to persuade the DUMA as a whole to adopt SenseViewer as a standard, and secure financing for additional development and (especially!) training. Opening up the standard for the development of third party tools and linkages with existing products such as Lotus Notes, and existing projects such as the Finnish Parliament’s XML initiative, could speed its adoption by other bodies.

The SenseViewer project has pointed the way towards a new generation of web-based e-government knowledge management systems. How long and in what directions its development will continue will provide an exciting sequel to this paper.

William McHenry joined the Department of Management in the College of Business Administration of the University of Akron as an associate professor in Fall 2000. Prior to this, he taught for 15 years at the McDonough School of Business, Georgetown University. He received his PhD in MIS from the University of Arizona in 1985. His articles have appeared in The Communications of the ACM, The Journal of MIS, Information Processing

and Management, The Journal of the Association of Information Systems, The Communications of the Association of Information Systems, and in other journals, books, and conference proceedings. Dr. McHenry’s research has long concerned international diffusion and adoption of information systems, with a strong focus on Russia and the former Soviet republics. His research interests include global Internet diffusion, knowledge management, and information retrieval systems. In September 1999, Dr. McHenry gave invited testimony on the Y2K problem in Russia before the U.S. Senate Special Committee on the Year 2000 Technology Problem.
