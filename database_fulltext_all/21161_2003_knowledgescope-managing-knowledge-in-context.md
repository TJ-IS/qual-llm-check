# KnowledgeScope: managing knowledge in context

M. Millie Kwan <sup>a</sup>, P. Balasubramanian <sup>b,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup>School of Business, University of Hong Kong, Pokfulam Road, Hong Kong, PR China

<sup>b</sup>Information Systems Department, School of Management, Boston University, 595 Commonwealth Avenue, Boston, MA 02215, USA

Accepted 29 May 2002

## Abstract

Knowledge repositories have been implemented in many organizations, but they often suffer from non-use. This research considers two key design factors that cause non-use: the extra burden on users to document knowledge in the repository, and the lack of a standard knowledge structure that facilitates knowledge sharing among users with different perspectives. We propose a design of a knowledge management system called KnowledgeScope that addresses these problems through (1) an integrated workflow support capability that captures and retrieves knowledge as an organizational process proceeds, i.e., within the context in which it is created and used, and (2) a process meta-model that organizes that knowledge and context in a knowledge repository. In this paper, we describe this design and report the results from implementing it in a real-life organization. <sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Knowledge repositories; Knowledge management; Process modeling; Workflow management; Organizational memory

## 1. Introduction

In today’s fast changing business environment, workers often need to learn from each other’s experience to function, to keep current, and to innovate. Rookies need orientations. Old-timers need refreshers and updates. Everyone needs ideas and methods to cope with new situations and to improve current practice. Making knowledge available to the right worker at the right time and the right place is vital for building and sustaining an organization’s competencies. According to Delphi Consulting Group’s 1998 survey, 51% of the respondents reported an active effort in knowledge management in their company [18]. A survey conducted by IDC in 2000 found that 50% of Fortune 500 companies plan to invest in knowledge management systems, and in companies with over 500 employees, this figure is higher [19]. Consulting companies are offering knowledge management engagement services while software vendors are touting various technologies for supporting knowledge management [33]. Much of this effort has been focused on developing knowledge repositories, which typically store documents with knowledge embedded in them, such as memos, reports, presentations, or they may be discussion databases in which participants record their own experiences on an issue and react to others’ comments [17]. The intent is to externalize the experiential knowledge gained by individuals who performed the organization’s activities, store it into repositories, and make it accessible across the organization via the corporate intranet.

Through knowledge repositories, knowledge sharing is enabled across space and time, and experiential knowledge becomes an organizational asset that stays within the organization even after the individuals who created the knowledge had left.

Early experience, however, showed that few workers contribute knowledge into repositories, or search for needed knowledge from them [15]. Three key factors have often been mentioned to account for the non-use of repositories. First, contributing knowledge to a repository amounts to an extra documentation task required of workers. Unless workers perceive an immediate benefit to them, extra work cannot be justified. Second, knowledge sharing requires a common mental framework between the source and the recipient, but people from different backgrounds with different interests have different knowledge structures and perspectives. Furthermore, knowledge repository designs typically focus on storing ‘content’, i.e., artifacts such as presentations, references such as best practices, and lessons learned. This content tends to provide little of the process context, such as who created the content, what task he was performing, when, where, and why. Without contextual information, workers cannot fully understand the rationale nor trust the source of the knowledge and therefore decide not to adopt it [3,40]. Thus, the lack of a standard, context-rich knowledge structure that can be understood and accepted by different types of users leads to non-use [17]. Third, a knowledge sharing culture generally does not exist in organizations. To motivate employees to use a knowledge repository, they must be given proper incentives to contribute, share and adopt new knowledge [17]. Another approach would be to create a nurturing environment for communities of practice to emerge and thrive [31]. These managerial strategies are beyond the scope of this paper and will not be further explored here. The system design proposed in this paper does not presume any particular management strategy with regard to knowledge sharing.

Drawing upon the experience of knowledge management projects as well as prior research on knowledge sharing, we created a design of a knowledge management system called KnowledgeScope that addresses the first two problems described above. KnowledgeScope manages knowledge in context through (1) an integrated workflow support capability that captures and retrieves knowledge as an organizational process proceeds, i.e., within the context in which it is created and used, and (2) a process meta-model that organizes that knowledge and context in a knowledge repository. In this paper, we describe this design and report our experience in implementing it in a real life organization. The organization of the paper is as follows. Section 2 develops a set of design objectives for knowledge management systems based on prior research. Section 3 reviews existing knowledge sharing technologies according to the design objectives. Based on the design objectives, we develop a process meta-model for structuring knowledge in a repository in Section 4. In Section 5, we present the KnowledgeScope architecture. Section 6 describes an implementation of KnowledgeScope in a real-life organization. Section 7 discusses the results of the case study. Section 8 is the conclusion.

## 2. Rationale

Many researchers of knowledge management have focused on capturing and sharing experiential knowledge gained from performing organizational processes [8,16,22,31,32]. The rationale is that this knowledge is the source of the core competence of an organization. For instance, Stein and Zwass [36] defined semantic knowledge as organizational practices contained in handbooks, manuals, standard operating procedures and the techno-scientific knowledge assimilated by the organization, and episodic knowledge as engineering work-arounds and contextually situated decisions and their outcomes. They believed that much of the semantic knowledge is rather broadly available to the world in which the organization competes so that ‘‘the core competence of an organization [really] derives from episodic memory and from an evolving (through organizational specialization) semantic base of knowledge.’’ Referring to standard operating procedures in organizations as canonical practices and noncanonical practices as those created by workers when they are faced with new situations, Brown and Duguid [8] claimed that it is the noncanonical practices that determine the success or failure of organizations. Thus, for a knowledge management initiative to effectively contribute to the performance of an organization, we believe that the scope of a knowledge management application should be defined around an organizational process and the knowledge to be managed in such an application should include semantic knowledge and episodic knowledge of the process. We categorize this knowledge into three types based on Stein and Zwass’s definition: process knowledge is the standard operating procedures of the process (specialized to the organization and is considered a component of semantic knowledge); case knowledge is the instances of executing standard operating procedures in special contexts (corresponding to episodic knowledge); and knowledge resources are the techno-scientific knowledge used in executing the process. We now state Objective 1 as follows.

Objective 1. A knowledge management system should organize knowledge around organizational processes, each process being the scope of an application. Each application should contain process knowledge, case knowledge as well as knowledge resources.

In his working definition of knowledge, Davenport and Prusak [16] specifically included contextual information as a component of knowledge. Empirical evidence has shown that contextual information is crucial for knowledge sharing and reuse. In field observations of the usage of an organizational memory system that stores knowledge about UNIX problems, Ackerman [3] found that users chose not to use the solution provided by the system because they could not determine the appropriateness of the solution without knowing the context in which the solution has been applied, such as the size of the UNIX installation and the organizational setting. In a study of the use of email in knowledge transfer, Watts [40] found that management consultants are more likely to adopt advice provided by a colleague if the credibility of the source can be determined from the contextual information included in the email. Tyre and von Hippel [38] found that learning occurs through people interacting in context. They believed that each setting presents a particular mix of resources (such as people, tools, and events) that can be used to help solve a problem, and the way in which such resources are used will vary with the particular setting. Referring to a study of copy machine technicians as a community of practice, Brown and Duguid [8] suggested that when technicians solved problems by constructing stories that ‘‘interweaves generalities about ‘this model’ with particularities about ‘this site’ and ‘this machine’’’, they were considering both the social and the physical context of the problem.

Context may mean different information for participants playing different roles in an organizational process. Within a functional group such as human resource managers, or across roles within a process such as the sales clerks, order clerks, warehouse clerks, supervisors, and customers in an order fulfillment process, communities of practice may develop [8,42], each having its own shared frame of reference—a shared understanding of events and ideas, a shared vocabulary and shared knowledge [9,11,44]. Each community looks at a process from a different perspective, has different knowledge requirements and therefore defines context by their own interest in the process. For example, a human resources manager may be interested in employee skills inventory, whereas a supervisor in operations may be interested in workload balancing. Thus, we believe that contextual information that accommodates the different perspectives of different roles in a process should be included in a knowledge repository.

Objective 2. Contextual information that accommodates the different perspectives of different roles in the process should be included in a knowledge management application.

To provide context to the knowledge that will later be shared by others, we need to document the context in addition to the new knowledge generated from the experience. This is an extra task required of the process participants. Without a direct benefit or a reward to compensate for this ‘documentation burden’, few workers would be motivated to contribute. Failing to reach a critical mass of useful content, the knowledge repository would fall into disuse [21]. This phenomenon has been observed in groupware and some researchers suggested that groupware features would fare better if integrated with features that support individual activity [12,21]. Thus, we believe that in order to encourage use, knowledge repositories should be integrated into the workflow of organizational processes so that the knowledge is automatically captured when it is created and presented when it is needed [20,37].

Objective 3. A knowledge management application should be integrated into the workflow of the process to reduce the burden of documentation and retrieval.

We have developed three design objectives for a knowledge management system. In the next section, we will review existing knowledge management technologies against these objectives.

## 3. Knowledge sharing systems

The most common knowledge management technology is the knowledge repository, therefore, we will first review their design against our design objectives. Then, given that Objective 1 calls for a processoriented design, we will review several process memory systems. Finally, because the concept of organizational memory information systems is closely related to knowledge management systems, we will also review the relevant literature in that area.

## 3.1. Knowledge repositories

Most knowledge repositories are built around information objects classified by subject area [5,6,29]. For example, Arthur Andersen’s Global Best Practices knowledge base is a repository of highly distilled research concerning the most effective ways of performing particular processes. The content of this knowledge base is organized using a process classification scheme that categorizes an enterprise’s activities into seven broad operating processes and six broad management processes. These are in turn broken down into a hierarchy of subprocesses. Materials under each process are grouped into 10 categories, such as descriptions of best practices, world-class performance metrics, presentations, diagnostic tools, etc. This kind of repository is designed to be reference libraries. The information from different sources is not integrated into the work process for the user. The capture and retrieval of knowledge from a business process is also separated from the business process itself. As a result, users perceive the knowledge capture and retrieval to be an extra step required of them for which they may or may not receive a benefit. Furthermore, specialists called ‘knowledge stewards’ are assigned to remove the context of the source materials to make them more generally applicable, or in some cases, package them into ‘knowledge objects’ to facilitate reuse [5,29]. Because these knowledge repositories cover many organizational processes, the knowledge is made available to many different functional groups in different processes. These users have different perspectives and as a result, the standard knowledge structure constructed by a small knowledge management team or by knowledge stewards may not be accepted by the users [17].

## 3.2. Process memory systems

Project Memory captures project histories that can subsequently be retrieved and applied to current problems [41]. The system design was based on a generalizable object-oriented model which has five discrete classes: projects, users, events, meetings, and documents, describing the people, temporal events (meetings and agenda items within meetings), and archival documents that are created within a project or support some aspect of the project. It does not satisfy our design objectives for several reasons. First, by focusing on one particular type of process, i.e., projects, and characterizing a project as a series of meetings, Project Memory cannot adequately represent other types of processes and tasks, such as functional tasks that transform inputs into outputs. Second, the system does not provide different perspectives for different roles. All users are represented as members of the project at hand without regard to their roles, organizational relationships, and affiliations. Third, Project Memory records project histories but provides no support for project designs, i.e., it captures case knowledge but not process knowledge.

Contrary to Project Memory, the Process Handbook [28] captures only the design of organizational processes, i.e., process knowledge. Process Handbook contains descriptions of different types of processes from different organizations. The processes are represented and classified using an approach that embodies the object-oriented concepts of inheritance and abstraction. Workflow management systems (WFMS) [20,23] capture both process designs (as workflow templates) and histories of process instances. However, many WFMS discard exceptions, which are actually a valuable source of episodic knowledge. WFMS implementations also tend to focus on facilitating operational efficiency in repetitive processes rather than supporting knowledge management. Knowledge resources are readily available but search and retrieval capabilities for case knowledge are not readily accessible to the users working in the process. Facilities for maintaining process knowledge are usually available only to the system administrator.

Process memory systems often organize knowledge from a particular perspective. Many systems, including Project Memory and Process Handbook, are designed from a functional perspective of the process, i.e., process knowledge is organized as sequences or hierarchies of tasks or events. Other system designs use rationale to structure and integrate all other documentation and process information. For example, gIBIS [13] organizes the rationale of a design process using an argumentation-based knowledge structure that defines knowledge exchange in a discussion into three categories: issues, positions, and arguments. gIBIS captures only the content but not the context in which the discussion occurs, such as the roles of the participants, and the tasks from which the issues arise. Thoughtflow [7] organizes process rationale in the form of an audit trail of goals and decisions. It captures the context of the decisions—roles, resources, schedules, and so on. However, both systems support only one perspective of the process.

## 3.3. Organizational memory information systems

Stein and Zwass [36] proposed a framework for organizational memory information system (OMIS) that includes four subsystems. The integrative subsystem supports sharing of organizational knowledge across time and space. The adaptive subsystem pro vides access to knowledge about the external environment. The goal attainment subsystem tracks the progress of organizational activities towards the organization’s goals and involves maintaining not only process histories but also the analysis of the process performance in regards to the goals. The pattern maintenance subsystem supports the preserva tion and development of human resources over time. In addition, five mnemonic functions are required to support the four subsystems: knowledge acquisition, retention, maintenance, search, and retrieval. Another study by Morrison [30] explored OMIS data and process requirements and salient implementation issues. Although these authors cited various information systems to demonstrate different functionalities ascribed in their frameworks, no one OMIS has been cited to cover most of the functions.

One example of OMIS is Answer Garden [4]. Answer Garden contains questions and answers categorized hierarchically by subject. The answers do not provide contextual information and users found it difficult to apply them to solve their problems [3]. Answer Garden also contains a database of subject experts. If a user cannot find the answer to his question from the repository, the system can select an expert from the experts database based on certain attributes, such as area of expertise, schedule, location, etc., and direct the question to him. The expert answers the question and updates the knowledge repository. Answer Garden was not integrated into the workflow of the users and the experts. Field observations showed that both users and experts were concerned about the extra time required of the experts to answer the questions and update the repository because the work is not a part of their job.

Other examples of OMIS include Project Memory (described in Section 3.2) and the KnowMore project [1,2]. KnowMore provides intelligent assistance for knowledge-intensive tasks by proactively providing context-sensitive information. KnowMore uses the concept of information assistants to integrate workflow support with an OMIS. Queries for relevant knowledge objects and actions are automatically executed and presented to the worker during the execution of the relevant task within a business process. The Know-More design focuses on retrieval of knowledge resources but not process knowledge and case knowledge.

Linger et al. [27] proposed a three-layer framework for a dynamic OMIS that includes a conceptual layer to store the concepts inherent in an activity, a process layer to store experiences of performing that activity, and a pragmatic layer to support the actual activity. Thus, this design framework captures case knowledge through the process layer, process knowledge through the pragmatic layer, and some knowledge resources through the conceptual layer. However, the need for contextual information that accommodates different perspectives of different roles in the process was not considered in this framework.

Wang [39] proposed a modeling method for building OMIS using an object-oriented approach. This method groups knowledge into five classes of objects: goal, document, cognizance, episode, and mnemonic instrument, which can be integrated over time, space, and medium. Although the design includes both semantic and episodic knowledge, there is no explicit treatment or specification for context and the different perspectives of different roles in an organization.

Our review of knowledge repositories, process memory systems, and organizational memory information systems shows that these technologies currently do not adequately satisfy our design objectives. Thus, in this research, we undertake to construct a knowledge management system called Knowledge-Scope. In the following section, we will develop a model called Knowledge-In-Context (KIC) for structuring knowledge in a knowledge repository. The design of this model will be according to Objectives 1 and 2. Then in Section 5, we will describe the KnowledgeScope design, which applies the KIC model and also integrates workflow with the knowledge repository according to Objective 3.

## 4. Knowledge-in-Context model

In accordance with Objective 1, a knowledge management system should organize knowledge around organization processes. For each process, the process designs, process instances, and knowledge resources are to be captured, stored, and retrieved from the repository. We propose to use a process meta-model as a classification scheme to organize this content. A process meta-model contains the process elements and relationships that are of interest to the users who occupy different roles in the process. According to Objective 2, the process meta-model should also be segmented into perspectives that the different roles would use to view the process.

To develop this process meta-model, we first reviewed the literature in process modeling and workflow modeling to identify an inventory of the elements and relationships of a process model. Then we reviewed prior research and found two frameworks that sliced a process model into perspectives. Sowa and Zachman’s framework [35] for information systems architecture analyzes a process in terms of six abstractions: who, what, when, where, why, and how. They detailed a process meta-model in entity-relationship modeling notation for three of the abstractions— what, how, and where. But in their opinion, ‘‘few formalisms, at least in the traditional data processing community, are available for the who, when and why column cells. Some formalisms exist in other disciplines, or even in some of the very specialized (but less generally understood) data processing circles. But their general unavailability causes the new [who, when and why] cell descriptions to be more theoretical and less empirical.’’

On the other hand, Curtis et al. [14] suggested that four perspectives are commonly used in process models: functional, behavioral, organizational, and informational. The functional perspective answers the question: what are the tasks performed and why? It describes the tasks, and the goals that the tasks are intended to achieve. The informational perspective describes the information objects that are used and produced by the tasks in the process, and the relationships between them. The organizational perspective answers the question: who, where, and with what resources are the tasks performed? It describes the roles, actors, and other resources that are employed to perform the tasks. The behavioral perspective answers the question: when and how are the tasks performed? It describes the sequencing, control flow, and conditions that govern the execution of processes. It also shows how resources and information objects are brought together to perform tasks in a process. We have chosen to use Curtis et al.’s [14] perspectives because they are likely to map more closely with the perspectives of the participants of the process.

Although Curtis et al. [14] provided examples of process modeling methods for each perspective, they did not operationalize the four perspectives and show how the elements and relationships in those methods map to them. Thus, we proceeded to construct a process meta-model bearing the four perspectives. Using the elements of the process models identified in the first step, we categorized them according to the definition of Curtis et al.’s [14] perspectives and checked the elements and perspectives that are represented in each method. We integrated the results of these comparisons, constructed a process meta-model, and refined it by applying it to some real-life processes. (Details of this process can be found in Refs. [24,25].) We call the final version of the process metamodel the Knowledge-In-Context model.

Sections 4.1–4.4 below describe the KIC model from the four perspectives and the objects and relationships in each perspective are illustrated in Figs. 1 –5 using the Object Modeling Technique (OMT) [34]. In the OMT notation, a rectangle represents a class—a group of objects with similar properties. A link is a conceptual or physical connection between objects. When there are a group of links with a common structure and common semantics they are termed as an association. A line represents an association within the OMT notation. Each class shown in the OMT diagram may relate to a single instance or multiple instances of an associated class. This is specified using a line terminator with a number or set of intervals, such as ‘‘1’’ (exactly one), ‘‘1 + ’’ (one or more), ‘‘3–5’’ (three to five, inclusive). In addition, there are special line terminators to indicate a certain common multiplicity values. A solid ball is the OMT notation for ‘‘many,’’ meaning zero or more. A hollow ball indicates ‘‘optional,’’ meaning zero or none. Another concept covered by the OMT technique is aggregation. Aggregation, represented by a diamond, is used to describe how objects represent assemblies of other objects. Generalizations and inheritance abstractions are represented using a triangle. In addition, we use dotted lines to show the boundaries of each perspective in the KIC model. In the description, the terms in italics define objects and relationships shown in Figs. 1–5.

## 4.1. Functional perspective

The functional perspective describes the view of managers and strategists (see Fig. 1). At the strategic level, organizational goals are broken down into lower-level goals, resulting in a goal hierarchy. At the operational level, a low-level goal is operationalized into organizational procedures designed to achieve the goal. A procedure consists of procedure steps. The functional perspective is concerned with procedure steps that transform input information objects into output information objects. We call this type of procedure step a functional task. For example, the functional task ‘receive order’ is defined by getting customer requirements and producing a customer order in the format required by the order fulfillment process. Functional tasks are traditionally modeled using the data flow diagramming technique [43].

## 4.2. Informational perspective

Most participants, from managers, frontline supervisors and workers, to customers, are involved in one way or another with the information objects in a process. Knowledge repositories, therefore, are always focused on storing information objects. The informational perspective describes the information objects that are used and produced in the process and the relationships between them (see Fig. 2). An information object contains information that is manipulated, created, destroyed, or referenced in a process. It may be an operational database object such as ‘supplier address’, a document such as a memo, or a discussion forum. (The current KIC model contains only these three information object types. However, the model is extensible to include other types of information objects.) The informational perspective describes information objects in terms of attributes such as their creation/update date and status, as well as the information object–information object relationships, such as ‘‘versioning’ between documents, and ‘classification’ of documents.

## 4.3. Organizational perspective

The organizational perspective describes how the organization configures its resources to perform a business process. There are two interrelated parts: (1) the resource inventory, which consists of collections of actors, tools, and information objects, the relationships between them, and the rules that govern the deployment of these resources, and (2) the organizational structure which describes the roles, organizational units, and the relationships between them (see Fig. 3).

A resource is an entity that is involved in the execution of a task. We distinguish three types of resources. An actor is a resource that can perform a task. An actor may be a human or a computer. A tool is a resource that is employed by an actor in performing a procedure step. Examples are a payroll system or a spreadsheet program. An information object (defined above) is also a resource. Resources need to be allocated to accomplish procedure steps. Each resource contains the rules that govern its deployment, such as its work schedule and authorizations. The collection of resource objects and the deployment relationships between them, i.e., which actor uses which tools, which actors uses which information objects and which tools can manipulate which information objects, form the resource inventory of the organization.

A role is a placeholder for an actor. An actor may take on multiple roles and a role may be assigned to multiple actors. An organization is a collection of roles governed by role–role relationships. Examples of role – role relationships are ‘supervisor’, ‘substitute’, and ‘delegate’ relationships. A process may involve roles from internal organizations such as accounting department as well as external organizations such as customer or supplier. Organizations also have organization – organization relationships (i.e., interorganizational relationships) such as customer– supplier, alliance partner relationships. Roles, organizations, and their relationships establish the organizational structure that assigns actors to procedure steps.

The organizational perspective is of interest to many different types of users. For example, managers, including strategists and human resource specialists, may be interested in organizational structure and resource planning, development and utilization. IS department personnel are interested in the management of the information resource from this perspective.

## 4.4. Behavioral perspective

The behavioral perspective describes the sequencing, control flow, and conditions that govern the way organizational and informational elements are brought together to perform the procedures as described in the functional perspective (see Fig. 4). A procedure is a collection of triggers and procedure steps. Each procedure step specifies a method by which it can be accomplished, i.e., which role is responsible, which role should perform it, which actors should take which roles, which information objects are manipulated in the step, and hence, which tools should be used. We distinguish between three types of procedure steps: functional tasks, workflow tasks, and knowledge management tasks. A functional task inputs information objects, transforms them into outputs which are also information objects. A workflow task, such as sorting, copying, prioritizing, and data transfer, inputs information objects and uses them to facilitate the efficient and effective use of resources but are not functionally required for the process. A knowledge management task, such as doing after action reviews [6], retrospective analyses [10] and discussions, involves searching for (inputs) and/or contributing to (outputs) the knowledge of the process (represented as information objects).

We distinguish three types of triggers. An eventtype trigger may be set by a temporal event such as March 15, 10 am or an external event such as the beginning or end of a procedure step. A conditionbased trigger is set by the condition of a resource such as the type of purchase order or its priority. A concurrency trigger is set by the state that a procedure step is being performed. When a trigger is set, it causes one or more procedure steps to be executed.

The behavioral perspective is a workflow model for describing standard operating procedures, i.e., process designs, and for recording process instances because it contains all the information necessary for executing a process—its steps, timing, and resources required. This knowledge is of most interest to process analysts and operational managers. Novice frontline workers may also consult process designs if they are not sure how to proceed. In unusual situations, frontline supervisors and workers may consult different process designs and similar past process instances for ways to handle the situation. Finally, knowledge resources (i.e., input information objects) are delivered at the right time in the right place to the right frontline worker based on the specifications in the behavioral perspective.

The behavioral perspective organizes process knowledge in the form of standard operating procedures, as well as case knowledge as instances of process execution. These are supported by contextual information from the functional and organizational perspectives. Thus, the behavioral perspective allows different procedures to be defined under different organizational contexts, and different process instances to be recorded under different contexts. This will allow users to find solutions to exceptional situations by searching for similar contexts. Different roles may view the process from different perspectives, and will have access in the other perspectives as the context. For example, an executive concerned about the goal performance would be looking at the functional perspective. Viewing the organizational context will inform him of the contribution of each organizational unit to the goal. Finally, knowledge resources are organized in the informational perspective.

## 5. KnowledgeScope architecture

In this section, we first describe the Knowledge-Scope architecture, showing how a workflow management capability is integrated with a knowledge repository structured according to the KIC model. This design is in accordance with Objectives 2 and 3. Then, we describe how KnowledgeScope would support knowledge management in an organizational process. Finally, we compare the KnowledgeScope design with other knowledge management systems.

The KnowledgeScope architecture is shown in Fig. 6 in four layers: the interface, application, control processing, and repository. We describe the components and their interactions below. The terms in italics refer to components shown in the diagram.

The knowledge repository contains three types of knowledge as required by Objective 1: the process designs database contains process knowledge, process instances database contains case knowledge; and knowledge resources, which are the information objects used in the process, are stored in the documents database, the discussions databases, and the operational databases.

The workflow management capability required by Objective 3 is illustrated by the five components: process modeling tool, workflow client application, workflow processor, process designs database, and process instances database. The process designs database stores process specifications based on the process elements and relationships in the KIC model, i.e., the goals, tasks and procedures, actors and roles, information objects, etc., and according to the four perspectives. The specifications in the behavioral perspective, called process templates, are used by the workflow processor to drive workflow. Process specifications are entered into the process designs database through the process modeling tool.

Workers start a new process instance by selecting a process template through a workflow client application. The workflow processor instantiates the selected process template, and records the new process instance in the process instances database. The workflow processor has a scheduler that schedules procedure steps for all process instances currently in progress. It presents the appropriate steps and associated input information objects (documents, discussions, or operational data) to the workflow client application by invoking a document management application, a discussion forum application, or other external workflowenabled applications, such as a payroll application, as appropriate. As a process instance progresses, the various components of the process template including the goals, the actors assigned, resources used, the actual start and end times of tasks, as well as the outputs logged into the documents database, discussion database, and operational databases, are instantiated and recorded into the process instances database, thereby capturing the process and context. In case of exception, the workflow processor may also invoke the KIC application (see below) to suggest relevant process templates and past process instances or to allow the user to search for such knowledge. Users may then modify instances on the fly by switching to a different template (new or existing). Thus, exceptional process instances and how they had been handled are also recorded in context.

Fig. 6. KnowledgeScope architecture.

The KIC application provides search and navigational facilities for viewing the contents of the process designs database and the process instances database from the perspectives of the KIC model. Process templates and process instances can be searched using the attributes of the objects in the KIC model. KnowledgeScope also allows searching by keywords for documents in the documents database and for discussions in the discussions database. The navigational facility is designed and constructed by treating objects in the KIC model as nodes and relationships as links. With both facilities, a user can use a keyword search to look for specific instances of an object in the KIC model, say, an actor, and then view it from the organizational perspective by traversing relationships such as the roles he takes, the organizations that he belongs, and so on. In particular, a user can look up relevant past process instances to deal with exceptions. For example, a new state regulation has been put into effect and a worker has to modify a contract to take the law’s impact into account. He would first search for similar contracts using attributes of the information object ‘contract’. He would then use the navigational interface to view each contract’s context, such as when and where the contract was written (traversing links in the behavioral perspective), the author’s background and organizational relationships (traversing links in the organizational perspective), and the supplementary data/documents (traversing links in the informational perspective) to determine the applicability of those contracts.

The KnowledgeScope interface provides a standard, intuitive, and easy-to-use interface for the applications. This is important because even though a knowledge management application is defined within the scope of a process, a knowledge management system may be supporting many processes across the rest of the organization and even its suppliers and customers. A standard interface would facilitate knowledge sharing across these communities.

Fig. 7 illustrates a scenario of how Knowledge-Scope may be implemented to support a business process. In the process planning stage, workers prepare a process plan, including their roles, their task schedules and deliverables, in KnowledgeScope, either by instantiating existing process template(s) or creating new process template(s). Based on this process plan, KnowledgeScope provides workflow services during the work-in-progress stage to help workers coordinate their work, presenting knowledge resources automatically through the knowledge applications when called for by the process plan and capturing events and outputs into the repository as the process proceeds. During this stage, workers may perform some knowledge management activities such as after action reviews [6] or participate in discussions of issues within the process instance or other instances. When new situations arise, workers may modify the process plan, again either by instantiating existing process template(s) or creating new process template(s). At the process completion stage, workers again may perform some knowledge management activities such as retrospective analyses to summarize events and produce lessons learned from the experience [10]. Throughout the process and afterward, workers may retrieve knowledge resources through the knowledge applications from the repository and view the process plans, the work in progress, as well as the final analyses of the process instances—all within the context of the process. In this way, KnowledgeScope provides a virtual environment for the community of process participants from distant locations where they can see each other’s work and discuss issues related to their work, i.e., share knowledge. Finally, at the process evaluation stage, process templates and process instances are retrieved from the repository for evaluation, resulting in new process templates and knowledge resources, i.e., new knowledge about the process.

![](/api/attachments/T5HTVS47/fulltext/images/1367eec88f85801df2658f107c421c418eab6d5e2b043e25fb5b45c4d6240cf9.jpg)  
Fig. 7. Using KnowledgeScope to support a business process [26].

We have presented the KnowledgeScope architecture. It differs from other knowledge repositories in that it presents knowledge within the context of the process in which it is produced. It stores not only knowledge resources employed in organizational processes, but also process knowledge and case knowledge. It differs from Project Memory which provides only case knowledge but not process knowledge. Compared to workflow management systems, it adds the ability to manage documents and retrieve and present process and case knowledge to workflow participants, not just system administrators.

In terms of Stein and Zwass’s framework for OMIS [36], KnowledgeScope provides all the mnemonic functions, i.e., acquisition, retention, maintenance, search and retrieval. Acquisition is achieved by capturing knowledge and context as work is performed through the workflow system. Retention is achieved by using KIC to structure the workflow model base and the workflow transactions database as well as using document management functions such as archiving and version control for documents in the document base. Maintenance is achieved during the process evaluation stage in the KnowledgeScope process support environment described in Fig. 7. Search and retrieval initiated by the user are provided by the knowledge presentation system and the document management system. The workflow system in KnowledgeScope is also capable of delivering to the user the knowledge resources needed to perform a task at the right time in the right place.

In terms of the four subsystems in Stein and Zwass’s framework [36], KnowledgeScope allows users access to both process histories and current process instances in the workflow transactions database from anywhere in the corporation via its intranet. Thus, it supports process memory integrated over time and space, i.e., the integrative subsystem. It provides the goal attainment subsystem functions through the functional perspective where the goal hierarchy and corresponding task hierarchy are represented. It provides for the pattern maintenance subsystem functions through the organizational perspective where individuals as well as organizational units are represented. Its support for the adaptive subsystem function is limited to the support of adapting process models by users for their own process instances. Analyzing the patterns of the changes made by the users during the process evaluation stage of Fig. 7 can aid in adapting the process models as the environment changes.

## 6. Case study

We implemented KnowledgeScope to support knowledge management for several processes at a global telecommunications company which we will refer to as Comco. In this case study, we describe an application called Real estate Authorization Letter (RAL). (A more detailed report of this case will be published in Ref. [26].) Comco has many offices, factories, and campuses worldwide. RAL is a process by which the authorization for new space, new construction, lease renewal, change in the configuration of a building/space, or disposal of space, is done for Comco’s business units (called customers). About 50 to 60 real estate strategists worldwide manage about 400 such projects each year, documenting customer’s requirements, the terms and conditions of the lease, rent rates, the construction costs over the time of the lease, etc. In addition to the strategists, the process also involves other parties at different stages, including landlords, lawyers, brokers, economics analysts, engineering design and construction, movers, contractors, and property managers. These parties were geographically dispersed, so communication, coordination, and knowledge sharing were not well facilitated prior to the knowledge management initiative. Knowledge-Scope was implemented with the following objectives:

(1) To provide users with easy access to knowledge, particularly emphasizing the capture of project histories so that strategists can follow up on tasks started by others who had left or are on vacation, or they can review similar projects for ideas and insights to handle unfamiliar situations such as a particular customer, a region, or lease terms.

(2) To improve customer relations and project coordination by providing all parties involved in the process with easy access to project status and other details.

(3) To capture project histories for process analysis and redesign.

(4) To engender a community by enabling process participants to communicate project and process issues and exchange experiences within their workspace.

A pilot team of experienced strategists developed a set of standard project templates for dealing with different types of RAL projects. For example, one type of project is renewing a lease with no changes to the terms and conditions. Another type of project is to change the configuration (layouts, furniture, etc.) of the space. Each project template provides a predefined set of tasks, their dependencies, timeframes, roles, and documents for a type of RAL project. When a lease is about to expire, a legacy financial application called PROMPT will trigger KnowledgeScope to create a new RAL project (a process instance) using some basic project information such as name, description, location, lease type, and business unit code. KnowledgeScope then notifies the strategist manager in charge of the region in which the lease is in effect. The strategist manager assigns the project to one of the strategists in his region, who will then apply project templates from the repository or create new ones for the rest of the project as appropriate. An RAL project may last over a year or several years and its requirements may change a lot over time, even from a simple lease renewal to constructing a new building. Thus, KnowledgeScope allows a strategist to add, modify, or delete any of the project components (tasks, roles, actors, documents, etc.) in his project and even switch to a different project template midstream.

After a project has been created, KnowledgeScope keeps track of its progress according to the project template. With its knowledge of all the projects in progress in the system, KnowledgeScope provides for each user a list of current tasks to be performed, including links to the contextual information and knowledge resources for each task. When exceptional events occur, KnowledgeScope notifies the appropriate roles, providing them with links to the tasks, knowledge resources, and contextual information as needed.

Fig. 8 shows a project summary screen on KnowledgeScope. The top left-hand corner shows that the project being displayed is identified as ‘475 South Street’. At the top right-hand corner is a link to a discussion forum associated with this project. The file tabs are shortcuts to important views into the project. The ‘Description’ tab links to the attributes of the project, such as its strategic goal and project description. The ‘Tasks’ tab links to the functional perspective—with the procedure steps, when they should be performed, their dependencies, and informational objects associated with the steps. The ‘Team, Role and People’ tab links to a list of the members on the project team with their roles, organizational affiliations, contact information, and background. The ‘Reference Material’ tab links to a list of the information objects that are created or used in this project. The ‘Issues’ tab, the ‘Notes’ tab and the ‘Lessons Learned’ tab are short cuts to three types of information objects of special interest to the users. All the file tabs are context-sensitive. As the user drills down a project into the task hierarchy, the task schedules, roles, people, reference materials, and even discussion forums are displayed in context. Thus, at the project level, all members of the project team will appear in the list of ‘Team, Role and People’, and at a task level, only those team members involved in the task will be shown. The main body of the screen shows the beginning of the content of the pages from each of the file tabs.

Fig. 8. KnowledgeScope summary screen for a RAL project (data alluding to Comco have been blocked out).

KnowledgeScope also provides a searching capability for finding specific items of interest. For example, a user may be looking for a project in which the customer requirements include 100 Mbps data transport capability, or one that involves moving workers across states. She can search for requirement documents that contain these criteria. Once the relevant documents are retrieved, she can use the navigational interface to view the project context such as the timeline, the project location, or issues, to see if they are applicable to her situation, if any adjustments need to be made, or to make contact with an experienced strategist.

KnowledgeScope was implemented on a Microsoft platform. The user workstations were Windowsbased, running Microsoft Office, Exchange, and Internet Explorer. The server was built on a Windows NT Server with Internet Information Server (IIS). The process designs database and process instances database were built on SQL Server. The documents database was implemented on the NT file system. Document uploads/downloads were done using PERL scripts. The knowledge applications were implemented using Active Server Pages, interfacing with SQL Server via ODBC, and ActiveX components with the NT file system. Simple workflow functions were implemented using stored procedures (TSQL). The development team included a project manager, a sponsor, three user representatives, a developer, an analyst and a research advisor. The development project lasted 9 months and training and implementation took another 2 months. Rapid prototyping was used as the system development strategy.

## 7. Discussion

We conducted an evaluation of KnowledgeScope at the end of the first 6 months of use by participants of the RAL process. We collected usage statistics from the KnowledgeScope server access log during a 60-day period. There were 20 users logged in during the period, and the median daily usage is 182 min. In particular, usage among telecommuting users who were using dial-up access was low as a result of slow response times. We also collected qualitative feedback through 20–30 min unstructured telephone interviews with 12 users. The interview focused on users’ experience in using the content of Knowledge-Scope.

User feedback showed that sharing of process knowledge, case knowledge, and knowledge resources had been facilitated by KnowledgeScope. Examples are detailed below.

## 7.1. Process knowledge

. The pilot team created project templates based on their experience. These were reviewed and used by other users who provided suggestions for improvements. In particular, accommodation for differences in specific contexts (including different geographical locations, strategic intents, and customers) was made. Thus, a process of managing knowledge of the standard operating procedures was initiated as a result of implementing KnowledgeScope. It is expected that as KnowledgeScope captured more projects over time, these templates will be reviewed and updated by a user group.

. Strategist managers and coaches used the project templates to teach new strategists.

. Strategist managers and construction managers monitored overall process status and performance by visually inspecting across projects.

## 7.2. Case knowledge

The purpose of case knowledge was to share prior experience in dealing with special circumstances. While users regarded this as a potential benefit, only one strategist came across a project he had not done before and had looked at how another strategist had worded his requirements document and planned the project in that situation. Users generally felt competent to do their work without a need to search for prior projects. Rather, the flexibility of being able to apply different templates (based on their own knowledge) as the project evolves was sufficient for dealing with exceptions. However, users found that sharing of information about current projects have been useful in other ways:

. Strategists reviewed the project history and status on KnowledgeScope before accepting transfer of project responsibility between each other.

. Asset managers working on multiple location projects were greatly facilitated by the ready access of project information. One user talked about a ‘‘consolidation’’ project in which he had to bring together folks from three separate cities to a single location. KnowledgeScope allowed him and his team to easily access the information at all the sites and relevant projects, as well as contact peers who were knowledgeable of the customers, landlords, and other parties in the project in those locations. The user believed that this convenient and timely access to project information contributed significantly to the success of the project and reduced costs for Comco.

. Strategist managers are alerted to projects in danger of missing deadlines by KnowledgeScope.

. Mentors monitor novice strategists by inspecting the project steps they took, providing them with prompt advice as needed. This enhanced the learning of novice strategists.

## 7.3. Knowledge resources

KnowledgeScope brought together many knowledge resources to support tasks in the RAL process. Users were nicely surprised to be given more useful information at times and also suggested other resources that could be helpful for their work:

. Certain financial details of leases, maintained by transactions managers on a separate legacy system, are now accessible through KnowledgeScope to other participants of the RAL process. As a result, a strategist found out about lease options that enabled her to create different project plans and hence save costs for Comco.

. Users suggested that certain knowledge resources, such as local real estate market research done by strategists, should be included in KnowledgeScope.

. Much of the communication and discussions in the projects that would have been useful for reference were still carried out on the phone. One suggestion was to incorporate support for multimedia capture in KnowledgeScope.

## 7.4. Context and perspectives

User feedback also showed that contextual information needs to be expressed in defining process knowledge and is sensitive to the needs of different roles. For example,

. In defining the project templates, the pilot team realized that project timelines and procedures are different geographically from region to region, as well as for different customers (Comco business units). This contextual information was captured in the templates.

. Some users felt that there was often more information than needed on a screen. While the four perspectives provided useful views into the process, different roles may be interested in different attributes or subsets of the objects in the perspective. For example, while strategist managers and asset managers both view the project from the functional perspective, the asset manager is interested in the financial goals, e.g., how the value of the real estate portfolio changes as properties are leased, built, sold, etc., whereas the strategist manager is concerned about the operational goals, e.g., how quick the turnaround for a lease renewal is. Thus, customization based on role may be necessary even within perspectives. In particular, users also pointed out that a financial perspective would be valuable for this application.

## 7.5. Integration with the process

KnowledgeScope had been integrated into the work of some users—one user remarked that KnowledgeScope was like a ‘palmpilot’ for her. Others, like the telecommuters, had various difficulties that the IT group had to work out, but were expected to use the system eventually. Some users commented, though, that KnowledgeScope would not be able to capture all the knowledge because there would always be conversations and meetings held outside of the system environment. Nevertheless, they felt that time could change their habits to carry out more of these interactions online and perhaps multimedia support would help the move in that direction.

## 8. Conclusion

We have proposed and implemented a design of a knowledge management system that addresses two main causes of non-use: the lack of a standard, context-rich knowledge structure accepted by different types of users, and the documentation burden. Unlike other knowledge repositories that use subject categories to organize knowledge, KnowledgeScope organizes knowledge around the organizational processes in which the knowledge is created, captured, and used. Its knowledge structure is a process metamodel called Knowledge-In-Context which specifies, in an object-oriented representation, the elements of a process and the relationships between them. The KIC model differs from other process models and workflow models in that the process elements and relationships have been organized into four perspectives proposed by Curtis et al. [14] to address the different knowledge needs of users with different roles in an organizational process. The operationalization of these four perspectives is a contribution to process modeling research.

We proposed to incorporate workflow support with a knowledge repository to enable KnowledgeScope to (1) reduce the documentation burden by automatically capturing knowledge and its context as it is created, and (2) provide knowledge with contextual information to the right person at the right time in the right place. The KnowledgeScope implementation at Comco demonstrated the feasibility of this design. Although workflow management systems themselves are already based on a process meta-model, they typically emphasize the facilitation of workflow to improve process efficiency and to prevent breakdowns in process coordination. Little support is available for providing users with knowledge resources, access to process histories during exceptional situations and during process evaluation to generate new process knowledge. By organizing process knowledge and case knowledge with the KIC model and providing knowledge applications for perusing that information, KnowledgeScope contributes to the research in workflow management systems by extending the functionality of workflow management systems to include support for knowledge management in organizational processes.

Future work will focus on further development of the KIC model and KnowledgeScope. User feedback suggested that while Curtis et al.’s [14] four perspectives helped to define broad views into a process, they are not specific enough to describe the knowledge needs of individual roles. Users in different roles may focus on particular objects and relationships within a perspective or even particular attributes of an object. Thus, one future research direction is to extend the perspectives in the KIC model so that ‘slices’ can be defined for each object, each perspective, and the context for each object for each role. A slice of an object defines a subset of the object’s attributes. A slice of a perspective defines a subset of the objects and relationships in one of the four broad-based perspective currently in the KIC model. A slice of the context of an object defines a subset of the object’s relationships to other objects in the KIC model. We will also extend the KIC model to include a financial perspective.

At Comco, KnowledgeScope had been implemented with limited workflow functions. We will attempt to incorporate a workflow management system in future implementations to further reduce the documentation burden.

Comco also had plans to expand the usage of the knowledge repository to real estate brokers, such as allowing them to access the RAL document. This would require Comco to add certain security features to KnowledgeScope. In fact, we are observing a general trend of companies providing access to their knowledge repositories to the extended enterprise that includes external parties such as trading partners and customers. For example, IBM provides select partners and vendors secured access to their intranet. This allows partners and vendors to learn about current IBM products and solutions. For example, to learn about the Web services offerings from Global Services, a partner can logon to a secure inbound network environment (SINE). The login protocol uses RSA’s SecurID authenticator. The SINE environment provides IBM employees/contractors on a LAN at a non-IBM location or over the Internet the ability to access their IBM applications such as Lotus Notes, etc. Once inside the environment, the user can download white papers and presentations on Web services. Also, the same environment can be configured to provide varying access to IBM employees and contractors. We will incorporate similar security systems in KnowledgeScope to ensure that only authorized users have access to the knowledge repository and also to restrict access to knowledge objects relevant to their needs.
