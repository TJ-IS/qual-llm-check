# Corporate disclosure dissemination: when more is less

David Bodoff \*, Hongtao Zhang

Department of Information and Systems Management (ISMT), Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, Kowloon, Hong Kong, China

Accepted 28 May 2002

## Abstract

The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission’s EDGAR on the Internet (EOI) initiative greatly expanded investors’ access to corporate disclosure documents. Investors refer to these documents when making investment decisions. Management science literature and the popular press assume that more complete disclosure and more widespread dissemination of these data are always beneficial. On the other hand, the analytical literature in finance and accounting shows that the benefits of disclosure, as measured by investor utility, are not straightforward. In this paper, we adopt a rational expectations equilibrium model to investigate whether investors benefit from the wider dissemination that resulted from EOI. Our analysis shows that many investors are unambiguously hurt by EOI. Moreover, even those investors who previously had no access to the documents are hurt under certain conditions. These results, which have an intuitive basis, suggest that the public policy of wider dissemination requires more carefully articulated motivations. <sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Disclosure; Information; Benefit; Trader; Internet

## 1. Introduction

In 1993, the National Science Foundation sponsored the EDGAR on the Internet (EOI) project [8]. The purpose of EOI was to disseminate EDGAR documents to the broad public via the Internet. EDGAR is the U.S. Securities Exchanges Commission’s (SEC) online database of corporate disclosure documents such as 10-K’s (annual reports), quarterly reports, proxy statements, etc. The EDGAR database contains information to support investment decisions, and EOI is essentially a decision support system.

The EDGAR database and the EOI project attracted a great deal of attention in the popular press. Prior to EDGAR, disclosure documents were filed in paper form to the SEC, where they were made available to the public in a reading room. Large institutional investors maintained staff in Washington, DC, to read these documents in their paper form. These documents were essentially inaccessible to the general public and to small investors, except of course indirectly through their financial advisers. Following EDGAR but prior to EOI, institutional investors had easier—but still very costly—access to the data. Individual investors were still largely left out, due to the prohibitive cost of subscribing to the data feed.

EOI changed all that. By 1998, the SEC EDGAR Web site was distributing 500,000 documents per day.

This number is in addition to a large (but not precisely known) number of documents that are sent to individuals through other content resellers such as EDGAR-Online. In addition to EOI, another more recent SEC rule also aims at a wider information dissemination. According to the Fair Disclosure rule adopted by the SEC in August 2000, companies cannot selectively disclose data to certain analysts or big investors before releasing it to the public.

EDGAR and EOI have been hailed as a success in the popular press. Numerous exchanges and regulatory agencies have therefore adopted EDGAR-like systems, and others, including the Hong Kong Stock Exchange and the mainland China’s China Securities Regulatory Commission, are planning to introduce such systems in the near future.

The academic management literature also discusses corporate disclosure [4]. In this management literature as in the popular press, more and wider disclosure is simply assumed to benefit all investors.

But there exists a vast literature in accounting, finance, and economics that shows the benefits of more—or more widespread—disclosure, are far from straightforward. This literature is mathematical, and is thus rather inaccessible to the popular press and even to many researchers. The unfortunate result is that an opportunity is lost to apply rigorous analysis to practical questions of disclosure regulation. The particular question for EDGAR and EOI (hereinafter, ‘‘EOI’’ for short) regards the value of disseminating an alreadymandated disclosure document to a wider audience of traders. As we will describe below in our review of related literature, the anticipated effects of this specific change are not straightforward and merit analysis.

The benefits of any disclosure regulation may be measured with respect to three different parties, i.e., traders, listing companies, and the exchange. Each of these groups measures benefits in a different way. Traders assess their wealth, listing firms assess their cost of capital, and the exchange measures its revenue stream. These three may be related. For example, Diamond and Verrecchia [3] investigates the effects of information disclosure on liquidity, which in turn, affects both traders’ welfare and firms’ costs of capital. In the current investigation, our main focus in on the benefits to traders.

EOI is an information system. To measure the value of an information system to its users, many different approaches are possible. The literature on decision support systems has used approaches based on decision theory, cognitive science, and equilibrium theory in economics (see Repo [11] for a review). An important feature of the EOI system is that it is used by many individuals who interact in a marketplace. The theory of rational expectations equilibrium in economics is one analytical approach that can model the value of information in this kind of reflexive market. A substantial literature has adopted this approach to studying various aspects of disclosure, and we adopt it here to study the value of EOI.

The main and underlying result—that more information can be bad—has been analyzed extensively in the finance and accounting literature that we partly review below. We adopt one of these analytical models as the basis of our work. However, the models used in the majority of these theoretical studies analyze the value of more information, as a way of assessing the benefits of regulation that requires greater disclosure. This is the natural focus in the accounting and finance fields.

EOI is a different case. In this paper, we analyze the effects of a new technology for more widely disseminating the same information. This is the natural focus from an information systems perspective, and represents the actual case of EOI. We derive results from existing models and apply these results to evaluate an important and implemented system, EOI.

EOI is a highly publicized information system and a result of public policy. It is a rare opportunity that the value of a real and well-publicized information system can be directly assessed and discussed by appealing to such a well-established analytical method as the theory of rational expectations equilibrium. This opportunity motivates our work.

In the following sections, we outline the results, review the literature of mathematical models, present our model, and use comparative statistics to show the expected effects of EOI. The significance of these results is discussed.

## 2. Outline of the model and results

The aim of this paper is to apply normative mathematical models to shed light on the supposed benefits of EOI. EOI resulted in a much larger fraction of traders receiving the public signals available in SEC disclosure documents.<sup>1</sup> We treat the fraction of informed traders as an exogenous variable k, and analyze the effects of changes in k on investors’ ex ante expected utility. This analysis will give some indication of the expected effects of a new technology that has the effect of increasing k.

As a starting point for an analytical model of EOI, we adopt the noisy rational expectations model found in Verrecchia [12] and Diamond [2]. Then we depart somewhat from the focus of those papers, by separately analyzing the effects of a change in k on the utility of three different groups of traders. The first group is those who were previously informed even without EOI. We refer to this group, which largely represents institutional investors, as the ‘‘informed bystanders.’’ The second group is those traders who remain uninformed even in the presence of EOI. This group represents individuals who cannot afford or are not interested in these documents. We refer to this group as the ‘‘uninformed bystanders.’’ The third group is those who become informed with the introduction of EOI. We refer to this group, which largely represents individual investors, as the ‘‘switchers.’’ This kind of split analysis is used in Alles and Lundholm [1] who also assumed an exogenously determined lucky group of informed investors.

We model EOI directly as an exogenous change in k, instead of actually modeling EOI in terms of reduced costs and easy access. The only effect of EOI that is not captured by k, is the reduced cost (surplus) to those ‘‘informed bystanders’’ who were previously paying more for the data. Our analysis ignores this effect, both because this savings is not related to the purpose of EOI and because mathematically it is simply a dollar figure (i.e., a constant) that needs no analysis.

We also do not discuss the possibility that more market transparency attracts more people to participate in the equity market; namely, we assume that the set of traders is the same before and after EOI. One could conceivably relax this condition but we believe it is a reasonable assumption to start with.

EOI intends to reduce the unfair information asymmetries that result when some traders have better access to information. As a challenge to conventional wisdom and policy, the interesting question we investigate below is whether ‘‘leveling the playing field’’ brings a net benefit, even to those traders who previously did not have access to the information but now do, i.e., our ‘‘switchers.’’ Intuitively, a trader may benefit from receiving more information, but he may not benefit—or may even lose out—if many or all traders are also going to be informed. Therefore, when the same information is disseminated to all, the net benefit to one particular trader may be positive or negative. Our model, which is based directly on previous work, shows that if the fraction of informed traders was small and if EOI greatly expands that fraction, then the effects of EOI are negative even for the ‘‘switchers.’’

We now present the intuition behind our results and the results that arise in the literature review. We assume the traders are risk averse and measure their trading benefits by the ex ante expected value of a utility, which is a negative exponential function of realized wealth, i.e., U(w) =  e <sup></sup> <sup>w/r</sup>. The qualifier ‘‘ex ante’’ means that the expectation is taken before the disclosure information is announced. Each individual trader is initially endowed with a mixed holding of cash and stock and intends to maximize his expected utility by trading with others. The access to disclosure documents enables a previously uninformed trader to make better trading decisions towards maximizing his expected utility and that is a positive effect of EOI for individual traders; that is, all things being equal, a trader prefers more information before trading begins. We refer to this as ‘‘reduced ignorance risk’’.

However, trading takes two parties who can both benefit—i.e., reduce their respective risk—from the trade. This occurs, for example, when one trader has a large number of stocks but little cash, and can benefit from selling stocks even at a low price, while another trader is in the opposite situation and is eager to buy at that price. But this kind of win–win risk sharing is less prevalent as the risk disappears and traders all become more informed, as through EOI. For example, in the extreme case, after the real value of the stock is known with certainty, it is too late for a win – win trade. In this way, more common information makes it more difficult to do beneficial trading altogether (‘‘increased endowment risk’’) and also undermines the potential positive benefit of ‘‘reduced ignorance risk’’ previously described. This is the basic intuition why the net effect of EOI is ambiguous.

Our model assumes that there is no trading before the information is divulged to the k fraction of traders. Any negative results might be ‘‘blamed’’ on this aspect of the model, with the argument that this element is unrealistic since in real life, trading is continuous and the disclosure policy is known in advance. On the other hand, though, if the model does allow traders to trade with knowledge of the disclosure policy but before the information is announced, then in many models the disclosure policy has no effect at all [2,10,13]! Clearly, such an approach is also unable to support the benefits of wider disclosure. The justification for our assumption is that in spite of the theoretical possibility for continuous portfolio rebalancing in real markets, not every trader—especially not every small investor—is in a state of equilibrium at all times.

The analytical model is necessarily highly ‘‘stylized’’ [13], and results must be interpreted with care because details of the model directly effect the results. This very point, however, is what our analysis aims to emphasize—the effects of widely disseminating disclosure documents are subtle and sensitive to the context, and not at all obviously beneficial to anyone at all, much less to everyone in every context. The main results of our analysis have intuitive explanations, and ought to be considered by policy-makers and management researchers when pronouncements are made regarding the benefits of widespread disclosure.

## 3. Related literature

Lev [9] argues that disclosure regulations are indeed meant to level the playing field, but not because uninformed traders would otherwise be exploited. Rather, in the event of information asymmetries, the uninformed take defensive positions to avoid being exploited, and these defensive mechanisms hurt everyone by limiting risk-sharing opportunities. Lev’s [9] view is based on the idea that any trader is free to become informed if he/she chooses, and at equilibrium, traders are indifferent between being informed and uninformed. This view is reflected in a set of theoretical investigations into the social value of information, in which information acquisition decisions are endogenous results. Verrecchia [12] presents a noisy rational expectations model in which at equilibrium, traders choose an optimum level of private information to acquire. Building on that work, Diamond [2] shows that if a costly signal of a given precision is available, then an equilibrium number of traders will choose to acquire that signal. In this first part of Ref. [2], the fraction of informed traders is endogenous.

In the second part of Ref. [2], Diamond proceeds to analyze the effect of the firm’s disclosing information to everyone, via a second informational signal. Diamond also shows that such a public release of information to all traders can be of social value, by lowering the amount of private information acquisition (i.e., acquisition of the ‘first’ signal). A closer examination of Diamond’s work is important for our purposes. In Diamond’s model, the release of a public signal (to everyone) of sufficient precision drives out any benefits from private information. Although the total amount of information revealed is equivalent in both cases, it is found that a public signal of precision d is preferred to a release of private signals (to an equilibrium fraction of traders) of precision s, where d < s. In other words, revealing less precise information to every trader turns out to be better for everyone than allowing an equilibrium fraction of traders to acquire more precise information. There are two main reasons for this benefit. First, there are savings in the production of information, since the one public announcement replaces the redundant informationproducing efforts of many private traders. But even if this savings is ignored and we focus only on changes in trading patterns, all traders may be better off in the public signal economy in cases where very little information is revealed through price. According to Diamond’s intuition, the explanation is that in these cases, private information would lead to large speculative positions and very unequal risk sharing. This explanation is in line with Lev’s arguments [9].

The main difference between Diamond’s analysis and ours is that Diamond analyzed the effect of a second public signal of sufficient precision to endogenously drive the original k to 0, whereas we study the effects of a technology that exogenously changes the original k to 1. An additional important difference is that because in Diamond’s analysis information acquisition is determined endogenously, the public signal being considered is either beneficial to all traders or to none.

Using Diamond’s model, Alles and Lundholm [1] study the case where the proportion of informed traders is fixed exogenously, rather than settled in equilibrium. The question once again is the utility of requiring that the firm announces an additional public signal. In this case, an analysis of benefits needs to separately treat two groups—those who were previously privileged and informed and those who were previously uninformed. Once again, the release of a public signal (to everyone) of sufficient precision drives out any benefits from private information. But in this case, the traders do not begin in a state of equilibrium indifference between being informed and uninformed. Rather, the informed are enjoying a privilege of access to (albeit costly) information. Thus, a higher level of precision for the public signal is necessary to drive out the benefits of being in the select group of informed traders, i.e., d>s. This case is more similar to the EOI application than the case of Ref. [2], for two reasons. First, a trader is not indifferent to being informed or uninformed. Second, the total amount of information revealed is greater in the public signal economy. In the results of this work [1], not only are the previously informed traders always worse off in the public signal economy, even the previously uninformed traders are worse off unless the fraction of previously informed traders had been very large.

But there is still an important difference between all the aforementioned models and the current case of EOI. The above models study the release of a second, distinct public signal to drive out the benefits of private signals. In the EOI case, the identical signal is being propagated ‘‘publicly’’ over the Internet instead of disclosed ‘‘privately’’ in an SEC reading room.

What general result can be learned from the above models, and how do they apply to the EOI case? Obviously, if a trader was previously uninformed, then, ceteris paribus, he would benefit from becoming informed. However, the literature has shown that widespread dissemination of the signal has the following two detrimental effects, whose intuition was outlined in Section 2 above. First, more common information makes it more difficult to trade altogether. This is known as ‘‘signal risk’’ [1] or ‘‘endowment risk’’ [7], since it implies that the traders may be stuck with their initial endowments. Second, as explained in Ref. [7], disclosing information to all traders undermines an individual trader’s ability to benefit from his increased knowledge by reducing the opportunities for win–win trades. This effect, which is related to the previous one, is known as ‘‘reduced risk sharing.’’ Together, these two effects can make the information harmful to traders’ expected utility.<sup>2</sup> All the above effects apply to EOI.

But there is one aspect of EOI that, according to the available literature, may hold promise for a positive impact on utility and that is reduced asymmetry. The intuition offered by Diamond [2] is that asymmetry leads the informed to take large positions based on their information. Thus, risk is not being efficiently shared, so average expected utilities suffer. According to this view, this positive effect of reduced asymmetry from EOI may counterbalance the loss of the average trader’s informational advantage.

In sum, previous literature leads us to expect that more widespread disclosure may effect investors’ utility through the following mechanisms:

\- Increased informedness benefits those traders who were previously uninformed;

\- Increased common information reduces utility through increased endowment risk;

\- Increased common information reduces utility by diminishing the relative significance of an individual trader’s information through reduced risk sharing;

\- Reduced asymmetry of information enhances utility through increased risk sharing.

Our analysis corroborates the first three of these expectations. However, as we will see below, we find that in the model we adopt, unlike the previous models of Diamond, there is no apparent benefit from reduced asymmetry.

In this paper, we revert to an early and straightforward form of the noisy rational expectations model. We develop the model in a direction that has not been previously pursued, namely, we study the effects of an exogenous change in the fraction of lucky informed investors, on the utility of the three different groups of traders defined earlier.

## 4. The trading model

A version of the noisy competitive rational expectations model used by Diamond [2] is adopted here and the assumptions and conventions common to the noisy rational expectations paradigm are utilized. Traders are risk-averse and have constant absolute risk aversion preferences (exponential utility) with a common level of risk tolerance r. There are two assets. One is a riskless asset that returns one unit of the single consumption good. The other asset is risky; its return is represented by the random variable $\tilde { u } ,$ whose realization is $u .$ Each trader’s prior beliefs are that u˜ has a normal distribution with mean $Y _ { 0 }$ and precision (inverse of variance) $h _ { 0 } { > } 0$ . There is a continuum of traders, each indexed by $t { \in } [ 0 , 1 ]$ . A feature of the economy, of particular interest to our study of EOI, is that an exogenous fraction $\lambda$ of traders are endowed with the right of access to costly private information while the remaining traders (fraction $1 - \lambda )$ are denied access to the information. EOI has the effect of increasing k from a small fraction to a high fraction. The information available to (informed) trader t is the return u plus some error, i.e., it is the random variable $\tilde { y } _ { t }$ defined as

$$
\tilde {y} _ {t} = \tilde {u} + \tilde {\varepsilon} _ {t}
$$

where $\tilde { \varepsilon } _ { t }$ is normal with mean zero and precision $s { > } 0$ The error $\tilde { \varepsilon } _ { t }$ is independent of u˜ and the errors $\tilde { \varepsilon } _ { j }$ of all other traders, $j \neq ~ t .$ The precision s can be interpreted as a measure of the quality of the information. Trader t is endowed initially with ${ \bar { B } } _ { t }$ units of the riskless asset and $x _ { t }$ units of the risky asset. The per capita supply of the risky asset, defined as the average of $x _ { t }$ over all traders, is a normal random variable $\bar { \tilde { X } }$ with mean zero and variance V. Each trader is an insignificant part of the total market and his information and his demand for the risky asset do not affect the equilibrium price of the risky asset.

The timeline is as follows: In period 1, each trader is endowed and a fraction k of traders is informed (of course, the identity of these informed traders is unknown); in period 2, trading occurs until equilibrium is reached; in period 3, payoffs are realized.

We begin the analysis by stating and adapting some results from previous work that provide closed-form representation for the equilibrium distribution of the risky asset’s price. These results require some adaptation since they were mostly developed for a model (e.g., the first part of Ref. [3]) where $\lambda$ is endogenous. In such cases, unlike ours, in equilibrium a trader is indifferent between being informed (i.e., paying for information) and being uninformed, so there is no need to separately follow the effects on informed and uninformed investors. In our model, there is very much a need to separately analyze the effects on the three groups of traders enumerated above.

An equilibrium price $\tilde { P }$ for a given value of k (the fraction of informed traders) is a linear function of the liquidating value u˜ and the per capita supply $\tilde { X }$ of the risky asset. It is stated in Ref. [2] that

$$
\tilde {P} = \alpha Y _ {0} + \beta \tilde {u} - \gamma \tilde {X}
$$

where

$$
\alpha = \frac {r V h _ {0}}{r \left\{V h _ {0} + \lambda s V + (r \lambda s) ^ {2} \right\}},
$$

$$
\beta = \frac {r \{\lambda s V + (r \lambda s) ^ {2} \}}{r \{V h _ {0} + \lambda s V + (r \lambda s) ^ {2} \}},
$$

$$
\gamma = \frac {V + r ^ {2} \lambda s}{r \{V h _ {0} + \lambda s V + (r \lambda s) ^ {2} \}}.
$$

Traders cannot observe u˜ or $\tilde { X }$ directly but do observe ${ \tilde { P } } ,$ which aggregates the total of all information known to the traders. By conditioning his beliefs on price (as well as his private information if any), each trader augments his knowledge. Specifically, upon observing the price realization $P$ and the private signal $y _ { t } ,$ an informed trader forms his posterior distribution of ${ \tilde { u } } .$ This distribution is normal [12] with precision (the inverse of variance)

$$
\theta_ {\mathrm{I}} = h _ {0} + s + \frac {(r \lambda s) ^ {2}}{V}\tag{1}
$$

and mean

$$
\begin{array}{c} E [ \tilde {u} \mid \tilde {y} _ {t} = y _ {t}, \tilde {P} = P ] = Y _ {0} + \theta_ {\mathrm{I}} ^ {- 1} s (y _ {t} - Y _ {0}) \\ + \frac {r \lambda s}{\gamma V} \theta_ {\mathrm{I}} ^ {- 1} (P - Y _ {0}). \end{array}
$$

The conditional mean $E [ \tilde { u } | y _ { t } , P ]$ is the trader’s prediction of u˜ and the posterior precision $\theta _ { \mathrm { { I } } } ,$ defined as his informedness [5], is a measure of his prediction accuracy. Similarly, an uninformed trader forms his posterior distribution of u˜ conditional on $P$ and this distribution is normal with precision

$$
\theta_ {\mathrm{U}} = h _ {0} + \frac {(r \lambda s) ^ {2}}{V}\tag{2}
$$

and mean

$$
E [ \tilde {u} \mid \tilde {P} = P ] = Y _ {0} + \frac {r \lambda s}{\lambda V} \theta_ {\mathrm{U}} ^ {- 1} (P - Y _ {0}),
$$

where $\theta _ { \mathrm { U } }$ is the trader’s informedness and can be interpreted as a measure of accuracy of his prediction for u˜ conditional on ${ \tilde { P } } .$ The price $\dot { P }$ is an additional source of information to the traders and it serves to improve their prediction accuracy: $\theta _ { \mathrm { U } }$ is greater than the prior precision $h _ { 0 } ; \theta _ { \mathrm { I } }$ is greater than $h _ { 0 } + s ,$ , the total precision of the prior knowledge and the private information.

Extending Indjejikian [6], we define the paired consensus of any two traders as the coefficient of correlation of their prediction errors. In our model, a pair of traders can be both informed, both uninformed, or one informed and the other uninformed. We use $\rho _ { \mathrm { I } , \mathrm { I } } , \ \rho _ { \mathrm { U } , \mathrm { U } }$ , and $\rho _ { \mathrm { I , U } }$ to denote the three types of correlation. It can be shown that

$$
\rho_ {\mathrm{I,I}} = \frac {\theta_ {\mathrm{U}}}{\theta_ {\mathrm{I}}},\tag{3}
$$

$$
\rho_ {\mathrm{U,U}} = 1,\tag{4}
$$

$$
\rho_ {\mathrm{I,U}} = \sqrt {\frac {\theta_ {\mathrm{U}}}{\theta_ {\mathrm{I}}}},\tag{5}
$$

and that $\rho _ { \mathrm { I , I } } { < } \rho _ { \mathrm { I , U } } { < } \rho _ { \mathrm { U , U } }$

The variance of the excess payoff of the risky asset is given by

$$
\varPhi = \mathrm{Var} (\tilde {u} - \tilde {P})
$$

and can be interpreted as economy-wide uncertainty and used as a measure of risk-sharing opportunity. It can be expressed as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \Phi = \left[ r ^ {2} h _ {0} + V \bigg (1 + \frac {r ^ {2} \lambda s}{V} \bigg) ^ {2} \right] \\ \times \left[ r ^ {2} \bigg (h _ {0} + \lambda s + \frac {r ^ {2} \lambda^ {2} s ^ {2}}{V} \bigg) ^ {2} \right] ^ {- 1}. \end{array}
$$

It is easy to see that $\theta _ { \mathrm { I } }$ and $\theta _ { \mathrm { U } }$ are increasing in $\lambda ,$ and that so are $\rho _ { \mathrm { I , I } }$ and $\rho _ { \mathrm { I , U } }$ . It can also be shown that $\varPhi$ is decreasing in k. As more traders receive private information (i.e., k increases), the equilibrium price ${ \tilde { P } } ,$ by aggregating the private information from more traders, becomes more indicative of the actual payoff $\tilde { u } .$ This enables all traders, informed or not, to make more accurate prediction of $\tilde { u }$ conditional on ${ \tilde { P } } ,$ thereby increasing their informedness. It also increases the consensus among traders since their predictions are all closer to u˜ and thus are closer to each other. The increase in informedness enhances traders utility while increased consensus means less risk sharing among traders and that reduces utility. As we will demonstrate, the overall effect could be such that utility is reduced for all traders.

To isolate the effect of increasing k on traders’ utilities, we assume that the information is available (to informed traders) at no cost. The ex ante expected utility of trader t is given by, from Lemma 2 of Ref. [2],

$$
\mathrm{EU} _ {t} = - \left(\theta_ {t} \Phi\right) ^ {- \frac {1}{2}} \exp \left\{\frac {- \bar {B} _ {t} - x _ {t} Y _ {0}}{r} + \frac {1}{2} \left(\frac {x _ {t}}{r}\right) ^ {2} \right.
$$

$$
\left. \times \left[ h _ {0} + \frac {\left(r V h _ {0}\right) ^ {2}}{V \left(V + r ^ {2} \lambda s\right) ^ {2}} \right] ^ {- 1} \right\}\tag{6}
$$

where s is its precision (0 if the trader is uninformed), $x _ { t }$ and ${ \bar { B } } _ { t }$ are the trader’s initial endowment of the risky and riskless assets, respectively, and $\theta _ { t } = \theta _ { \mathrm { I } }$ if he is informed or $\theta _ { t } \mathrm { = } \theta _ { \mathrm { U } }$ if uninformed. Since $\theta _ { \mathrm { { I } } } { > } \theta _ { \mathrm { { U } } }$ , an informed trader is always better off than an uninformed one; so information does indeed bring relative trading advantage. The first component of the expected utility, $( \theta _ { t } \Phi ) ^ { - 1 / 2 }$ , or more conveniently $\theta _ { t } \Phi ,$ , captures informedness and risk sharing. The second component is essentially

$$
- \frac {\bar {B} _ {t} + x _ {t} Y _ {0}}{r} + \frac {1}{2} \left(\frac {x _ {t}}{r}\right) ^ {2} \left[ h _ {0} + \frac {(r V h _ {0}) ^ {2}}{V (V + r ^ {2} \lambda s) ^ {2}} \right] ^ {- 1}.
$$

and it captures the effect of initial endowments, namely, the endowment risk. The terms $\bar { B } _ { t } + x _ { t } Y _ { 0 }$ equals the prior expected value of the initial holding. Given a fixed prior expected value, an initial holding consisting of the riskless asset only is preferable to one consisting of both the riskless and risky assets, for the trader is risk averse. In other words, an initial holding with $x _ { t } = 0$ is ideal. The second term measures the effect on expected utility when the initial holding is not ideal, i.e., when $x _ { t } { > } 0$ . It reduces the utility as k increases and we interpret this as follows. When more traders receive private information, all traders become better informed through price and thus risk sharing is reduced. This makes it more difficult for a trader with nonzero initial endowment of the risky asset to find trading partners in order to adjust his risky asset holding to a desired level. In summary,

Proposition 1. Increasing the fraction of informed traders impacts the expected utility through three effects: informedness, risk sharing, and endowment risk. Increased informedness enhances utility; reduced risk sharing hurts utility; and the endowment risk effect reduces utility.

## 5. Trading model applied to EOI

EOI is modeled as an exogenous change in the value of k. In the following, EOI will therefore always be analyzed through the effects of increasing k on increased informedness and reduced risk sharing, and the negative endowment effect. This analysis departs from the development in the second part of Ref. [2]. Diamond investigates the effects on utility of a company policy of disclosing a second, distinct signal to all traders, while the following analysis is interested in the effects of more widely disseminating the original signal.

## 5.1. Effects of EOI on bystanders

First, we examine the combined informedness and risk-sharing effect of increasing k on the expected utility of bystanders. We write $\theta _ { \mathrm { I } } ( \lambda ) , \theta _ { \mathrm { U } } ( \lambda )$ , and $\varPhi ( \lambda )$ to denote the dependency of these quantities on k. It can be verified that

$$
\frac {\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} \lambda} [ \theta_ {\mathrm{I}} (\lambda) \Phi (\lambda) ], \frac {\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} \lambda} [ \theta_ {\mathrm{U}} (\lambda) \Phi (\lambda) ] <   0.\tag{7}
$$

That is, the combined informedness and risk-sharing effect of increasing k on expected utility is negative. Since the endowment effect is negative, we have, by Eq. (6):

Proposition 2. As k increases, (1) those who were previously uninformed and remain uninformed (uninformed bystanders) become worse off; and (2) those who were previously informed and remain informed (informed bystanders) also becomes worse off, except for perhaps a reduction in the cost of information.

Discussion of Proposition 2: In addition to the above primary negative results, this finding raises questions about the concept of reduced asymmetry that has been used both as an intuition to explain mathematical results [2], as well as the basis for public policy [9]. In the current model, there are no conditions under which reduced asymmetry (or any other possible positive benefit) dominates the net result of an increase in k. The positive benefits of reduced asymmetry are apparently very sensitive to the details of the model, and must be used cautiously as a justification for public policy. The main difference between Diamond’s analysis and ours is that Diamond analyzed the effect of a second public signal of sufficient precision to endogenously drive the original k to 0, whereas we study the effects of a technology that drives the original k to 1. This difference is apparently responsible for the very different—and less optimistic—result.

## 5.2. Effects of EOI on switchers

The more important challenge to theoretical justifications for EOI comes from an analysis of the impact it has on the individual investors who would otherwise not have access to the data, but who gain access with the introduction of EOI. These are the ‘‘switchers.’’ We begin by modeling EOI as a change in k from a small fraction to one. The question is, when k is changed to one, whether those who previously belonged to the uninformed $1 - \lambda$ fraction will be better or worse off. We start by comparing two extreme cases of $\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda = 1$

## 5.2.1. EOI model: $\lambda { = } 0 { \longrightarrow } \lambda { = } I$

## Lemma 1. It holds that

$$
\begin{array}{l} \theta_ {\mathrm{I}} (1) \Phi (1) - \theta_ {\mathrm{U}} (0) \Phi (0) \\ = h _ {0} ^ {- 1} \left\{h _ {0} + s + \frac {(r s) ^ {2}}{V} \right\} ^ {- 1} \left[ \left(h _ {0} - \frac {V}{r ^ {2}}\right) - s \right] s. \end{array}
$$

And $\theta _ { \mathrm { I } } ( 1 ) \varPhi ( 1 ) < \theta _ { \mathrm { U } } ( 0 ) \varPhi ( 0 )$ if and only if

$$
s > s ^ {*} \equiv h _ {0} - \frac {V}{r ^ {2}}.\tag{8}
$$

Proposition 3. If condition (8) holds, then the ex ante expected utility is smaller when all traders are informed $( \lambda = 1 )$ than when all are uninformed $( \lambda = 0 )$

Since the endowment effect is negative, this negative result holds for the expected utility as a whole.

Condition (8) does not seem too stringent as one would often expect that $s { > } h _ { 0 } ,$ , namely, that the private information has greater precision than the prior distribution. The following observations distinguish two cases depending on traders’ risk tolerance.

. When $r { \le } \sqrt { V / h _ { 0 } }$ or $h _ { 0 } - V / r ^ { 2 } { \le } 0 .$ , condition (8) holds for any s. Note that $V / h _ { 0 } { = } \mathrm { V a r } ( \tilde { X } ) \mathrm { V a r } ( \tilde { u } )$ . Therefore, when risk tolerance is small relative to the variability in the per capita supply and payoff of the risky asset, risk sharing is crucial to the welfare of traders. Disseminating information to the public, no matter how poor the precision, will result in a negative risk-sharing effect that is greater than the positive informedness effect and the net result is that everyone’s utility will be lower than if no traders are informed.

. When $r > \sqrt { V / h _ { 0 } }$ or $h _ { 0 } - V / r ^ { 2 } > 0$ , condition (8) holds only if $s { > } s ^ { * }$ . When risk tolerance is larger, disseminated information must be of a certain quality, that is, the precision must be greater than $s ^ { * }$ , in order for it to cause enough reduction in risk sharing to negate the positive informedness effect and result in a lower expected utility.

In either case, a stronger claim can actually be made using continuity argument; that is, public disclosure makes everyone worse off than if the information was previously available to a small $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } }$ fraction of informed traders. The ensuing analysis will develop along this line.

## 5.2.2. EOI model: $\lambda { = } \lambda _ { P R E } { \longrightarrow } \lambda { = } I$

The difference $\theta _ { \mathrm { I } } ( 1 ) \varPhi ( 1 ) - \theta _ { \mathrm { U } } ( \lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } ) \varPhi ( \lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } )$ can be regarded as a measure of combined informedness and risk-sharing effect on the expected utility of the previously uninformed traders when the population of informed traders is expanded (e.g., by EOI) from a fraction $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } }$ to one. For example, if no traders were previously informed and condition (8) holds, then $\theta _ { \mathrm { I } } ( 1 ) \varPhi ( 1 ) - \theta _ { \mathrm { U } } ( 0 ) \varPhi ( 0 ) < 0$ by Lemma 1 and the effect on utility is negative. On the other extreme, if all but one singular trader was previously informed, making this one trader also informed will clearly benefit him since $\theta _ { \mathrm { I } } ( 1 ) \varPhi ( 1 ) - \theta _ { \mathrm { U } } ( 1 ) \varPhi ( 1 ) = s \varPhi ( 1 ) \succ 0$ . In general, by inequality (7), $\theta _ { \mathrm { I } } ( 1 ) \varPhi ( 1 ) - \theta _ { \mathrm { U } } ( \lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } ) \varPhi ( \lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } )$ is increasing in $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } ;$ that is, as the base of previously informed traders gets bigger, the combined informed ness and risk-sharing effect of EOI on the previously uninformed traders will become less negative and more benign.

Graphically, the curve $\theta _ { \mathrm { I } } ( 1 ) \varPhi ( 1 ) - \theta _ { \mathrm { U } } ( \lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } ) \varPhi ( \lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } )$ as a function of k is below zero at $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } = 0$ and above zero at $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } = 1$ and it ascends as $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } }$ increases. Furthermore, it can be verified through algebra that $\theta _ { \mathrm { I } } ( 1 ) \varPhi ( 1 ) - \theta _ { \mathrm { U } } ( 1 / 2 ) \varPhi ( 1 / 2 ) { > } 0$ , i.e., the curve is above zero after $\lambda { > } 1 / 2 ;$ therefore it must intersect the zero horizontal line at certain $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } ^ { \ast } < 1 / 2$

Lemma 2. Under condition (8), there is a unique $\lambda _ { \mathrm { { P R E } } } ^ { * } { \in } ( 0 , 1 / 2 )$ such that $\theta _ { \mathrm { U } } ( \lambda _ { P R E } ^ { * } ) \varPhi ( \lambda _ { P R E } ^ { * } ) = \theta _ { I } ( I ) \varPhi ( I )$

For example, if $s = h _ { 0 }$ (the disclosure precision equals the prior precision), and if $r = \sqrt { V / h _ { 0 } }$ (or $s ^ { * }$ in condition (8) equals zero), then $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } ^ { \ast } = 1 8 . 9 \%$

When $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } < \lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } ^ { \ast }$ , the combined informedness and risk-sharing effect of EOI on the previously uninformed traders is negative. Since the endowment risk effect is always negative, we reach the following conclusion.

Proposition 4. Suppose condition (8) holds. Then, $i f$ the fraction $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } }$ of informed traders is less than $\tilde { \lambda } _ { \mathrm { P R E } } ^ { * }$ the move to public disclosure by making $\lambda = 1$ will make the previously uninformed worse off.

In light of this result, it can be argued that the implementation of EOI might harm the previously underprivileged and uninformed traders whom it intends to help by ‘‘leveling the playing field.’’

It should be pointed out that if we start with a $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } { > } \lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } ^ { \ast }$ the effect of EOI on the previously uninformed would be ambiguous because in this case, while the combined informedness and risk-sharing effect is positive, the endowment risk effect is negative.

The intuition of this result is quite straightforward: The effect of a jump to universal coverage is more beneficial to a switcher, if he/she is the only trader, or one of only a few remaining traders, who were previously left out. Otherwise, the negative effects of reduced risk sharing dominate the positive effect of becoming informed.

## 5.2.3. General EOI model: $\lambda _ { P R E } {  } \lambda _ { P O S T }$

The foregoing analysis is based on the hypothesis that EOI will result in $\lambda = 1$ , namely, that all traders will receive the same disclosure information. This is the goal of EOI, and so this result is significant. But in actuality, EOI may result in less than universal access. We therefore continue with an analysis of the case where EOI results in widespread but not universal access. We denote the post-EOI fraction of informed traders as $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } } .$ From Proposition 2, those investors who were either already informed or who remain uninformed are both worse off. How about the ‘‘switchers’’? An analysis shows qualitatively the same conclusion: the switchers will be worse off if the information is of sufficiently high quality and if the pre-EOI proportion of informed traders is sufficiently low. For example, assume that EOI will make 90% of the traders informed, i.e., $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } } { = } 9 0 \%$ . If $s = h _ { 0 }$ and $r = \sqrt { V / h _ { 0 } }$ as in the previous example, then $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } ^ { * } = 1 2 . 8 \% ,$ that is, the switchers will be worse off if the initial proportion of informed traders is smaller than 12.8%.

In general, the informedness and risk-sharing effect of EOI on the switchers will depend on the initial proportion of informed traders $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } }$ and on the impact of EOI, $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } } .$ A graph of the value of EOI to switchers, as a function of $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } }$ and $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } } ,$ would show that the benefits are greater with higher $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } }$ and lower $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } } .$ In other words, even the switchers are worse off unless EOI is less necessary (higher $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } )$ or less effective (lower $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } } )$ . These are hardly encouraging results.

The intuition is once again quite straightforward: A jump in the fraction $\lambda$ is always most beneficial to the switchers, if they are few in number. The switchers would be few in number if $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } }$ is not much higher than $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } .$

If we are given that EOI will result in a particular fraction $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } }$ of informed traders, then we find (as above) the cutoff fraction $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } ^ { \ast } \left( \lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } } \right)$ of previously informed traders, below which EOI is harmful even to the switchers. Conversely, if we are given a fraction $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } }$ of previously informed traders, then we can find a post-EOI fraction $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } } ^ { \ast } \left( \lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } \right)$ above which EOI is harmful even to the switchers. The space is the fraction of pre- and post-EOI informed traders $( \lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } , \lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } } )$ . Presumably, EOI will only enlarge the informed base, $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } } { > } \lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } .$ , so the feasible space is restricted to the left-upper half (surrounded by dotted lines). The function $\lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } } ^ { \ast } \left( \lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } \right)$ divides the feasible space into two separate regions. $\mathrm { I f } \left( \lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } , \lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } } \right)$ is in the upper left region (above the curve), then informedness and risk-sharing effect is negative and EOI’s overall impact on the switchers is harmful. If $( \lambda _ { \mathrm { P R E } } , \lambda _ { \mathrm { P O S T } } )$ is in the lower right region (below or to the right of the curve), then the informedness and risk-sharing effect is positive, and in this case, the net effects of EOI are ambiguous. In short, whether EOI benefits or harms the switchers depends on the setting.

## 6. Conclusion

Prevailing wisdom is that securities exchanges need to enhance the transparency of their listed firms for the benefit of investors. In keeping with this view, numerous exchanges have already implemented or are planning to introduce systems that disseminate corporate disclosure documents over the Internet. In this study, we have applied the theory of rational expectations equilibrium to assess the effects of more widespread dissemination of disclosure documents on investors’ utility. We find that the effects of such a system are questionable at best, and harmful under some conditions. As usual, results must be interpreted with care, because of the sensitivity of the model to its assumptions. However, our analysis aims to emphasize this very point—the effects of widely disseminating disclosure documents are subtle and sensitive to the context, and not at all obviously beneficial to anyone at all, much less to everyone in every context. Empirical work is necessary to help establish the benefits of these dissemination systems.
