# Collective memory support and cognitive-conflict group decision-making: an experimental investigation \$

Souren Paul <sup>a,</sup>\*, William D. Haseman <sup>b,1</sup>, K. Ramamurthy <sup>b,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management, College of Business and Administration, Southern Illinois University at Carbondale, Carbondale, IL 62901-4627, USA

<sup>b</sup>School of Business Administration, University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee, Milwaukee, WI 53201, USA

Received 1 May 2002; accepted 1 July 2002

## Abstract

Collective memory is an active research area and researchers have focused on several social psychological perspective of collective memory. A form of collective memory is organizational memory that has emerged as a topic of considerable importance in recent years. Significant work has been appearing about how information technology (IT) can enable and support organizational memory systems to be developed that, in turn, facilitate organizational learning. However, much of the existing work on this area is conceptual or theoretical with very little empirical examination. In this research we discuss how a hypermedia-based prototype of collective memory, was used in a controlled experiment to investigate empirical validity of the consequential effects of using memory support on cognitive-conflict type of decisions. The results indicate that the use of collective memory information provides the study participants focused attention on the cognitive-conflict task domain and leads to faster decision-making. The results also indicate that there may be a potential for collective memory support to lead to encased learning. However, the results do not manifest any effect of collective memory on the perceived decision quality. Implications for research and practice and directions for future research are discussed. A revised, more robust but also more complex model derived from the results of the study is proposed for future research and validation. <sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Collective memory; Organizational memory; Group memory; Decision analysis; Decision-making efficiency; Decision-making process; Cognitive-conflict tasks; Decision-making speed; Thoroughness in decision-making

## 1. Introduction

Many organizational decision-making situations are recurring in nature. The decision issues are similar yet the groups engaged in decision-making may be different so far as the composition of the groups is concerned. Committees making decision on studentadmission or new faculty recruitment are some examples of this type of decision. Each time a new committee gets involved in this type of decision, decisionmakers are likely to spend significant time in learning the decision situation. The situation becomes more complicated if the decision task is of the cognitiveconflict type, that is, the decision-making activities are fraught with conflicting viewpoints of the stakeholders on how to reach the final decision. For example, the members of a committee making decision on offering admission may have conflicting views on how different attributes (such as, standardized test scores, GPA, recommendation letters) will be prioritized. Faced with cognitive-conflict issues, the decision-makers may want to know how these conflicts were resolved in the past. To the extent this information is available to all of them, a shared meaning of the conflict resolution scheme (that has worked in the past) may get developed and used in the current decision situation. In addition, in the context of organizations, overarching issues such as, organizational objectives, policies and procedures often influence group decisions; yet, in large organizations, even senior management may not always be fully aware of all the policies and procedures. To the extent that such information and knowledge are not easily available, individual and group decisions can get significantly compromised. Thus, it appears logical that the development of a shared repository that stores the experience invoked in prior decisions and retains the rules, policies and standard procedures of an organization can be useful for subsequent groups engaged in similar decision-making activities. This shared repository together with the appropriate means for managing its content is in fact an implementation of collective memory, which is the focus of this research.

The recent interest in collective memory can be traced to the works of Halbwachs [16] who asserted that memories have a collective context within which they are formed and organized. Zarecka [48] describes collective memory as a socially articulated and socially maintained reality of the past. Collective memory is contrasted from history. In the words of Schwartz [40,p,470], ‘‘History is objectively conceived, sustained by evidence, and unaffected by the social context in which practitioners work. In contrast, collective memory, the way ordinary people conceive the past, reflects the concern of the present.’’

An instance of collective memory is organizational memory that has emerged as an area of considerable research interest in recent years. Significant work has appeared in organizational literature describing various types of and perspectives about memory [7,17, 28,37,45]. Its potential consequential benefits include organizational learning, lower transactional costs and lower resistance to decisions [18,45]. Interest has surfaced about how information technology (IT) can enable and support organizational memory systems to be developed that can, in turn, facilitate organizational learning [20,24,30,42]. However, much of the extant work on this area is conceptual or theoretical with very little empirical examination.

This paper examines the effect of using collective memory on cognitive-conflict type of group decisions. Drawing from relevant theory bases, a research model depicting the influence of memory on the degree of group consensus, speed of decision-making, thoroughness in analysis of the decision problem, and perceived decision quality is presented in this paper. Moving beyond, we discuss how a prototype system displaying collective memory information was used in a controlled experiment to investigate empirically the consequential effects of using collective memory in cognitive-conflict type of decisions.

The paper is structured as follows. Section 2 discusses the concepts of collective/organizational memory and its potential influence on cognitive-conflict type of decisions. In Section 3, the research design is discussed. The results of the study are presented and analyzed in Section 4. Concluding remarks including implications and limitations of the study are discussed in Section 5.

## 2. Research framework

## 2.1. The collective memory

Memory is the faculty of retaining and recalling things from past (American Heritage Dictionary, 1969). The word ‘‘memory’’ usually refers to individual memory. Although the primary focus of memoryrelated research is on individuals, Halbwachs [15] proposed the idea of there being memory at the collective level. Collective memory is a socially constructed notion [35]. Members of different social groups and institutions (such as family, associations, organizations) draw on their current context to recreate the past. Commemoration of past events, festivals, social get-together all evoke collective memory among the participants. An instance of collective memory is organizational memory, which is defined as ‘‘the means by which knowledge from the past is brought to bear on present activities, thus resulting in higher or lower levels of organizational effectiveness’’ [42,p,89]. It has been viewed in many ways: as a function of people and artifacts [28], as being dependent on the dominant coalition or upper echelon in an organization [17], as an interpretative system [7], and as the organization itself [12]. Organizational memory is an active research area in information systems and organizational science.

The organizational memory has two different roles—interpretation role and action guidance role [29]. Organizational memory can act as a filter to categorize information and knowledge (interpretation role); it can also influence the action of an individual or group (action guidance role). A major use of organizational memory is to facilitate organizational learning [18]. Conditions favorable to learning occur when organizational knowledge is stored in organizational memory and made available for shared interpretation by others [6]; organizational memory plays a significant role in the process of organizational learning [20]. Other uses of organizational memory are reducing resistance to making decision choices and lowering transaction costs to implement the chosen decision [45]. However, a major misuse of memory by users has been cited to be the encased learning that may invoke routine responses to nonroutine situations [42]. Appropriate enabling information technologies (e.g. databases, knowledge management systems, hypermedia) to support various dimensions of organizational memory have been proposed and technical issues in each discussed [10,24,42].

Another form of collective memory is group memory, which can be used in various types of group work such as, meetings and projects. The usefulness of group memory has been highlighted by the researchers in group support systems (GSS) [24,32,47] and is summarized next in this section. Although GSS literature has almost always used the label ‘‘group memory’’ we prefer to use it interchangeably with collective memory in this paper.

## 2.2. Group/collective memory in group work and cognitive-conflict tasks

Nunamaker et al. [32] mention group memory as a component in the University of Arizona’s GroupSystems, an electronic meeting system. Weiser and Morrison [47] describe the use of a project memory that captures, retains, and indexes project information so that people external to the project can use it later. The key motivations for using a group (or collective) memory in these situations are [24]:

n Provide access to common organizational/group information (e.g. mission, goals, objectives and basic policies).

n Capture, store and integrate information generated during group interaction.

n Provide means to educate the new members about the previous activities of the group.

n Support multi-stage group tasks that last for months or years and have attrition of existing group members and inclusion of new members.

Although the collective memory support is useful for most of the task types presented in McGrath’s [27] group task circumplex, in this research, we concentrate on the cognitive-conflict tasks. The group members engaged in a cognitive-conflict task have a clear goal but diverse viewpoints to start with. In addition to having different viewpoints and preferences, participants in cognitive-conflict tasks are likely to have systematically different preference structures [27]. ‘‘They may interpret information differently, may give different weights to different dimensions, and/or may relate dimensions to preferences via different functional forms’’ [27,p,64]. The key issue in the cognitive-conflict task is to resolve the conflict of preference structure. The task participants can benefit from the use of a repository that includes various preference structures used for similar tasks in the past. For each preference structure, it can store the difference instances of its use and the consequent outcome. For example, a committee making decision on admission of students to a program can use a collective memory that contains information on various criteria (such as, GPA, test score, etc.) used in the past, the decision rule employed (such as, lexicographic, elimination by aspect, additive linear, etc.), values/weights of each criteria under a specific rule and the consequent decision outcome. The realities of the past that are embedded in the repository and shared by later groups constitute the collective memory. The participants can interpret it to develop a better understanding about the preference structures that have resolved cognitive conflicts in the past and resulted in solutions; they can revise their decision/judgment policy accordingly so that conflicts of views are resolved and agreement on decision is reached.

In the rest of this section, we discuss the dependent variables that are relevant in situations involving cognitive-conflict type of decisions and are likely to be influenced by the use of a collective memory.

## 2.3. Influence of collective memory on cognitiveconflict outcome variables

An important dependent variable in cognitive-conflict type of decisions is the agreement among group members [27]. Consensus is the complete agreement of a group on its outcome and is one of the most widely studied dependent variable in GSS research [11]. Other dependent variables that have been studied frequently in GSS research and have some degree of relevance in cognitive-conflict decisions are decision quality, decision time, participation, satisfaction with decision-making process, and thoroughness of task analysis. As cognitive-conflict tasks do not have any correct answer, determination of decision quality by comparing the outcome of group work with any prespecified/desired outcome may not be feasible; however, the decision quality as perceived by the participants is an important dependent variable for this type of group work. Participation is a process variable that is important in GSS research. By supporting anonymity and parallel communication, GDSS reduces conformance pressure, evaluation apprehension, and domination by any particular group member [33] and, as a result, encourages equal participation among group members. As the use of collective memory does not provide any additional communication support, it seems unlikely that equality of participation of group members will change due to the use of collective memory. Furthermore, as would be discussed later, the same underlying electronic meeting software (VisionnQuest) is used for both the treatment (those with memory support) and control groups (those without memory support). Since it is the underlying GSS software that enables anonymity and parallel communication, helps to reduce conformance pressure, and domination by any particular group member noted above, the variable ‘‘satisfaction with the decision process’’ is kept outside the realm of this research. Collective memory has the potential to facilitate group learning; thus, it is expected that the users of collective memory will acquire critical knowledge on the group task and be capable of analyzing the group task more thoroughly. The collective memory can be viewed as an internal information/knowledge source for the groups using it. The existence of this internal source within a group helps to even out differences in perceived task complexity among the members [36]. It is our conjecture that groups having even perception about task complexity will have greater level of agreement and hence a shorter/better decision time.

In the following section, we discuss the nature of influence that collective memory may have on dependent variables and state our hypotheses. The four dependent variables reviewed here are group agreement, time to reach decision, thoroughness of the task analysis, and perceived decision quality.

## 2.4. Study hypothesis

## 2.4.1. Group agreement

Prior research on group consensus in computermediated group work has shown that either there is no effect on consensus or that computer-mediated groups are less likely to reach consensus [21]. Watson et al.’s [46] study did not find any difference in the post-meeting consensus among the GDSS, manual, and free interacting groups. However, the study did find that post-meeting consensus was positively related to pre-meeting consensus in manual and GDSS groups, whereas there was no relationship in the free interacting groups. This implies that the structure provided to the manual and GDSS groups enabled them to reach pre-meeting consensus and build upon it to achieve post-meeting consensus. Dennis [8] finds that GSS groups do not manifest greater consensus change than non-GSS groups. He attributes this to the lack of adequate information processing support for the GSS groups. We propose that the integration of collective memory with the GSS is likely to provide additional information processing support. A collective memory that has wellorganized historical information and anticipated trajectories (i.e. the projected consequences of the later uses of the memory) is expected to assist decisionmakers in evaluating new information and identifying its linkage with the existing knowledgebase of the memory. By critically analyzing the decision task in the context of historical information, decision-makers can arrive at a choice that has its roots in the collective experience of the previous groups engaged in similar tasks. A decision thus made has attached with it a sense of legitimacy [45] that is less likely to be contradicted by any member of the group. In being able to improve their understanding of the task and provide a framework for justification of the group task, the use of collective memory is expected to improve the degree of group agreement in cognitive-conflict tasks. Hence:

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 } } .$ . The use of collective memory will improve the degree of group agreement in cognitive-conflict type of decisions.

## 2.4.2. Speed of decision-making

The time to reach a decision is related to the ability of a group to reach consensus [13]. Prior empirical work on decision time indicates that computer groups usually take longer time (i.e. slower) to reach a decision than noncomputer supported groups [13,21]. A plausible explanation for this is put forward by [13]; they suggest that unlike face-toface meetings, GDSS based meetings perhaps suffer from the absence of a leader who normally guides the actions of a group and pushes the group to his/ her preferred solution. While by no means a substitution for a group leader, the addition of a collective memory support to a GSS can alleviate this problem to some extent. Collective memory with its ‘‘action guidance’’ role can provide a direction or focus to the group work. Group members can justify their decisions by drawing reference from the collective memory. Additionally, collective memory plays an ‘‘information interpretation’’ role and provides increased information processing support. As a result, group members are likely to spend less time trying to comprehend the task. The combined effect of faster comprehension of the task and convergence on a group outcome (that is based on past experience) is expected to reduce the decision time or enhance the decision-speed significantly. Hence:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 } .$ . The use of collective memory will improve the speed of group decision-making in cognitive-conflict type of decisions.

## 2.4.3. Thoroughness of task analysis

A GSS provides support for parallelism and anonymity and, in the process, increases information exchange in a group and enhances the potential for experiencing information overload [8]. A collective memory that contains information about similar cognitive-conflict tasks may be used by decision-makers to identify the relevant preference structures (of the participants) that underpin the prior decision-making sessions. The group members may draw on this historical information to frame sharper questions [31] and build up trajectories for the future [19]. The use of collective memory can facilitate group learning. By becoming familiar with the relevant preference structures that worked in the past and is likely to work in future, the group members can concentrate only on the relevant information and analyze it thoroughly to arrive at a decision. Hence:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 3 } .$ . The use of collective memory will improve the thoroughness in the analysis of the cognitive-conflict type of decisions.

## 2.4.4. Perceived decision quality

As noted above, the use of collective memory is expected to promote learning and provide increased level of information processing support. By analyzing the contents of the collective memory, the group members can develop a better understanding of the task environment. Also, the group can assess and justify the group outcome (i.e. the group level preference structure) by drawing upon references from the collective memory. This is likely to enhance the group members’ confidence and their perceived usefulness of their own work. Improved confidence in and perceived usefulness of the decision are expected to be reflected as greater perceived decision quality of the group members with the decision outcome. Hence:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 4 } .$ . The use of collective memory will improve the perceived decision quality of the group members with their group’s decision outcome.

## 3. Research design

## 3.1. Choice of research type and setting

In their meta-analysis of all GSS studies based on experiments, Fjermestad and Hiltz [11] report that by mid-1998, about 200 different controlled experiments have been published in major refereed articles and conference proceedings. Of these 200 experiments, 90% used students. The group size was two to five members in about 72% of the studies. About 95% of the studies used ad hoc rather than established groups. Since it is even more difficult to identify a priori natural (real-world) settings where collective memory information systems are in use, research approaches such as field study or field experiment were not feasible. Therefore, in line with previous research tradition as noted above, laboratory experiments using ad hoc groups were deemed the most appropriate to conduct research on collective memory at this time. Laboratory experiments are replicable and are suitable for research on decision-making processes [2]. We took extraordinary precautions to address the shortcomings of previous studies that have been recently reported by Fjermestad and Hiltz [11]. As would be noted later, our study was better designed so far as sample size, the number of groups per treatment condition, and the session duration are concerned. In addition to the selection of appropriate type of laboratory experimental approach, the research design involves identification of the cognitive-conflict task that will be employed in the experiment. We start with the description of the task.

## 3.2. The cognitive-conflict task

The decision situation considered in this research is the development of a group level judgment policy on the attributes of MBA admission. Undergraduate students are assigned to groups (of five members). Each participant identifies and prioritizes a set of attributes of MBA programs that a typical undergraduate student is likely to consider while evaluating various programs. Each participant may differ from others in terms of their judgment (of the importance of various attributes and the combination of attributes to be considered in ranking MBA programs). The participants receive feedback on their own judgment policy (i.e. attribute weights) and that of the group as a whole. Each individual in the group can then revise his/her judgment policy until a jointly decided judgment policy is reached. This task is classified as a cognitive-conflict task because multiple conflicting solution schemes may be presented by the participants depending on their individual preference structures for MBA attributes. The decision task has significant similarity with the techniques followed in social judgment theory (SJT) paradigm [27]. Turban and Aronson [43] also discuss a similar group task where students were engaged in group work to identify the criteria for college selection.

After reviewing different publications and web sites on MBA admission (such as, the Graduate Management Admissions Council, Princeton Review, Peterson’s Guide, etc.), a fixed set of attributes (Appendix A) was selected. Each decision-making group selected a subset of these attributes and prioritized them by allocating 100 points among them (attributes). Based on the attribute weights allocated by a group, a rank-ordered list of schools was generated following the principle of the ‘‘simple additive weighting’’ method [4,22,26]. For example, if the minimum and maximum values for tuition fees of schools are $T _ { \mathrm { m i n } }$ and $T _ { \mathrm { m a x } } ,$ respectively, the rating of a school $^ { \mathfrak { s } \mathfrak { e } } _ { 1 } ^ { \mathfrak { s } }$ having a tuition fee of $T _ { \mathrm { i } }$ is given by $[ 1 - ( T _ { \mathrm { i } } - T _ { \mathrm { m i n } } ) / ( T _ { \mathrm { m a x } } - T _ { \mathrm { m i n } } ) ]$ . This will produce a rating of schools on the attribute ‘‘tuition’’ in the range of 0–1. An overall score for each school (across the various attributes) is computed by multiplying the weight on each attribute by the normalized rating for the school on that attribute [4,26]. Finally, the schools are ordered in the sequence of their final overall scores. The rank-ordered list of schools produced in this approach was, thus, a consequence of the decision made on the selection of attributes and allocation of their weights. By examining the combination of attribute weights and rank-ordered list of schools, group members could develop an idea about how schools are rated on various attributes. If a group was satisfied with the combination of attribute weights and rank-ordered list of schools, the decision-making process could come to an end; otherwise, attribute weights had to be revised and a new rankordered list of schools generated.

## 3.3. The prototype

The prototype consisted of two major modules: MBA admissions information system and collective memory information system. Both the information systems were implemented as hypermedia-based web pages. The main page of this system had links to the individual attribute pages and MBA program pages; in addition, the main page for the treatment groups had links to the collective memory information pages. The hypermedia-based information system was integrated with groupware (VisionQuest) system used to drive the electronic group meeting.

Collective memory information system: After discussing with faculty experts and a few prospective users, the following information was included in the collective memory information system:

(1) various attributes selected by the previous groups,

(2) percentage of groups selecting an attribute,

(3) average weights allocated to each attribute,

(4) the rank-ordered list of schools based on the attributes selected and the average weights allocated by peer groups.

Various other types of information could be included in the collective memory, such as the dispersion of views of group members in arriving at the final outcomes, rationale for allocating the attribute weights, messages exchanged to resolve (any) cognitive-conflict, etc. Given that the system would be used in a laboratory setting with sessions of limited duration, a judicious choice had to be made on the type and amount of information embedded in the prototype. Had the collective memory information system been more elaborate, the participants would probably spend much more time in understanding the memory information, thereby, resulting in only limited time available for analyzing and making the decision.

The use of a hypermedia-based system enabled the participants to navigate in nonsequential, multi-path manner. The attributes and the rank-ordered schools (items 1 and 4) presented in the collective memory were linked to individual attribute and school pages. Not only could (and did) treatment groups look at details of these pages but also browsed information on attributes/schools not listed in the memory.

The hypermedia prototype was placed in a web server on an Intranet, which was integrated with VisionQuest, the electronic meeting system. Since the web pages of the prototype were deployed on the Intranet, they could be accessed only by the participants in the experiment. One of the dependent variables in this research was decision speed, which is related to decision time. Had the hypermedia-based prototype been placed on the World Wide Web, group members might have wasted time in browsing pages that were not related to MBA program attribute selection. This would have confounded the decision time measurements.

## 3.4. The nature of experiment

After reviewing various forms of laboratory experiment designs discussed in the literature [3,5], the ‘‘Posttest-Only control group design’’ was chosen as the most appropriate approach for this research.

The next important issue for experimental design was the group size. Non-GSS research indicates that regardless of the nature of task, context or group, the ‘‘optimal’’ group size is three to five members [41]. However, research on GSS has usually shown that larger groups generate more ideas and are more satisfied [9]. In our research, the groups did not have to generate new ideas. It was easier to administer experiments on smaller groups. In view of this, smaller group size (having three to five members) was considered in this study. Three-member groups would be too small for our research where group consensus was a dependent variable. Four-member groups could result in a ‘‘tie’’ while voting on the decision outcome. Thus, in this research, the group size was fixed at five. This is also in line with most previous studies in GSS research as observed by Fjermestad and Hiltz [11]; they found that nearly 88% or 146 of the 166 studies (out of a population of 200) reporting group size had two to five members.

## 3.5. Operationalization of variables

The independent variable of this research is the availability of collective memory. The dependent variables are degree of group agreement, speed of group decision-making, thoroughness in analysis of the decision, and perceived decision quality. The control variables are the decision task, experimental task, motivation of the subjects and familiarity with the system. The detailed research model is shown in Fig. 1.

Availability of collective memory: The ‘‘control’’ groups did not have any access to the collective memory prototype used in this experiment, whereas this was made available to the ‘‘treatment’’ groups. The variable can assume only one of the two possible values: having or not having access to the prototype of collective memory.

Degree of group agreement: Participants in each group voted for the decision outcome. The degree of group agreement was measured in terms of the number of votes cast in favor of the decision (referred to as ‘‘for votes’’) in the final round of decision-making. Maximum ‘‘for votes’’ could be 5; the larger this value the greater would be the degree of consensus. This has some similarity with the approach followed by George et al. [14] who measured group consensus as the presence or absence of complete agreement. A second approach to measuring consensus was to recognize the number of iterations undertaken by the group in its deliberations before making the final decision. Lesser number of iterations might indicate fewer differences of opinion within the group.

Speed of group decision-making: This was measured as the elapsed time between the start and end of decision-making activities of a group. The lower the elapsed time, the higher is the speed of decisionmaking.

Thoroughness of task analysis: The following two measures were chosen (as proxy) for the thoroughness of task analysis:

Total number of information pages browsed by a group: At the beginning of the experiment, each group had to browse web pages on MBA programs and attributes of the MBA program for school selection. Participants could also browse the web pages at any time during the experiment. The total number of school pages and attribute pages browsed by the members in a group was an indicator of the group’s thoroughness of analysis of the problem. Greater number of pages browsed suggested a more complete information search behavior and a more thorough analysis.

n Number of attributes selected by the group: A fixed set of 18 attributes (Appendix A) was presented to the participants. Group members allocated weights to one or more attributes in the list. The selection of larger number of attributes by group members from this set can be viewed as an effort to analyze the MBA programs from various perspectives. The more the number of attributes considered by the participants, the greater is the extent of analysis being sought. As such, the number of attributes selected was considered to be one more indicator of thoroughness of decision-making.

Perceived decision quality: This construct was measured with the help of a Likert scale questionnaire. The seven indicator variables (#1 to 7) are shown in Appendix B. Drawing upon the ideas from existing literature on user satisfaction [1,23], the scale includes indicators of various aspects of decision outcome, such as relevance, usability, reliability, comprehen siveness and accuracy. Item #8 was used to evaluate predictive validity of the multi-item measure for ‘‘perceived decision quality’’.

Control variables: The control variables were the cognitive-conflict task selected, experimental tasks to be performed, motivation of subjects and familiarity with the system. The cognitive-conflict task in this study was identical for both ‘‘control’’ and ‘‘treatment’’ groups. Both groups performed the same set of experimental tasks, with the only exception being the access to collective memory made available only to the ‘‘treatment’’ groups. All subjects were expected to have the same degree of motivation to participate in the experiment because the participation resulted in a waiver for an assignment in the course. In order to ensure that students had some personal stake and interest in this group task, as noted above, it was announced (at the start of each session of the experiment) that an expert panel would assess the quality and performance of group work and that the grade of each student in this assignment would depend on this evaluation. In addition, to identify whether the participants of this study had sufficient stake and motivation, we gathered information from them on their plan to pursue an MBA. Almost 70% of the subjects (186 out of 270 participants; 37 out of 54 five-person teams) expressed a clear plan to enroll into an MBA program within 1 to 2 years of graduation. Of the 54 five-person groups participating in the experiment, 37 groups indicated future MBA plans (17 belonging to the control groups and 20 to the memory-supported groups). There was, however, no statistically significant difference in the distribution of MBA plans by the two groups (v<sup>2</sup> = 0.773, df = 1, p = 0.379).

The subjects in the study did not have any familiarity with the experimental set-up prior to their participation in it. Instruction sheets were provided for each workstation and participants were not allowed to take these with them after the experiment was over. They were advised not to discuss the experiment with the classmates. All these controls were instituted to ensure similar degree of familiarity with the system for the users. ## 3.6. The experiment

The subjects in the experiment were undergraduate business students of a large state university in the midwestern part of the US. A total of 54 five-member groups participated in the experiments; such a large number of groups have been quite infrequent in previous research [11]. Subject to the time preferences indicated by the students, participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups in the experiment. The group members were subjected to a structured task (discussed in points #1 through #9 in Appendix C). Each group had a coordinating workstation. The participant using the coordinating workstation had to do some additional tasks (discussed in points #4 and #5 in Appendix C). Face-to-face communication among the group members was discouraged. However, communication between the coordinator (i.e. the participant with coordinating workstation) and other members of the group was allowed but only at some specific steps to indicate transition from one activity to another (such as, end of browsing the Intranet web pages, end of allocation of weights, beginning next iteration, and ending of experiment). The groups, therefore, worked under restricted task performance process and restricted interaction. McGrath [27] calls such groups as quasi-groups and advocates that information generated from studies involving these types of groups can bear on a number of research questions about groups and should not be discarded.

Each experiment session could last up to 2 h, and a maximum of four groups could participate in a session. Three sessions were scheduled on each day, and the experiments were administered over a period of 10 days. The experiments were conducted in an electronic meeting room equipped with VisionQuest. The subjects in the ‘‘control group’’ were scheduled first in the experiment. Data from these sessions were analyzed to identify the judgment pattern of the ‘‘control’’ groups. This pattern was presented in the collective memory, which was then made available to the ‘‘treatment’’ groups.

Pilot sessions were held before conducting the actual experiment sessions. The participants in the pilot sessions did not have any difficulty in working with the system. Based on the performance of the participants in the pilot sessions, it was decided that a two-hour session would be adequate for conducting the study. Most of the participating groups in the pilot tests as well as in the actual experiments completed their task well within two hours. They did not work hastily. Their observed behavior in the experimental sessions did not leave any impression that they viewed the two-hour duration as a time constraint. A systems specialist was present in the sessions to clarify any questions about the system and/or the task.

The experimental procedure is presented in Appendix C.

## 4. Study results

## 4.1. Reliability and validity issues

The measures of the dependent variables clearly fall into two categories: objective and self-reported perceptions. Some of the objective measures such as the number of ‘‘for votes’’, ‘‘number of iterations’’, and ‘‘number of attributes’’ for each group were measured by VisionQuest. VisionQuest is a commercial Groupware that has been used extensively, and its measures are considered to be reliable. The server log file data and Microsoft Access database programs were used to measure ‘‘decision time’’ and ‘‘the number of information pages browsed’’. The processing mechanism for raw data had been tested extensively to ensure its reliability.

Likert type scales were used to measure selfreported perceptual variables such as computer skill and perceived decision quality. Three experts on group decision-making and attitude measurement conducted an initial review of these measures to establish their face validity. In this research, Cronbach Alpha Coefficient was used to assess the internal consistency reliability of the measures. Since the measurement scales used in the experiment have not been tested and validated before and in view of the exploratory nature of this research, a cut-off value 0.70 for Alpha was considered acceptable [34]. Cronbach Alpha value was found to be 0.789 for ‘‘perceived decision quality’’ measure. The result obtained is higher than the 0.70 threshold thus indicating satisfactory reliability of the measure.

As suggested in the literature, content validity was established by examining the correlation matrix of the indicator variables for a construct [34]. For ‘‘perceived decision quality’’ scale, all inter-item correlation coefficients were 0.214 or better $( p \le 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Another indication of content validity of the scale was reflected in the statistically significant correlation each item in the scale had with the corrected composite measure of ‘‘perceived quality of the decision’’ (i.e. total of the indicator variables less the particular indicator). All correlation coefficients were greater than 0.445 ( p V 0.0001).

To examine convergent validity, factor analyses employing VARIMAX orthogonal rotation was carried out. The factor analysis of seven items representing ‘‘perceived decision quality’’ scale produced a single-factor structure with a factor loading ranging from 0.563 to 0.767.

Finally, predictive validity was evaluated by examining the correlation between the pre-defined/emergent multi-item scale (of perceived decision quality) and a second measure of perceived decision quality; that is, ‘‘overall satisfaction with the decision’’ indicator variable (#8 in Appendix B). The correlation between the seven-item scale and the overall satisfaction with the decision was strong (r = 0.566; p V 0.0001) indicating quite a high predictive validity of the multi-indicator scale [34].

## 4.2. Analysis of the profile of participants

Although lot of care was taken to randomly assign subjects across experimental groups, it was necessary to ensure if such an assignment indeed occurred. A number of person-specific, demographic variables (age, gender, GPA, and Internet skill) were statistically tested for random distribution of subjects across the two experimental groups. Chi-square, for categorical variables (e.g. gender) and t-Test (for metric variables) were performed to ascertain random assignment of study subjects. The results are displayed in Table 2.

The analyses reveal that some degree of bias existed with respect to GPA; it was in favor of the control group (mean GPA 3.14 for control versus 2.98 for treatment groups; $t { = } 2 . 8 9 1 , p { \leq } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . There was no bias in the distribution of members by gender, age or Internet skill across the two groups. Subsequent data analyses will control for the bias in GPA in Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) tests.

## 4.3. Hypothesis testing

In addition to t-tests, in light of the above-noted bias (GPA in favor of the ‘‘control group’’) analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was employed to test the hypotheses. GPA was treated as a covariate. Furthermore, to make sure that the results were not in any way confounded by a lack of stake or interest on the part of the subjects in the study, their plan to pursue MBA was included in the ANCOVA models as an additional main factor. SAS software was used for the statistical analyses to develop General linear models (GLM) for ANCOVA. Significance level of 0.05 or better was used for testing the hypotheses in this research. Any significance level in the range of

0.05 – 0.10 would indicate weak significance and was treated as suggestive of the nature of relationship between the variables. The results of hypotheses testing are shown in Tables 3 (t-Test) and 4 (ANCOVA).

## 4.3.1. H<sub>1</sub>: Degree of group agreement

The hypothesis on the degree of group agreement was not supported in the t-test or ANCOVA. However, the treatment groups made use of fewer rounds of iteration. The group members undertook additional iteration of decision-making when the total number of votes in favor of the decision was less than 4. It can be argued that the treatment groups had a lower level of disagreement. The issue of group consensus/agreement, therefore, depicts two different situations in this research:

Once a group has reached the final decision, what is the degree of agreement among the group members? This is reflected in terms of ‘‘for votes’’ which is the number of votes cast in favor of the decision in the final round of decision-making.

n What is the overall level of agreement among the group members since the beginning of the experiment? This is reflected (indirectly) in terms of the numbers of iterations undertaken by a group. From this perspective, an indication of a higher degree of group level agreement may be the pursuit of fewer iterations of decision-making.

While the first perspective takes a cross-sectional view of the final round of decision-making, the second view traces the entire decision-making process. As would be observed from Tables 3 and 4 (note that we use declared ‘‘MBA Plan’’ of the participants as an additional main factor), the treatment groups undertook lesser number of iterations than the control groups. In order to eliminate the possibility that the participants (for innumerable reasons of their own) may have wanted to complete the experiment quickly and get out, the ‘‘decision time’’ was included as covariate in an additional ANCOVA. After controlling for decision-time (shorter in the case of groups using collective memory information), a significant difference $( F { = } 3 . 5 0 6 , p { = } 0 . 0 6 8 )$ in the number of iterations is obtained in favor of the treatment groups. This demonstrates the possibility that treatment groups perhaps achieved higher degree of group agreement. However, the ‘‘for votes’’ of treatment and control groups were not significantly different (even after controlling for the covariates) suggesting that both types of groups had similar degree of group agreement in the final round of decision-making. However, they reached this final state via different paths; the groups with memory support are more likely to have reached this (somewhat similar voting consensus) with less conflicting views.

Further analyses of the voting pattern revealed that 70.4% of the control groups (19 of 27 groups) realizes consensual decisions, i.e. 100% agreement while only 51.9% (14 out of 27) of treatment groups realizes consensual decisions. The treatment groups may decide to conclude the sessions with majority (not necessarily consensual) decisions perhaps because they use the historical information (on the preference structure of prior groups) to justify their decision. On the other hand, the control groups do not have any access to the historical information and as a result, they feel more confident to end the group work only when a complete agreement on the final outcome is reached. It will be desirable for future research to replicate the findings observed here.

## 4.3.2. $H _ { 2 } ;$ Speed of group decision-making

The results (of both the t-test in Table 3 and ANCOVA in Table 4) reveal that the use of collective memory significantly increased the speed of decisionmaking (Table 3: t = 1.88, $p \leq 0 . 0 6 6$ ; Table 4: $F { = } 4 . 1 6 2 , \ p { < } 0 . 0 4 8 )$ . The (somewhat obvious) explanation could be the fact that the treatment groups benefited from some degree of focused attention on the decision situation after using the collective memory support. Also, as just discussed above, the use of collective memory perhaps enables treatment groups to finalize decisions when the majority of the members agreed on the outcome, whereas the control groups strive to reach a complete agreement among its members and hence perform more iterations to reach the final decision. Focused attention and reduced number of iterations lead to shortened decision time for teams using collective memory support.

## 4.3.3. H<sub>3</sub>: Thoroughness in task analysis

The results indicate that collective memory significantly influences the thoroughness in the analysis of the cognitive-conflict task. However, the nature of the influence is opposite to that of the stated hypothesis. The two (surrogate) measures of thoroughness used in this research are the number of attributes considered by a group in the final decision and the total number of information pages browsed (and perhaps analyzed) by members of the group.

The t-test shows that the number of attributes selected was significantly higher for the control groups, which is contradictory to the theory presented in this study. Even after the effect of covariates (GPA in Table 4 and decision-time) was controlled in the ANCOVA, the number of attributes continues to be significantly different between the control and treatment groups.

Total number of information pages is the aggregate of the MBA program pages and the attribute pages browsed by all members of a group. The results of ANCOVA reveal that the use of collective memory has a very significant influence on the total number of information pages browsed by the groups. Again, the mean for the total number of information pages was higher for the control groups, suggesting that the control groups may have searched for more information and perhaps analyzed the task more thoroughly. This is just the opposite of what we stated in Hypothesis 3. A possible explanation of this information search behavior can be found in the work of Rao and An [36]. The treatment groups perhaps had greater level of internal knowledge (i.e. the collective memory information) and needed lesser amount of information from other sources (i.e. the attribute and school pages) to make the decision.

Additional analyses were undertaken to examine if the groups using memory information ended up moving through the decision-making process mechanistically falling into a trap of ‘‘group think’’ and ‘‘mental lazing’’. Their pattern of browsing the attribute pages of the seven attributes presented in the memory as well as the remaining 11 attributes is presented, respectively, in Table 5A and B.

Two key facts emerge from these additional analyses. While it is true that control groups access and evaluate more number of attribute pages than treatment groups on both the 7 attributes (that were finally placed in the memory) as well as the remaining 11 attributes, memory-supported groups do not become a prey to ‘‘group-think’’ or ‘‘mental lazing’’. They do go beyond the information/knowledge presented in the memory and engage in additional analysis (in this case, examining the information on other attributes). Furthermore, if the average number of times an attribute page is accessed is used as an indicator of their ranking of importance of that attribute for evaluation, then both the control and memory-supported groups appear to behave quite similarly. Additional analysis of these tables indicates that their ranking pattern for information acquisition and evaluation is identical for 9 out of 18 attributes (#2, 5, 9, 10, 11, 13, 14, 16, and 18), their ranking varies by one point in either direction in four instances (#1, 7, 8, and 12), and the ranking varies by two points for the remaining 5 attributes (#3, 4, 6, 15, and 17). This suggests that memory support does not bind the recipients to the information presented in the memory and blind them from further exploration and additional analyses.

In summary, the statistical analyses reveal that the use of collective memory has significant effect on both measures of the ‘‘thoroughness in analysis of decision problem’’. However, the nature of influence is, prima facie, opposite of what has been stated in Hypothesis 3. This lends some credence to the rationale put forth by researchers [42] that the use of memory (group and organizational) may lead to encased learning. However, as discussed above, the memory-supported groups in this study appear to have behaved in a much more complex fashion.

The collective memory presented to the treatment groups in this research included only seven attributes. These attributes were ordered in the sequence of the average weights attached to them by other peer groups. A final set of additional statistical analysis was done to investigate whether control and treatment groups allocated significantly different weights to these attributes. The total weights for all seven, top-six, top-five, topfour, top-three, and top-two and the top-one attributes were taken into consideration and t-tests were conducted on them. The results are shown Table 6.

The results show that the treatment groups allocated significantly more (cumulative) weights to the top seven attributes than did control groups. At the same time, however, it can also be noted that the treatment groups assigned more (individual) weights to the ‘‘Top 1’’ (i.e. Tuition Fee) and ‘‘Top 4’’ (i.e. Time to Degree) and less (individual) weights to the ‘‘Top 7’’ attribute (i.e. Existence of Executive MBA Program). There are no significant differences on the remaining four top attributes. It may also be noted that except in two instances (of assignment of individual weights), there is lesser variability (see standard deviation values) in the values assigned by treatment groups vis-a\`-vis control groups. In conjunction with the results presented in Table 5A and B, such behavior demonstrates that memory-supported groups do evaluate additional pieces of information and there is no systematic bias. However, they may tend to concentrate more on the attributes presented in the collective memory. At a cumulative level, the best level of significance is obtained for the top-four and top-five attributes, after which the level of significance drops. Perhaps, this indicates the existence of an optimum level of focused attention on decision situation by the treatment groups. The issue of focused attention may explain why treatment groups took less time to arrive at a decision, undertook fewer number of iterations, browsed fewer information pages and perhaps considered fewer attributes before making a decision.

## 4.3.4. H<sub>4</sub>: Perceived decision quality

The results of the statistical analyses do not indicate any significant influence of collective memory on perceived decision quality. One possible reason for this finding is the fact that groups had only a onesided view of the decision situation. This was a ‘‘oneshot’’ experiment. Had the groups been engaged at least twice (with and without group memory) in the experimental sessions, a relative assessment of perceptual measures would have been possible.

## 5. Conclusions

The importance of organizational memory support systems has been advanced in the literature for quite some time and researchers [29,42,45] have proposed beneficial consequences of organizational memory. This study is among the first to make a maiden attempt to evaluate the effects of collective memory on cognitive-conflict type of group decision.

The findings of the research study confirmed some of the consequential effects. The use of collective memory instilled in the participants more focused attention on the decision situation. There are enough indications that with appropriate changes in measures, other consequential effects (such as degree of group agreement) may be better demonstrable. Replication and extension of this research by conducting multiple trials with different types of group tasks and groups are needed to ensure external validity of the model and arrive at any generalized theory on the consequences of using the collective memory.

## 5.1. Implications for practice

While the concept of organizational memory or group memory has been mentioned in the research literature for quite some time [25,45] with the exception of a few context-specific applications [30,32,38], no major implementation of memory has actually occurred. Organizations can benefit from a proper implementation of collective/organizational/group memory. This research has the potential to enlighten IS practitioners on the design and implementation issues of collective memory.

A hypermedia-based prototype of collective memory was deployed on an Intranet and integrated with a Groupware in this research. This category of system can be useful for various types of group work, such as project management, budget development, and strategic planning. Hypermedia allows users to explore ideas in a free and nonlinear fashion. An Intranet offers a powerful communication environment that can be used in publishing, and community applications; it also imposes a boundary on the movement of users browsing of web pages. Integration of the Intranet-based application with a GroupWare offers a powerful environment for conducting group work in organizations.

Decision-makers using collective memory tend to focus more on the judgment pattern stored in the memory. Although this is not ‘‘group-think’’, this resembles ‘‘availability bias’’ discussed by Tversky and Kahneman [44]. The findings of this study establish that collective memory should be used judiciously in practice. The misuse of organizational memory discussed by Walsh and Ungson [45] seems relevant. ‘‘Without stopping to consider the similarities and differences between the past and the present, decision-makers can be blinded by past analogies.

Ironically, such an awareness of history can promote a routine response to a situation, just when a nonroutine response is called for’’ [45,p,76].

## 5.2. Limitations of the research

The participants in this study were undergraduate business students who had the required motivation to participate in the experiment (they received waiver for an assignment in a course and were excited at the prospect of using state-of-the-art electronic decisionroom facility). They also had a stake in the participation and outcome of the study given that a large proportion indicated their plan to pursue graduate business education. However, as is true with most laboratory research, there was really no way to detect and ensure that they put their best effort to arrive at the decision. Furthermore, it may also be noted that these groups were ad hoc groups and the members did not share any history of working with one another. A better approach for collective memory-based research will be to use groups who are actually engaged in projects and/or decision-making activities. Field experiments are ideal approaches for this type of research.

The memory support system developed for this research was quite simple; the information provided in the collective memory was straightforward. The memory was static and did not have the provisions to capture dynamically group level transactions. It may be desirable to include a ‘‘richer’’ conceptualization and implementation of ‘‘memory’’ by including various types of ‘‘what-if’’ scenarios and complex analysis in memory-support systems in future research.

The task employed in the experiment was classified as a cognitive-conflict task because multiple conflicting solution schemes could be presented by the participants depending on their individual preference structures (for the MBA attributes examined in this study). However, we would concede that the task chosen here might not have evoked stronger emotions, values, individual staking of positions, and opportunities for cognitive conflict. Future research should consider other types of cognitive-conflict tasks (e.g. the judgment task discussed by Saunders and Miranda [39]). Furthermore, we measured the ‘‘for votes in the final round’’ and ‘‘number of iterations’’ to measure ‘‘degree of consensus/agreement’’. This study does not provide much clue on whether and how the voting pattern evolved; it is possible that higher level of agreement in earlier rounds may deteriorate due to conflictive positions/ values in later rounds.

The ‘‘thoroughness of task analysis’’, a dependent variable in this study, is a highly qualitative and subjective concept. The measures (‘‘number of attributes selected’’ and ‘‘number of information pages browsed’’) may not have adequately captured the ‘‘thoroughness of analysis’’. These measures may reflect the information search behavior (measured by ‘‘number of information pages browsed’’) and the range of decision analysis (measured by ‘‘number of attributes selected’’). Future research studies should attempt to better capture this concept. For instance, (automatic) tracking of repeat visits to specific information or attribute pages and time duration spent on such visits may be a better measure of thoroughness of analysis.

Although the set of 18 attributes (Appendix A) of MBA programs presented to the participants was prepared after consulting various sources of information on MBA school selection, we realized later that some important attributes (such as, quality of program of interest; internship opportunities, prestige of the school etc.) were not included in this list.

## 5.3. Implications for research and future directions

The initial (somewhat simplistic) model depicting the consequential effects of collective memory (Fig. 1) may not be completely valid. Based on our study’s observations, we propose a more robust but also a more complex revised model as shown in Fig. 2.

According to this revised model, the use of memory support could lead to focused learning and a reduction of ambiguity about the decision situation. As a consequence of these influences, the group agreement improves and the groups make faster decisions. However, because of the effect of focused learning, the groups using collective memory support may display lesser propensity to more thoroughly investigate the decision or problem situation/solution space. This is a negative effect of collective memory. Greater degree of group agreement, faster decisionmaking and reduced (cognitive) load in investigation of the problem are expected to generate greater levels of perceived decision quality. However, the link with ‘‘decision quality’’ is not unequivocal especially if the thoroughness of analysis is less than optimal. Note that we did not directly measure ‘‘focused learning’’ or ‘‘level of ambiguity’’ in this study. A preliminary support for the right half (to the right of the dotted vertical line) of this proposed revised model could be established with the study data. Pearson correlation coefficients (and their levels of significance) are indicated on the appropriate links in Fig. 2. All links relating the three intervening variables (group agreement, decision-time, and thoroughness of decision analysis) with perceived decision quality are significant. Group agreement is positively associated with perceived decision quality $( r = 0 . 2 7 5 , p \le 0 . 0 4 4 )$ . Speed of decision-making (i.e. lower time) is negatively related to perceived decision quality $( r = - 0 . 3 2 3 , ~ p \leq 0 . 0 1 7 ) ;$ it is possible that participants in this study may have concluded that the decision quality is good because they are able to solve the problem and make the decision faster and without too much of a conflict. Thoroughness of decision analysis (e.g. number of attributes to be analyzed) when interpreted as information/cognitive overload is negatively related to perceived decision quality $( r = - 0 . 3 3 4 , p \leq 0 . 0 1 4 )$ . Obviously, future research must examine the validity of this entire model.

In discussing the study’s results (Table 6), we pointed out the role ‘‘focused attention’’ seems to play during decision-making for groups with memory support. We alluded to the existence of an optimum level of focused attention on decision situation by the groups operating with memory support. If such a pattern does indeed exist, it would suggest that organizations must research more carefully the type and amount of knowledge/information that they want to build on their memory support systems. Further research is warranted to validate this proposition.

We pointed out a number of limitations of this study earlier. In addition to addressing these, there exist at least two key directions in which future research needs to extend this study. First, attempts should be made to establish external validity for the findings of this research. Second, future research could incorporate refinements in the experiment design.

## Appendix C. Experimental procedure

(1) Participants completed a consent form. They could withdraw from the study even now.

(2) Participants were shown a video presentation about the experiment. The same video presentation was used for all 54 groups who participated in the research.

(3) With the help of Netscape, each participant browsed the Intranet on MBA programs and attributes for school selection. Additionally, as noted earlier, the ‘‘treatment’’ groups had access to the web pages on collective memory.

(4) With the help of the ‘‘allocating tool ’’ within VisionQuest software, each participant assigned 100 points among one or more attributes. Once all team members had allocated weights, the coordinator instructed the system to display group results. Each member could view both their own and group weights. The system also displayed the dispersion in weights submitted by group members. After viewing the dispersion and mean weights for each attribute, the member(s) could change their allocation of weights. This process of readjustment of weights would go on till group members agreed upon the group level weights that could be submitted to the system.

(5) At this point, the coordinator exported these average weights to a database in Microsoft Access and instructed the database program to compute rankings of the schools. Finally, the rank-ordered list of schools was imported back to VisionQuest from the database.

(6) Participants viewed the rankings of the schools.

(7) Participants electronically voted using VisionQuest to confirm that the judgment policy expressed in terms of selected attributes and their weights was final.

(8) If four or more group members voted in favor of the judgment policy, Step 9 was performed, otherwise Steps 3– 8 were repeated. A maximum of seven iterations was allowed.

(9) Each participant browsed the web page titled ‘‘End of Experiment’’ and completed the questionnaire. The questionnaire completed by each participant provided data on his or her perceived decision quality. In addition, some demographic data such as age, gender, GPA, and computer skill were also collected with the help of the questionnaire.
