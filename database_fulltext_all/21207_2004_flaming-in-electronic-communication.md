# Flaming in electronic communication

Mei Alonzo, Milam Aiken\*

School of Business University, University of Mississippi, University, MS 38677, USA

Received 12 October 2000; accepted 27 August 2002

## Abstract

Communication through computer networks, electronic salons, and virtual communities has its price. Often relatively anonymous and socially detached, electronic communication allows people to write things online that they would seldom consider saying face-to-face, sometimes generating flames. In a study of the motives to flame based upon Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT), 160 subjects generated comments anonymously in parallel with a group support system (GSS) idea generation program. Results showed that high levels of assertiveness and sensation seeking predicted flaming, and males tended to participate more in the activity than did females. <sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Flaming; Electronic meetings; Group support systems; Disinhibition; Uses and Gratifications Theory

## 1. Introduction

Although researchers have provided several explanations as to what constitutes a flame in online communication, the term generally requires hostile intentions characterized by words of profanity, obscenity, and insults that inflict harm to a person or an organization resulting from uninhibited behavior [25, 38]. For some users, a flame evokes disbelief, anger, and other negative emotions that are somewhat tolerable. Other times, however, it becomes excessive and defamatory, resulting in lawsuits [41]. The phenomenon may be a familiar sociological curiosity to the public in general, but it poses a serious threat to businesses engaged in electronic commerce as the ex ponential growth of the Internet has transferred enormous power to individuals who are able to express themselves freely in a medium that has few or no laws governing such behavior. While flaming in private email may be the equivalent of illegal telephone harassment, flaming in a public newsgroup or discussion board may be defamatory and has serious consequences to an organization’s products, services, and goodwill. A flame posted to a discussion board on the Internet has an enormous potential readership and any disgruntled Internet users may flame with impunity [9,33].

Research on flaming in electronic media has suggested several antecedent variables or motives. However, only limited relationships have been examined empirically, and the results of these studies have been mostly inconclusive. For example, flaming has been suggested to be dependent upon the topic of discussion, participants’ proximity, familiarity with the group members, and confidence in the provision of anonymity [17,19,24,36,44]. However, some have suggested that flaming is socially or culturally dependent [23]. Unlike face-to-face conversation, there are no visual cues or observations of social etiquette in electronic written communication. People who are completely removed from each other geographically can assault each other verbally without fear of bodily harm [7]. There are no cues such as the physical size, facial expression, or tone of voice of the person making the comment. In addition to a lack of social cues, flaming may be the result of deindividuation, difficulties of coordination and feedback, depersonalization and/or attentional focus, and conformity to a particular norm or etiquette associated with the computing subculture [26].

Flaming may be the result of demographic (e.g., gender and age), psychological (e.g., external locus of control and creativity), and behavioral variables (e.g., a perception that vested interests have been or are about to be violated) [27]. For example, the relationship between flaming and gender has been examined, and the results showed that male participants tend to flame more than female participants [3]. Other researchers have suggested that the tendency to flame is behavioral and caused by hostility [38] or psychological, affected by disinhibition resulting from two subjective states, public versus private self-awareness [31,36].

Overall, research in electronic communication has qualitatively explained and described the conditions supporting and the possible causality of flaming incidences, but has neither predicted nor examined who or why one might participate in flaming. The purpose of this research is to provide a theoretical explanation of flaming motivations based on Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT). Specifically, we argue that personality traits are an important factor in flaming motivation due to the expected gratification received from such behavior.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Uses and Gratifications Theory

Anonymous electronic communication often is associated with deviant or antisocial behavior such as flaming. One possible explanation for this behavior is found in Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT) which has been applied successfully to model communication through radio and television [40], advertising [4,35], the Internet [12,34], and online services [28].

Uses and Gratification Theory suggests that people use certain types of media to satisfy their needs. UGT is founded upon three basic tenets: (1) people are goal directed in their behavior, (2) they are active media users, and (3) they are aware of their needs and select media to gratify these needs [5,22]. While previous research has utilized UGT in understanding individual patterns of media use, recent models have incorporated and stressed the importance of personality as they influence individual needs. Individual needs influenced by their personality then affect the gratification individuals seek. UGT considers how situational and dispositional factors produce expectations of need gratification that media messages or functional alternatives may satisfy. It is the psychological perspective that considers the relationships among the stimulus, one’s internal state, and the response to the stimulus. A person’s values, beliefs, needs, and motives affect behavior such as media use [8], and the theory suggests that its use is associated with a set of psychological motives. These motives prompt the audience to purposefully select certain media in order to satisfy a set of psychological needs associated with those motives. Thus, media use is goal-oriented.

The basic principles and assumptions of Uses and Gratification Theory are useful in the investigation of flaming motivation in electronic communication because the theory assumes that people may have an inclination toward this behavior to satisfy certain needs or to achieve certain personal goals. Motives then reflect needs and are observable and measurable. Research of television-viewing motives using UGT has identified information seeking, relaxation, pass time, entertainment, arousal, social interaction, companionship, escape, and time consumption [1,18,39], for example. Gratification can be sought in an electronic communication medium such as electronic bulletin boards by the need for surveillance, personal identity, informational learning, and socialization [16,21]; and in other online activity by the need for escape, entertainment, interaction, surveillance, communication, and personal identity [32].

While television viewing provides a certain type of gratification, the uniqueness of interactivity on the Internet allows users to seek immediate gratification. For example, television viewers’ needs may be gratified after extra effort through telephone calls to the television station. Online users’ needs, on the other hand, may be gratified immediately by sending an email message or posting a notice on a bulletin board for anyone with access to the Internet. While a defamatory remark through the telephone may be contained immediately, a flame posted on the bulletin board may remain on the Internet for a long duration, allowing further exposure.

## 2.2. Motives of flaming

Research in communication has found that personality traits can mediate exposure and response to messages. Personality traits have been related to types of media [13], to various media content [45], and to viewing motives. Perhaps, the most relevant study on personality traits and its relation to motives that may be applied to flaming is the study by Conway and Rubin [8], where the authors examined the relationship between personality traits and television viewing motives. Personality traits were associated with McGuire’s [29] psychological theories on human motivation to explain television-viewing motives. For example, a personality trait such as sensation seeking represented McGuire’s motivational need for stimulation. McGuire’s other motivational theories include the need for consistency, hermeneutic, tension reduction, expression, autonomy, attraction, and assertion. In examining flaming motives, four of McGuire’s human motivation theories have the most relevance: stimulation, tension reduction, expression, and assertion. These motivation theories were examined in this study to explain flaming motives because individuals are goal-oriented active communicators seeking to gratify felt needs.

The four selected psychological theories represent one cognitive and three affective orientations that reflect flaming motivation. Individuals can be motivated to flame in electronic communication to fulfill cognitive or affective needs. Cognitive motives stress the person’s information processing and attainment of ideational state. Such motivation to flame in electronic communication, for example, may be to confirm the anonymous condition of a media by testing it. If no repercussion occurs, then the anonymous condition is confirmed. Affective motives stress the person’s feelings and attainment of certain emotional states. One may be motivated to flame, for example, to reduce negative tension resulting from anxiety. Fig. 1 illustrates the conceptual model for this study.

## 2.2.1. Stimulation and sensation seeking

McGuire’s stimulation theory is a cognitive paradigm that explains an individual’s need for stimulation to escape from the aversive state of boredom. Stimulation theories can be represented by sensation seeking, a personality trait defined by the need for ‘‘novel, varied, complex, and intense sensations and experiences and the willingness to take risks for such experience’’ [47]. The sensation seeking scale by Zuckerman [46] measures and quantifies optimal desired levels of stimulation and arousal. While sensation seeking has four dimensions, the dimension most relevant to flaming in electronic communication is disinhibition. Disinhibition occurs when social constraints break down and people engage in deviant or antisocial behavior, as when communication is anonymous [19]. Sensation seekers, by nature, are willing to take risks to experience certain sensations; however, when they are using anonymous communication channels, no repercussions typically occur—thus allowing sensation seekers more leverage to flame. Therefore, the first hypothesis of our research is:

## 2.2.2. Tension-reduction and anxiety

McGuire’s [30] tension-reduction theory is an affective dimension. The theory states that individuals need stability and will actively seek to reduce arousal in their lives and pursue experiences to reduce negative tension. Anxiety is a form of stress or tension that people seek to lessen; as such, tension-reduction theory can be represented by anxiety. Different individuals handle stress or tension differently. For example, in an electronic meeting, some individuals may seek to reduce tension by ‘‘free-riding’’ (not contributing ideas, but reading others’ comments). Others may seek to reduce tension by flaming. An active participant in an electronic meeting might relieve anxiety by flaming to ‘‘loosen up.’’ Therefore, the second hypothesis is:

Hypothesis 2a. A higher level of anxiety is significantly and positively associated with flaming for escape.

Hypothesis 2b. A higher level of anxiety is significantly and positively associated with flaming for relaxation.

## 2.2.3. Expression and creativity

McGuire’s expression theory, like tension reduction theory, is an affective paradigm. Expression theory states that people need stability and seek gratification by self-expression and acting out feelings. The theory can be represented by creativity and can be measured using the Reflection of Self and Environment (ROSE) creativity scales [6]. Selfexpression can be achieved positively (such as through success) or negatively (such as through destructive behavior). Flaming in electronic communication can be a form of negative self-expression and may be invoked by hostile messages. Thus, the third hypothesis is:

Hypothesis 3a . A higher level of creativity is significantly and positively associated with flaming for pass time.

Hypothesis 3b . A higher level of creativity is significantly and positively associated with flaming for escape.

## 2.2.4. Assertion and assertiveness

McGuire’s assertion theory, like expression theory, is an affective paradigm. Assertion theory states that people are competitive and desire success and power. When people achieve their goals, they gain selfesteem and admiration by others. Assertion theory explains people’s need to have control, dominance, and power over others, which confers status and selfesteem [29]. Flaming in electronic communication might allow one to express opinions, beliefs, attitudes, and needs to control and exert power over others. Therefore, the fourth hypothesis is:

Hypothesis 4 . A higher level of assertiveness is significantly and positively associated with flaming for pass time.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Subjects

Subjects consisted of 160 junior and senior business students (57% male) enrolled over two semesters in an introductory MIS course (they received course credit for their participation). The subjects were assigned randomly to 20 groups of eight participants each because prior research has indicated this may be the minimum size needed for efficient and effective use of group support systems [2].

## 3.2. Task

All subjects used an electronic gallery writing program in a face-to-face environment for 15 min to generate ideas on how to solve the parking problem on campus, a topic considered relevant to the students and used in many studies of electronic meetings. Using the group support system (GSS) gallery writing program, participants could write comments anonymously and simultaneously while viewing all other comments generated by the meeting participants. Upon the completion of the task, all subjects completed a self-assessed questionnaire.

## 3.3. Variables

Subjects were asked to assess their confidence in the anonymity of the comments as well as the level of topic controversy, interest, importance, and appropriateness. In addition, four psychological variables were measured on a 5-point (1 = disagree, 5 = agree) Likert scale:

1. Sensation seeking was measured using the Disinhibition Sensation-Seeking Scale [46]. For example, subjects were asked to rate their agreement with the statement: ‘‘I like wild/uninhibited parties.’’

2. Anxiety was measured using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory [42]. The A-state scale was used, and the questions were tailored to using a GSS. For example, subjects were asked to rate their agreement with the statement: ‘‘I feel nervous when using the GSS.’’

3. Creativity was measured using a portion of the Reflections of Self and Environment (ROSE) Scale, which is a measure of adult self-actualization. The scale’s strongest factor is creativity, which was the only factor used. For example, subjects were asked to rate their agreement with the statement: ‘‘I am a creative person, even in the ordinary affairs of my life.’’

4. Assertiveness was measured with the Rathus Assertive Schedule [37]. For example, subjects were asked to rate their agreement with the statement: ‘‘I am open and frank about my feelings.’’

## The dependent variables included:

1. Flaming Motives were measured using selfassessed, 5-point Likert scales. Statements about reasons for watching television [14,15,39] were reworded for four flaming motive categories: (1) pass time (e.g., ‘‘I would flame because it gives me something to occupy my time.’’), (2) relaxation (e.g., ‘‘I would flame because it relaxes me.’’), (3)

escape (e.g., ‘‘I would flame so I can forget about the topic being discussed.’’), and (4) entertainment (e.g., ‘‘I would flame because it amuses me.’’).

2. User Satisfaction is a measure of information system success [10], and statements about usefulness were re-worded to measure satisfaction with the GSS meeting [20] (e.g., ‘‘I find the GSS useful for contributing ideas on the topic.’’).

3. User Comments were recorded automatically with subjects’ identification numbers onto a disk file. Two objective raters independently counted the number of relevant (e.g., ‘‘build a parking lot’’) and irrelevant (e.g., ‘‘Is anyone hungry?’’) comments from transcripts of the 20 groups. In addition, the number of flames was counted by category: hostile (e.g., ‘‘Kill all people who are dumb’’), swearing (e.g., ‘‘The parking area sucks’’), and name-calling (e.g., ‘‘Suzanna is a psycho’’). The reviewers agreed on 93.4% of classifications, indicating adequate inter-rater reliability.

About 40% of the flames were classified as hostile, 28% as swearing, and 33% as name-calling. ## 5. Discussion

A model of flaming motives based on Uses and Gratifications Theory was proposed and tested. Consistent with uses and gratifications expectations, antecedents of flaming motives (e.g., disinhibition seeking, anxiety, and assertiveness) might explain the motive to flame in electronic communication.

Sensation seeking is a personality trait defined by the need for ‘‘novel, varied, complex and intense sensations and experiences and the willingness to take risks for such experience’’ [47]. In anonymous electronic communication, individuals become disinhibited from a lack of social cues, and thus, might engage in deviant or antisocial behavior [19]. The results of this study suggest that disinhibition seekers are willing to take risks and might therefore engage in flaming for pass time and for entertainment.

McGuire’s tension reduction theory was represented by anxiety. According to the theory, individuals who experience anxiety or stress seek to reduce them to experience pleasure. Anxiety was found to predict flaming for escape and relaxation. In electronic communication, one may experience stress in several ways. Stress may be the result of hostile comments posted by others during an online discussion. As a result, one may return the comments with a flame to escape or diffuse such stress. Others may flame as a relaxation technique resulting from frustration with the system (e.g., frustration with a slow connection or one’s typing skill). In a GSS meeting, one might experience stress from the pressure of trying to contribute ideas under time pressure, and it might be intensified when one has nothing new to contribute. Individuals who experience stress or anxiety might flame for escape and relaxation.

McGuire’s assertion theory measured by the Rathus Assertiveness Schedule represents individuals who are competitive and desire success and power. Assertion theories address the need to have control, dominance, and power over others, which confers status and selfesteem. A high level of assertiveness was found to predict flaming for pass time. In anonymous electronic communication where social cues are not visible, flaming may be a way to have control and dominance over others. Thus, one might exert control over others through flames for pass time.

Gender was found to be a significant predictor of the entertainment motive. Consistent with past research [3], males tended to flame more than females. Our results showed that 68% of males and only 32% of females wrote flames for entertainment.

Overall, individuals who have a high level of disinhibition seeking and assertiveness tend to flame for pass time. In addition, disinhibition seeking and male gender also contribute to flaming for entertainment. Individuals rated high on anxiety level tend to diffuse such a condition by flaming for escape and relaxation. These findings are consistent with uses and gratifications expectation in other contexts, such as television viewing [1,8] and online activity [11,28,32].

Flaming has been suggested to reduce productivity in the work place [38] and has been found to contribute to loss of business [9]. As part of a risk management program, organizations should take proactive steps to prevent or reduce flaming in intranet electronic communication such as through e-mail or electronic bulletin boards. Organizations should consider evaluating employees’ stress levels and attempt to reduce their anxiety. Providing information and updating the customers on products, services, and organizational changes may also reduce anxiety in customers as well as promoting goodwill for future business relations.

In electronic meetings, reducing stress might decrease the level of flaming and increase the number of relevant comments. Although sufficient time should be provided for all participants to contribute their ideas, too much time can increase the likelihood of flaming for pass time and entertainment.

## 6. Conclusion

This research presents the first attempt to explain flaming motives in electronic communication incorporating the uses and gratification perspective, and the study examines only four of McGuire’s psychological paradigms. Future research should examine other psychological variables and their relationships to flaming in electronic communication. In addition, the experiments were conducted in the context of a GSS meeting. More research is needed to establish the generalizability of our findings to other electronic communication modes such as e-mail, chat rooms, and electronic bulletin boards.
