# Contribution of institutional DSS to organizational performance: evidence from a longitudinal study

Rajiv Kohli\*<sup>,1</sup>, Sarv Devaraj<sup>1</sup>

Department of Management, Mendoza College of Business, University of Notre Dame, Notre Dame, IN 46556, USA

Received 1 October 2002; accepted 4 December 2002

## Abstract

Most studies have reported decision support systems (DSS) impact on individual performance under simulated conditions. There is need to examine the impact on performance of widely used institutional DSS. Utilizing longitudinal data from multiple healthcare organizations, this study measures the contribution of an institutional contract modeling DSS to user organizations performance.

Least squares regression analysis results indicate that actual usage of DSS is significantly and positively related to the reimbursement rate for services. We find confirmation of lag effects in realizing revenues following DSS usage. <sup>D</sup> 2002 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Decision support system; DSS; IT payoff; Effectiveness; Organizational impacts; Usage; Organizational performance; Longitudinal design; Lag effects

## 1. Introduction

In a 1987 article, Peter G.W. Keen set the agenda for the next decade to include ‘building an organizational mandate’ for decision support systems (DSS) [32]. He proposed that, to move DSS ahead in organizations, it is essential to define both the applications that matter as well as payoff following the investment. Although DSS ‘applications that matter have grown in businesses in the past decade, the expected payoffs are frequently unrealized [1,18].

Traditionally, the DSS evaluation and management process has followed four stages—justification, deployment, usage, and implementation evaluation [52]. Much of the DSS evaluation research thus far has focused upon the first three stages. Examples of such research are justification of the investment in deploying DSS through cost-benefit analysis or value of DSS [31,49,61], anticipated usage [62], and implementation issues [1,27,62]. Thus, there is a need to examine the evaluation of benefits after the implementation of a DSS.

Although a significant amount of research has been conducted in developing DSS applications and assessing their effectiveness through improvements in individual decision-making, there is a paucity of research in organizational DSS payoff [52]. Organizational or institutional DSS are systems developed for a given purpose and recurrently used by a number of people in the organization [23,52]. Perhaps, the evaluation of an organizational DSS that serves multiple managers and functions is much more challenging than a personal DSS [42]. A few studies that examined DSS organizational impacts found mixed results [24,37,58]. We recognize that organizational DSS payoff is the result of the accumulation of individual decision-making improvements. However, to approve continued investment in an institutional DSS, senior managers expect evidence of improved organizational performance attributable to the institutional DSS. The purpose of this paper is to measure the contribution of an institutional DSS to the organizations’ performance.

Many DSS studies, both at the individual as well as the institutional level, have been conducted in simulated and laboratory-based settings [19,46,53]. While such studies provide valuable insights under controlled settings, the impact of an organizational DSS is more likely to be observed in an actual organizational field setting. This study contributes to this objective by examining the organizational impact of DSS using field data from multiple healthcare organizations.

A recent perspective in the IT payoff literature is the ‘process view’ of examining IT investment [11,43,56]. Essentially, the process view seeks to examine how the outcomes occurred in addition to what outcomes occurred from IT investments. This paper contributes to the DSS literature by examining the DSS payoff through the ‘process-based’ lens.

Brynjolfsson and Hitt [8] and Mahmood and Mann [39] have stressed the need to observe payoff from IT over longer time periods. This may be particularly true if there is a time lag between the IT investment or use and the organizational impact. In institutional settings, benefits may trickle down to the organizational level over time from the use of a DSS. Thus, it becomes necessary to observe use and payoff from DSS use over an extended time period. In this paper, we employ longitudinal monthly data over a 3-year time period to test the proposition that increased DSS usage leads to improved organizational performance. We attempt to contribute to the DSS effectiveness literature by presenting evidence that actual usage leads to improvement in decision outcomes as well as organizational performance.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present an overview of past evaluation studies and its implications for our study. In Section 3, we briefly examine the role of DSS in healthcare organizations and a description of the institutional DSS reported in this study. Section 4 presents the research methodology and the description of variables, followed by our proposition. Section 5 presents the regression models and the results of the statistical analysis. Finally, Section 6 presents the limitations of the study and areas for further research.

## 2. Literature review

The majority of DSS effectiveness studies are dedicated to measuring the effectiveness of DSS on individual decision-making. Reviews of DSS impact on individual decision-making are cited in several previous studies [1,19,20,45,53].

As indicated in Table 1, few past studies report organizational DSS usage and among those that do, many are conducted in laboratory or simulated settings. For instance, Sharda et al. [53] report results of the effectiveness of a simulated organizational DSS and, in two laboratory-based simulations, Forgionne and Kohli [19] demonstrate the effectiveness of DSS users versus non-DSS users in healthcare and business policy decision-making scenarios [20]. In each case, the DSS model calculated the return on revenue (ROR) based upon the decision-maker’s choices. Similarly, Newman [46] examined the effects of a DSS on the organizational performance under simulated business conditions. Such studies form the basis for implementing organizational DSS by providing insights into the individual decisionmaking and its impact upon the organizational profitability. Evidence of DSS effectiveness upon managerial decision-making serves as the basis for measuring anticipated payoff when organizations make investments in large-scale institutional DSS. However, once implemented, an examination of organizational impacts resulting from actual DSS usage is warranted.

We find that only a few case studies of institutional DSS payoff are reported in the literature. Tavana et al. [58] examine the organizational effectiveness of a DSS applied to determine fees for legal services provided by law firms. These studies examined organizational impact by comparing DSS availability with no DSS or manual decision-making. Our study contributes to the literature by providing a DSS effectiveness evaluation approach for organizations that have implemented and utilized a DSS for an extended period of time. Such organizations require a comprehensive analysis of DSS use and its subsequent impact upon the organizational performance. To our knowledge, the only institutional DSS study measuring actual DSS usage and examining the usage-effectiveness link was reported by LeBlanc and Kozar [37]. They caution that usage or user satisfaction measures alone are not a guarantee of higher performance and that utilization of the system should be treated as an independent variable as opposed to an indicator of achievement. In doing so, they correlate the outcomes with the usage of the decision support system.

Notwithstanding the significant contributions of past research to measure DSS effectiveness, it has limitations related to the choice of dependent variables and research design. We find that past effectiveness measures were generally based upon productivity measurement approaches usually suitable for transaction systems. DSS, by definition, are used for decision-making and their effectiveness depends upon ‘value’ measured by profitability in addition to enhanced productivity [41].

Given that DSS are generally used to support longterm goals, their impact may be evident only after a time lag. To detect such lag effects, the research design should prescribe longitudinal usage and performance data. Our review of past literature (see Table 1) indicates that most studies reported in the literature examine pre- and post-DSS implementation data to examine the DSS impact and do not account for lag effects. The failure of past research to incorporate lag effects has also been of concern in overall IT payoff studies [40].

2.1. Past approaches to measuring DSS organizational impact

DSS measurement, and indeed information systems measurement, has generally relied upon financial measures such as return on investment (ROI), net present value (NPV) revenue, and costs [20,36,51,53]. Financial measures are well understood by senior management who usually demand the justification in IT investment. Furthermore, financial measures also allow comparison for the substitutability of IT for other resources such as human, machines, and real estate. Laboratory as well as field studies that measured DSS outcomes have generally used financial measures to assess DSS effectiveness [25,51,62,63]. However, the need for progressing from measurement of individual ad hoc processes to organizational process impact has been highlighted in the DSS literature [32].

## 2.2. Usage and information systems effectiveness

In the past decade, there has been significant amount of research involving usage as an effectiveness measure of information system, much of it using the theory of planned behavior, theory of reasoned action, and technology acceptance model (TAM) [12,38,57].

This research stream follows two paths—expected usage and actual usage. Expected system usage is suggested as a proxy for user satisfaction [16], as well as predictor of system success [12]. Fig. 1 shows the variables used by TAM to predict usage of information systems. However, follow-up research has suggested that TAM variables may not be good predictors of system usage and success [38,57]. Furthermore, users’ perceived system usage and actual usage, tracked by system log, have been found to be inconsistent [33]. The availability of actual usage data for well-established and widely used institutional DSS circumvents the need to capture TAM-based predictor variables that usually capture expected usage of newly implemented systems. Doll and Torkzadeh [15] articulate the importance of usage in determining system success. In validating an instrument to support claims that usage is critical to organizational impact of a system, they note that:

Although expected usage as an ex-ante approach to DSS evaluation is a useful measure for predicting future success of information systems [22], it has limited utility for organizations that have an established, widely used institutional DSS for which actual usage data are available. For such institutional DSS, a relevant and useful evaluation measure would be the impact or payoff to the organization resulting from actual DSS usage [6]. However, caution should be exercised that payoff is examined after the DSS has approached stability within the organization [37].

To validate the relationship between system usage and DSS effectiveness, Udo [62] surveyed 201 experienced users of DSS and found that system usage had significant relationship with gaining competitive edge for the organizations and suggested that more realistic measures of DSS effectiveness are needed. Further, much of the variance in intent to use technology was explained by, among other factors, result demonstrability and subjective norms such as the influence of senior policymakers.

The process of DSS usage leading to organizational impact is also promoted by the process approach in the firm level IT payoff literature. The process approach can serve as a guide for measuring DSS organizational payoff. We briefly examine the process approach in measuring IT payoff literature for guidance on examining DSS organizational impacts.

## 2.3. Process approach to measuring firm-level IT payoff

Past studies approached IT investments and firm performance analysis as variance-based or processbased. The variance-based studies examine what is the outcome, while the process-based studies are interested in how the outcome occurs, in addition to what outcome [55]. Given that the variables representing the process of how IT investment creates business value are still evolving in the healthcare information systems literature, understanding the process and intermediate steps offers an opportunity to understand the institutional processes leading to changes in financial performance. Therefore, examining the investment in the context of IT assets created and utilized prior to organizational impact is proposed as an approach to identify accurate results of IT investment [56]. The process perspective is exemplified in recent studies using application-level data within firms. In a two-stage model, Barua et al. [4] examine intermediate-level variables in addition to the traditional investment and performance variables. The process approach circumvented some of the measurement problems and also provided a practical management tool to address the question of why (or why not) certain IT impacts occur. Additionally, key variables that may require managerial attention and subsequent action were highlighted. Similarly, Mukhopadhyay et al. [44] examine electronic data interchange (EDI) application-level impacts at nine plants of the Chrysler. Prior to examining the EDI impact on the inventory levels, the study examined the steps that can affect inventory levels such as transportation costs, EDI partner information accuracy, and variety in parts. In following the process approach, the study identified and implemented industry and economic control variables so that an accurate EDI payoff could be determined. Another study investigated the process of IT investment-performance in the manufacturing sector [65]. In each of these studies, the process perspective provided insights into the progression of how IT investment contributed to organizational performance. The additional benefit of the process approach is evident when IT’s contribution is limited and does not permeate to affect organizational improvement. The process approach is likely to identify such limited contribution whereas the variance approach will show that the IT payoff did not occur.

Further, in the context of a DSS, the fundamental result is intended to be better decisions or improved decision quality. Better decisions, in turn, should lead to both process and financial impacts.

In summary, the literature review indicates that:

(i) Few DSS effectiveness studies have examined organizational impacts in field settings;

(ii) Usage, preferably actual usage, is one of the measures of system success;

(iii) Following individual-level DSS effectiveness, there is a need to examine and measure organizational DSS payoff; and

(iv) Process-oriented studies that measure intermediate steps can yield insights into how DSS payoffs occur.

The study reported in this paper builds upon LeBlanc and Kozar’s [37] approach of relating actual usage with performance outcomes of a DSS. We utilize a financial measure as a dependent variable in a multi-organization setting. Further, our research design examines the DSS contribution through a process approach while utilizing longitudinal usage data.

## 3. Decision support systems

DSS are generally designed to help improve the effectiveness and productivity of managers by delivering models to systematically evaluate policies and alternatives [19]. DSS help managers gain insight into the operations, consider alternatives, and develop business strategies by integrating them with conventional management information systems [34]. The DSS reported in this paper is used by a number of healthcare organizations for business decisionmaking.

## 3.1. DSS in healthcare

Given the decrease in reimbursement amounts for healthcare services, particularly in the US but also in other countries, the focus of healthcare DSS is generally to monitor and enhance financial performance. Similar to other disciplines, DSS in healthcare are used for operational, managerial and strategic hospital decision-making [7,13,19,20]. Operational decision-making includes resource allocation, activity-based costing and patient care decisions. Managerial decision-making in healthcare includes cost containment, evaluating profitability of departments, and integrating services among departments. Strategic uses of a DSS involve modeling contracts with payers, pricing decisions, and merger/acquisitions planning. Section 3.2 describes the research sites and their use of DSS contract modeling reported in this study.

## 3.2. DSS research sites

Holy Cross Health System (HCHS) is a national organization with member organization hospitals in various regions of the United States. Each hospital, called a member organization, is an independent legal entity with its own board of directors and separate financial statements. Combined, the member organizations have over 4000 beds, employ about 20,000 people, and have a total operating revenue of approximately US\$1.5 billion. All hospitals are located in suburban areas, offer similar services and range from 350 to 550 beds.

Among the organizations reported in this study, the DSS also serves as a repository for financial, clinical, and quality outcomes for each patient admitted to any of the hospital for the past 10 years. Each hospital captures patient demographics at the time of registration, the status from the time of admission to discharge captured and billing information. The DSS processes this patient information and, using contract terms, models reimbursement scenarios by taking into account the discounts, and write-offs for bad debts and charity care.

Using historical data, finance decision-makers execute reports and ‘‘what-if’’ analysis to prepare contract proposals for insurance companies. In addition, departmental managers utilize the DSS to monitor the profitability of their departments and service lines, while marketing managers monitor the service levels agreed upon in the terms of the contracts. A close and constant scrutiny of such contracts is crucial to continued financial viability of the organizations (shown later in Figs. 3 and 4). To evaluate the impact on the organizations’ performance, we utilize decision-makers’ usage of the DSS contract modeling application as the IT of interest.

## 4. Research design and propositions

This study utilizes longitudinal data across 36 monthly periods from eight HCHS member organizations that evaluate contracts using the DSS. A set of hospitals within the same broad administrative context, combined with longitudinal data is ideal to examine the effect of the usage of IT on measures of performance, while at the same time controlling for other factors. We utilized a longitudinal study because it permits the assessment of the effects of the independent variables on performance that may be realized after a period of time [9,39]. Brynjolfsson and Hitt [8] suggest that, ‘‘if there is some lag or adjustment time required to match organizational factors and IT investments, we would expect to see more benefits over longer time periods.’’ Along similar lines Mahmood and Mann [39] state that ‘‘attempts should be made to use a time-lagged regression analysis to allow for the fact that benefits derived in a given year may be due to IT investments made in previous years.’’ Specific lags vary based upon the nature of the industry and the processes being considered. While in manufacturing and engineering applications the lag effects can be measured in years, the healthcare industry is likely to experience shorter lags due to critical and timesensitive nature of the business [30]. Furthermore, the maturity of the IT infrastructure within the organizations can also affect the duration of lags [66].

Given that the DSS is designed to enhance decision effectiveness, if DSS usage can be associated with hospital reimbursement for services and subsequently with the revenue of the organizations, then decisionmaking and the resulting performance of the organizations would have likely improved through increased DSS usage [37].

## 4.1. Criterion variables

Fig. 2 presents a diagrammatic representation of our process-based approach to examining the organizational impact of DSS investment in the hospitals. In our study, the DSS availability for decision-makers represents the investment, while the DSS usage represents the asset created by the investment in DSS. We view the change in reimbursement as the DSS impact created by the usage asset. Finally, the organizational impact is represented in the model by the hospitals performance—operationalized by the rate of reimbursement. Given that the DSS is a modeling system for insurance/reimbursement contracts, reimbursement and revenue amounts represent the dependent variables in the new variable ‘rate of reimbursement’ calculated as reimbursement/revenue. Following is the description of criterion variables—reimbursement and revenue (Fig. 3).

## 4.1.1. Reimbursement

Reimbursement represents the sum of payments from insurers and patients for services rendered by the hospital. Although intuitively it appears that revenue should equal reimbursement, there are factors that affect the amount of reimbursement. A crucial factor is the pricing agreed with insurers and payors through contracts modeled in the DSS. Such contracts can be complex and based upon criteria such as days-of-stay in the hospital, a predetermined percentage of charges, number of medical exams, and differential rates for each day in the hospital. Bad debt and charity care can also affect reimbursement. The objective of the DSS modeling function is to develop financial models that maximize the reimbursement favorable to the hospital revenues. Therefore, if decision-makers use the modeling and reporting capabilities of DSS, the reimbursement is likely to be positively correlated to revenue.

## 4.1.2. Revenue

Traditionally, profitability measures, calculated by subtracting costs from the revenues, are utilized in measuring payoff. However, given the market configuration, profitability as a performance measure for hospitals has several weaknesses. First, due to lack of accurate cost accounting systems, most hospitals use a fixed ratio of cost to charges (RCC), instead of true costs, to determine the cost of services. RCC does not benefit payoff analysis any more than revenue because RCC is a direct transformation of revenue. Second, even when costs are calculated, they are allocated to chargeable items and are likely to change from one accounting period to another based upon the expenses incurred by departments for that particular period [34]. For instance, expenses incurred for purchased supplies in a given period will be allocated to services performed in that month, even when many of the supplies are used over succeeding accounting periods. In such cases, the costs for the period of purchase activity will be overstated, while those of the succeeding period will be understated. Third, contractual agreements, i.e., discounts offered by hospitals to insurers and write-offs for charity care affect the true costs of reimbursed services. Each of these deductions can convolute the profitability measures. Finally, with the increase in managed healthcare, premium revenues are considered as profits and expenses are treated as a charge against these profits. This can lead to large fluctuations in the profitability depending upon patient influx as opposed to a reflection of hospital performance. To account for the above weaknesses, revenue is utilized as a dependent variable of hospital performance by several studies [21,36,54,60,67].

## 4.2. Explanatory variables

Utilizing variables from past studies in hospital performance research [21,36,60], we choose the following independent or explanatory variables—usage, competition, casemix index, employees, Medicare, Medicaid, patient income and outpatients, net operating income (NOI). The chosen variables are commonly available in hospitals and many are routinely captured for regulatory reporting. These variables will be examined in the regression equation. Following is a brief explanation of each explanatory variable. A description of the variables utilized in the model is provided in Table 2 and descriptive statistics are provided in Table 3.

## 4.2.1. Usage

Usage is utilized in several past studies to indicate increased decision performance resulting from use of DSS [3,13,19,20,53]. Each decision-maker logs on to the DSS and is identified by the site as well as the username. This is followed by the decision-maker’s choice of creating, modifying or examining an existing model or ‘scenario’ within the DSS modeling system. In order for the decision-maker to examine the results of the model, he or she has to execute the model and await the results in a report. In addition, the decision-maker can also execute one of the existing reports to monitor the current status of a contract. As the model or report is executed, an automated DSS utility program records the user’s name, report name, date and time, CPU time utilized, elapsed time, and disk input/output activity.

We operationalize usage of DSS modeling and reimbursement through CPU time consumed by executed reports. We choose CPU time, as opposed to the count of reports or elapsed time, because CPU time reflects the complexity as well as the frequency of executed reports. CPU time has been utilized as a measure of system performance in several past studies [2,10,41,68]. The variation in CPU time depends upon the number of variables utilized for the analysis, the duration of timeframe for which data are analyzed, and the number of records that meet the analysis criteria. Each of these conditions accounts for the analytical intensity and is expected to impact the revenue. We did not utilize other usage variables, such as elapsed time and disk input/output activity, because they vary due to other processing activities at the time of report(s) execution and therefore can confound the usage variable.

## 4.2.2. Casemix

The casemix index is a measure of the range of services offered by the hospital. The higher this measure, the more complex the services rendered by the hospital. Revenue and reimbursement are affected by higher casemix due to higher resource consumption generally expected for such services.<sup>2</sup>

## 4.2.3. Employees

Our operationalization of the ‘employees’ variable was through the number of full-time employees to provide a measure of the labor intensity. The relationship between the number of employees and organizational performance has been of interest to healthcare management researchers and is accounted for in our model.

## 4.2.4. Patient income

It is conceivable that with higher patient incomes, hospitals may be able to charge more for their services or incur lower bad debt. For this reason, we include the per capita income of region/market for each hospital in the analyses.

## 4.2.5. Medicaid and Medicare

These two variables represent the percent of services offered by the hospital for Medicare and Medicaid patients, respectively. We control for these effects because Medicare and Medicaid reimbursement is typically less than that from other payers for similar services, and such patients can be more costly to treat. Therefore, the reimbursement of a hospital can be affected due to a greater percentage of Medicare and Medicaid patients.

## 4.2.6. Net operating income

Net operating income represents the income result ing from reimbursement of services following deduction of costs of offering the services. NOI is generally the metric of concern to the senior management of the member organizations.

## 4.2.7. Outpatients

The current trends indicate that outpatient healthcare business is growing. Outpatient business can have different resource requirements than inpatient services. To account for any effect of variation due to outpatients, we include the ratio of outpatient revenue to total revenue as a control variable in the revenue model.

## 4.3. Estimation models

Given the extant literature in healthcare management with respect to the drivers of hospital perform ance and the proposition of this paper, the following model will be estimated:

Reimbursement=Revenue

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \times (\text { Lagged   value   of   usage }) \\ & \quad + \beta_ {2} \times \text { Medicare } + \beta_ {3} \times \text { Medicaid } \\ & \quad + \beta_ {4} \times \text { Casemix } + \beta_ {5} \times \text { Outpatient } \\ & \quad + \beta_ {6} \times \text { Income } + \beta_ {7} \times \text { Employees } \\ & \quad + \beta_ {8} \times \text { Net   Operating   Income } \end{array}
$$

This equation represents our best theoretical prediction of the relationship between DSS use and reimbursement rate for the hospitals.

## 5.1. Regression model of DSS performance

The proposed model presented in Section 4.3 was estimated to assess the association of DSS usage and other variables with rate of reimbursement. The above model was estimated using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression.

## 5.2. Diagnostics

In longitudinal data, a check for the possibility of serial correlation is typically the first diagnostic performed. The two steps employed were (i) an examination of the correlation between the residuals for period t with period t  1 for each hospital and (ii) an observation of the Durbin–Watson statistic reported for each model. The correlation between the residuals was not statistically significant and the Durbin – Watson statistic was close to 2.0. Therefore, on both counts, the estimated models indicated that serial correlation was not an issue. We also checked for serial correlation between error terms across two, three, and four time periods, and did not detect a statistically significant correlation. We checked for heteroscedasticity or nonconstant variance using White’s test and did not observe any problem. We then performed two checks for violations of the normality assumptions (i) an examination of the residuals using normality plots and (ii) the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Both checks did not indicate violations of the normality assumption.

To check for multicollinearity problems, we confirmed that variance inflation factors (VIF) and Belsley – Kuh – Welsch [5] condition indices did not indicate presence of multicollinearity among the independent variables in the model.

We adopted three approaches to assess the lag effects—review of literature, interviews, and statistical analysis. Our review of the healthcare literature provided initial indication of lag periods in the delivery of healthcare to be considered [14,35,64]. Our interviews with hospital managers also confirmed that the effects for a modeling process would appear in the revenue after about two to four periods. A vignette (Fig. 4) of an actual account of DSS use also suggests the above lags based upon the process of contract renegotiation using data extracted from DSS modeling. Based on the literature and interviews, we tested models with lags ranging from two to four time periods and evaluated models with varying time lags from a purely statistical standpoint. The criteria employed to determine the best model were (i) Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), (ii) Schwarz’s Criterion, and (iii) Log Likelihood ratio for the models under consideration. A smaller value for all three of these criteria indicates a better fitting model. In line with our expectations, we observed that the model with a two-period time lag was the best fit. The primary results reported in this paper employ a two-period lag for the DSS usage variable. However, we also estimated a model with all reasonable lagged values of the usage variable. As was expected, there were severe multicollinearity problems in the estimation. An examination of the variance inflation factors and the Belsley–Kuh–Welsch condition indices provided strong evidence of multicollinearity. High $\hat { R ^ { 2 } }$ values with insignificant t-statistics are also indicators of this problem. Under these circumstances, the estimated parameters are not reliable and, therefore, we present these results in Appendix A.

## 6. Conclusions and implications

Through the results provided in this paper, we have provided evidence that DSS usage leads to improvements in organizational performance. The results of this study have implications for businesses as well as academic researchers. For businesses, this study provides evidence that DSS have a long-term impact on organizational performance. Such evidence is critical for authorization of continued investment in DSS maintenance, training, development, and support. Second, the evidence of improved organizational performance reinforces the need for a computing environment with decision support capabilities. Third, it makes the case for continued DSS usage by strategic and operational managers in making decisions. In order for it to be effective, managers have to incorporate DSS outcomes in their decision-making process. There have been calls to ensure that investment in information technology is effectively converted into impacts [11,50].

For academic researchers, this study has numerous methodological and research implications. First, through this study, we provide senior management with objective evidence of the impact of institutional DSS on performance, an advice given by several researchers [17,18,20,28,32,47,51,52]. Second, this study supports the usage-performance link for institutional DSS and confirms the findings of another study in which the perception of managers indicated that organizational computing is a facilitator of decision-making activity [59]. Third, by utilizing the process view to examine DSS impacts on the institution, this study furthers the notion that examining intermediate impacts strengthens the reliability of payoff outcomes. Fourth, due to the longitudinal design and the capturing of monthly data, this study is able to account for lag effects in the organizational performance following the DSS usage. The need for longitudinal data to measure IT payoff has been expressed in the literature [13,39,40].

In addition to healthcare organizations, the results of this study have implications for other organizations such as electric and gas utilities that contract with the government and heavy industry that rely upon reimbursement for services. As the demand for services fluctuate, the reimbursement rates may need to be negotiated to account for the fixed costs. Similarly, organizations that file claims with insurance companies for services rendered to an insured party will find this study useful in determining the business value of the investment in decision support systems.

Notwithstanding the above contributions, this paper has the following limitations. First, this study was conducted in a limited number of organizations and, therefore, the generalizability of the results is yet to be established. Similarly, our conclusions of the DSS impact may need to be modified when applied to organizations other than hospitals. Second, we did not gather user perceptions of usage because the institutional DSS is an established information system and actual usage data were available. However, we recommend that organizations in the early stages of an institutional DSS implementation should gather perceptual usage data in addition to actual usage.

Future research may combine objective data, such as actual usage, and perceptual data through user surveys, e.g., to select specific system features [26]. Such blending may provide valuable information for improved DSS design and help maximize organizational impacts. Further, researchers may examine user characteristics such as duration of experience and the level of training in assessing their usage of DSS. Future research can also combine longitudinal design with a representative cross-sectional sample, thus enhancing the generalizability of the results. Such research can help provide a deeper understanding of how institutional DSS effects occur.
