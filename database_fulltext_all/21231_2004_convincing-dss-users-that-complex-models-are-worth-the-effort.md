# Convincing DSS users that complex models are worth the effort

Tim Chenoweth, Karen L. Dowling\*, Robert D. St. Louis

College of Business, School of Accountancy and Information Management, Arizona State University, Tempe, AZ 85287-3606, USA

Received 1 November 2002; accepted 1 December 2002

## Abstract

Many management science/operations research (MS/OR) models have been developed to improve decision making. Too often, these models are underutilized. Users generally favor less effortful strategies. The challenge then is to increase the users’ willingness to expend effort. We perform a study using cognitive feedforward and feedback to increase awareness of the improved accuracy to be obtained from complex models. Models of increasing complexity are presented to subjects with feedback regarding their accuracy. Participants overwhelmingly indicated an intention to use the most complex model. The implication for decision support system (DSS) designers is that users will expend more effort if the potential accuracy gains are made more salient.

Keywords: Cognitive feedforward and feedback information; Complex models; Effort vs. accuracy framework; Decision support systems

## 1. Introduction

As organizations and the world in which they operate have become more complex, the decisions that must be made have become more complex. Decision support systems (DSSs) are computer-based systems designed to support decision makers [11] and usually include a database and a model base. Management science/operations research (MS/OR) has developed many models that could be useful in supporting decision makers. Similar to the decisions that confront managers, these models are often complex. DSSs that incorporate complex models have been ‘‘underutilized’’ [23]. The reluctance to use complex MS/OR models has gone so far that some authors have said that ‘‘decision support systems are used only for queries’’ (Ref. [1], p. 28).

Use of a complex model requires the adoption of a new decision strategy (i.e., the complex model rather than the old, simple model). Unlike the adoption of a technology such as word processing [9], the user must accept both a new technology (the DSS) and a new decision strategy (the complex model). We argue that it is necessary for DSS designers to address both the technology adoption and the strategy change to successfully achieve system use.

Generally, users favor less effortful strategies, even when the DSS makes all strategies easier to use. The effort vs. accuracy trade-off in decision making has been shown repeatedly to favor less effort [5,17,27]. A proposed explanation for this phenomenon is that decision makers are able to effectively evaluate only the effort component [27]. They have a much more difficult time evaluating the potential benefits to be obtained from the increased effort. We propose extending research with cognitive feedforward and feedback [25] to convince the user of the benefits (i.e., increased accuracy) of using a complex model. The effort vs. accuracy trade-off should then weight accuracy more heavily because the decision maker is able to see the benefits (better decisions) to be obtained from the increased effort.

The purpose of this paper is to examine whether decision makers would be willing to adopt a more effortful decision strategy if the improved accuracy of the more effortful strategy were made salient. An exploratory study is conducted where decision makers are introduced to increasingly complex models to solve an important work task. As model complexity increases, accuracy of result increases. The relative accuracy is presented to the subjects through feedback. The subjects’ belief in the increased decision accuracy is measured as well as their perceived usefulness of the complex model and their willingness to use it. The results show that as model complexity increased, subjects’ perceived usefulness of, and likelihood to use, the models increased.

The paper is organized as follows. First, the literature on the use of complex models for decision making is reviewed briefly. The next section outlines the conditions necessary to change a decision maker’s strategies and how cognitive feedforward and feedback can support those conditions. This is followed by a description of the study that was conducted to examine the effect of feedback information on perceived usefulness and likelihood to use. The paper ends with a discussion of the results, their implications, and additional research directions.

## 2. Background

Forecasting is one example of an area where decision makers could benefit from the adoption of complex models. It is a critical task for effective and efficient business planning. Areas of forecasting include, but are not limited to, marketing, manufacturing, and human resources. Quantitative methods for forecasting range in difficulty from relatively simple extrapolative methods, such as a simple straightline regression, to complex causal models, such as econometric models with hundreds of simultaneous equations [14]. Although a wide array of quantitative forecasting methods is readily available, decision makers often use subjective estimates (theirs or a colleague’s) rather than statistical methods [19]. This occurs despite judgmental forecasts having been shown to be less accurate than forecasts using even the simplest models [15].

Complex forecasting models have been developed, but they have not been widely implemented in decision support systems; when they have been implemented, it has been with little success [23]. This is consistent with the substantial body of research demonstrating that when decision makers are confronted with complex situations, they adopt strategies that minimize effort [17,27]. This tendency conflicts with attempts to get decision makers to use more complex and more accurate models. This is not a single problem, but rather two problems. First is the challenge of changing the decision makers’ strategy from a known strategy to a more complex strategy. Second is the challenge of getting a decision maker to use a computerized system. Considerable attention has been paid to the second problem [9,29]. In this research, however, we focus on the first problem. For a solution, we draw on the set of necessary conditions for decision makers to change their strategies identified by Payne et al. [18].

## 2.1. Changing decision strategies

A critical influence in the selection of strategies by decision makers is the level of effort required to use the strategy [17,26]. In many situations, it is difficult or impossible to determine the correct decision. However, it always is possible to determine the level of effort expended to make the decision. ‘‘It may be that decision makers react to changes in effort considerations because that is the only salient variable’’ (Ref. [27], p. 546).

Payne et al. [18] identify three necessary conditions for decision makers to change their strategies. First, they must be dissatisfied with their current strategy. Second, the decision makers must believe a better decision-making strategy exists. And third, they must be enabled to use the new strategy. Therefore, to change decision makers’ strategies or, in this case, the models they use to reach a decision, it will be necessary to induce these three conditions.

If decision makers are satisfied with their current decision-making method, they have no reason to change strategies, and every reason to reject more complex strategies. Decision makers may become dissatisfied with their current strategy for many different reasons, such as a feeling that they are omitting relevant information when making decisions, unhappiness with decision outcomes, or a request by a superior to find a better strategy.

Training can be used to introduce a new strategy to decision makers. Complex MS/OR models are not widely known among decision makers. Training provides an opportunity to educate decision makers about alternative strategies, and the conditions under which those strategies will result in improved decision making. Thus, training can be used to convince decision makers that a new strategy will result in improved decision making in their specific environment.

Payne’s third required condition for change to occur is that decision makers must be enabled to use the new strategy. This typically involves two components: knowledge of the new strategy and support for performing the new strategy. The knowledge can be developed, in part, through training. However, for very complex tasks, training may be insufficient. This is especially true when the task is not performed frequently, so that forgetting becomes a significant problem. In such cases the framework of the knowledge persists, but the details fade. Decision aids can be used to reduce the overall complexity of the task and to reduce the cognitive load involved in remembering task details [26].

It may be that Payne’s three conditions are necessary but not sufficient conditions for a change in strategy to occur. If the new strategy is more complex than the current strategy, it may require more effort to implement. The decision maker will make a trade-off between the increased accuracy that results from using the new strategy and the increased effort required to use the new strategy. Therefore, DSS designers need to convince decision makers that: a) the more complex model results in improved decision quality; and b) the increase in decision quality is worth the increase in effort.

## 2.1.1. Feedforward and cognitive feedback as agents of change

In the cognitive learning paradigm, there is an important distinction between knowledge and skillknowing and doing. To get decision makers to use complex models, they need to both understand the general framework of the model and be able to implement the model. The ability to correctly implement the model in a specific environment requires skill beyond understanding the general framework. Feedforword and feedback are agents or learning operators that have been shown to be useful in achieving the skill to appropriately use a model [25].

Feedforward (sometimes called cognitive feedforward) is generalized task information that is provided before making a decision, usually in the form of instructions or training on a relevant decision model [6,10]. It is ‘‘generalized information pertaining to the input cues of an analysis that is provided to users prior to the performance of an analysis’’ (Ref. [10], p. 348). Instead of expecting decision makers to discover a model based on the results of decisions, the model is given to them before any decisions are made [20]. This can provide the user with confidence that an improved solution will be found. An important component of feedforward is giving decision makers the ability to correctly implement a new model.

Cognitive feedback is information about the decision-making process [25]. Not all feedback is cognitive feedback. On the contrary, an important distinction should be made between cognitive feedback and outcome feedback. Outcome feedback is ‘‘knowledge of results’’ (Ref. [6], p. 152). There is ample evidence that outcome feedback alone does not result in more accurate decision making [24]. ‘‘The information content of outcome feedback is inadequate for decision makers to form a suitable model of the system’’ (Ref. [20], p. 412). People have a bias toward ‘‘confirmatory evidence, assumptions about causality, and disregard of negative information’’ (Ref. [7], p. 223) that prevents them from accurately interpreting outcome feedback. This is especially true for complex tasks where decision makers can find many reasons for why their decisions failed. Cognitive feedback, on the other hand, has a substantial body of literature which supports its value in improving decision making (see Ref. [3] for an extensive review of the literature). Cognitive feedback has been especially helpful with complex tasks [13].

Cognitive feedback, such as outcome feedback, is presented after the decision has been made, but also includes an explanation of the decision outcome. ‘‘Cognitive feedback provides information that clarifies case specific outcome feedback. It uses outcome feedback as the starting reference point for improving the decision maker’s understanding of the task’’ (Ref. [10], p. 349). Cognitive feedback has been divided into three categories [3]: (1) task information or objective information about the task system; (2) cognitive information or feedback about the subject’s apparent decision-making strategy; and (3) functional validity information or feedback about the relationship of the task information to the cognitive information. The most useful of these for improving performance is task information [3,4]. ‘‘Task information enables a decision maker to learn more about the environment’’ [20]. Better understanding of the environment should lead to improved decision making.

Both feedforward and cognitive feedback provide task information. A major difference is when they are presented to the decision maker. Feedforward is presented before a decision is made and, possibly, even before a decision process is selected. Feedback is presented after a decision is made. Therefore, feedforward can only be general information about the decision environment. Cognitive feedback has the advantage of being able to utilize outcome feedback information (i.e., the results) and; thus, can be specific to the particular decision being made.

Decisional guidance within a decision support system is used to step decision makers through a complex strategy. ‘‘Decision guidance is the enrichment of decision models with cues that direct decision makers toward successful structuring and execution of model components’’ (Ref. [12], p.387). (Decisional guidance is not to be confused with mechanical guidance, which assists the user with the system and not the decision. See Ref. [22] for an extensive discussion.) If the decisional guidance is informative, rather than just suggestive, it also helps the decision maker learn more about an unfamiliar decision-making strategy. Decisional guidance can be implemented in a decision support system with feedforward and feedback (see Ref. [12] for a detailed discussion of this relationship). For complex tasks, informative guidance has been found to be more helpful than suggestive guidance [16]. It is not enough for decision makers to know what to do next, they must also understand why they are doing it.

Cognitive feedback has been shown repeatedly to improve decision quality, although it is difficult to implement in manual situations. However, ‘‘computerized systems offer opportunities for providing feedback effectively—opportunities that rely on instant retrieval and computation that are impractical under manual conditions’’ (Ref. [21], p. 87). Feedback has been implemented in decision support systems in a wide variety of situations. Te’eni [25] explored how the timing of feedback can enhance cognitive control and [20] used feedback with dynamic decision-making tasks. Sengupta and Te’eni [21] explored if feedback could facilitate convergence in group decision making and [12] used feedback for multicriteria decision-making tasks. Tuttle and Stocks [28] tested if feedback could affect the way individuals perceive that they make decisions and [10] proposed that feedback could improve knowledge-based system explanations. Feedback combined with feedforward in a system has been studied to a lesser extent, although both are known to be effective [10,12].

Complex models are by definition difficult for subjects to understand. Feedforward or model-centered training can be used to demonstrate that a new decision strategy may be able to produce a more accurate solution. As results become available, cognitive feedback can be used to help explain these results. Some complex decisions involve a series of more focused decisions. When that is the case, cognitive feedback can be used within the overall process to help explain the intermediate results as they occur. Reducing task uncertainty improves subject performance [6]. Because uncertainty contributes to intuitive thinking, feedforward and cognitive feedback, which reduce uncertainty, should support analytical thinking much better than outcome feedback.

We propose using cognitive feedforward and cognitive feedback to make explicit the benefits to be gained from the additional effort necessary to use a more difficult decision strategy (the complex model). In turn, we anticipate that decision makers will be willing to work harder.

## 3. The study

As stated in the introduction, the objective of this paper is to examine whether decision makers would be willing to adopt a more effortful decision strategy if the improved accuracy of the more effortful strategy were made salient. The effort vs. accuracy literatures make it clear that adoption of a more effortful strategy is dependent on the trade-off that the individual makes between increased accuracy and increased effort. This effort vs. accuracy trade-off depends on how salient the increase in accuracy can be made. As argued in the previous section, feedforward and feedback can be used to both increase the saliency of the increased accuracy (by showing how much better the more effortful strategy is) and minimize the impact of the increased effort (by enabling the decision maker to use the more effortful strategy).

The use of a complex model can be expected to increase both accuracy and effort for the decision maker. Because both accuracy and effort are increased, it is not possible to predict what is going to happen with respect to use of a complex model. We conduct an experiment to determine whether decision makers indicate they will adopt a more effortful complex model when they are both aware of the effort associated with using the more complex model and convinced that a more accurate decision will result from using the more complex model. We test to make certain that the decision makers associate a more accurate result with the more effortful strategy and test the decision makers’ intention to use the more effortful strategy [2]. The subjects and the experiment are described in the remainder of this section.

## 3.1. Participants

Fifteen research analysts from 15 different state unemployment insurance service agencies agreed to participate in the experiment. The analysts were attending a week-long seminar sponsored by the United States Department of Labor to train state agency personnel on the use of forecasting methods. All 53 unemployment insurance jurisdictions (the 50 states plus the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and the Virgin Islands) were invited to nominate research analysts to attend the seminar. The National Unemployment Insurance Service then selected 15 analysts to attend the seminar, based on the goals of the seminar and an examination of each nominee’s education and experience.

These research analysts were either currently responsible for, or about to become responsible for, forecasting workloads for their state agencies. The workload forecasts made by these analysts are used by their agencies both to plan staffing (determine how many full-time vs. part-time workers to employ) and to negotiate budgets with the National Unemployment Insurance Service. Because it relates directly to their jobs and is an important problem for the agencies for which they work, it is reasonable to assume the participants in the study were highly motivated to understand the procedures that were presented.

All seminar participants were given feedforward in the form of one full day of instruction (four 90-min sessions with midmorning, lunch, and afternoon breaks) on each of four different approaches for constructing prediction intervals (prediction intervals based on the mean, prediction intervals from simple regression, prediction intervals from multiple regression, and prediction intervals from multiple regression with seasonality modeled). The manual used to train the participants contained 153 pages. The seminar focused on forecasting weeks claimed (the total number of claims for unemployment insurance payments that are filed with a state agency in a given month).

No effort was made to manipulate or control whether the analysts were dissatisfied with their current solution. Participants were not asked how their states currently forecast weeks claimed, and no attempt was made to criticize the approach that the analysts used previously. The objective of the training was simply to explain how to construct prediction intervals based on means, simple regression, multiple regression, and multiple regression with seasonality. Hence, for this study, dissatisfaction with the current solution was an uncontrolled extraneous variable.

The same data set was used each day so participants could see the improvement that resulted from using progressively more complex models. The Number Cruncher Statistical System (NCSSk) was used to develop the forecasts and to construct the prediction intervals. During the last session of each day, participants constructed the best forecast they could (narrowest prediction interval) using the method that was discussed that day. The last day of the seminar was a hands-on working session, where the participants used data from their own states to make forecasts. At the beginning of the last day, participants were given the survey that is described below.

## 3.2. Survey instrument

By the beginning of the fifth day, participants already had constructed prediction intervals using each of the four models. The widths of the best (narrowest) prediction intervals that participants constructed are shown in Table 1. Participants had this information available to them when they completed the survey.

The survey instrument appears in Appendix A. It contains three parts. Part A1 was used to determine whether subjects were familiar with the four models for constructing prediction intervals before they attended the seminar. Part A2 was used to determine how useful the participants felt each of the four models would be to them in their job. Part A3 was used to determine the participants’ perceptions with respect to the difficulty of using and the likelihood of using each model.

Questions 2 –4 in Part A2 were adapted from Ref. [29]. They were used to assess the perceived usefulness of the models. The questions had to be modified slightly because the focus of this paper is on complex models, whereas the focus of their paper was on systems. Question 1 in Part A2 was used to assess perceived accuracy. Presumably, there would be a strong correlation between perceived accuracy and perceived usefulness.

## 3.3. Hypotheses

The use of a complex model can be expected to increase both accuracy and effort. The increase in effort can be expected to decrease the likelihood to use the system. The increase in accuracy can be expected to increase the likelihood to use the system. From the literature, it is not possible to predict what is going to happen with respect to the use of the complex model. This section presents hypotheses to explore what will happen when a complex model is introduced.

Our objective is to examine whether participants will be willing to adopt a more complex/effortful strategy if the improved accuracy of the more complex/effortful strategy is made salient. To do this, we must determine whether the more complex model is perceived to be more accurate, whether the more complex model is perceived to be more useful, whether the more complex model is perceived to be more effortful, and whether the more complex model is more likely to be used.

Accuracy is operationalized as the width of the prediction interval that is associated with a model. The payoff from using a more complex forecasting model is a more accurate forecast. The actual accuracy of a forecast cannot be assessed until a future time period, but the perceived accuracy can be immediately assessed. The width of a prediction interval quantifies the likely accuracy of a point forecast. This quantification is based upon assumptions that the forecaster may or may not accept. Thus, a narrow forecast interval might not convince a forecaster that the forecast is accurate. Some test is needed to see if the narrower prediction interval associated with the more complex model does impact the perceived accuracy of the complex model. The null hypothesis to test for this relationship is:

${ \bf { H } } _ { 1 \mathbf { { 0 } } } \colon$ There is no relationship between model complexity and perceived accuracy.

A repeated measures one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) is an appropriate technique for testing this null hypothesis. A repeated measures design is necessary because each participant evaluated all four models.

The literature shows that it is frequently difficult for decision makers to evaluate the accuracy gains to be achieved by expending greater effort [27]. However, in some instances, a decision maker may not value additional accuracy. If the decision maker is satisfied with a low level of accuracy (e.g., if the decision maker needs just a rough estimate), then greater accuracy will not be worth any additional effort. Therefore, it is necessary to test if greater levels of accuracy for this task are perceived as being more useful. The null hypothesis to test for this relationship is:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 \mathbf { o } }$ : There is no relationship between model complexity and perceived usefulness.

An appropriate way to test this null hypothesis is a repeated measures one-way analysis of variance.

In this study, model complexity was determined a priori. That is, prima facie evidence was presumed to exist for the claim that calculating a mean is less complex than running a simple regression which is less complex than running a multiple regression which is less complex than running a multiple regression that accounts for seasonality. To show that reducing outcome ambiguity can induce analysts to expend more effort, there must be a relationship in this study between the amount of effort required to use a model and the complexity of that model. The null hypothesis to test whether the participants felt using the more complex models required more effort is:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 3 \mathbf { o } }$ : There is no relationship between model complexity and perceived effort.

This can be tested using a repeated measures one-way analysis of variance for ranked data (the Friedman test).

Finally, even if there is a relationship between model complexity and perceived usefulness, this relationship may not be strong enough to compensate for the increased effort required to get the increased accuracy. If model complexity is positively related to effort and model complexity also is positively related to likelihood of use, then the relationship between model complexity and perceived usefulness is strong enough to compensate for the increased effort required to get the increased accuracy. This relationship can be tested using the following null hypothesis:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 4 \mathbf { 0 } }$ : There is no relationship between model com plexity and likelihood of use.

A repeated measures one-way analysis of variance for ranked data (the Friedman test) is appropriate for testing this hypothesis.

The alternative hypotheses for the above ANOVA tests are simply that the means are not all equal. Follow-up tests are required to determine which means are not equal. Because all of these tests involve repeated measures data, the appropriate follow-up tests are paired t-tests. The Bonferroni method can be used to control the overall level of significance for these pairwise comparisons. These follow-up tests can be one-tailed and, hence, can be used to determine the direction of any relationships that exist. In particular, they can be used to test the following directional, alternative hypotheses:

${ { \mathbf { H } } _ { \mathbf { 1 a } } } \mathrm { { : } }$ : There is a positive relationship between model complexity and perceived accuracy.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 \mathbf { a } }$ : There is a positive relationship between model complexity and perceived usefulness.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 3 \mathbf { a } }$ : There is a positive relationship between model complexity and perceived effort.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 4 \mathbf { a } }$ : There is a positive relationship between model complexity and likelihood of use.

If accepted, Hypothesis 1a would indicate that the subjects believed the widths of the prediction intervals reflect the accuracy of the model, and that the more complex model is perceived to be more accurate. If accepted, Hypothesis 2a would show that the more complex model is perceived to be more useful. Acceptance of Hypothesis 3a would show that the more complex model is perceived to require more effort, and acceptance of Hypothesis 4a would show that the more complex model is the one most likely to be used. Acceptance of all four alternative hypotheses would indicate that if the increase in accuracy is made salient, then decision makers will use the more effortful model. This has important implications for the designers of DSSs.

## 3.4. Results

For the data set that was used in the seminar, the mean and standard deviation for the weeks claimed were 221,693 and 169,975. This was a very volatile series; volatility is reflected in the prediction interval that is based on the mean. Given that the series mean is 221,693, the forecast interval based on the mean has little usefulness for prediction, but does have some usefulness for seeing what the average workload is likely to be and what the variability in the workload could be. This is mentioned so the reader can understand why the mean value for usefulness reported in Table 2 is not 7 or very close to 7.

The results reported in Table 2 came from Part A2 of the survey instrument. The reported values are the means for the thirteen participants that completed the survey (one participant had to leave on Thursday night, and one participant’s survey was not useable). The accuracy measure was a single question (Using the mean (simple regression/multiple regression/multiple regression with seasonality) would provide an accurate forecast of weeks claimed). The responses were coded on a seven-point, semantic differential scale, with 1 indicating the most accurate model and 7 indicating the least accurate model. Eight of the thirteen participants responded with a 7 for the mean, and eight participants also responded with a 1 for multiple regression with seasonality.

The usefulness measure included three questions. As explained in the previous section, these questions focused on whether the participants thought the techniques would be useful to them in their jobs. The responses were coded on a seven-point, semantic differential scale, with 1 indicating the highest usefulness, and 7 indicating the lowest usefulness. As expected, the participants also felt multiple regression with seasonality was the most useful of the four techniques for forecasting weeks claimed.

The data underlying Table 2 can be used to test Hypotheses 1 and 2. Because each participant provided data on all four models, a repeated measures one-way analysis of variance is the appropriate statistical technique for testing the overall hypotheses, and the Bonferroni pairwise comparison procedure using paired t-tests is an appropriate follow-up procedure when the null hypothesis is rejected. Results from the analysis of variance and Bonferroni follow up are presented in Table 3. The values reported in Table 3 are p-values. The $M _ { j } { ' }$ s are the mean scores for the four model types; that is, $M _ { 1 }$ is the mean score for the simple mean model, $M _ { 2 }$ is the mean score for the simple regression model, $M _ { 3 }$ is the mean score for the multiple regression model, and $M _ { 4 }$ is the mean score for the seasonally adjusted multiple regression model. For an overall level of significance of $1 \% ,$ the Bonferroni requirement for all one-tailed pairwise comparisons is that the p-value be less than 0.01/6 or 0.0017. For an overall level of significance of 5%, the Bonferroni requirement is that the p-value be less than 0.05/6 or 0.0083.

Because each participant ranked all four models, a repeated measures one-way analysis of variance for ranked data (the Friedman test) is the appropriate statistical technique for testing the overall hypotheses, and the Bonferroni pairwise comparison procedure using paired t-tests is an appropriate followup procedure if the null hypothesis is rejected. Results from the analysis of variance and Bonferroni follow up are presented in Table 5. The values reported in Table 5 are p-values. The M<sub>j</sub>’s are the mean scores for the four model types. For an overall level of significance of 1%, the Bonferroni requirement for all onetailed pairwise comparisons is that the p-value be less than 0.01/6 or 0.0017. For an overall level of significance of 5%, the Bonferroni requirement is that the $p \mathrm { - }$ value be less than 0.05/6 or 0.0083.

The data in Table 5 show that there clearly is a positive relationship between model complexity and perceived effort and a positive relationship between model complexity and likelihood of use. This means that for this example, the relationship between perceived accuracy and perceived usefulness is strong enough to compensate for the increased effort required to get the increased accuracy. The implication for DSS designers is that one way to increase the use of DSSs that incorporate complex models is to use feedback to increase the perceived accuracy associated with the use of those models. The issue for DSS designers is how to increase the perceived accuracy.

The effort vs. accuracy trade-off can be visualized as a teeter-totter, with immediate effort on one end and future payoff on the other. With high uncertainty about the future payoff, the fulcrum is placed very near the future payoff end. Because future payoffs are heavily discounted, a large improvement in the future payoff is needed to compensate for a small increase in effort. With less uncertainty, the fulcrum is shifted away from the future payoff and toward the immediate effort; hence, a more moderate future payoff is needed to compensate for an increase in effort. In our study, 14 of the 15 participants said the most complex model is the one they would use although they also said the most complex model is the most difficult to use. The forecast intervals appear to have reduced the uncertainty associated with the future payoff to the point where the uncertain future payoff (increased accuracy) outweighed the immediate effort.

A check was run to determine whether the participants were familiar with the four forecasting models prior to attending the seminar Appendix A, Part A1. The results are shown in Table 6. The numbers reported in Table 6 are the fraction of respondents that said they knew how to construct prediction intervals for the given model prior to attending the seminar. Note that only 1 of the 15 respondents had prior knowledge of the most complex model (multiple regression with seasonality), and only 5 had prior knowledge of the simplest model (mean). Hence, the results reported in Tables 2 and 4 should not be due to participants having prior knowledge of the forecasting models.

## 3.5. Summary

Changing a decision strategy requires effort. As the effort to use a strategy increases, the literature suggests subjects’ likelihood to use the system will decrease. However, as accuracy increases, subjects’ perceived usefulness will increase. To determine the outcome of these competing forces, we performed an experiment. In our study, providing participants with forecast intervals overwhelmingly convinced them that the increase in accuracy from using the more complex model compensated for the increased effort required to use the more complex model. Much work has been done on reducing the effort associated with using complex models. This is one of the first studies to focus on increasing the salience of the improved accuracy to be obtained from using a more effortful model.

## 4. Implications and summary

Decision makers could benefit from using the complex models developed in management science/operations research [23]. Nonetheless, even the majority of enterprise resource management packages, such as SAP and Oracle applications, use only simple models for decision support. The effort vs. accuracy trade-off contributes to this. To overcome that influence, this study used cognitive feedforward and feedback to increase awareness of the improved accuracy to be obtained from using a complex model. The subjects were a group of analysts who needed to perform a forecasting task. Their intention to use a model increased as accuracy increased. This suggests DSS designers should focus more effort on increasing the perceived accuracy of complex models, which is consistent with the repeated findings that perceived usefulness is much more influential with system use than perceived ease of use (e.g., Refs. [8,9]). The issue for DSS designers is how to increase perceived accuracy. We discuss the generalizability of the results of this exploratory study in terms of the cognitive feedforward and feedback used to increase perceived accuracy.

## 4.1. Cognitive feedforward

A critical aspect to changing strategies is being enable to use the new strategy [18]. Enablement involves knowing what to do as well as having the tools to do it. The best way to learn how to perform a decision strategy will depend on the specific strategy being learned as well as the decision maker’s cognitive capabilities and experience. Likewise, the tools necessary to support a strategy will vary depending on the strategy. Decisional guidance provides a framework for examining the tools that systems can include and the support for using those tools. Decisional guidance also addresses support for judgments the decision maker has to make in the course of implementing a strategy [22].

In this study, the participants were trained on the complex model (regression analysis), the decision support system (Number Cruncher Statistical System), and a database. They also were given written, step-bystep instructions to follow for building models using the DSS. All of this was done outside of the DSS. Additional research is required to determine how much of this can be done inside the DSS and the best manner in which to do it. How much training the decision makers will require and how much of that training can be incorporated into the DSS are also open questions.

## 4.2. Cognitive feedback

Cognitive feedback in the form of prediction intervals was used in this study to increase the perceived accuracy of the complex model. An issue is whether the result that was obtained is unique to the forecasting application that was used, or whether similar results can be obtained for other problem domains. This issue has two components: (1) Can accuracy measures comparable to prediction intervals be constructed for other problem domains?; and 2) Will decision makers accept those as reliable measures of increased accuracy?

Many decisions can be formulated as processes. Consider, for example, the decision on how to allocate one’s portfolio of financial instruments. A process can be defined for reallocating the portfolio each quarter. This process could be quite simple, such as invest only in a mutual fund that is indexed to the S&P 500. It also could be much more complicated such as a strategy based on Markowitz’s mean variance optimization (MVO) algorithm. Given historical data on each stock in the S&P 500, a process for forecasting the price of each stock one quarter in advance and a target level of risk, it is possible to calculate the return and the risk associated with a strategy based on Markowitz’s MVO algorithm and compare that with the return and the risk associated with using a market capitalization-based index fund strategy. The simulated risk and return are concrete measures of accuracy for the outputs of the complex and simple models. Regression and mean variance optimization are only two examples of complex models; however, they illustrate that through the use of historical or simulated data, it should be possible to construct accuracy measures for a wide range of complex models.

The analysts in our study accepted the accuracy measures, but they also had just completed 4 days of training on the models and the measures. Decision makers may not accept the accuracy measures if they do not understand the underlying model or how the accuracy measure was constructed for the model. Over time, research should be able to clarify which measures of accuracy are acceptable to DSS users, and how much training is required to get them to accept those measures. However, until that research takes place, DSS designers will have to verify that users will accept the accuracy measures they employ.

## 4.3. Summary

By reducing the cognitive effort required to use a strategy, decision makers can be influenced to use different strategies (see Ref. [5] for a review). As discussed previously, the tendency of decision makers to select the least effortful strategy is a powerful motivator. Supporting computational effort and cognitive effort have been useful tools for decision makers, but neither has broken the ‘‘complexity ceiling’’ of decision support systems that Singh and Singh [23] point out. This research shows that analysts can be motivated to use complex models under tightly controlled circumstances; and provides a rich platform for launching additional studies that may ultimately break the complexity barrier or at least raise it for a significant number of modeling paradigms.
