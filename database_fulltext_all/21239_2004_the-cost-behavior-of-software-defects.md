# The cost behavior of software defects

J. Christopher Westland\*

Department of Information and Systems Management, The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, Kowloon, Hong Kong SAR, China

Received 1 September 2000; accepted 1 December 2002

Available online 6 March 2003

## Abstract

The current research attempts to update our knowledge of the way in which defects and the expense of correcting them (or alternatively, leaving them uncorrected) influences the final cost of software. Current industry models are based on data gathered decades ago, which has raised the question of their applicability in a world of modular, graphically intensive, object-oriented software development. The results of the research show that the evolutionary growth model of software errors appears to be valid for contemporary software development programs, and is not just an artefact of the particular techniques used several decades ago. In addition, it concludes that serious defects have less influence on cost than other defects (assuming they are resolved); that defects only generate significant costs if their resolution requires system redesign; that uncorrected defects become exponentially more costly with each phase in which they are unresolved; that the number of days that a project is open is a log-linear predictor of the number of software defects that will be discovered; and that testing must occur over a substantial portion of the useful life of the system in order to detect a substantial portion of the total defects in the software. As the software industry becomes more complex, this research will help us manage defect costs as they contribute to the overall programming costs of the software. <sup>D</sup> 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

Defects in software are believed to be responsible for a large proportion of the total cost of software development. Much of the evidence concerning the behaviour of software defect cost is either anecdotal, or based on data that is at least a decade old— preceding the widespread adoption of object-oriented programming, graphical interfaces and other contemporary practice.<sup>1</sup> The current research reports the results of statistical analysis of a single software release sold by a software vendor with global operations and a number of competitive products. The vendor developed, researched, specified, and tested the software release at corporate headquarters before allowing global release.<sup>2</sup> Each completed, tested and stable product was then released in the vendor’s home market simultaneously with its being passed on for localization to specific global markets. The vendor’s effort in ‘localization’—i.e., customizing the user interface of a working software kernel for a particular local market—was expended primarily on defect search, detection and correction, despite the fact that the software being localized was full release software in the vendor’s home market. Though language translation and local idiosyncrasies of help screens and keyboards contribute significant costs, the largest costs and delays were associated with defect detection and correction.

Empirical studies of software costs suffer to some extent from the problems of all econometric studies— they lack at least one of two properties that characterize true experiments, randomization or a control group (in this case both). Thus we are limited to conducting a quasi-experiment—a quantitative research design conducted to explain relationships and to examine causality, where these shortcomings are addressed in the data analysis.

Obviously, it is desirable to insure that, to whatever extent possible, the data collected has desirable characteristics up front, whether or not a true experiment can be conducted. This is one of the advantages in choosing only the localization phase of this vendor’s software development. The limited domain of software localization (in contrast to the entire concept to delivery process) provides the researcher with a reasonably well controlled vehicle for software cost analysis, because a full release version of the software already exists, implying that any significant operation or technical defects have been resolved. This significantly controls and limits the sources of defects, and assures that projects are comparable. Localization defects must then arise in the process of converting from the full release software to the particular environment being localized for. Problems may arise from idiosyncrasies of documentation and presentation; from differing traditions for performing similar tasks required by the software; and from different character sets.

## 2. Data

The firm uses a variation on the traditional ‘‘test deck’’ method for finding defects. For a specific release, a dedicated testing group runs a predefined number and type of tests (treatments) with the localized software in an attempt to produce defects (effects). This approach is systematic and labour intensive due to the exponential growth in degrees of freedom of testing as complexity of the product grows. For example, assume 30 data points in the test event stream; if the software needs to be tested on several hundred parameters, for several hundred effects, the size of the test deck can be in the millions with labour intensive effort to figure out what effects actually exist.

The defect data obtained from the vendor consisted of 3851 detailed records of defects for the 31 software releases localized for specific markets. These were complete defect logs that were restructured into statistics of defect attributes for each software release. In addition, general ledger data provided tracking of labor, materials and other costs for each of the 31 separate localization software releases. These two datasets were merged across the 31 releases for local markets.

The volume of defects found depends on (1) how many defects exit in total, (2) how hard each is to find, and (3) how intensely, systematically and intelligently the testers look for defects. Software testers generally apply the same tests over all localized versions of a software release, but sequence the particular localized releases in order of market importance. The four markets earning the highest revenues—the vendor calls these the ‘first tier’— are tested simultaneously and before other releases. Defects found are investigated in the remaining releases prior to any other testing. The subsequent 15 markets then receive additional testing (the vendors call these the ‘second tier,’ while any remaining receive only a cursory testing, and correction only of severe defects). These latter markets are called the ‘third tier’ markets and are often markets that are loss-leaders, or with high levels of piracy. In tier three markets, it is considered economically feasible to localize only portions of the package, e.g., documentation, help screens and menus, but not defect messages. There is a significant amount of learning that takes place in the localization process for tier 1; the faults and idiosyncrasies of programs ascertained from this limited set allow much faster coding of the code released in the next two tiers. Typically there is a delay of one month between the start date of programming on tier 1 and tier 2; and another month delay between tier 2 and tier 3 (Table 1).

## 3. Analysis

Analyses of historical economic information (such as the current study) tend to share a common weakness—the unavailability of controlled, replicable experiments. These are addressed through appropriate choice of statistics for analysis, and through careful choice of datasets. In this study, data collection was restricted to localization activities on an already completed software product. Rigorous and formal testing procedures were used by the vendor, which limited potential weaknesses and confounding effects from this nonexperimental data. Because part of the data used is accounting cost data, there is the possibility of results being confounded by double-entry bookkeeping. Doubleentry bookkeeping, by definition, records information about the same transactions in multiple places around the corporate accounts simultaneously. To assure that the data obtained was adequate to answer the research questions, it was tested for multicolinearity using principal components analysis. Principal components analysis indicated that the model parameterizations used to answer research questions in the confirmatory and exploratory analysis were appropriate.

Analysis of the software defect dataset was conducted in two stages: (1) confirmatory testing answered the question of which (if any) prior model of software defects is supported by the current dataset, and (2) specification search for the form of model with the greatest explanatory power. The data is best suited for testing evolutionary models where the number of defects depends on time, with an underlying exponential distribution. The second set of models assumes that a change in defect rate occurs upon each defect discovery. Unfortunately, this premise is difficult to test with the current data, which provides a count of defects by type, rather than a rate, which is tracked over time. Mosteller and Tukey [3] comment ‘‘numbers are primarily recorded or reported in a form that reflects habit or convenience rather than suitability for analysis.’’ Models of software defect occurrence tend to reflect a concept, whereas data derive from whatever measurement systems are available. It is not surprising that we often find a poor match in confirmatory analysis. Habit or convenience in recording is reason also for investigation with tunable transformation such as the Box– Cox.

## 4. The exponential defect model

Research over the past two decades has found that the number of errors discovered in software tends to follow an evolutionary growth model, where the number of errors discovered follows an exponential distribution with the mean number of errors increasing linearly in time [2,4–6]. Evidence for this is primarily based on older software data, preceding a widespread move to object-oriented programming, so this research attempted to confirm whether an evolutionary growth model still fits the data well. Confirmatory analysis tested the hypothesis $\mathbf { H _ { 0 } }$ that defect count is exponential over time.

## 5. Cost implications of defect severity and resolution

When the vendor’s test program identifies any defect, it is prioritized from 1 to 3—roughly spanning the range from a serious defect (priority 1) requiring resolution to a minor defect which may be cosmetic (priority 3). When priority 3 defects are removed the regression results improve significantly, suggesting that there may be a good deal of arbitrariness in recording priority 3 defects, since they are unlikely to be resolved if their resolution consumes any programming resources. Note that these three priority levels— which are qualities of individual defects—are completely independent of the ‘tier’ designation—which designates the timing of market release. Tier is included in the regression to capture differences in the sequence of localizing for different markets. Because it is a significant cost factor, its effect needs to be removed to prevent its masking the influence of other variables. This will be discussed further on in the paper.

## 6. Undetected, unresolved defect costs as defects are inherited by subsequent phases of the software development

Boehm [1] argued that the cost of correcting any defect increased exponentially for each phase of the systems development life cycle that resolution is delayed. We can consider the localization process to have two distinct phases—(1) the creation of the base software in the vendor’s home market<sup>3</sup>, and (2) localization to a particular global market. Defects are either presumed to be in the delivered base model, which serves as the prototype for the localized packages, or to occur in the localization process.

## 7. Search time and error count

Up to this point, it has been assumed that the number of defects reported is solely a function of technical parameters such as inherent complexity, quality of programming and so forth. But reported defects are also a function of the intensity of search, and the impact of more or less thorough searches on each project needs to be taken into account. Localization of software is performed as well-defined tasks, within a tight schedule, on a base of code that is already in release. The search for defects can begin as soon as the base software is received by the localization programmers (generally this is the case). The term ‘‘project is open’’ refers to the ability of programmers to modify code up to compilation and release. The company for which this dataset was produced had a policy of setting clear-cut deadlines for compilation, test, and cloture on all programming modifications for a ‘‘build’’—after that deadline, the project was designated as ‘‘closed’’ and all subsequent work was to the next ‘‘build’’ of the software. Because of this, the length of time that a project is open is a good measure of the length of time spent searching, and an appropriate surrogate for the thoroughness of search.

## 8. Discussion

Given the substantial proportion of effort and cost dedicated to error correction, the insights into cost behaviour contributed by this paper should provide useful guidance to software developers in better targeting and managing their software projects. The research presented here showed that short software development life cycles appear to favor higher rates of detection; but that for any reasonable development cycle, most errors will go uncorrected. Defect correction itself is a highly defect prone process, and often generates secondary, tertiary, and so forth defects where only one existed previously. Short life cycles are likely to force constrained software development cycles, and are likely to exacerbate the risk from post-release defects.

The quality assurance viewpoint adopted in this paper suggests that information systems testing might benefit from methods and perspectives that have evolved in production. Consistent with product quality assessment literature, we could consider there to be fully five classes of metrics to measure the quality of software—defect, technical, satisfaction, warranty and reputation. Defect measures are not available until the system can be run. Control of defects is the main objective of the testing and debugging phase of software development. Technical measures assess whether the software code is well structured, whether manuals for hardware and software use are adequate, whether it is complete, correct and up-to-date. Technical measures are available for any system at any time. User satisfaction measures actually describe the value received from using the system. The vendor engages in market studies and beta testing to ascertain user satisfaction. But this data tends to be quite difficult to directly link to development costs, despite the best wishes of the vendor. Warranty costs include technical support and training, and may be one of the most significant costs of software. Warranty costs are influenced by the level of defects, but also by the willingness of users to come forth with complaints, and ability and willingness of the software vendor to accommodate the user. Reputation measures the perceived user satisfaction with the software. Reputation could be significantly different from actual satisfaction for two reasons—(1) because individual users may use only a small fraction of the functions provided in any software package (e.g., consider the fraction of functionality actually used in any word processor); and (2) because marketing and advertising often influences buyer perceptions of software quality more than actual use.

This strongly favors a strategy of continuous process improvement, rather than the test-and-forget strategy that is so often applied. Software, by its nature, is not deterministic, since much of its operation depends on an uncertain data input stream. In a well-designed system, the majority of input will have been specified in the software; data ‘‘surprises’’, even if handled gracefully, are likely to result in noticeable malfunction (i.e., defects) since the system will not know what to do with them. Cost effective and ongoing maintenance of the systems is likely to be highly beneficial, since one cannot hold a system back from release for half of its useful life. It also supports the inclusion of effective feedback loops to report on defects when they occur, and to analyze and correct them quickly.

The dataset analysed in this research shows that the evolutionary growth model of software errors appears to be valid for contemporary software development programs, and is not just an artefact of the particular techniques used several decades ago. It also provided six insights useful in designing enhanced models for software cost estimation.

(1) The regression shows that the serious defects have less influence on cost than other defects (assuming they are resolved). This likely reflects greater discretion available in how and when to resolve less severe defects. One explanation is that severe defects are likely to need immediate attention, and may be thought of as a fixed cost associated with staffing quality and complexity of localization.

(2) Defects only generate significant costs if their resolution requires system redesign. Prior studies have not considered how defects are resolved—only their occurrence. Thus prior models are likely to overstate costs, because they fail to discount defects for which no action is taken (and also for which there is likely to be no impact on the user after release).

(3) The data strongly support Boehm’s contention that uncorrected defects become exponentially more costly with each phase in which they are unresolved.

(4) The data indicates that the number of days that a project is open is a log-linear predictor of the number of software defects that will be discovered.

(5) The research found that as development progresses on a given piece of software, participants will learn more about the idiosyncrasies of the product, and thus find it less costly to perform tasks scheduled later in the life cycle.

(6) Testing must occur over a substantial portion of the useful life of the system in order to detect a substantial portion of the total defects which will ever occur. Cost effective testing thus should be incorporated into the maintenance of the system, with user feedback loops to report on defects when they occur, and to analyze and correct them quickly.

This research has shown that, not only does software defect detection and correction contribute a substantial proportion of the total cost of software, but that the management of defect detection and correction can be complex. Because human judgments are made throughout the detection and correction process, the cost of the defects may either appear as part of the programming budget, or as increased warranty or user service costs after release. The software industry is becoming structurally complex, and this research helps us to understand the nature of this complexity, in exploring defect costs as they contribute to the overall programming costs of the software.
