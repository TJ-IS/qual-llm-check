# The influence of communication mode and incentive structure on GDSS process and outcomes

Reza Barkhi<sup>a,</sup>\*, Varghese S. Jacob<sup>b</sup>, Hasan Pirkul<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Accounting and Information Systems, Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University, 3092 Pamplin Hall, Blacksburg, VA 24061-0101, USA

<sup>b</sup>School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas, P.O. Box 830688 J052, Richardson, TX 75083-0688, USA

Received 1 May 2001; accepted 1 November 2002

Available online 3 April 2003

## Abstract

Given the orientation towards groups and the increase in the use of Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) for distributed groups, we study communication mode and incentive structure to learn how these two factors influence group decision making. This paper compares the decision process and outcomes of groups that use a face-to-face GDSS (FGDSS) to those that use a distributed GDSS (DGDSS) operating under two different incentive structures. Results indicate that communication mode and incentive structure can influence the effects of each other. Hence, the appropriate design of incentive structures may be important to the success of virtual organizations. <sup>D</sup> 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: GDSS; Communication mode; Incentive structure; Negotiation

## 1. Introduction

Incentive structures affect the decision-making process and outcomes [31,44]. Incentive structures influence the strategy that individuals employ to protect their stakes in the organization [5], the decision of whether or not to share information [4,34], and the decision of what type of information to share [71].

Members in organizations learn how to maximize their payoffs given their incentive structures, sometimes at the expense of the organization. Organizations respond by utilizing new technologies to change their forms [29,50]. Given that organizations have become increasingly global and need to provide technology to support group problem solving when group members are distributed [32], it is important to understand the interaction between incentives and different forms of communication modes. Computerbased communication technology is a key enabler of virtual organizations and is affecting various dimensions of new organizational forms [26].

Communication mode can affect the degree to which a shared cooperative context can be established [73]. In the absence of a shared cooperative context, members may misrepresent their information and engage in deceptive behavior [25,42,44,45,77,78] to mislead others in an effort to maximize their individual payoff.

This paper considers the influence of two incentive structures on group interaction in two types of groups using Group Decision Support System (GDSS): faceto-face (FGDSS) and distributed (DGDSS). FGDSS groups consist of members who use a GDSS at the same place and same time. DGDSS groups consist of members who use a GDSS at the same time but at different places. One incentive structure rewards group members based on a function of individual decision outcome and the other incentive structure rewards group members based on a function of the group decision outcome.

## 2. Background

This section provides a background on the effect of incentive structure, communication mode, and their influence on group decision-making process and outcomes.

## 2.1. Incentive structures

Incentive structures can be designed to promote a distributive (win – lose) or an integrative (win – win) negotiation [24]. Individuals engaged in a negotiation task are likely to behave cooperatively if they view the negotiation as win–win and are likely to behave individualistically if they view negotiation as win –lose. Incentive structures can affect the way members cooperate or compete, the way they exchange information [71] and perform or shirk their responsibility [5]. Incentive structures that reward the members equally, based on the performance of the group, may result in free riding especially when the effort of individual members is not observable by others. When the incentive structure is individualbased, a member’s payoff for a specific outcome is affected by his own effort and private information. The fact that every member’s reward is tied to his performance is likely to motivate the individual to work hard to maximize his reward. With individual incentive both FGDSS and DGDSS users are less likely to engage in free riding [5]. Hence, with individual incentive, unlike group incentive that promotes free riding, each individual is more productive given that he receives a higher reward tied to his productivity.

Members in a group can adopt a cooperative orientation or an individualistic orientation based on the different incentive structures [21]. With the cooperative orientation, one has an incentive to do well while being concerned about the welfare of others. With an individualistic orientation, one has an incentive to do as well as s/he can without concern for the payoff for others in conflict situations.

Negotiation and conflict can be viewed as a twophase process [70]. In the differentiation phase, members define their conflicts and state the reasons for conflict so that the parties can recognize the severity of their conflicts. In the integration phase, parties acknowledge their common goals, express warmth and respect, build a productive working climate, exchange information, attempt to generate acceptable solutions, and engage in other positive actions to manage their conflict. As parties iteratively move between differentiation and integration, conflict intensity varies. Poole et al. [51] suggest that the level of conflict will affect the difficulty in moving between integration and differentiation. An individualistic orientation is generally helpful during the differentiation phase, and a cooperative orientation is more useful in the integration phase, of group negotiations.

This paper studies two incentive structures that we refer to as a group-based incentive structure and an individual-based incentive structure. The first incentive structure provides bonuses to each member based on the group decision outcome and is often designed to promote a cooperative orientation while the latter provides each member a bonus based on individual decision outcome and is often designed to promote an individualistic orientation. Deutsch [21] suggests that mutual awareness of a shared cooperative orientation is likely to help establish mutual trust that, in turn, positively influences information sharing; whereas, mutual awareness of a shared individualistic orientation is likely to result in a relationship of mutual suspicion that negatively influences information sharing. Members in a group generally negotiate on the basis of their perceptions of the other person’s trustworthiness and fairness [57]. Negotiation entails selfpresentation and face-saving; negotiators engaged in self-presentation and face-saving generally attribute more competitive motives to others than to themselves, consequently, promoting escalation that blocks integration [51]. The effects of incentive structure may be influenced by the communication mode that members in a group use for interaction.

## 2.2. Communication mode

The current research compares groups using a GDSS at the same time and same place to those using a GDSS at the same time but different places. Communication mode may affect group interaction and information exchange. Johansen et al. [35] refers to a GDSS designed for distributed members at the same time as a DGDSS or DGSS. Prior research suggests that DGDSS groups communicate differently than FGDSS groups, and that design requirements for a GDSS that supports each of these groups is substantially different [33,34,67]. In DGDSS groups, physical structures and/or distance are used to separate the members [55,56]. FGDSS groups are in the same location where there is both the opportunity and psychological obligation for face-to-face communication [45].

DGSS groups cannot engage in face-to-face, verbal, and other forms of nonverbal (i.e., body language) communication. Hence, there is less social presence associated with DGDSS than with FGDSS [62]. Social presence is the aggregation and cognitive synthesis of such factors as direction of looking, physical appearance and posture, facial expression, and the feeling of intimacy and trust as perceived by the individual. Low social presence is characterized as insensitive, cold, and impersonal. Low level of social presence can make negotiation easier when issues are framed in an impersonal rather than a personal manner. Low social presence, however, makes it more difficult to establish a shared cooperative context [73] that can lead to more truthful exchange of information.

The level of social presence affects the way individuals perceive their discussions and their relationships with others. High level of social presence in FGDSS groups affects decision processes and outcomes [30,74]. Face-to-face communication has a positive influence on nonverbal compensatory reactions [12] and attraction [46]. It also has a negative relationship with the arousal of anxiety and hostility [1]. FGDSS groups provide the highest social presence followed by groups that use video, multichannel audio, speakerphone, or written text [62].

Much of the nonverbal and verbal communication cues, which form a normal part of human interaction, is filtered in a DGDSS group resulting in lower social presence. High level of social presence helps resolve conflicts and can facilitate integration [72]. Low social presence may translate into reduced sense of responsibility for one’s actions, less fear and guilt, and diminished concern for personal standards of judgment and morality [52].

Another theory that addresses communication modes is Media Richness Theory (MRT) [13,66] which has been the focus of many recent studies [2,3,10,14,26,39,41,47,63,73]. MRT classifies communication media along a continuum of low to high ‘‘richness’’ using four criteria that include feedback, multiple cues, language variety, and personal focus. The basic tenet of MRT is that ‘‘rich’’ media provide a better ‘‘fit’’ for tasks that involve high equivocality while ‘‘lean’’ media provide a better ‘‘fit’’ for tasks that involve low equivocality. Hence, for complex negotiation tasks, richer media is a better ‘‘fit.’’ Equivocality can be resolved through the mutual development of common goals and the enactment of a common solution, and this is facilitated when members use a rich communication mode.

Although many studies have found support for MRT, other studies have found that MRT cannot explain their findings. An alternative theory that explains the results that cannot be explained by MRT is Social Influence Theory [27,28]. It extends the basic assumptions of MRT and postulates that media perceptions are, in part, socially constructed. Social influence theory assumes that (1) perceived properties of media vary by user and the social context, and therefore, are subjective; (2) not all features of media are equally salient to individuals; and (3) making choices about media is retrospectively and subjectively rational. Schmitz and Fulk [59] found support for the Social influence theory, but they also found that ‘‘perceived richness’’ is consistent with the objective characteristics of richness defined by MRT. They found that individuals in their study perceived face-to-face and computer-mediated communication as the extremes of richness continuum from highest to lowest. Others have found that media affect social information processing [56].

We note that FGDSS groups use verbal and nonverbal communication (i.e., body language and facial expressions), while DGDSS groups only use computer-mediated communication; hence, they differ in the richness of their communication with respect to both the mode of communication and the proximity of the members. Previous studies have explored each of these (i.e., communication mode and proximity) in isolation. For example, Valacich et al. [68,69] have studied communication currency, physical proximity, and computer-mediated communication. They conducted a 2  2 experimental study crossing communication mode (verbal versus text) and proximity (same place versus different places) and found that the communication mode has a significant influence on performance, while proximity had no significant effects on performance. Also, Rice and Aydin [56] found that social information operates positively through communication mechanisms, but not through spatial proximity. Our objective is to study communication modes that differ in the degree of richness.

A DGDSS, by reducing richness and social presence, may promote deindividuation/depersonalization, thus making the communication less personal. Deindividuation is defined as the process whereby submergence in a group produces loss of identity for the individual. The members’ obligations to group norms often lead to the weakening of social norms by relaxing social constraints on the individual [23,76]. We study both the communication mode and incentive structure, and how these two variables influence decision process and outcomes.

## 2.3. Communication mode and incentive structure

Incentive structure may induce a social context (i.e., cooperative or individualistic) that can, in turn, mitigate media perceptions [59]. Hence, according to social influence theory, incentive structure and communication mode can influence each other. For example, a particular communication mode may intensify the level of conflict that an incentive structure promotes.

The level of conflict is a function of the incentive structure [5,71]. The communication mode that members use may influence the intensity of the expression of conflict [51]. Hence, communication mode and incentive structures can mitigate the effects of one another. If parties cannot communicate properly and are ‘‘out of synch’’, one participant’s resistance may undermine the other participant’s willingness to integrate. Successful movement between differentiation and integration depends on the synchronization of the parties’ tendencies to differentiate or to integrate [70].

Communication mode may mitigate participants’ perceived awareness of a shared orientation. For example, FGDSS groups are likely to enjoy their communication and communicate more [11,45]. Communication mode shapes the framing of issues by encouraging or discouraging information sharing as parties attempt to develop shared viewpoints [51]. For example, a FGDSS may make it easier for parties to define issues together to develop common ground [49] and achieve integration. This implies that for group-based incentive, where building common ground is more crucial than it is for individual-based incentive, FGDSS group structure may be more appropriate than DGDSS group structure.

Sheffield [61] has found that media with reduced eye contact lead to better negotiated outcomes than do face-to-face mode in individualistic climates, but not in cooperative environments. In another study, Sheffield [60] examined the effects of the bargaining orientation (maximize individual outcome, or maximize joint outcomes) and media (1. Text, 2. Text + visual, 3. Audio, 4. Audio + visual) on group negotiations. The results showed that the absence of visual communication inherent in computer conferencing was better when the individuals were told to maximize their individual outcomes (individualistic bargaining orientation). The ‘‘lean’’ communication mode prevented bargainers from being distracted by visual cues, from the structure of the bargaining task. These visual cues may be misinterpreted as conveying competitive or hostile intent. The ‘‘lean’’ communication mode, however, was found inappropriate when the individuals were told to maximize their joint outcomes (cooperative bargaining orientation). Maximizing joint outcomes may require members to establish a shared cooperative context that may be more effectively accomplished in groups that use FGDSS [73].

A DGDSS promotes deindividuation that may have a negative effect on mixed-motive tasks in that it can foster the view of negotiation as a win–lose situation [54]. Negotiators with a win–lose orientation become more individualistic and may not exchange truthful information. Hence, they may not be able to find a noninferior solution—a solution that is best for every member involved. By depersonalizing communication, ‘‘lean’’ media induce group members to exchange minimal information because the parties do not think the information is important to communicate [51].

Given that a multitude of factors can affect the decision process and outcomes of groups, in order to study the influence of communication mode and incentive structure, it is important to isolate the factors that can potentially confound the results. For example, greater experience with a task reduces the influence of social information regarding that task [64]. Groups with experienced members perform less external search, and perceive less task complexity than groups that do not have an experienced member [53]. In addition, an individual’s experience with communication mode affects perceptions of media appropriateness [37]. Individuals perceive different degrees of social presence in the same medium based on their experience with the medium and the task [28]. However, overall, individuals rank face-to-face communication mode higher than computer chat with respect to the level of social presence [62]. Hence, with relatively homogeneous subjects having similar social interactions, it is possible to study the impact of communication mode and incentive structures on group interaction, performance, and information exchange in groups using a Level 2 GDSS [20].

## 3. Research hypotheses

Media richness theory suggests that FGDSS is a better fit for group incentive given that it involves high equivocality, while DGDSS works well for individual incentive given that it involves low equivocality. Social presence theory suggests that there is a higher level of social presence in FGDSS groups than in DGDSS groups, and hence FGDSS groups are more likely to be more cooperative and DGDSS groups are more likely to be more individualistic. The need for self-preservation and face-saving may be less important in DGDSS groups where social presence is low relative to FGDSS groups where social presence is high. Members in DGDSS groups may not feel as strongly as members of FGDSS groups that they belong to a group and hence deindividuation is higher in DGDSS groups than it is in FGDSS groups.

We expect that there would be more cooperation among members in FGDSS groups than among members in DGDSS groups. Specifically, a DGDSS having less social presence and media richness and higher level of deindividuation is more likely to promote free riding when the incentive allows for this (i.e., groupbased incentive). We propose three hypotheses concerning the influence of communication mode and incentive structure on group performance and information exchange that are derived based on the theoretical concepts of communication modes and incentive structures discussed above.

## 3.1. Group performance

Is the performance different between FGDSS and DGDSS groups when there is high equivocality (i.e., group-based incentive)? The group-based incentive structure requires more interaction between members than would the individual-based incentive as one member’s productivity will affect the reward that others receive. We compare the performance of FGDSS and DGDSS groups with a group-based incentive. A group-based incentive structure requires the group members to exchange information, develop a common goal, and build a productive working climate and engage in other positive actions to manage their conflict. Lean media used by DGDSS groups can result in a feeling of being ‘‘out of synch’’, experiencing low social presence and depersonalization. Hence, it is expected that groups with groupbased incentive structure will benefit from FGDSS that facilitates building a shared cooperative context [73] and the construction of a productive working climate to manage their conflict. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: The performance of FGDSS groups will be better than that of DGDSS groups when the incentive is group-based (high equivocality).

We do not have a hypothesis corresponding to H1 for individual-based incentive because, based on theories presented before, given the low equivocality level associated with individual-based incentive, we do not expect a significant difference between FGDSS and DGDSS groups when incentive is individual-based.

In comparison to group-based incentive, individual-based incentive requires less interaction to establish a shared cooperative context [73]. The groupbased incentive induces a level of conflict that makes integration more difficult than individual-based incentive because one member’s reward depends on other members’ productivity. The group members with individual-based incentive are concerned only with what they can get for themselves; therefore, they do not need to work with other members, as much as they do with group-based incentive, to determine a common goal and reduce equivocality. There is less equivocality associated with individual-based incentive structure than with group-based incentive structure—MRT suggests that FGDSS groups will perform better with group-based incentive structure (i.e., higher equivocality) than will DGDSS groups. Hence, the difference in performance between FGDSS and DGDSS groups is less when the incentive is individual-based than when it is group-based, suggesting that:

H2: The difference between the performance of FGDSS groups and DGDSS groups will be less with individual-based (low equivocality) than with groupbased (high equivocality) incentive structure.

## 3.2. Truthfulness of information exchange

When the information necessary to solve a problem is distributed among different group members, the accuracy and truthfulness of the information that is exchanged becomes critical for making good decisions [8,40,58]. Exchanging untruthful information could result either from intentional deception or unintentional omission. Deception is a deliberate falsification of information by a communicator, with the intent being to mislead the conversational partner [42]. When individuals misrepresent information, they often provide detection clues through discrepant verbal and nonverbal messages [25]. Even when the verbal message may be convincing, the nonverbal cues (i.e., body language, increased pitch voice) make it easier to detect untruthfulness [15 – 19]. Deceivers are likely to be more uncertain and vague, more nondominated and reticent, show more negative influence, display more arousal and noncomposure, and generally make a poorer impression than truth tellers [7]. Individuals may detect untruthfulness in face-toface communication better if the individuals knew each other [77,78]. A member in FGDSS groups tends to focus on his or her public self that is social and concerned with relationships [9]. On the other hand, when the member is alone and uses a lean communication mode, s/he tends to focus on his or her private self that is hidden and inaccessible to others. For example, situations that do not obligate a person who engages in untruthfulness to attend to an evaluator’s presence may enable him or her to better effect control over deceptive behavior [38].

Whenever the nature of communication requires the individuals to constantly assess each other’s reactions, to assess accurately the ‘atmosphere’ of the meeting, and to be sensitive to personal feelings of the group members, a medium high in social presence is preferred [62]. FGDSS would be more appropriate for establishing a shared context, while DGDSS would be more appropriate for operating within an already established context [73]. Conflicts are more pronounced in DGDSS groups than in FGDSS groups resulting in more extreme behavior in DGDSS groups [36]. This may imply that, if there is incentive to engage in untruthful information exchange, a DGDSS may exacerbate this tendency.

In DGDSS groups, communication richness is low [13], social presence is low [62], and depersonalization is high. These factors may make it easier for members in DGDSS groups to act in their own selfinterest, withhold information, and communicate less truthful and timely information. We expect that members of FGDSS groups will reveal information more truthfully to others than will members of DGDSS groups, when everything else remains constant.

H3: The members of FGDSS groups will reveal their information more truthfully than members of DGDSS groups.

## 4. The experiment

## 4.1. Research methodology

The experimental study consisted of a 2-by-2 factorial design. We compared FGDSS with DGDSS under two different incentive structures that we refer to as group-based and individual-based, respectively. Each group consisted of three members and a leader.

Members of FGDSS groups were seated around a table and could communicate both verbally (face-toface) and via computer during the problem solving session. Members of DGDSS groups were physically separated so that they did not have face-to-face contact, rather communicated electronically using computer chat. All groups had access to the same GDSS for task-related decision support. There was no explicit time limit imposed and the groups, on average, took about 2 h to complete the experiment. We will elaborate on the experiment after discussing the task, as the description of specific measures depends on the underlying task.

## 4.2. The task

The task used in this experiment is a production planning problem. A company manufactures four products. Although each product has a set of basic characteristics, they differ in some features depending on customer requirements and specific orders. A customer order consists of some combination of all four products, each tailored to the customer’s specification. Associated with each order is a total revenue value that depends on the number of products and the complexity of modifying the products to satisfy the specific requirements. For example, an order might consist of 200 units of product 1, 450 units of product 2, 200 units of product 3, and 400 units of product 4. Total revenue generated when this order is filled might be US\$865. The information available to the subject is the details of the order and the total revenue the order generates. A decision to fill an order means that the order is satisfied completely; no partial orders are shipped.

A group represented by three department managers and a leader will meet to decide which orders to fill. In order to study how information is exchanged among group members, we design the task such that members receive asymmetrical information and hence the decisions would involve equivocality. The level of equivocality would depend on the incentive structure that will be explained next. To operationalize information asymmetry, we introduce the following features into the task. Each department has information about its own internal costs, the Actual Departmental Cost (ADC). Associated with each order is a departmental projected cost that is the best estimate the organization has on how much it should cost that department to fill a particular order. A department may reveal ADCs to others. ADC may decrease as a department increases effort. Moreover, each department has information about Uncompensated Departmental Effort Cost (UDEC). This represents the costs the department incurs for filling an order, but is invisible to the company and, hence, the department is not directly compensated for them. In general, the harder the departments work, the lower the ADCs would become. The harder the department works, however, the more it consumes departmental resources.

The UDEC captures the extra cost of departmental resources that the department has to absorb internally and is not directly compensated by the organization. In essence, the ADC is a decreasing function of effort while the UDEC is an increasing function of effort. Effort level decisions depend on incentive structures and the tradeoffs between ADC and UDEC. The subjects have to communicate information about the set of orders that they are going to adopt, and this set of orders should be the same for all members in a group. Given that information is distributed among the group members, they need to exchange their private ADC information with others, and the truthfulness of this information becomes critical in the identification of noninferior solution.

## 4.2.1. Incentive structures

Consider an organization with three departments. The surrogate measure for departmental performance (individual-based performance) in many organizations is how well the departments control their costs compared to some pre-specified projected standard costs. The corresponding surrogate for departmental contribution to the organizational profit (group-based incentive) is often a bonus that is tied to organizational profit.

## 4.3. Individual-based incentive

Typical of many organizational incentive structures, member (department manager) bonus is based on how well each department controls its ADCs in comparison to Projected Costs (PCs). A department receives a bonus equal to a percentage, without loss of generality, of the difference between the ADCs and PCs. To lower the ADCs in an attempt to maximize bonus, each department incurs some UDECs. The UDEC is borne by the department and the organization does not compensate the departments for these costs, hence the name ‘‘uncompensated’’.

## 4.4. Group-based incentive

A percentage of the organizational profit is assigned for bonuses and each member receives an equal percentage of this allocated bonus pool. Organizational profit is calculated by subtracting the sum of ADCs incurred at the three departments from the revenues generated by the selected orders. Hence, the bonus each member receives depends on the ADC of other members as well as his own. If costs of other members are assumed constant, then each member may find the optimal effort level for each order, and this information is available locally at the department. Due to the interaction between a member’s bonus and other members’ costs, isolated local effort decisions do not result in the best reward for all members. Therefore, there is higher equivocality with group-based incentive than with individual-based incentive.

Fig. 2 shows an example of the relationship between costs, reward, and bonus when incentive is group-based.

As a member increases his effort level, the ADC decreases and, thus the deviation between revenue and sum of the ADCs at the three departments widens, resulting in a higher bonus for each member. Because increased bonus is associated with increased UDEC, the reward that is bonus minus the UDEC is not necessarily an increasing function of the effort level.

## 4.5. Organizational profit

A group leader tries to maximize the organizational profit as his reward is directly tied to it. The organizational profit generated by each order selected is the revenue it generates minus the sum of the ADCs incurred at the three departments. The leader gets a percentage of the organizational profit as reward. The leader’s problem, PROB-LEADER, is modeled as follows:

PROB-LEADER:

$$
\operatorname{Max} \sum_ {i \in S} \left[ 0. 0 9 \left(\operatorname{Rev} _ {i} - \sum_ {d \in D} \mathrm{ADC} _ {i d}\right) \right] Y _ {i}
$$

S.t.

$$
\sum_ {i \in S} Q _ {i k} Y _ {i} <   \text { Capacity } _ {k} \quad \forall k \in K
$$

The leader has to select a subset of the incoming customer orders, and has the authority to impose his solution on the managers. The leader may also let the members negotiate a commonly acceptable set of orders. The leader’s reward depends on the ADC incurred at each department. The ADCs are private information at the departmental level, and the leader originally has only information about the PCs, which are best estimates of how much it should cost each department to fill an order. The department managers may electronically update their ADCs by transmitting task-specific templates of the GDSS to other members.

## 4.6. Subjects

Ninety-six subjects<sup>1</sup> were randomly assigned to experimental conditions in a 2  2 experiment with balanced design. The participants in the experiments were junior- and senior-level students in a decisionmaking course who were familiar with the optimization model, the GDSS, and the problem that we used to construct the task. Solution of a case that involved solving the type of problem described above was a course requirement and an integral part of the course. Subjects were required to solve the problem in ‘management teams’.

The exercise was worth 15% of the course grade. Each student’s grade for the experiment was based on the reward points s/he received on the exercise. Once the group agreed on a solution, the reward obtained by each individual was calculated based on the type of incentive structure (i.e., individual-based or groupbased) that s/he was trying to maximize. For the individual-based incentive, one’s reward did not depend on another person’s effort; while for the group-based incentive, the reward that each person received depended on the effort level of other members as well as his/her effort. Grade points could range from a maximum of 15 to a minimum of 0 points out of the total 100 points allocated for the course grade. The students were told in the instructions that, to maximize their grade points, they had to maximize their reward. The grade on the experiment was a substantive contributor to the student’s final grade and it provided strong motivation to do well in the experiment.

Each subject was randomly assigned to either a FGDSS group or a DGDSS group and to either a group-based or individual-based incentive. The groups were formed the first week of class and were given multiple assignments during the term before they were given this case. To alleviate the problem of unfair comparisons across treatment conditions, each student was compared to individuals in the same treatment condition. Student grades were determined with respect to how well others with comparable conditions performed. To acquaint the subjects with the GDSS, a training session was held and a sample problem was solved. The formal experiment began after it was determined that the subjects fully understood the problem and the features of the GDSS.

## 4.7. GDSS features

A GDSS was designed to support the problem solving and communication needs of the groups. It ran in a PC windows environment with a LAN that supported Novell Netware. Both FGDSS and DGDSS groups were supported by this GDSS. The GDSS provided modeling and optimization capabilities, information exchange facilities, ‘‘what-if’’ capability, and the ability to capture group memory. We classify the GDSS as a Level Two GDSS as defined by DeSanctis and Gallupe [20] because of its modeling capability. The GDSS provided process support (i.e. information exchange via pre-defined templates), task structure (i.e. modeling and ‘‘what-if’’ capability), and task support (i.e. optimization capability based on the model that was formulated for each decision maker presented earlier) as proposed in a framework by Nunamaker et al. [48]. The GDSS provided communication support (real-time chat, and pre-defined templates), process structuring (memory of group interaction), and capabilities for information processing (evaluate information, cross-impact analysis) as defined by Zigurs and Buckland [75]. They suggested that information processing and process structuring capabilities should be the focus of a GDSS that is designed to aid group decision makers faced with complex tasks. The GDSS consisted of three main screens: GDSS Menu, Outgoing Messages, and Public Message Board.

The modeling capability of the GDSS selects the appropriate model for the problem from the modelbase component of the GDSS. It uses the information in a group member’s database to formulate specific problem instances and to solve each one optimally.

Because each member has to specify the effort s/he would exert, a preliminary screen asks each member to assign a specific effort level (from lowest = 1 to highest = 4) to each order. The GDSS then uses the costs (ADC and UDEC) associated with these effort levels to formulate the model of the problem that we described earlier. The GDSS would then use an optimization module to compute the optimal solution to the problem along with the corresponding reward to the user (Fig. 3).

The information exchange facility allowed users to send identified textual messages as well as taskspecific templates of information. The screen for textual message exchange had two major windows. In one window, the user could type messages for others. In the second window, the user could observe all messages and identify the participant who had sent a particular message.

The GDSS provided the means to exchange information by using pre-defined task-specific templates. This facilitated the exchange of structured and numeric information. Examples of such templates include those that allowed members to transmit their departmental cost information to others (Fig. 4), and to propose solutions to other members of the group.

As Fig. 4 illustrates, each individual can change his departmental costs for each of the 20 orders to reflect their true costs. Their true costs depend on their effort levels and they can transmit their costs to other group members. The GDSS does not force members to report truthful costs to others. When a task-specific template is transmitted, the local database of each recipient is automatically updated.

A participant could evaluate another participant’s proposed solution by using the GDSS’s ‘‘what-if’’ capability. The what-if capability allows each person to examine the incremental effect of changes to a solution. The ‘‘group memory’’ capability keeps a history of all proposed solutions. Group members have information about the solutions that have been thus far proposed by the various group members. This feature aids in the negotiation process by providing a participant with information about the preferences of other individuals, and how these preferences are changing over time.

The GDSS used in all groups was similar in every respect except that the FGDSS groups could also communicate verbally and using body language, but the DGDSS groups could only communicate electronically.

## 5. Results

We carried out data analysis following the assumptions in which the independent variables could be main effects, or could combine to create interaction effects. Data analysis for this type of theoretical model calls for multiple regression when combination of main effects and interaction effects are posited [6].

In addition to multiple regression, we used a Duncan multiple range test to isolate differences in the means of the dependent variables. After we determined the significance of each independent variable in the model using multiple regression, we used a two-factor ANOVA for each of the independent variables to determine statistical significance. ## 5.1. Truthfulness

The truthfulness of the information that is exchanged among members is a significant factor influencing the effectiveness of a group decision aid. Truthfulness is measured by dividing the number of times that untruthful information about ADC is transmitted using the cost exchange template of the GDSS (Fig. 4) to other members by the total number of times that information on ADC is transmitted using this feature. This provides a percentage of untruthful information exchange.

Hypothesis H3, that members of FGDSS groups reveal their information more truthfully than members of DGDSS groups, is supported. When the incentive was group-based, FGDSS groups reported untruthful information 19.70% compared to 82.54% for their DGDSS counterparts. Hence, with group-based incentive structure, Hypothesis H3 is supported $( p <$ 0.0005). When incentive was individual-based, FGDSS groups engaged in untruthful information exchange 11.43% while this figure was 53.97% for their DGDSS counterparts $( p < 0 . 0 0 5 )$ . These two results, together, lend support to Hypothesis H3 that, regardless of the incentive structure employed, FGDSS groups are more truthful than their DGDSS counterparts. This is consistent with prior findings [2].

Note that, in this experiment, as individuals selected the effort levels, they knew what their true ADCs associated with those effort levels were. If they did not update, or if they misrepresented their accurate ADCs, then it was counted as untruthful information exchange. We found that this phenomenon was more pronounced in DGDSS groups than in FGDSS groups. We acknowledge that sometimes individuals leave out or simplify details so as not to bore or confuse their audience [17]. By depersonalizing communication, ‘‘lean’’ media induce group members to exchange minimal information because the parties do not think the information is important to communicate [51]. The results of this study show that the significant difference between the measure used for truthful information exchange for DGDSS and FGDSS groups suggests that communication mode can mitigate the nature of information that is exchanged.

## 6. Summary and implications

This study investigated the impact of two different incentive structures, and two different communication modes on several objective and measurable outcome and process variables for GDSS groups. Fig. 5 provides a framework that summarizes the concepts discussed in this study and outlines the experiment reported in this paper. This framework was used to develop a set of hypotheses that were experimentally tested in this manuscript. All groups were supported by a Level Two GDSS [20]. Because a GDSS operates in a socio-technical environment, the impact of variables that are outside the scope of the GDSS, such as incentive structures and communication modes, should be well understood before distributed decision making can be managed to support efficient and effective decision making in human organizations. The results of this study suggest that proper design of incentive structure can enhance the effective use of GDSS and can mitigate the influence of communication mode.

One result of this study was that, for a DGDSS, individual-based incentive structure results in higher group performance, more truthful information exchange, and more rational<sup>2</sup> decisions relative to a group-based incentive structure. Hence, there may be a better fit between individual-based incentives and DGDSS group structure than there is between DGDSS group structure and group-based incentives if a shared cooperative context is lacking. Given that FGDSS group structure may be better for developing a shared cooperative context [73], DGDSS groups may benefit from periodic face-to-face meetings. Rich media can help establish a shared context, and makes the subsequent DGDSS meetings more effective.

Using a DGDSS, on average, the decision makers seem unable to develop mutual awareness of a shared cooperative orientation to establish mutual trust and a shared cooperative context. Instead, they seem to develop a relationship that is represented by mutual suspicion, free riding, and untruthful information exchange. This supports the work of Zack [73] that found the ‘‘lean’’ communication mode is not appropriate when maximizing joint outcomes (cooperative bargaining orientation). It also lends support to social presence theory and MRT as it relates to mixed-motive negotiation tasks that involve high equivocality.

Deutsch [21] proposed that trusting behavior can occur, even when the participants behave individualistically, if their trustworthiness can be enforced by a third party (i.e. a group leader). Although groups in our study had a leader in charge, s/he could not overcome the shortcomings of the DGDSS and group-based incentive structure. Hence, if a groupbased incentive is used, management should promote an atmosphere that is conducive to cooperative orientation. This may be possible by providing periodic face-to-face meetings [73].

The results of this study show clear differences on a number of objective measures between FGDSS and DGDSS groups solving a mixed-motive task. Given that the task we used was a numeric task with objective measures, and that the variations in the social processes surrounding the media use were minimal [41], we attribute the differences in performance and information exchange between FGDSS and DGDSS groups to the differences between the communication modes and the incentive variables.

The performance was higher for FGDSS groups relative to DGDSS groups for group-based incentive, but the difference was not as pronounced for individual-based incentive. This indicates that the effect of incentive structure dominates the effect of communication mode. Although the dysfunctions that result in worse performance when incentive is group-based are still persistent for FGDSS, performance was substantially higher for the FGDSS groups relative to the DGDSS groups with group-based incentive structure.

In addition, the untruthful information exchange was more pronounced in DGDSS groups than in FGDSS groups. We acknowledge that sometimes individuals leave out or simplify details so as not to bore or confuse their audience [17]. However, the significant difference in untruthfulness between FGDSS and DGDSS groups supports prior research that suggests that by depersonalizing communication, ‘‘lean’’ media induce group members to exchange minimal information because the parties do not think the information is important to communicate [51]. The results suggest that communication mode mitigates the nature of information that is exchanged. This implies that communication mode and incentive structures may be designed to compound or reduce the negative influences of each other.

The results of this study imply that group-based incentives may be dysfunctional in a distributed GDSS decision environment in the absence of a shared cooperative context. The lesson for managers may be that, when incentive structure is group-based in virtual environments where monitoring is difficult, management should pay particular attention to group dysfunctions (i.e., free riding and untruthful information exchange), penalize untruthful information exchange after a future audit, and attempt to establish trust and promote an awareness that, if they all cooperate, they will all be better off. The ‘‘dilemma’’ of free riding and not trusting each other to ‘‘pay their fair share’’ may vanish if the parties can trust each other, or if the GDSS features can monitor effort and enforce penalties on members for such dysfunctional behavior.

Given that GDSS users are likely to adapt their strategies to the available features [65], it should be possible to design GDSS features to guide them into making decisions that benefit all members. For example, the what-if capability may calculate the incremental changes of a decision on group outcomes. It is important to note that such a change to the GDSS feature results from first modifying the incentive structure and then incorporating the change in the GDSS. A GDSS should make it cognitively easier to make decisions that are aligned with organizational objectives, and cognitively harder to maximize their own interests at the expense of the organizational interest. A GDSS that truly aids decision-making groups may be one that operates in an environment where proper incentives are designed and are incorporated into the GDSS features. In other words, the design of incentives, the design of GDSS features, and the communication mode that GDSS users are provided to utilize are all design variables that may result in the success or failure of the GDSS.

This is the first study to empirically show that the incentive structure under which groups operate can significantly mitigate the influence of the technological support for group decision making. This points to the need for explicit consideration of the design and incorporation of the incentives into the technological decision-making aids provided to groups. The combination of technological support, communication mode, and incentive structure can affect group decision making; hence, future studies of GDSS should be very explicit on the incentive structure under which the group members are operating. Future research should focus on the incentive structures and their impact on various forms of virtual organizations that are proliferating today as individuals become more and more distributed. These new organizational forms and work arrangements make direct monitoring difficult and make the design of appropriate incentive structures that enhance productivity crucial as organizations move towards virtuality.

We acknowledge that different tasks may require different types of incentives, and that one should be cautious about generalizing the results of this study to other tasks. In addition, there are many different ways to operationalize individual or group incentives and it is important to not extend the results of this study beyond the specific incentives described here. We tried to capture two incentive structures used in organizations, however, there are many other types of incentives that one can use. Despite the limitations, this study should enhance awareness about the importance of incentive structures in virtual groups, and we hope that it will generate other studies that address the design of proper incentives in technology-supported virtual groups.
