# An investigation of effort–accuracy trade-off and the impact of self-efficacy on Web searching behaviors

Feng-Yang Kuo<sup>a,</sup>\*, Tsai-Hsin Chu<sup>b</sup>, Meng-Hsiang Hsu<sup>c</sup>, Hong-Ssu Hsieh<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Management, National Sun Yat-Sen University, Kaohsiung, Taiwan, ROC

<sup>b</sup> Department of Information Management, National Dong Hwa University, Hualien, Taiwan, ROC

<sup>c</sup> Department of Information Management, National Kaohsiung First University of Science and Technology, Kaohsiung, Taiwan, ROC

Received 17 December 2001; accepted 12 December 2002

Available online 4 April 2003

## Abstract

One of the major barriers to the electronic commerce is that Web users may waste a lot of time and effort to search for information. Previous research suggests that both goal and experience are important factors influencing user’s Web search behavior. In the aspect of goal motivation, the effort– accuracy trade-off model provides a good explanation for the choice of a strategy. Yet, this trade-off model does not consider the impact of the experience. The Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) may make up for this weakness by means of the construct called self-efficacy, which reflects the effect of experience. In this study, we explore if there indeed exists a trade-off effect between effort and accuracy in Web searching behaviors and how selfefficacy may impact the trade-off effect. The results show that while the emphasis of accuracy will increase the level of effort by low self-efficacy subjects, the trade-off between effort-saving and accuracy-seeking does not exist for individuals of higher selfefficacy.

Keywords: Effort and accuracy trade-off; Self-efficacy; Web search

## 1. Introduction

The rapid growth of World Wide Web (WWW) has significantly increased the quantity of data available to WWW users but at the same time reduced the accessibility of information [14]. Information explosion causes significant cognitive overload. The nonlinearity of the hyperlinked documents may further cause disorientation of people who lose their sense of location and direction. WWW users may waste a lot of time and effort in struggling with inconsistent information that is distributed on Web. Therefore, how one may find information effectively has become an important issue to WWW users. Research must be directed at understanding users’ Web search behavior to provide guidance for the design of WWW applications.

Several previous studies have responded to this concern. Hoffman and Novak [10] suggest two kinds of browsing behaviors: goal-directed and experiential. In this view, people who adopt a goal-directed strategy look for specific information, while the experiential users mainly attempt to make sense through their acts and the resulting system’s responses. Similarly, Murphy [17] suggests that searching and surfing are two major ways to browse the Web sites. Searching refers to specific information finding activity while surfing means that people simply browse Web pages for curiosity or fun. In another study, Vandenbosch and Higgins [23] find that scan- and focus-search are two ways to acquire information through Internet. In scansearch, people simply browse with no specific question to resolve, while focus-search occurs when people want specific information for specific problem. Collectively, these studies suggest that goal is very important. People who have an explicit goal will adopt a directed, focused strategy to search.

To be specific, in the aspect of goal motivation, the effort–accuracy trade-off model [12,20] provides a good explanation for the choice of a strategy. According to this model, people, as rational and adaptive decision-makers, have a reservoir of strategies from which they can choose to realize their goal. Furthermore, they will attempt to minimize the cost and/or maximize the return when selecting search strategies. Thus, people will choose a more sophisticated, and therefore, more effort-demanding strategy when their goal is to seek for an accurate result. Conversely, a less effort-consuming strategy will be employed when they do not care much for a specific outcome. Several laboratory experiments have confirmed this prediction [20].

However, many other studies have shown that in naturalistic environment, people often stick to the same strategy regardless of situational differences [13]. For example, in researching computer usage, Olson and Nilsen [19] find that expert users do not change their access method. Similarly, Hammond et al. [9] show that people rely on a rapid, low analytical control strategy to solve their problem if they are experienced and if there are a large number of cues. Finally, Beach and Mitchell [6,16] propose the image theory that stresses the intuitive and automatic aspects of decision-making in real-world settings. In their view, people are creatures of the past experience, which produce a strong image that they can use to test the acceptability and compatibility of an alternative when making decisions. An option is rejected when the weighted violation of the criteria exceeds some critical threshold. Furthermore, making judgment about the compatibility of an option with one’s image is a rapid, smooth process that can be characterized as intuition. An analytical process is evoked only if there are more than one acceptable alternative. These findings collectively suggest that people intuitively adopt one well-known alternative to solve problems when they become experienced with this particular problem domain. Thus, to effectively apply the effort–accuracy trade-off model to explain realworld search behaviors, an additional factor, experience, must be considered.

We should note that goal motivation and experience are not mutually exclusive. Rather, according to the Social Cognitive Theory [3], they are reciprocally determined. In this theory, experienced people who are also confident in their capability may adopt a goal level that is higher than one adopted by novices. Conversely, people of insufficient experience and low self-conviction may wonder and give up easily when facing trivial challenges. More importantly, experts may rely on intuitive strategies over analytical ones and can still manage to maintain their level of performance without expanding more effort. The Social Cognitive Theory therefore provides an appropriate perspective to complement the effort–accuracy tradeoff model.

Thus, in this study, we set out to reexamine the effort –accuracy trade-off theory by considering the impact of experience. Two questions are asked in this study. First, ‘‘is there an effort–accuracy trade-off on WWW search decision?’’ and, second, ‘‘how does one’s experience influence the effort–accuracy tradeoff on WWW search decision?’’ Note that according to the Social Cognitive Theory, people’s success or failure in their past experience can be indexed by a psychological construct called self-efficacy. Thus, the second question is rephrased as ‘‘how does one’s perceived self-efficacy influence the effort–accuracy trade-off on WWW search decision?’’ A three-trial experiment was conducted to address these issues.

This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the theoretical basis from which four hypotheses are developed. Sections 3 and 4 describe the research design and data analysis, respectively. Finally, we discuss the effect of self-efficacy on the effort–accuracy trade-off model in Section 5 and provide a conclusion in Section 6.

## 2. Theory and hypothesis

## 2.1. Effort– accuracy trade-off

Prior research concerning people’s strategy for processing information suggests that the number of alternatives is a critical factor in their decision. People have limited cognitive resources, a phenomenon referred to as bounded rationality [21]. Johnson and Payne [12] and Payne et al. [20] argue that, because of bounded rationality, people will make trade-off in their choice of strategies. Often, individuals rely on multiple strategies to adapt to different situations. Each strategy requires a certain amount of mental effort and produces an outcome of a certain accuracy level. Which strategy people may choose is predictable when both the benefits (as a function of the outcome’s accuracy level) and costs (as a function of the effort) of specific decision strategies are taken into account. Thus, they suggest that choice decision is a trade-off between accuracy-seeking and effortsaving.

Payne et al.’s [20] basic premise is that people tend to prefer choices that have a high level of accuracy and that require less effort. But strategies yielding more accuracy often require more effort. Therefore, people’s strategy selection is the result of a compromise between the desire to make the most accurate decision and that to minimize effort. The relation governing the selection of strategies can be depicted as an indifference curve based on people’s goal (see Fig. 1). As shown in this figure, when people are committed to an accuracy goal, they choose strategies that require a significant amount of effort in order to yield an accurate result. Conversely, people will adopt less effortful strategies that yield to results of lower level of accuracy.

Thus, according to this effort–accuracy trade-off model, WWW users will vary their search behaviors in order to adapt to the situational requirement and this decision is the result of a trade-off between effortsaving and accuracy-seeking. That is, in general situations, the accuracy of a Web search depends upon the level of effort expended for that search. Furthermore, in situations when accuracy of results is mandated, people will expend more effort in their search than when accuracy is not required. Note that the effort –accuracy trade-off model relies on time as the measurement for effort. We can therefore formulate the following hypothesis, which can be specified further in two subhypotheses.

H1: There is effort–accuracy effect in Web search.

H1a: In general situations, the accuracy of a Web search is positively correlated to the level of effort expended for that search.

H1b: In situations when the demand for outcome accuracy is required, people will expend more effort in their search than when outcome accuracy is not required.

## 2.2. Self-efficacy

Strategy choices are affected not only by goals, but also by people’s capability and their confidence in this capability to carry out the chosen strategies, both of which can be measured by the psychological construct of self-efficacy [2,5]. Self-efficacy is not an estimation of skills; rather it is a judgment about what one can do with one’s skills that influence choice of activities, effort level, and persistence. According to the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), successful past performance results in a high level of self-efficacy, which in turn determines how people will perform and how they may persist in the face of obstacles or aversive experiences. When beset with difficulties, people of low self-efficacy will entertain serious doubts about their capabilities, whereas those who have a strong sense of efficacy will direct their cognitive and motivational resources to meet the challenges [3,5].

While Payne et al. [20] suggest that people adopt time-consuming, analytical strategies when they are seeking for accuracy goal, the Social Cognitive Theory implies that a high level of performance (i.e., accuracy) is not necessarily at the price of time. Much research has suggested that experts use intuitive strategies that are less effortful (in terms of time) and yet, they still attain accuracy [6,9,16,22]. It appears that experts can make good use of their cognitive resources without varying their strategies. They are able to holistically filter out the ambiguous and uncertain information while sticking to the methods with which they are most experienced. In so doing, they can devote their cognitive resources at solving the problem itself, rather than at the choice of strategy. The result is both effective and efficient use of the same strategy that leads to quick and accurate result. Applying this concept to Web search, the high self-efficacy users may not vary their effort (in terms of time) to attain better accurate result than the inexperienced ones. As a consequence, the trade-off between effort-saving and accuracy-seeking may not occur for people of high self-efficacy.

For these reasons, in this research, we hypothesize that there will be more effort – accuracy trade-off effect for individuals who are lower in Web search self-efficacy than those who have higher. Furthermore, the high-efficacy individuals will show consistent level of effort (measured by time expended) despite the accuracy requirement. In statistical terms, there is a significant interaction effect of effort with self-efficacy on the accuracy of search outcome. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis.

H2: There is a significant interaction effect of effort with self-efficacy on the accuracy of search outcome.

Moreover, according to SCT, prior accomplishments affect future ones via self-efficacy. Successful prior performance enhances subsequent judgments of self-efficacy, which in turn enables individuals to set challenging goals for themselves and mount the level of motivation needed to reach higher levels of performance. Conversely, prior failures reduce subsequent self-efficacy and goal setting, creating selfsustaining cycles of poor performance [5,25]. Within the context of computing behavior, several researchers have also observed this reciprocal effect between performance and self-efficacy. For example, Compeau and Higgins [7,8], Agarwal et al. [1], and Johnson and Marakas [11] have theorized that individuals whose past use of computers has been successful and rewarding are more likely to develop a high level of computer self-efficacy in the future. In addition, computer self-efficacy would directly influence computing behavior. Thus, the third and fourth hypotheses of this study are formulated:

H3: For individuals who achieve a higher level of prior performance in Web search, their subsequent self-efficacy of Web search will be higher than those with a lower level of prior performance in Web search.

H4: People with a higher level of self-efficacy of Web search will perform better in Web search than those with a lower level of self-efficacy of Web search.

## 3. Research design

## 3.1. Overview

Eighty research subjects completed a three-trial Web search experiment, which is designed based on the Social Cognitive Theory and the effort–accuracy trade-off model. According to SCT, the reciprocality between self-efficacy and performance is an important issue to be observed. The three-trial format therefore allows us to conduct a close examination of the effect of prior performance on self-efficacy and that of the self-efficacy on the performance. In addition, according to the effort–accuracy trade-off model, people vary their search strategy to adapt to the different accuracy requirement. In this study, Trials 1 and 2 represent general situations, i.e., both effort-saving and accuracy-seeking are equally emphasized. But in Trial 3, a higher level of accuracy requirement is specified so that the trade-off effect can be examined. That is, in Trial 3, the award for finding the lowest price item is increased, allowing us to observe if the subjects conduct the effort–accuracy trade-off to meet the new goal. Collectively, the three trials enable us to observe of the impact of self-efficacy on performance, the effect of subjects’ self-learning from prior performance, and the effect of accuracy –effort trade-off.

## 3.2. Subjects

Eighty-eight volunteers participated in this experiment and 80 subjects completed all of the trials (see Table 1 for the demographics). There are 24 females and 58 males. Their age ranges from 18 to 58, with a mean of 26 years. Thirty of them are part-time students who major in Management Information Systems while 50 are full-time students who major in hospitality. All of them have basic Internet skills, such as Web browsing, telnet, and E-mail.

## 3.3. Procedures

Three successive trials were conducted in this study (see Fig. 2). Before starting the three trials, a pilot session was conducted. In this session, the instructor briefly introduced the experiment and its procedure. Guidelines of how to find information on WWW and how to submit the information were also reminded. A test trial was then conducted in order to make sure that the subjects could follow the procedure successfully and would finish the task correctly.

The trials were constructed according to the microanalytical research strategy outlined by Bandura [5] that required a particular sequence of task execution. First, at the beginning of each trial, a detail specification of the search target was popped on each subject’s screen. A self-efficacy questionnaire on searching this target was then administered to each subject by the instructor before the actual searching activity. The subjects had 5 min to complete the questionnaire, followed by the search task that required the subject to find the lowest price of the given target at the shortest time was then implemented. The search targets for the three trials are RAM, a scanner, and a notebook, respectively. The subjects could choose his or her preferred browser (Microsoft IE or Netscape Navigator) to complete this task. After the subjects completed the search, they entered the resulting URL and price, which were then recorded in a centralized database. The expended time and the browsing log were recorded automatically at the same time. The time allowed for each search task was 20 min, which was deemed enough since the average times expended in each trial were 12, 12, and 13 min, respectively. The performance was measured by weighted multiplication of the expended time and that price. The best performance was announced at the end of each trial as a feedback to the subjects so that all of the subjects could evaluate their own searching performance. This evaluation, in turn, influenced the subjects’ level of self-efficacy in the next trial. Finally, the top five performers received financial incentives (about US\$5 –30) as a reward to their achievement. This incentive served to ensure that subjects had a high level of interest in their participation.

## 3.4. Measurements

## 3.4.1. Self-efficacy

The design of the self-efficacy measurement was based on four guidelines suggested by Bandura [4].

First, the assessment of self-efficacy should be domain-specific. Next, perceived self-efficacy should be measured against levels of task demand that represent gradations of challenges. Participants were asked to judge their ability to meet the challenges or to surmount the various impediments. Third, the item should be phrased in terms of ‘‘can do’’ rather than ‘‘will do’’ because ‘‘can’’ was a judgment of capability and ‘‘will’’ was a statement of intention. For tasks the individuals judged they could do, they rated ‘‘how sure’’ they are of their perceived efficacy on solving cognitive problems in a 100-point scale, ranging in 10-unit intervals from 0 (‘‘cannot do’’); through intermediate degrees of assurance, 50 (‘‘moderately certain can do’’); to complete assurance, 100 (‘‘certain can do’’). To be an effective instrument, the definition of the items must refer to the person’s perception of their capabilities concerning a specific task [15]. For example, Compeau and Higgins [8] devised a Computer Self-Efficacy instrument tha included a range of basic computer tasks such as ‘‘I could complete the task using the software if there was no one around to tell me what to do as I go’’ and ‘‘I could complete the task using the software if I had never used a package like it before.’’ Note that as the task became more and more specific, the items might refer to the same specific task but varied in the level of challenges. For example, Bandura [4] measured people’s self-efficacy concerning problem-solving by asking if students could ‘‘solve 10% of the problems’’, ‘‘solve 20% of the problems,’’. . ., and ‘‘solve 100% of the problems’’ [4]. In this study, we adopted the latter formulation because the task was very specific. The efficacy strength scores were summed and divided by the total number of items to indicate the strength of perceived self-efficacy for the activity domain. Finally, a pretest must be conducted to ensure that the measurement items contained sufficient gradations of difficulty. We have done so to come up with the final items of measurement that had been attached as an Appendix A, which shows an example of the instrument that can be used for a group of 40 subjects. The Cronbach’s a of the resulting instrument were 0.95, 0.96, and 0.96 in each trial, respectively, indicating a sufficient level of reliability [18]. All the factor loadings for self-efficacy are above 0.8, indicating convergent validity is accepted (see Appendix A).

## 3.4.2. Accuracy

The level of accuracy was assessed through standardizing the price that was identified by each individual. For example, the accuracy of a particular subject who found price p would be $1 - ( p - y ) / ( x - y )$ given that the highest price found was x and the lowest one was y. The larger was the value, the more accurate was the result.

## 3.4.3. Effort

Each subject’s effort in each trial was measured by the total time each subject expended in that trial. In previous research, Payne et al. [20] accounted the effort by dividing a decision activity into a number of elementary information processes (EIPs), which included mental operations such as reading a piece of information, comparing the values of two alternatives, and multiplying a probability and payoff. These EIPs were then converted into the total time needed. Note that Payne, et al could do so because they could control the pool of strategies in the experiment [20]. However, using EIPs to count effort was not feasible in our experiment because the possible strategies for Web search were open-ended. As a result, the measurement of effort is accounted through standardizing the time expended by a particular subject in each trial.

## 3.4.4. Performance

The performance was measured by weighted multiplication of the expended time and the price found by the subject. A subject with a low level of effort and a high level of accuracy was regarded as a better performer. This conformed to the criteria set by both the effort–accuracy trade-off model and the Social Cognitive Theory. Since Trials 1 and 2 did not particularly emphasize either effort or accuracy, the performance on these trials was measured as 1  standardized (time\* price). However, the reward formula of the third trial was altered to be 1  standardized (time<sup>1/4</sup>\*price) because accuracy was emphasized at the price of effort.

## 4.2. Does self-efficacy affect effort – accuracy tradeoff?

In order to understand the effect of self-efficacy on effort –accuracy trade-off, we divide the subjects into two groups based on the average of their self-efficacy scores. There are 35 subjects in the high-efficacy group (above the average) and 45 in the low-efficacy group. Tables 5 and 6 show the means and Pearson correlations for both groups. Fig. 3 further shows the trade-off curves for both groups.

As shown in Table 6, for the high-efficacy group, there appears to be a trade-off between effort and accuracy at Trial 1 (r = 0.341, p = 0.076 < 0.1), although the level of significance is rather weak (only at $p { = } 0 . 0 7 6 { < } 0 . 1$ level). In the two subsequent trials, the direction has reversed (Trial 2: r =  0.218, p = 0.265>0.05; Trial 3: r =  0.613, $p = 0 . 0 0 0 < 0 . 0 5 )$ This means that not only the trade-off effect disappears in Trials 2 and 3, but also those expend less time achieve a higher level of accuracy. In contrast, for the low self-efficacy group, there are positive correlations between effort and accuracy in all of the three trials (Trial 1: r = 0.28, p>0.1; Trial 2: r = 0.295, p < 0.05; Trial 3: r = 0.01, p>0.1), although the correlation is significant only in Trial 2.

## 5. Discussion

Does there exist a trade-off between effort-saving and accuracy-seeking in WWW search behaviors? How does one’s perceived self-efficacy influence the effort–accuracy trade-off on WWW search decision? These are the initial questions that we attempt to address in our study. We have found that, when effort is measured in terms of time, there is no trade-off between effort and accuracy in both Trials 1 and 2. But in Trial 3, effort has a significant negative correlation with accuracy. In addition, self-efficacy has a significant impact on effort–accuracy trade-off. That is, the trade-off effect exists for the subjects of low selfefficacy but does not exist for the high self-efficacy group except at the beginning trial.

How do the subjects of high self-efficacy group get better performance without expending additional effort (time)? While checking their Web browsing log files, we find that most users of low self-efficacy rely on the search engine to find the information they want. However, for the high-efficacy group, users become reliant more and more on particular sites, indicating that they believe these sites may provide the best price information of many computer products. This effect is getting stronger and stronger as the trials continue, and yet, the same is not seen for the low-efficacy group. Furthermore, high self-efficacy subjects appear not to change their strategy when they shift to an accuracy goal. In our study, subjects who are more experienced with WWW also perceive themselves as having higher level of self-efficacy (i.e., more capable and more confident) and rely on intuitive search strategies to complete their task. This is consistent with previous studies showing that by adopting intuitive strategies, experts have better performance than the novices [9,22]. This can be attributed to successful past experience that enables subjects to develop a sophisticated mental model of the particular sites. More importantly and somewhat surprising, although high self-efficacy people generally perform well, some of the worst performances come from this group. For example, some high self-efficacy subjects were too confident to check the specification and then found a wrong item, while others did not bother to check if their price was low enough.

It becomes clear that self-efficacy moderates the effect of the effort–accuracy trade-off. One’s prior performance influences his or her level of self-efficacy. People of low self-efficacy will expend more effort if they are seeking for accuracy (see Fig. 3). But the high self-efficacy individuals consistently spend less effort (time) than the low self-efficacy ones, and in general, they are still able to achieve a high level of accuracy. They do so by employing a strategy that is focused on a certain Web sites. This superiority of high self-efficacy individuals over the low ones in Web searching may be attributed to the mental model of helpfulness concerning the few Web sites with which they are most experienced. Like all other expertise, experience leads to a crisp mental model that allows people to make creative use of the existing tools to meet their specific goals. In Simon’s [21] words, the efficiency of experts’ memory is improved through chunking. They are therefore less likely to be confused by the vast volume of cues and can quickly focus themselves on those relevant to their task. This is not to say that high self-efficacy individuals are without any risk. Their confidence may lead to very bad decisions.

## 6. Conclusions and future research

In summary, this study investigates if there is a trade-off effect between effort-saving and accuracyseeking as well as the impact of experience, represented by self-efficacy, on this trade-off for Web searching behaviors. The result shows that self-efficacy is an important factor to Web search accuracy. On average, people with low self-efficacy will vary their levels of effort to seek for different accuracy goals, while people with high self-efficacy do not significantly alter their level of effort (i.e., Web search time) but still can find accurate result in their search more quickly than those of low efficacy. The reason appears to be because high self-efficacy individuals may hold a strong mental model of usefulness in the Web sites that they use. Conversely, low self-efficacy people apparently may get lost easily because they lack a similar strategy and are still trying to identify one.

This study has several limitations. First, our sample population was predominantly young males with little computer background, and therefore, our result cannot be generalized to all populations. Nevertheless, in theory, the effect of self-efficacy on the choice of strategy (e.g., high self-efficacy people tend to use intuitive strategies and low self-efficacy ones use analytical strategies) has been shown to exist in general [5]. We believe that this issue deserves more research for further clarification. Next, we have not followed the procedure of typical laboratory experiments that investigate the trade-off between effortsaving and accuracy. In these experiments, subjects are taught a specific set of strategies that differ from one another in the required level of effort and the expected level of accuracy. The shortcoming of such arrangement is that the experimental tasks will depart substantially from those of the real world. Because our interest is in finding out if the trade-off effect exists in real-world environments, we have decided to allow subjects to choose their preferred strategies in our experiment. Third, the 20 min in each trial may not be enough for some nonexperiential users to find the result although it is far more than the average time spent. Fourth, this research relies on the use of time as the measure of effort. While this use is reasonable in the tradition of effort –accuracy research, it may be inadequate for assessing the mental effort of high selfefficacy WWW users. Indeed, the issues concerning expert intuition and how mental effort of intuition can be measured are difficult and beyond the scope of the present research. Finally, we only announce the time and price of the top five subjects, so that the others should justify their performance subjectively by comparing their answers with the announced ones. This indirect feedback may make these users misjudge their performance and affect the self-efficacy in the next trial.

The contributions of this study are several. To research, we examine how self-efficacy may affect the trade-off between effort-saving and accuracy-seeking in Web search behavior. The results suggest that this kind of trade-off may not exist for the high selfefficacy group. For researchers who are to base their work on the trade-off model proposed by Payne et al. [20], it is important to consider the effect of selfefficacy. To practice, we find that experienced users who have a high level of self-efficacy will not change their level of effort in different situations. This kind of users may have developed a strong image of helpfulness for particular sites, which is both powerful and dangerous. People relying in this intuition can be quick and accurate, and yet, when they are wrong, they often become the worst performers. The Web site designer should be aware of this tendency of Web users and provide ways for recovery.

Many opportunities for future research exist. One is that studies can be conducted to evaluate different designs of screen presentation. According to Vessey’s [24] Cognitive Fit Theory, a fit between the search strategy and the presentation may enhance people’s performance. Researchers can also compare the different effects of self-efficacy between direct and indirect feedback. Some past studies have suggested that feedback is an important factor influencing the level of self-efficacy. Thus, it is interesting to study the impact of the format, content, and speed of feedback toward self-efficacy. In addition, while this study roughly classifies the strategies used in Web search, future work may consider a microlevel analysis of user’s mental model of strategies used.

This will help to build a cognitive model of Web search behavior and aids in our understanding concerning how people form their image toward particular sites. A third potential area of research is error detection and recovery in Web search behaviors. Finally, research effort can be directed at studying how socialization may affect the image of Web sites. All of these can lead to useful guidelines for designing friendly and helpful Web sites.
