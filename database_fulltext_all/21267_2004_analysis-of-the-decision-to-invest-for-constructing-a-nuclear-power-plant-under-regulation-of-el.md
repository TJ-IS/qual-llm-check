# Analysis of the decision to invest for constructing a nuclear power plant under regulation of electricity price

Shinichiro Takizawa\*, Atsuyuki Suzuki

Department of Quantum Engineering and Systems Science, University of Tokyo, Tokyo, Japan

Received 1 June 2002; accepted 1 December 2002

Available online 4 April 2003

## Abstract

In this study, the effect of the regulation of electricity prices on the decision to invest in power plants is rationally explained using a real-options approach. Conditions under price regulation are described by a simple model in which the variable cost follows a stochastic process; free market conditions are described using Cortazar’s model. The resulting comparison could explain the promotion of nuclear power plant construction in Japan from about 1980 until the middle of the 1990s, and the avoidance of such investment in recent years. This difference between economic environments is not confirmed by the traditional net present value (NPV) approaches <sup>D</sup> 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Real-options approach; NPV approach; Investment decision; Regulated price; Free market; Nuclear power plant

## 1. Introduction

When a large-scale plant such as a power plant is constructed, the final construction cost often exceeds the scheduled cost because the duration of the construction period exceeds expectations. This particularly applies to the construction of a nuclear power plant (NPP) and is but one of the uncertainties facing investors under deregulation. The drive to deregulate industries is finally taking place in Japan as it has in other countries, and is dramatically affecting the investment patterns of electric companies there.

A real-options approach helps explain why the actual investment behavior of companies is more cautious than the standard suggested by the more conventional net present value (NPV) approach when future income is uncertain [10]. Uncertainty about total construction cost negatively affects the decision to invest; in unstable environments, the incentive to avoid uncertain investment makes companies even more sensitive to changes in construction cost [26]. In addition, there is a tendency to avoid large capital investment projects under free market conditions because of the motivation for quick returns [9]. Under deregulation, the investment required for the construction of an NPP becomes a stumbling block because of the financial risk in amortizing the large investment and the uncertainty of the total construction cost. This tendency to scrutinize financial risk is being formed in the present Japanese electricity industry. Studies evaluating power plants or electric power alternatives have been the common response to price deregulation of the industry [8,12,14,24].

In contrast, nuclear power generation has been strongly promoted by Japan’s economic environment up until the middle of the 1990s; in other countries, this period ended in the late 1980s when deregulation set in. In Japan, the total capacity of NPPs increased from 14.4 GW in 1981 to 42.3 GW in 1997 [31]. Although variables such as construction cost and location requirements have not shifted dramatically, the price deregulation of electricity affects attitudes toward construction. Under regulation, investment decisions were not affected by changes of construction cost; recouping increased construction cost was an insignificant problem in an environment in which the price of electricity was fixed. Instead, they depended on the rate of reward that was used as the electricity price regulation. Under regulatory constraint on the rate of return, companies tend to invest in excess [1]. As an example, Brennan and Schwartz [3] evaluated the value of a company under regulation and found the significant effect of regulation on investment policy. On the contrary, Dixit and Pindyck [9] studied the case in which the government imposes a ceiling and/or a floor on the price. Under this price control, their model suggests that the firm should also wait before investing. On the other hand, Bar-Ilan and Strange [2] specify a model of a two-stage sequential investment where each of the stages takes time to complete. In this model, the probability of promotion of the investment is shown under certain conditions. Recently, studies about greenhouse gas and economics are being carried out as an expansion of the regulatory policy [6,16,27].

In this study, the effect of the regulation of electricity price on a company’s decision to invest in power plants is rationally explained using a real-options approach. Conditions under price regulation are described by a simple model in which the variable cost follows a stochastic process; free market conditions are described by Cortazar’s model [6] using a real-options approach. The results of the conventional NPV analysis are the same whether under the regulation of price or under the condition of free market. Thus, we used NPV analysis as our ‘‘control,’’ against which we compared realoption analyses of investment under price regulation and deregulation. Using the real-options approach, we found that under price regulation, there are more possibilities for investment than the NPV approach would suggest. Under free market conditions, the results using real-options approach indicated more limitations to investment than the results of the NPV approach would suggest. These tendencies are confirmed by analyzing the response of the critical cost and critical price to the construction cost. All data are calculated from the annual reports of each of Japan’s electric companies from 1981 to 2000.

## 2. Assumptions

In this section, the choice of stochastic models and levels of the goodness-of-fit, and the parameters for calculations as well as cost data for obtaining the parameters are described.

In a real-options approach, the choice of a stochastic model is one of the most important factors [8]. There are numerous stochastic models [4,5,29]; modeling in which a variable production cost follows a stochastic process while the product’s price remains constant was assumed to be convenient and effective in industries (e.g., electricity) with price regulation. Actually, there are no models with adequate goodness-of-fit to describe Japanese electricity price under the regulation. They all show the same level of goodness-of-fit as when assuming the electricity price as constant. In many countries including Japan, the rate of reward was taken as the method for deciding electricity prices under regulation. In Japan, it was necessary to obtain governmental permission for revisions to the electricity price. Moreover, although deregulation has been completed as far as gradual liberalization and the price adjustments are concerned, dealings are mainly by bid or individual contract. Therefore, the model was simplified by assuming the electricity price as constant. For this price, we used estimates from the Agency of Natural Resources and Energy, and the value is 5.9 (w/kW h) [21].

In contrast, there are stochastic models for the variable cost that show high levels of goodness-offit. The models and the results of the goodness-of-fit tests are shown in Table 1. There is a similar study by Chan et al. [5] estimating and comparing a variety of models of riskless rates using a generalized method of moments (GMM) [13]. The same methodology is applied in this study. The df and overidentifying restrictions (OIR) test in Table 1 show the degree of freedom and the probability of error by rejecting the model, respectively. a, b, r, and $\gamma$ are the parameters representing the rate of mean reversion, mean, volatility, and degree of the volatility; they were decided on at the same time. Especially when the degrees of freedom are standardized at 1, the values of the OIR test for CIR’s model and Dothan’s model rise to the 90% level.

It is expected that a free trade market for electricity will be achieved in Japan in the future. In the liberalized market, it is assumed that the margin of fluctuation of the product price is larger than that of the variable cost. Therefore, it is possible to suppose the variable cost as constant. This is the assumption that is taken in the real-options approach generally. However, in this study, we specifically use Cortazar’s model. This model was developed to evaluate the value of a smelter, taking into account the fluctuations in price of the raw material by linking the price of the raw material proportionally with the product price. Because the rate of the variable cost occupied in the electricity price is high and the variable cost significantly affects the price of electricity, this model was applied in this study to describe investment in NPP after liberalization.

## 3. Mathematical models

This study utilizes two types of models for its realoptions approach: the convenient model, in which variable cost follows a stochastic process, and Cortazar’s model, in which costs are tied to price. Finally, we used the NPV approach for comparison. These three models are explained below.

## 3.1. Convenient model

This approach takes uncertainty of the variable cost into consideration by assuming that cost follows a stochastic process. The unit variable cost of the production C must satisfy the following equations:

$$
\mathrm{d} C = C \sigma_ {\mathrm{C}} \mathrm{d} z _ {\mathrm{C}},\tag{1}
$$

where $\sigma _ { \mathrm { C } }$ is the volatility of the unit variable cost of the production and dz is the increment of the Wiener process.

The value of the option to invest in the project, $H _ { 1 } ( C )$ , is defined as a function of C. Using Ito’s lemma [15], the $H _ { 1 } ( C )$ is given as:

$$
\mathrm{d} H _ {1} = H _ {1 \mathrm{C}} \mathrm{d} C + 1 / 2 H _ {1 \mathrm{CC}} (\mathrm{d} C) ^ {2},\tag{2}
$$

where the subscript C denotes the differential.

A riskless portfolio can be made by assuming that the option value $H _ { 1 } ( C )$ satisfies Ito’s lemma and that the market to completely hedge the price risks exists. Then, the following differential equation for $H _ { 1 } ( C )$ is obtained according to Pindyck’s model [25]:

$$
\sigma^ {2} C ^ {2} H _ {\mathrm{1CC}} / 2 + (r - \delta) C H _ {\mathrm{1C}} - r H _ {1} = 0,\tag{3}
$$

where the subscript C denotes the differential. d is obtained by using the CAPM theory [18,22,31] and is defined as follows:

$$
\delta = \mu_ {\mathrm{C}} = r + (\mu_ {\mathrm{M}} - r) \rho_ {\mathrm{CM}} \mathrm{STD} _ {\mathrm{C}} / \mathrm{STD} _ {\mathrm{M}},\tag{4}
$$

where $\mu$ is the risk-adjusted expected rate of return, $\rho$ is the correlation coefficient, STD is the standard deviation, and subscripts C and M denote the production cost and the market portfolio, respectively.

The differential equation for project value $V _ { 1 } ( C )$ [19] can be obtained by going through same procedure. The income from this project is denoted as: $k _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } ( P - C ) \mathrm { d } t .$ , where k $( 0 \leq k \leq k _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } )$ is the amount of production, $k _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ is the maximum amount of production, and P is the mean price of the products. Going through same procedure, the following equation can be obtained:

$$
\sigma^ {2} C ^ {2} V _ {1 \mathrm{CC}} / 2 + (r - \delta) C V _ {1 \mathrm{C}} - r V _ {1} + k (P - C) = 0.\tag{5}
$$

If C is smaller than P, the facilities of the project will be in operation. At the optimal critical cost $C ^ { * }$ , the smooth-pasting condition and the value-matching condition, denoted by [20,28]:

$$
H _ {\mathrm{1C}} (C ^ {*}) = V _ {\mathrm{1C}} (C ^ {*}),\tag{6}
$$

$$
H _ {1} (C ^ {*}) = V _ {1} (C ^ {*}) - Q,\tag{7}
$$

are satisfied ( Q denotes construction cost).

Boundary conditions differ from the general realoptions approach. They are defined as:

$$
\lim _ {C \to 0} V _ {1} (C) = k _ {\max} P / r,\tag{8}
$$

$$
\lim _ {C \to \infty} V _ {1} (C) = 0,\tag{9}
$$

$$
\lim _ {C \to \infty} H _ {1} (C) = 0.\tag{10}
$$

Eq. (8) shows that the project value has a maximum at $C = 0$ and the value is $k _ { \mathrm { m a x } } P / r$ . The existence of the upper limit of project value is the unique characteristic of this model and is the different point from the realoptions approach generally used. Eqs. (9) and (10) denote that the value of the project has no value when the variable cost is infinity, and the value of the option to invest also has no value.

From these equations, we can obtain values of $H _ { 1 } ( C ) , \quad V _ { 1 } ( C )$ , and $C ^ { * }$ at once. If variable cost C is smaller than $C ^ { * } { } _ { }$ , then investment is justified.

## 3.2. Cortazar’s model

Cortazar’s model was developed to evaluate the value of a smelter and takes into account the fluctuation of the price of the raw material by linking the price of the raw material proportionally with the product price. In this study, the model is applied to the investment decision problem for constructing an NPP after liberalization or deregulation. We redivide variable cost into fuel cost X and O&M cost d. Then, electricity price and fuel cost are both taken into consideration as variables. Assuming that the fuel cost follows stochastic process, the electricity price must satisfy the following equations:

$$
\mathrm{d} P = \left(\mu_ {\mathrm{P}} - \delta_ {\mathrm{P}}\right) P \mathrm{d} t + \sigma_ {\mathrm{P}} P \mathrm{d} z _ {\mathrm{P}}.\tag{11}
$$

where $\delta _ { \mathrm { P } }$ is the dividend rate of the electricity price. In order to obtain analytical solutions to our investment problem, we assumed that the fuel cost should link proportionally with the product price and satisfy $X { = } ( 1 - \lambda ) P .$ By this assumption, $\sigma _ { \mathrm { P } } { = } \sigma _ { \mathrm { X } }$ and $\delta _ { \mathrm { P } } = \delta _ { \mathrm { X } }$ are satisfied. Then, the option value $H _ { 2 } ( P )$ and the project value $V _ { 2 } ( P )$ are shown as functions only of $P ,$ which satisfies the following equations:

$$
(2 \sigma_ {\mathrm{X}}) ^ {2} P ^ {2} H _ {2 \mathrm{PP}} ^ {2} / 2 + 2 (r - \delta_ {\mathrm{X}}) P H _ {2 \mathrm{P}} - r H _ {2} = 0,\tag{12}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} (2 \sigma_ {\mathrm{X}}) ^ {2} P ^ {2} V _ {2 \mathrm{PP}} ^ {2} / 2 + 2 (r - \delta_ {\mathrm{X}}) P V _ {2 \mathrm{P}} - r V _ {2} \\ \quad + k (\lambda P - \mathrm{d}) = 0. \end{array}\tag{13}
$$

At the optimal critical price $P ^ { * }$ , the smooth-pasting condition and the value-matching condition are also achieved. The boundary conditions are as follows:

$$
\lim _ {P \rightarrow \infty} V _ {2} (P) = k _ {\max} \left\{\lambda P / \left(2 \delta_ {\mathrm{X}} - r\right) - \mathrm{d} / r \right\},\tag{14}
$$

$$
\lim _ {P \to 0} V _ {2} (P) = 0,\tag{15}
$$

$$
\lim _ {P \to 0} H _ {2} (P) = 0,\tag{16}
$$

$$
H _ {2 \mathrm{P}} (P ^ {*}) = V _ {2 \mathrm{P}} (P ^ {*}),\tag{17}
$$

$$
H _ {2} (P ^ {*}) = V _ {2} (P ^ {*}) - Q.\tag{18}
$$

In this case, the value-matching condition and the smooth-pasting condition of $V _ { 2 } ( P )$ are also satisfied. Eq. (14) shows the boundary condition when $P$ goes to infinity. Eqs. (15) and (16) denote that the value of the project has no value when the price is zero, and the value of the option to invest also has no value. At the optimal critical price $P ^ { * }$ , Eqs. (17) and (18) are also satisfied.

Using the data books [31 –33], we obtained parameters in these equations: $\lambda { = } 0 . 6 4 1$ 1， $\delta _ { \mathrm { X } } = 2 . 8 7$ (%/year), and d = 2.08 (w/kW h).

From these equations, we can obtain values of $H _ { 2 } ( P ) , \ V _ { 2 } ( P )$ , and $P ^ { * }$ at once. If price $P$ is smaller than $P ^ { * }$ , then investment is justified.

## 3.3. NPV approach

The difference between the present value of total cash benefit and total cash cost generation in the life of the project is defined as NPV. If NPV>0, then the investment is justified, and the value of the price or the cost at this point is defined as critical price or critical cost. Cash benefit and cash cost at each year are discounted appropriately. The value of the discount rate is estimated to be 7.02 (%/year) by using CAPM in this study. Taking every factor into consideration, it is thought to be appropriate to take 5% or

10% for a discount rate; the OECD report also uses this value [23].

## 4. Results

In the NPV analysis, the same conditions of calculation are applied for the regulated condition and free market condition. In other words, they correspond to the same theoretical condition in economy. Thus, they are used for a standard.

First, responses of the variable cost model to the change of the r and the d of the variable cost were analyzed. The values of $V _ { 1 } ( C ) - Q$ and $H _ { 1 } ( C )$ are shown in Fig. 1 for $\sigma { = } 0 . 1$ and $0 . 3 ;$ the effect of the volatility of the variable cost on the critical cost is analyzed. The larger the volatility, the larger the value of the option to invest. Because the uncertainty of future income from the project becomes larger, the investment decision is not justified at that time. This means that under large uncertainty of income, one should decide to invest more carefully, and shows the same tendency as the result of the model of the realoptions approach generally used. Therefore, the critical cost $C ^ { * }$ decreases.

Secondly, values of the $V _ { 1 } ( C ) - Q$ and $H _ { 1 } ( C )$ are shown in Fig. 2 for $\delta = 2$ and 4 (%/year). When $\delta$ increases, receipts of dividends arise by taking a long position and thereby make the investment attractive. This is in contrast to the real-options approach generally used, in which a short position is taken to hedge the risk, and payment of dividends is necessary. Although the direction of the effect is opposite, the change of d brings about the same effect.

We then compared the responses of critical cost calculated by the variable cost model and the critical price calculated by Cortazar’s model to changing construction costs. Figs. 3 and 4 show the dependencies of the critical cost and critical price on the construction cost. Fig. 3 compares the results of variable cost model and NPV analysis. Fig. 3 shows that the critical cost of the variable cost model exceeds that of NPV analysis. In other words, the possibility of investing easily under regulated conditions is suggested. Furthermore, the response to changes in construction cost is more moderate in the variable cost model than in the NPV model. This implies that the decision to invest is less affected by the changes in construction cost than the NPV model would suggest; it is easier to decide in favor of construction under conditions of regulation. Even given the possibility that a variable cost will fall in the future, it turns out to be easier to invest in the present construction of an NPP than the NPV approach would suggest.

Furthermore, the response to changes in construction cost is more sudden than that resulting from the straightforward NPV approach. This indicates that the decision to invest is drastically affected by changes in construction cost, and explains why it has been even harder to decide in favor of construction in deregulated environments than NPV analysis would suggest.

## 5. Conclusion

Using the real-options approach, we have demonstrated in an economic sense why companies facing the option of whether to invest in an NPP find the investment more favorable under regulated conditions than as evaluated in an abstract environment. This result is in stark contrast to the effect of deregulation on investment. The environment of price regulation of the electrical industry is described by a simple model in which the variable cost follows a stochastic process, while the free market environment is described by Cortazar’s model. The results could explain the promotion of NPP construction in Japan from about 1980 until the middle of the 1990s, and the avoidance of this investment in recent years. This difference is not described by the traditional NPV approach or real-options approach to investment decisions. Although there is a need to develop advanced models to evaluate this more precisely, the tendency is clearly outlined by the results of our convenient model.
