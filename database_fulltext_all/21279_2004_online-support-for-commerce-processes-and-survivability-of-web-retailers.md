# Online support for commerce processes and survivability of web retailers

Steve Muylle<sup>a,1</sup>, Amit Basu<sup>b,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> Vlerick Leuven Gent Management School, University of Ghent, Ghent, Belgium

<sup>b</sup> Edwin L. Cox School of Business, Southern Methodist University, PO Box 750333, Dallas, TX 75275-0333, USA

Received 1 August 2002; accepted 29 April 2003 Available online 21 November 2003

## Abstract

Understanding the significance of various online processes in establishing and sustaining electronic commerce (EC) is a key concern for retailers and business in general. This paper replicates and extends the Basu and Muylle [Decision Support Systems 34 (2003)] study examining the extent to which companies in various industries are supporting online processes through their retail Web sites. Using the framework of an Electronic Commerce Architecture (ECA), the same set of commercial Web sites is analyzed in order to determine how the online support for commerce processes in each industry and each type of industry has evolved. In addition, the impact of Web retailers’ extent of Web technology adoption and electronic commerce practices on survivability is investigated.

Keywords: Technology adoption; World Wide Web; Electronic commerce; Commerce processes

## 1. Introduction

Online retailing is less than a decade old. Yet this nascent industry has already gone through a boom and bust. While it is clear that online retailing is here to stay, and even thrive, successful implementation of online commerce solutions requires consideration of far more than installing a Web server. Previous research work in this area by Basu and Muylle described an Electronic Commerce Architecture (ECA) specifying processes that comprise electronic commerce (EC) [1], and applied this architecture to investigate the extent to which retailers in a wide range of industries in the United States supported these processes based on an analysis of their Web sites [3].

This paper replicates and extends the Basu and Muylle [3] study. First, this paper addresses the following research issues: (1) the extent of support for commerce processes and sub-processes in EC evolved; and (2) the differences in support for each of these commerce processes across various types of industries evolved. In addition, the Basu and Muylle [3] sample is revisited to address the following research issue: (3) does the extent of online support for commerce processes impact Web retailers’ survivability?

In order to address these issues, the previous empirical study conducted in early 1999 has been replicated in early 2002. A brief description of the data collection, the ECA measures, and the data analysis is provided in the next section. Specific attention is devoted to sample composition. The results addressing the first two research questions are outlined in Section 3, while the question on survivability is discussed in Section 4. Section 5 concludes the paper with a discussion of the study and directions for further research.

## 2. Research method

The results presented in this paper are based upon a replication of the empirical analysis of the commercial Web sites considered by Basu and Muylle [3] in early 1999. First, the Web sites that still are in operation today were analyzed in terms of the Electronic Commerce Architecture developed by Basu and Muylle [1]. Subsequently, the Basu and Muylle [3] sample was revisited to investigate the impact of support for commerce processes on survival record. In this section, the data collection method, the measurement instruments, the sample, and data analysis techniques used for the Web site analysis are described.

## 2.1. Data collection

The site analysis was structured using a comprehensive checklist based on the ECA. This score sheet consisted of 96 checkbox items relating to the commerce processes in EC. The unit of analysis was a company’s Web site, which was scored in terms of support for specific commerce processes. Binary response formats were used in order to avoid the influence of subjective biases on the results.

## 2.2. Measures

For each commerce process identified in the ECA, as well as for each of their sub-processes, meaningful aggregate variables were constructed (see Table 1). These variables all range between 0 and 1 as they represent the mean of selected binary questions pertaining to particular sub-processes in EC. The measures for basic commerce processes in EC are derived by averaging over the related sub-processes (a detailed specification of these measures is available in Basu and Muylle [2]).

## 2.3. The sample

Each site in the sample group of US Web sites considered by Basu and Muylle [3] in early 1999 was revisited and re-analyzed in early 2002. From the 141 Web sites across 10 industries that provided consumer retail services early 1999, 111 were still operational. Twenty Web sites no longer existed (e.g., www.onsale.com), five were acquired (e.g., TWA by AA), 4 only showed a homepage simply referring visitors to other sites (e.g., www.texttrader.com), while one site, www.webreservations.com, is now geared toward travel resellers rather than consumers. A description of the sample is provided in Table 2, showing the sample composition in early 1999 and early 2002, as well as the changes in each category (which will be discussed in Section 4).

In order to compare the same sample data across the replication and the initial study the analyses for the 1999 sample data (Tables 3, 4, 5b and 7b) are based on the 111 operational sites only (at the expense of potential discrepancies with the data reported in the earlier paper). However, it is of interest to note that the exclusion of the 30 Web sites from the current analysis did not significantly impact the results in Section 3. In other words, the scores for the 1999 data in Tables 3, 4, 5b and 7b did not change much when all 141 sites used in Basu and Muylle [3] were included for the 1999 data.

## 2.4. Data analysis techniques

The extent of support for commerce processes in EC was analyzed using descriptive statistics of central tendency and dispersion. Pearson or non-parametric Kendall Tau-b correlation coefficients were used to study the relationships between the commerce processes in EC. The analysis of the differences across types of industries was based on independent samples t-tests, Mann – Whitney U-tests, or Kruskal – Wallis one-way ANOVAs. Likewise, the analysis of the differences between the surviving and unsuccessful Web retailers was based on Mann–Whitney U-tests. In order to statistically account for multiple testing, the Bonferroni inequality for developing joint confidence intervals with a specified family confidence coefficient was used. Given the conservative nature of the Bonferroni procedure, the family confidence coefficient was specified at 90% [5].

## 3. Evolution of support for commerce processes in EC

This section starts by analyzing the overall patterns of evolution of support for commerce processes in EC. It addresses how both the extent of support for commerce processes and sub-processes and the correlations between these processes evolved over the 1999 – 2002 period. Subsequently, the patterns of evolution of support for commerce processes across different types of industries are documented. Finally, the evolution in the extent of support for commerce processes in EC in individual industries is described.

## 3.1. Overall patterns of evolution

A key finding of this replication study is that Web retailers are moving beyond reliance on a few, basic functionalities to support their online businesses (see Table 3 for an overview of the extent of support for each commerce process in 1999 and 2002). While companies operating in the online marketplace still dedicate most of their efforts to facilitate the product search process (77%), search-related support services (55%), display price information (67%), and offline logistics (60%, combining core logistics and logistics customization), support for almost all other commerce processes is growing. This pattern of evolution is also seen in the 78.4% of Web sites that support online sales (see Table 4). In the latter set, the highest levels of mean support are observed for the following processes: searching the products online (82%), display of price information (81%), offline logistics (72%), and searchrelated support (62%) processes. In addition, a wide array of notable patterns of evolution can be discerned at the level of each of the commerce processes.

With respect to valuation support, the strongly hyped dynamic pricing opportunity of the Internet has not materialized. Indeed, the extent of support for valuation customization is barely 5%. Both the use of price determination and valuation customization mechanisms such as price matching and product–price mix negotiation have remained at about the same low level. Clearly, Web retailers still do not seem to be exploiting the power of the Internet to offer personalized discounts or other ways of tailoring prices to individual customer’s levels of price sensitivity, instead continuing to rely on traditional fixed pricing mechanisms. The mean extent of support for auctions for instance was merely 6%, while 85% of the analyzed retailers listed product prices on their Web sites.

In the realm of authentication, a substantial increase in the extent of overall authentication support is observed (from 15% to 26%). To examine this further, the authentication framework developed by Basu and Muylle [4] was used. This framework investigates authentication in EC from three different perspectives: the buyer, the seller, and the product. For the total sample, the following trend is discerned: buyer authentication and Web site authentication increased substantially (from 10% to 28%, and from 19% to 32%, respectively), whereas the extent of support for authentication of product quality shows a smaller increase (from 24% to 29%). Authentication customization also grew strongly, from 8% to 18%. It is important to note that the observed increase in support for buyer and Web site authentication, as well as the minor increase in product authentication support, are in line with recent developments in infrastructure for online authentication. Indeed, while several mechanisms exist to support buyer and seller authentication in EC, a major open problem is the issue of overall product authentication for which no ubiquitous, robust online mechanisms are currently available [4].

Another important observation is that the extent of payment support has grown considerably (from 26% to 35%) by virtue of the surge in support for online payment (from 26% to 36%) and payment customization (from 25% to 40%). However, the extent of offline payment support increased only marginally (from 27% to 30%). This suggests that Web retailers are recognizing the critical role of online payment and that consumers are starting to feel more comfortable providing credit card information online. Indeed, widespread support for online credit card payments and even electronic wallets was found (e.g., Microsoft Passport Wallet at www.Crutchfield.com and the Quick Checkout e-wallet from AOL-Compuserve-Netscape at www.Blockbuster.com). However, support for Secure Electronic Transaction (SET), smart cards, or e-cash payments was nonexistent. Another interesting observation is that support for mediated payment processes such as PayPal (e.g., at www. UltimaOnline.com) is becoming viable.

While the adoption of online logistics has grown considerably, its level is still low. Interestingly, Web retailers in digital product industries are increasingly supporting online logistics for free product try-outs, while the full product is sold through physical containers. For instance, in the music-CD industry, Web retailers such as Amazon and Best Buy typically offer one song for free downloading through the MP3 or Liquid Audio format, while the album can only be bought on CD. Clearly, by releasing these digital products from their physical containers, the need for expensive, time consuming, offline delivery could be eliminated and huge opportunities for customization, discriminatory pricing, and cost effective online logistics could be exploited [3].

Another important development is that the biggest increase in support was observed for customer service (from 25% to 43%). This is consistent with the view that the lack of customer service among Web retailers was a major deterrent for consumers to buy online in the late 1990s.

One of the most hyped aspects of the Internet has been customization. From this research study, it is clear that Web retailers have made substantial progress in exploiting the unique customization and personalization capabilities of the Internet, apart from the surprisingly low level of valuation customization, as mentioned before. Indeed, in addition to the increases in support for logistics, authentication, and payment customization, the extent of support for search customization through customized product design/bundling and personalized Web sites has grown considerably (from 10% to 19%).

In order to examine the evolution of the patterns of support, the correlations among the various commerce processes in 2002 were compared to the corresponding data in 1999 (see Tables 5a and 5b). Interestingly, these patterns have remained quite similar. Most companies that extensively support online sales are found to provide increased support for the valuation, payment, and offline logistics processes. The observed correlations between extent of support for valuation, payment, and offline logistics are again high, while the extent of correlation between these three processes and support for online sales respectively amounts to 0.69 (from 0.74, P < 0.0001), 0.79 (from 0.75, P < 0.0001), and 0.66 (from 0.72, P < 0.0001).

Another major observation is that there has been a significant increase in the level of support for buyer authentication by firms selling digital products online. Indeed, the mean extent of support for authentication of the buyer for companies selling digital products online currently is 31%, up from 17% in 1999. One explanation for this is that these sites are targeting ‘‘Internet only’’ customers who have to be authenticated online, versus retailers of other products whose customers can more easily be authenticated offline, using traditional methods.

An encouraging observation is that, unlike the Basu and Muylle [3] study, digital product industries show a noteworthy increase in support for customized product design/bundling (mean: 0.20, from 0.15) versus physical product industries (mean: 0.06, from 0.06) ( P = 0.08). For instance, 41% (up from 23.5%) of the news companies offer personalized, online news services. On the other hand, a few companies in physical product industries are already exploiting the mass customization opportunities offered by EC and the Internet. For instance, The Knot in the gift industry offers personalized wedding supplies, while Land’s End continues to provide extensive online support for customized product design/bundling.<sup>2</sup>

When looking at the patterns of correlation among commerce processes for digital product industries, the present study shows that these patterns have remained highly similar to the ones discerned in the Basu and Muylle [3] study in 1999. Support for the search process is still found to be less consistent than support for the other commerce processes, as online retailers of digital products have not gone beyond focusing primarily on support for online search of products (which enjoys relatively high levels of support) and forego the benefits of increased awareness, an extended customer base, and personalized customer relationships that can be realized through broader search support. Conversely, at the payment level, companies in digital product industries get beyond traditional offline ways to support the payment process and continue to take advantage of the online payment opportunities offered by the Internet.

Unlike the similar pattern of correlations among commerce processes in EC for digital product industries, a more elaborate pattern of correlations was observed for physical product industries. As can be seen from Tables 7a and 7b, physical product companies are now found to devote specific attention to increasing authentication support as they increase online sales support. These correlations are predominantly driven by the extent of authentication of product quality sub-process for the search and valuation process, and the buyer authentication sub-process for the payment process. While no significant correlation was observed between the authentication and offline logistics processes, a significant correlation was observed between the latter and the buyer authentication sub-process (r = 0.41, P < 0.0001).

In this section, the extent to which support for the various commerce processes in EC has evolved in individual industries is examined.

While the Basu and Muylle [3] study found jointly significant differences for the extent of support for all basic commerce processes, the present study did find a significant difference for authentication, valuation, and offline logistics but not for the search, payment, and digital logistics processes. The significance levels and the mean extent of support for commerce processes in EC across individual industries are shown in Table 9. While the retail clothing and the gaming industry were again found to provide higher levels of support for most commerce processes, other industries such as consumer electronics, gift services, and music-CD, are catching up. Also, while the automotive parts industry again was found to provide weaker support for most commerce processes, it has made some advances and caught up with the home entertainment, book and news industries.

When a distinction is made between commerce processes that support the customer’s pre-purchase decision-making (i.e., search, valuation, and authentication) and commerce processes that support the transaction and the post-purchase stage (i.e., payment, logistics, and customer support), some noteworthy observations can be made concerning the extent to which support for these processes in EC across individual industries has evolved. Another important observation is that, again, no clear differences in support for both processes emerge in terms of type of industry. Both physical and digital product industries, as well as low cost and high cost product industries, are present at both the lower and higher levels of concurrent support for the pre- and post-purchase commerce processes.

## 4. Support for commerce processes and survivability

In this section, the Basu and Muylle [3] sample is revisited to investigate the impact of Web retailers’ extent of online support for commerce processes on survivability. In Section 4.1, the extent of online support for commerce processes for successful versus unsuccessful Web retailers is described. Section 4.2 then analyzes how the pattern of correlations differs according to Web retailers’ survival records.

It is important to note that support for commerce processes cannot be considered the sole factor influencing Web retailer survivability. Indeed, the observation that surviving Web retailers show significantly higher levels of support for particular commerce processes does not imply that increased support for these processes will lead to survival. Rather, support for these processes should be viewed as a means to increase online retailers’ chances of survival, in addition to potential other impact factors, such as the composition and quality of the management team, funding, competitive factors and quality of business partnerships.

## 4.1. Extent of support for commerce processes and survival record

As discussed in Section 2.3, only 111 of the 141 Web sites analyzed in early 1999 were still providing consumer retail services in early 2002. Clearly, the strongest shake out occurred in the music-CD industry (see its 46% attrition percentage in Table 2). Typically, Web retailers with the highest brand equity survived (e.g., CD Now, Columbia House, Tower Records, Best Buy) and even acquired others (e.g., CD Now acquired Music Boulevard). Unknown players, as well as Web sites offering free music downloads, typically disappeared from the market (e.g., MP3 Box, Music4free). Similarly, in the book industry (42% attrition), the branded Web retailers prevailed (e.g., Amazon, Barnes and Noble, Booksamillion), potentially acquiring others (e.g., Borders teaming with Amazon), while small online bookshops folded (e.g., Cherie’s Books, Booksmart). Conversely, some industries such as the news industry (no attrition) and the retail clothing industry (6% attrition) do not appear to be affected by the dot.com shakeout.

Looking at the impact of support for online commerce processes on survivability, a major observation is that Web retailers’ survival records appear to be positively influenced by their extent of support for the payment process. Indeed, when comparing the extent of support for this process between the 111 Web retailers still up and running to the 20 companies that were no longer operational in early 2002, a jointly significant difference was found. As can be seen from Table 10, the substantially higher extent of support for payment was driven by the observed differences in support for both the online payment and the payment customization sub-processes. Clearly, Web retailers appear to increase their chances of survival by moving beyond the support of traditional offline payment processes and providing higher levels of online payment and payment customization support.

Another important observation is that Web retailers benefit from increased logistics support, predominantly in the area of core logistics. Basic logistics support services such as offline delivery, providing information on the nearest dealer or store, and offering online order tracking services appear to contribute to Web retailers’ chances of survival.

Even though no jointly significant difference in support for the overall search process between successful and unsuccessful Web retailers was observed, the former seem to benefit from their higher level of search-related services support. Web retailers assisting the visitor in his/her search process by providing site maps, product catalogs, and offline mechanisms to support search (e.g., a 1–800 number) are thus more likely to succeed. Furthermore, the extent of support for online sales was also found to be important. Companies providing stronger support for online walk-in and/or subscription/account based sales are more likely to succeed than companies providing weaker support for closing the sale online.

A striking observation is that no significant difference was observed in mean extent of support for searching the seller online between successful and unsuccessful Web retailers. Thus, in contrast to the general belief that tremendous effort should be put in driving as much traffic as possible to the Web site in order to be successful, our study indicates that this does not seem to be a critical success factor. Actually, while not jointly significant, the failed Web sites provided a slightly higher level of support (mean of 0.32, s = 0.16) than successful sites (mean of 0.30, s = 0.13). This finding is also corroborated in the replication study, as the level of support for this process among successful Web sites has decreased (see Section 3.1).

Another notable finding is that no jointly significant difference was observed for customer service support. Indeed, while the two major processes that differentiate most between successful and unsuccessful Web retailers occur at the transactional (payment) and post-transactional (logistics) level, the third postpurchase stage process does not seem to make a difference. However, as discussed in Section 3.1, the low levels of customer service support observed in 1999 have dramatically increased.

## 4.2. Pattern of correlations between commerce processes in EC for successful versus unsuccessful web retailers

While both types of Web retailers show the overall intuitive pattern of correlations between valuation, payment, and logistics (see Tables 11 and 12), the major factor distinguishing successful from unsuccessful companies appears to be support for the search process. Indeed, successful Web retailers who are more extensively supporting online sales are providing significantly higher support for the search (r = 0.43, P < 0.0001), valuation (r = 0.74, P < 0.0001), payment (r = 0.76, P < 0.0001), and logistics (r = 0.71, P <

0.0001) processes, whereas unsuccessful companies only increase support for the last three processes (Kendall Tau-b correlations equal to 0.73, 0.72, and 0.70, respectively).

It is important to note that the key factor driving this increased support for the search process is successful Web retailers’ increased extent of support for searching products online. Again, the extent of support for searching the seller online does not seem to be related to success.

Another important observation is that successful Web retailers support a wider range of sub-processes as they increase their level of ECA sophistication than unsuccessful Web retailers. Indeed, when looking at the significant drivers of the common correlation pattern observed in Tables 11 and 12, successful Web retailers typically show increased support for all valuation and payment sub-processes, whereas unsuccessful companies only show enhanced support for the online price display and the online payment sub-process. At the level of the logistics process, both successful and unsuccessful Web retailers involve the core logistics and logistics customization sub-processes.

## 5. Conclusion and directions for future research

This paper replicates and extends the Basu and Muylle [3] study examining the extent to which companies in various industries are using the World Wide Web and its associated technologies to conduct retail business. Using the framework of an Electronic Commerce Architecture (ECA), the same set of commercial Web sites is analyzed in order to determine how the online support for commerce processes in each industry and each type of industry has evolved. In addition, the impact of Web retailers’ extent of Web technology adoption and electronic commerce practices on survivability is investigated.

Our analysis supports three broad propositions. First, more online retailers are realizing the complexities involved in effectively implementing EC, and online support for all commerce processes is becoming more robust. Second, Web retailers do not yet fully understand the relationship between product type and commerce processes, thus foregoing the development of Web sites that are specifically geared to their product and industry characteristics. Indeed, notwithstanding the natural affinity of the EC environment for digital products, we find that companies in physical product industries provide similar or even higher levels of support for specific commerce processes. However, when comparing Web retailers across the product cost dimension, we find more encouraging results showing that companies selling high cost products have become as progressive as companies selling inexpensive products online. Third, broad support of all commerce processes differentiates successful from unsuccessful Web retailers.

Our analysis so far has not considered one important characteristic of EC, its ability to globalize markets. This can be viewed in two ways: (1) that retailers broaden their marketing efforts across geographical boundaries; and (2) that retailers across geographical markets adopt similar methods in the virtual marketplace. We are currently investigating the latter question by augmenting our US data with data from other markets such as Latin America and Europe.
