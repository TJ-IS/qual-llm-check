# Cross-cultural differences and information systems developer values

Atreyi Kankanhalli<sup>a</sup>, Bernard C.Y. Tan<sup>a,</sup>\*, Kwok-Kee Wei<sup>a</sup>, Monica C. Holmes<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Systems, School of Computing, National University of Singapore, 3 Science Drive 2, Singapore 117543, Singapore <sup>b</sup> Department of Business Information Systems, Central Michigan University, Mount Pleasant, MI 48859, USA

Received 30 April 2002; accepted 30 June 2003

Available online 5 August 2003

## Abstract

The increasingly global nature of software development and the increasing diversity in the information systems (IS) workforce have led to failures arising from inadequate understanding of cultural influences on IS design and development. This study aims to examine the relationship between the cultural background and development values of IS developers through a field survey in Singapore and the United States. The relationship between two cultural dimensions (individualism – collectivism and masculinity –femininity) and three development values (technical, economic, and socio-political) of IS developers were investigated. A model relating cultural dimensions to development values was formulated. The findings indicate that both individualism – collectivism and masculinity – femininity are related to the technical, economic, and socio-political values of IS developers (including DSS developers). Implications for further research and practice are discussed. <sup>D</sup> 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: IS developers; Individualism – collectivism; Masculinity – femininity; Economic values; Technical values; Socio-political values

## 1. Introduction

Software development is going global in several ways. The top 100 software companies in the United States now have 50% or more of their sales outside their native country and it is common for software products to be released simultaneously in 10 or more languages [51]. Offshore outsourcing of a portion of or entire information systems (IS) functions is also becoming increasingly prevalent. As many as 203 out of Fortune 500 companies in the United States now engage in offshore outsourcing [6]. Most major software vendors such as Microsoft and IBM offer business intelligence

DSS tools that are also developed globally. Countries like India, Ireland, China, and Israel provide skilled IS labor for international corporations [1].

Within nations, the demographic diversity of the IS workforce is increasing as mobility of IS professionals increases. A Workforce 2020 report by Hudson Institute [25] documents the increase in workforce diversity in the United States. The United States Department of Labor estimates that the country would need an additional 1.3 million IS professionals by 2006 that is partially to be filled by either outsourcing or importing labor [59]. The increasing diversity in IS development workforce is bringing cultural issues to the fore [6].

IS developers from different countries may have cultural perspectives that are dissimilar from those adhered to by the companies that hire them. Incompatibilities in language and culture [57] can pose constraints to offshore IS development. Therefore, the increasingly common international IS development endeavors are not without risks. The Interface Hall of Shame website [23] compiles a list of cases of poor IS design for global use. Such examples illustrate that there is a need to better understand the cultural assumptions embedded in IS development efforts.

The IS development process is complex and ambiguous, often wrought with uncertainty. Often, IS developers have to make choices that significantly affect the IS development process and its outcomes [40]. To a great extent, these choices are based on the values of IS developers, subject to environmental constraints. Prior research indicates that the process of IS design and development can vary depending on the cultural context [9,32,43]. However, there is a lack of cross-cultural empirical comparison of IS development efforts that are theoretically motivated. Although there have been studies comparing IS developer values across cultures (e.g., Refs. [10,32,33]), the effects of cultural dimensions on IS developer values have not been empirically investigated.

The objective of this study is to develop and test a model relating cultural dimensions to IS developer values. Having a better understanding of the relationship between cultural dimensions and IS developer values may help to improve global development of IS in general and DSS in particular. Data was collected via a survey conducted in Singapore and the United States, based on established measures of cultural dimensions [22] and IS developer values [32]. Both Singapore and the United States are similar in terms of having an English-educated population, a technologically advanced workforce, and a software industry that is important to the economy. Using data from more than one country serves to enhance the generalizability of the findings of this study.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Cultural dimensions

Culture is characterized by a set of unique values that guides the behavior of people belonging to that culture [58]. Hofstede [21,22] proposes four cultural dimensions: individualism –collectivism, masculinity – femininity, power distance, and uncertainty avoidance. The major assertion of Hofstede’s framework is that there are shared values, beliefs, and norms that are culture specific and these factors can predict a wide range of human behavior and practices. These four cultural dimensions have been the bases for numerous empirical studies in a variety of disciplines [52]. Several studies have lent support to the validity of these cultural dimensions (e.g., Refs. [46,48]). A growing number of IS studies have used these cultural dimensions to account for results observed (e.g., Refs. [36,53–55]).

Although all four cultural dimensions may be useful for predicting human behavior and practices, Hofstede [22] argues that uncertainty avoidance and power distance tend to be especially relevant for research pertaining to organizational behavior and practices. This is because uncertainty avoidance and power distance jointly determine key aspects of corporate behavior such as distribution of decision authority, establishment of rules and methods, and management of risks and ambiguity [22]. Studies conducted at the organizational level have made use of these two cultural dimensions to account for empirical results (e.g., Refs. [15,45,49]). Hofstede [22] also suggests that individualism – collectivism and masculinity–femininity may be more relevant for research on individual behavior and practices. Empirical studies have successfully used individualism – collectivism (e.g., Refs. [7,26,54]) and masculinity – femininity (e.g., Refs. [16,19]) to predict human behavior at the individual level. Given that the unit of analysis in this study was the individual IS developer, individualism–collectivism and masculinity–femininity were used as a basis to build the theoretical model.

Among the various cultural dimensions, individualism –collectivism has been found to be the most stable [17,47,50,58]. Individualism and collectivism are conceived as two extremes of a bipolar continuum. An individualistic culture is one where ties between people are loose so that they base their self-understanding on their own actions, which are usually taken independently of what others think [11]. People are frank and candid. When making decisions, personal views prevail over collective concerns [22]. A collectivistic culture is one where people are integrated into strong cohesive groups [4] so that they base their selfunderstanding on the reactions of others around them [11]. When making decisions, collective concerns prevail over personal views [22]. Although differences exist among people within each country, people in Singapore tend to lean towards collectivism while people in the United States tend to lean towards individualism [22,47,50,54].

Masculinity and femininity are also conceived as two ends of a bipolar continuum. A masculine culture is one where people are task oriented [16]. People value earning power and career advancement, and emphasize efficiency. When making decisions, performance prevails over welfare [19,22]. A feminine culture is one where people are relationship oriented [16]. People focus on interpersonal issues and quality of life, and emphasize service. When making decisions, welfare prevails over performance [19,22]. Although differences exist among people within each country, people in Singapore tend to lean slightly towards femininity while people in the United States tend to lean slightly towards masculinity [22].

## 2.2. Development values

Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen [32] develop a model of the role of IS developer values in IS development (see Fig. 1). Based on this conceptual model, IS developer values are influenced by the background (e.g., education and training) and context (e.g., culture and constraints) of IS developers [32]. Background and context affect each other to some extent [40]. IS developer values can influence IS design and implementation, which in turn can determine how effectively the IS meets organizational needs.

England et al. [12] developed and validated a value measurement instrument known as the Personal Value Questionnaire (PVQ). This instrument has been used by scholars to measure the value system of managers and professionals. It is based on the rationale that the meaning an individual attaches to a set of value dimensions gives a useful description of his or her personal value system, which in turn is strongly related to his or her behavior in systematic ways. Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen [32] extend the PVQ to apply to IS developers. This extended PVQ includes three sets of values traditionally found in the IS literature [5,39]: economic, technical, and socio-political. These three sets of values may refer to the means or the ends of IS development process. Means related values are known as development process values (corresponding to IS design and implementation process in Fig. 1) while ends related values are known as development product values (corresponding to resulting system in Fig. 1).

Economic values emphasize management of resources required for IS projects. Concerns such as IS development costs and time, manpower requirements with regard to IS developers and end-users, IS skills necessary for the projects, as well as the planning and control of IS projects are important components of economic values. Technical values emphasize coordination of work done in IS projects. Issues such as promptness in responding to end-user requests, use of appropriate standards for IS development, use of latest methodologies and computer-based tools for IS development, as well as quality of IS developer work are critical components of technical values. Socio-political values emphasize involvement of interested parties in IS projects. Issues such as job autonomy for IS developers, learning opportunities for IS developers, social interaction among IS developers, end-user involvement in IS development decisions, as well as end-user understanding of IS development process are core components of socio-political values.

The existing literature suggests that there is a tradeoff between economic and technical values on one hand and socio-political values on the other hand [32]. Many IS developers are believed to emphasize economic and technical values at the expense of sociopolitical values. Some scholars suggest that having an overly rational (economic and technical) value orientation, coupled with a lack of attention to sociopolitical issues, is a cause of deficiencies in existing IS design and development practices [5,33,40].

## 3. Hypotheses

The cultural orientation of IS developers is an important component of context (see Fig. 1). Based on the conceptual model by Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen [32], cultural orientation can impact IS developer values, which in turn can influence the process and outcome of IS development efforts. This study focuses on the front-end of Fig. 1. It aims to establish the relationship between cultural orientation (individualism–collectivism and masculinity–femininity) and IS developer values (economic, technical, and sociopolitical). Fig. 2 depicts the research model.

## 3.1. Individualism –collectivism

Compared to people with collectivistic inclination, people with individualistic inclination tend to take a personal perspective in their actions and decisions [22]. Thus, when staffing IS projects, IS developers with individualistic inclination may view each individual in terms of his or her contributions (e.g., time and skills) to the projects. This corresponds to the economic values of Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen [32]. When running IS projects, IS developers with individualistic inclination may view end-users as external clients that require prompt service and may develop ways to ensure that each individual contributes to the projects in the form of quality work. This corresponds to the technical values of Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen [32]. Compared to people with individualistic inclination, people with collectivistic inclination tend to take a team perspective in their actions and decisions [22]. IS developers with collectivistic inclination tend to value the collective welfare of everyone in IS projects and work on the basis of trust. Learning opportunities may be available as part of welfare. Job autonomy may be present as evidence of trust. This corresponds to the socio-political values of Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen [32].

H1a . Individualism (relative to collectivism) is positively related to the economic values of IS developers.

H1b . Individualism (relative to collectivism) is positively related to the technical values of IS developers.

H1c . Individualism (relative to collectivism) is negatively related to the socio-political values of IS developers.

## 3.2. Masculinity –femininity

Compared to people with feminine inclination, people with masculine inclination may be more task-oriented [22]. When managing IS projects, IS developers with masculine inclination tend to emphasize bottom-line results and performance-based rewards. To enhance results, IS development costs and time are key areas that require careful planning and control. This corresponds to the economic values of Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen [32]. IS developers with masculine inclination are likely to focus on attaining high standards. Latest development methodologies and computer-based tools may be employed to help achieve high standards. This corresponds to the technical values of Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen [32]. Compared to people with masculine inclination, people with feminine inclination may be more relationship-oriented [22]. When running IS projects, IS developers with feminine inclination tend to value long-term relationship building among all team members, including endusers. This can be achieved through social interaction among IS developers and by encouraging end-user involvement in IS development decisions so as to facilitate their understanding of the IS development process. This corresponds to the socio-political values of Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen [32].

## 4. Methodology

The survey research method was used to collect the data for testing the theoretical model because an objective of this study was to have results that could be generalized to IS developers, at least to some extent [44].

## 4.1. Operationalization of constructs

All constructs were measured using tested questions from established sources to enhance face validity. The independent constructs (individualism–collectivism and masculinity – femininity) were measured using questions taken from Hofstede [21]. These questions (see Table 1) were anchored on a seven-point scale ranging from ‘extremely unimportant’ (1) to ‘extremely important’ (7). For individualism – collectivism, a higher score reflected collectivism, whereas a lower score reflected individualism (these scores were reversed during data analysis so that a higher score represented individualism). For masculinity – femininity, a higher score represented masculinity, whereas a lower score represented femininity. The dependent constructs (economic, technical, and socio- political values) were measured using questions taken from Kumar [31]. These questions (see Table 2) were also anchored on a seven-point scale ranging from ‘extremely unimportant’ (1) to ‘extremely important’ (7).

## 4.2. Survey administration

The survey was conducted in Singapore and the United States. In each country, about 10 large organizations with comparable profiles were contacted. Among these, seven organizations in the United States and nine organizations in Singapore indicated an interest to participate in this study. A parcel containing 40 sets of questionnaires with return envelopes was sent to the Chief Information Officer of each organization. The Chief Information Officer helped to distribute the questionnaire to IS developers (e.g., systems analysts and programmers) within his or her organization. A total of 115 responses were obtained from respondents in Singapore and 96 responses were obtained from respondents in the United States, giving an overall sample size of 211. The industry background of respondents is shown in Table 3.

About 59% of the respondents were males and about 41% were females. The majority of respondents (about 47%) were in the 30 to 39 years age group.

## 5. Analysis

Partial least squares (PLS), a structural equation modeling technique, was used for data analysis. PLS assesses the measurement model (relationships between questions and constructs) within the context of the structural model (relationships among constructs). This technique does not require multivariate normal distribution or large sample sizes for its data [14]. Also, it is primarily intended for causal predictive analysis in early stages of theory development. Since this study is an early attempt at developing a theoretical model to predict the impact of cultural dimensions on IS developer values and the sample size available was not large, PLS was appropriate for this study.

## 5.1. PLS measurement model

The PLS measurement model linked each construct in the structural model to questions that measured the construct. The strength of the measurement model could be established through convergent and discriminant validity. All constructs measured using reflective or formative questions must be assessed for discriminant validity. In addition, all constructs using reflective questions must be assessed for convergent validity [18].

## 5.2. PLS structural model

Given an adequate measurement model, the hypotheses could be tested by examining the structural model. The explanatory power of the structural model was assessed based on the amount of variance in the dependent constructs (economic, technical, and socio-political values) for which the model could account. The structural model could explain 27% of the variance for economic values, 32% of the variance for technical values, and 14% of the variance for socio-political values (see Fig. 3). This exceeded 10%, which was suggested by Falk and Miller [13] as indication of substantive explanatory power.

After computing parameter estimates for all paths in the structural model, PLS employed a jackknife resampling technique to compute T-values for all paths. Each hypothesis corresponded to a path in the structural model. Thus, support for each hypothesis could be determined by examining the sign (positive or negative) and statistical significance for its corresponding path (see Table 7). The hypotheses relating the two cultural dimensions to economic (H1a and H2a) and technical values (H1b and H2b) were supported. But the hypotheses relating the two cultural dimensions to socio-political values (H1c and H2c) were not supported. Instead, the results were significant in the opposite direction (see Fig. 3 and Table 7).

## 6. Discussion

In this study, a theoretical model linking cultural dimensions to IS developer values was formulated and empirically tested. The results reveal that individualism – collectivism and masculinity – femininity hadsignificant impact on economic, technical, and sociopolitical values of IS developers (see Fig. 3). Specifically, IS developers with individualistic inclination appear to have stronger economic, technical, and socio-political values than IS developers with collectivistic inclination. IS developers with masculine inclination also seem to have stronger economic, technical, and socio-political values than IS developers with feminine inclination.

Previous literature has speculated on the tradeoff between economic and technical values (rational orientation) and socio-political values (social orientation) [5,33]. In other words, IS developers with a rational orientation are believed to focus on economic and technical values at the expense of socio-political values, whereas IS developers with a social orientation are believed to focus on socio-political values at the expense of economic and technical values [32]. The results of this study do not support such predictions. Instead, these results suggest that IS developers with certain cultural characteristics (individualistic and masculine inclination) can simultaneously have strong economic, technical, and socio-political values. The tradeoff among development values does not seem to exist. Instead, some IS developers seem to have both a rational (economic and technical values) as well as a social (socio-political values) orientation.

Contrary to H3 and H6, the results indicated positive relationships between individualism and socio-political values as well as between masculinity and socio-political values. There are some plausible explanations for these results. IS developers with individualistic inclination may emphasize learning opportunities as a means to upgrade individuals to make them more competent in their work as well as job autonomy to better handle project management tasks. This corresponds to the socio-political values of Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen [32]. To enhance the effectiveness of knowledge sharing, IS developers with masculine inclination may encourage social interaction among team members. To ensure the final products meet end-user expectations, IS developers with masculine inclination may encourage end-user involvement in IS development decisions. This corresponds to the socio-political values of Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen [32].

## 6.1. Implications for practice

The results of this study offer some useful suggestions for the management of IS projects, including DSS projects. First, IS developers with collectivistic or feminine (or both) inclination may be weaker in terms of economic, technical, and socio-political values. Such IS developers may exist in larger numbers in collectivistic or feminine cultures [9,22]. Organizations can use the instrument by Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen [32] to identify such IS developers so that the necessary training to strengthen development values can be provided.

Second, IS developers with different cultural inclinations may work together in global teams. Since different cultural inclinations may result in different development values, there is potential for much conflict in such teams [29]. Again, if the necessary training to strengthen development values can be provided to IS developers who are weak in such values, this may help to alleviate conflict in global teams. Indeed, a number of offshore software outsourcing partners such as Tata Consultancy Services and Wipro Technologies have enforced the Capability Maturity Model standards and the ISO 9001 standards to assure consistent quality in software development process across global teams [27].

Additionally, when cross-cultural conflicts surface in software development teams (e.g., due to differences in meaning systems or norms regarding project time deadlines), they need to be channelized into productive task debates and not degenerate into dysfunctional affective conflict that can mar performance [24]. Conflict handling strategies like the integrative resolution approach can help promote greater understanding among team members [38]. Education to make developers sensitive to cultural differences and the corresponding differences in IS development values and increase tolerance for such differences may also be beneficial.

Economic values of IS developers can be strengthened by training them on the planning and control of IS project resources (e.g., skills, time, and finances). Costing models such as COCOMO II and Bailey– Basili estimation methods [2] can be implemented. Scheduling methods such as CPM, PERT, and Gantt Charts can also be incorporated into IS project management. In addition, latest risk management techniques pertaining to IS projects [29,34,35] can be introduced.

Technical values of IS developers can be strengthened by helping them to be sensitive to end-user requirements and training them on IS development methodologies and tools [37]. Requirements engineering methods such as joint application design and rapid application development (prototyping) can help IS developers to understand end-user requirements better [20]. New IS development methodologies and tools such as Watts Humphrey personal software process (PSP) and Harlan Mills Clean Room software process may help to raise the quality of IS products [20]. There are also ways to develop good metrics to reduce defects in IS products [3].

Socio-political values of IS developers can be strengthened by emphasizing the need for training and the need to facilitate communication and teamwork among team members. Job rotation may help to increase the variety and challenge of IS development work, thereby providing training opportunities for IS developers. Having shared goals can help to foster communication and teamwork within IS development teams. The software project management literature suggests how shared goals may serve as bases for team building techniques to facilitate communication in IS project teams [8,41].

## 6.2. Implications for research

An obvious direction for future research would be to replicate this study across a broader range of countries. The data for this study was collected in Singapore (a collectivistic and moderately feminine culture) and the United States (an individualistic and moderately masculine culture). Data from individualistic and feminine cultures (e.g., Sweden and Norway) as well as collectivistic and masculine cultures (e.g., Venezuela and Columbia) [22] can be collected to assess the robustness of the results of this study.

Another avenue for future research would be to empirically investigate the relationships among other constructs in Fig. 1. Besides cultural orientation (which has been studied here), the impact of organizational constraints (e.g., chosen IS development methodology) and organizational context (e.g., control and reward structure) on IS designer values can be examined. In addition, knowledge about the relationship between IS designer values and the IS design and implementation process can provide more precise guidelines about the kinds of training that may benefit IS designers with different background. Also, studying the relationships between cultural differences, software design and implementation process and performance outcomes (quality of the end-product and success in the marketplace) would be beneficial from the commercial point of view.

Yet another area for future research would be to study the impact of cultural differences on IS project teamwork. Training (in the form of strengthening economic, technical, and socio-political values) may help to alleviate but probably not eliminate the differences among IS developers. It would be interesting to observe how the residual cross-cultural differences may impact the actual IS design, project management and implementation functions of IS development teams, and whether this impact is contingent upon organizational constraints and context.

With regard to IS design, issues such as how differences in meaning systems, metaphors, and language affect the design could be worth investigating. Differences in power structures, coordination mechanisms, interaction with users and their effect on team development work could be studied. Future work could also study how differences in norms for adhering to time deadlines and budgets affect software project management outcomes. Such research work may be carried out via in-depth case studies involving longitudinal tracking of activities in real-life IS development teams. Also, comparison between culturally homogeneous and heterogeneous teams could help to elucidate the effects of differences in values among team members.

## 6.3. Limitations

A limitation of this study is the relative lack of rigorous sample procedure. In each country, the original set of organizations contacted had been chosen because of the availability of high-level contacts in these organizations. A more rigorous sample procedure would entail first choosing the target population of interest (e.g., a comprehensive list of global IS development organizations) and then selecting a sample of respondents that is representative of the population in terms of key characteristics [30]. Therefore, it becomes difficult to generalize the results of this study to an overall population of IS developers. Nevertheless, the objective of this study is to develop some initial understanding of the relationship between cul tural dimensions and IS developer values. This has been achieved.

Another limitation of this study is the exclusive reliance on survey research method. Though survey research method can generate some useful findings, it cannot offer the in-depth understanding on relationships among constructs that typically results from the case study method. For example, results from follow up case studies may yield even more plausible explanations on the lack of support for H3 and H6. Such results can enrich our understanding of the relationship between cultural dimensions and IS developer values.

## 7. Conclusion

In spite of its limitations, key conclusions have emerged from this study. IS developers with individualistic or masculine (or both) inclination have been found to possess stronger economic, technical, and socio-political values. These results suggest that IS developers with collectivistic or feminine (or both) inclination may benefit most from training. Like several prior studies (e.g., Refs. [28,29]), the results of this study add a cultural perspective to our understanding of the IS and consequently DSS development process.

An increasing number of organizations are embracing global IS development teams or relying on offshore outsourcing as a means to fulfill their software needs [6]. Besides raising productivity, understanding the cultural implications of the IS development process can help organizations to produce software that is useful internationally. This study adds to our understanding in this direction.
