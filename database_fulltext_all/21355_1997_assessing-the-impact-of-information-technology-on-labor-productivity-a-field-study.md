# Assessing the impact of information technology on labor productivity A field study

Tridas Mukhopadhyay $^{*}$ , F. Javier Lerch, Vandana Mangal

Carnegie Mellon University, Graduate School of Industrial Administration, Pittsburgh, PA 15213. USA

Received 12 June 1993; revised 22 January 1994; accepted 23 May 1994

## Abstract

Measuring and understanding the productivity impact of information technology (IT) is a significant and difficult problem facing researchers. We propose that the effect of IT applications can be best understood through an analysis at the information process level. We report on a field research conducted to study the impact of IT on the toll collection system of 38 interchanges at the Pennsylvania Turnpike. We focus on an information process that is relatively uncoupled from other processes in the organization to constrain the measurement problem. In addition, this process consists of well structured information processing tasks allowing us to gain a clear understanding of the economic impact of IT on different types of transactions. Our results indicate that the new IT at the turnpike had a substantial impact on the efficiency of processing complex transactions but no impact on simple transactions. These results can only be understood by examining the nature of the toll collection process and the changes generated by the new IT on some of the information processing tasks.

Keywords: Information technology; Labor productivity; Business value; Productivity paradox; Public sector; Information process

## 1. Introduction

Information Technology (IT) poses a serious dilemma for management today. On one hand, continuing IT innovations have the potential of changing the competitive game for many organizations. On the other hand, the size of the IT investment puts increasing pressure on managers to assess its business value. One key to this dilemma is to improve our ability to measure and trace the impact of IT on organizational performance.

The results of recent studies are at best inconclusive. The majority of the studies have found little or even negative impacts of IT on firm productivity. Loveman [17], for example, examined the productivity impact of IT in the manufacturing sector, and concluded that “the marginal dollar would best have been spent on non-IT inputs into production, such as non-IT capital”. Roach [21] found that information worker productivity has either declined in some sectors or not kept in pace with production worker productivity in the manufacturing sector.

A few recent studies, however, have found some evidence of positive impacts. Bresnahan [3] found that the gains to consumers was five or more times the expenditures by firms in the financial services sector on computer rentals during 1958–1972. Weill [25] identified significant productivity gains due to transactional IT, but did not find any positive impacts for strategic or informational (IT infrastructure) systems. Finally, Barua et al. [2] detected sizable impacts of IT on intermediate variables such as capacity utilization, inventory turnover and product quality, but little impact on return on assets or market share.

Recent reviews of this literature provide a comprehensive assessment of this line of work $[1,5,8,14]$ . These reviews have identified several reasons for the controversy on IT impact such as measurement problems, possible lags between IT investments and impacts, poor quality of data and a lack of understanding of the role of IT in the production process.

We propose that we can gain a better understanding of IT impacts by examining individual applications due to at least three reasons. First, unlike assessing IT impact using the organization as the unit of analysis, it does not involve the aggregation across a large number of processes where some applications may be effective while others may not be [14]. Second, it allows us to open the black box of information technology usage and trace the effect of IT on specific processes and tasks. Third, from a practical perspective, the evaluation of IT applications is important because investment decisions are often made at the application level.

Although examining individual applications allows us to study the link between the system design and value, the measurement problem still demands much care in such analyses $[20]$ . To alleviate this problem, we limit our study to a well defined set of information processing tasks. Furthermore, the selected tasks are relatively uncoupled from other processes in the organization, so we can constrain the measurement issue and start accumulating knowledge about the economic impact of IT applications. However, the analysis becomes difficult as the coupling among processes increases.

We report on a field research that studies the impact of the toll collection application on 38 interchanges at the Pennsylvania Turnpike. The nature of this application is to support the collection and book-keeping of approximately one hundred million fares per year. This application affects a well defined information process that is relatively independent from other organizational processes. Our results indicate that the introduction of a new application at the turnpike did not have a uniform impact on two different types of transactions. While this new technology had a substantial impact on complex transactions, it failed to improve the processing of simple transactions. These results can be understood by examining the nature of the tasks in the two transactions and the characteristics of the system design.

In sum, our study emphasizes the need for an information process orientation in IT impact measurement. The results of this study also debunks the claims on both sides of the IT productivity controversy. The impact of IT is not necessarily positive or negative, but rather conditional on the fit between the information process and the IT application design. The nature of this fit explains why we found little impact on simple transactions and substantial improvement in the case of complex transactions.

This paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we discuss our approach for assessing the business value of IT applications. Section 3 presents a detailed description of the research site and the underlying information process. Next, we discuss our research hypotheses in Section 4 and data collection in Section 5. We present and discuss the results in Section 6. In Section 7, we outline the advantages and limitations of assessing the economic impact of IT on relatively uncoupled information processes. We also speculate on how our study may help improve the appraisal of IT impact for other types of applications.

## 2. Research approach

There has been a growing concern among some authors $[8,13,18,19]$ that the effects of IT on enterprise level performance can be identified only through “a web of intermediate level contributions”. Some recent studies have taken a process orientation to understand the impact of IT on firm performance. For example, Kauffman and Kriebel $[13]$ use “Business Value Linkage” to measure the impacts of treasury management work stations on demand balances from customers. Barua et al. [2] propose a two stage model to operationalize the process orientation in the manufacturing sector. These studies indicate the need for more process oriented models instead of traditional “black-box” approaches.

We take the process orientation one step further in examining the business value of IT applications. We propose that the impact of IT applications can be best understood through an analysis at the information process level. Information processes refer to any goal directed transformation of information by an organizational unit. An information process consists of a set of information processing tasks performed by either human or computing processors.

Measuring productivity gains from IT investments can be hindered or facilitated by the nature of the information process. We decided to select a process with multiple tasks (not just an isolated task) that would allow us to analyze the impact of IT on different transactions within a single process. Furthermore, we were looking for a process that was relatively independent from other organizational activities so measurement problems would be minimized permitting us to focus on how and why productivity gains were (or were not) materialized. Our objectives were to demonstrate that IT impacts can be assessed with relative ease for relatively uncoupled information processes and to learn the impact of IT on different transactions within a single process.

One approach for classifying the independence of processes was to use Thompson's typology of (1) mediated processes with pooled interdependence, (2) long-linked processes with sequential interdependence, and (3) processes with reciprocal interdependence [23,24]. In mediated processes, activities between processes are not directly linked but there is a pool of shared activities. For example, selling units in a department store are not directly linked. Productivity gains in one selling unit do not change the process in other units, although all units share central resources such as maintenance of the sale processing technology. Long-linked processes such as in an automobile assembly plant and a loan application processing, are sequentially dependent. In this case productivity gains on one process (e.g., painting or loan risk assessment) may or may not result in productivity gains for the whole organization since productivity gains in one process may be dissipated in other downstream processes. In the department store case, increases in one selling unit should be observed at the organizational level since the selling units are relatively uncoupled. Finally, processes with reciprocal interdependence are characterized by intricate and often poorly understood interdependencies. Classic examples are manufacturing design processes and large software engineering projects.

This typology was later extended by adding a relatively pure case of an uncoupled or additive process $[10]$ . In this case, there is no interdependence among organizational processes. Our assertion is that different forms of interdependence facilitate or hinder the generation (and measurement) of productivity gains at the organizational level. In particular, we expect it to be easier to trace the impact of IT on uncoupled and mediated processes than on long-linked or reciprocally interdependent processes. We can gain a clearer understanding of how to study the role of technology in organizations by first focusing on “leaner” processes before addressing more complex dependencies. This research studies the impact of introducing new IT in the toll collection process of the Pennsylvania Turnpike. This process is relatively uncoupled from other production processes in the turnpike such as road maintenance and general administration.

The selection of this process also has other advantages. First, there are clear process boundaries. That is, it is easy to isolate inputs and outputs from other organizational units. This facilitates the selection of measurements. Second, we had access to data of excellent quality. The system collects detailed information of every process transaction (output) and every process input. Third, this process has two distinct types of transactions and we were able to isolate the impact of IT on each of them. This provided us with an opportunity to trace the impact of IT by examining the fit between the IT design and the individual tasks for each transaction type.

Besides these advantages, the toll collection process consists of relatively well defined information processing tasks that are algorithmic in nature. This type of processing is characterized by the existence of well understood rules for transforming information inputs into outputs [22]. For example, the toll collection process requires to calculate fares for each transaction according to vehicle type and distance; this information processing task can be performed by either a human or machine processor through a table look-up.

The measurement of IT impacts should be easier for those processes that require structured (algorithmic) forms of information processing. This is because we can identify individual tasks for such a process and analyze the changes caused by a new IT design on each information processing task. In contrast, it is often difficult to decompose unstructured information processes and to predict how IT might affect the overall process.

## 3. Research site

The fare collection system at the Pennsylvania Turnpike can be considered a gigantic cash register. It collects about 250 million dollars in toll each year. The turnpike classifies vehicles into nine types based on the number of axles and the weight of the vehicle. The toll charged for each vehicle is based on its class and the distance travelled. About 85% of all vehicles using the turnpike are passenger cars and belong to class one. Passenger cars can use any lane to enter or exit the turnpike, but commercial vehicles (classes 2–9) can not use “Car Only” lanes because these lanes are not equipped with the automatic sensing and weighing mechanisms required to classify commercial vehicles.

The old toll collection system at the turnpike was replaced in July 1987. The old system used an IBM electro-mechanical machine. When a patron entered the turnpike, the vehicle was sensed, weighed and classified automatically. Key data including the classification, date and time were impact printed on a ticket (with interchange number preprinted) and handed over manually by a toll collector. At exit, the vehicle was classified again to prevent possible fraud by commercial traffic. Next, the machine read the pinholes on the toll ticket. Finally, the toll collector matched the entry and exit classifications, manually looked up the toll from a chart, and collected the toll.

The new information system automatically classifies the vehicle upon entry, records the key data on a magnetic strip on the ticket (and prints the same data), and discharges the ticket automatically. At exit, the system reclassifies the vehicle, matches the two classifications, and calculates the toll. The toll collector receives the payment by cash or charge.

Table 1 shows the tasks required by the toll collection process with both the old and new systems. The main differences are:

\- The old system required one toll collector per entry lane. The new system uses only one toll collector for all entry lanes at an interchange to assist handicapped patrons and deal with occasional mistakes made by other patrons.

Fare collection at the turnpike is primarily performed by permanent toll collectors (735 in 1990) whose work arrangements are guided by a union contract. The turnpike also employs a significant number of temporary collectors (85 in 1990) who are outside the union contract. The temporary collectors typically work less than forty hours a week and may work at more than one interchange.

A senior collector, called shift leader, is given charge of fare collection at each interchange. Typically, the shift leader performs management tasks such as scheduling workers and dealing with routine maintenance. The shift leader also attends to exception conditions such as equipment failures, unexpected absenteeism, security problems, etc. At interchanges with low traffic volume, the shift leader attends to the occasional patrons needing help at the entry lanes. At high volume interchanges, however, a dedicated collector usually attends to the entry lanes. During peak hours, precoded tickets are often manually handed to the drivers to expedite traffic movements at (car only) entry lanes.

## 4. Hypotheses

The measurement of IT impact in the toll collection process is simplified because it is uncoupled from other processes in the organization. Thus if the introduction of IT generates productivity gains in this process, then it is unlikely that such gains will be dissipated due to interactions with other processes. Furthermore, the toll collection at every interchange is uncoupled from the same process in other interchanges. Therefore, the impact of IT at the turnpike level can be determined by adding the gains (losses) at each interchange. Obviously this approach will not work in other types of processes such as long-linked or reciprocally interdependent processes.

The toll collection process, of course, utilizes both labor and capital inputs. One way to capture capital input in this case is through lane configuration – number and type of lanes (general or car only) available at each interchange. However, the lane configuration is primarily determined by safety and congestion considerations and not by production efficiency reasons. In addition, the lane configuration has not been affected by the IT intervention. Therefore we assess the impact of the IT intervention at the turnpike by estimating changes in labor productivity.

From the description of our research site, it should be clear that the tasks performed by shift leaders and toll collectors are not uniform. To get a deeper understanding of the toll collection process, we draw on managerial accounting principles and divide the labor at each interchange into direct and indirect categories. Direct labor processes one transaction at a time and is primarily driven by transaction load [12]. Indirect labor supports the transaction processing activities and is not determined by transaction volume alone. The tasks performed by indirect labor (e.g., scheduling, maintenance and problem solving) are managerial in nature. These are semistructured tasks and can not be easily decomposed into individual tasks. However, the nature of IT impact on these tasks can be ascertained in this case (see below).

The IT deployment at the turnpike has changed the need for each type of labor. For the old system, direct labor was responsible for both vehicle entry and exit leaving management tasks to indirect labor. With the new system in place, direct labor handles vehicle exit (fare collection) only whereas indirect labor is responsible for both management tasks and vehicle entry tasks (e.g., helping the occasional patrons needing assistance).

Table 1 showed the information processing tasks performed by direct labor. However, there are significant differences on how these tasks are performed for the two types of transactions in the fare collection process (both before and after IT deployment). Passenger cars are defined as simple transactions because they can be classified by sight (without weighing and counting axles) thus eliminating the need for matching entry and exit classifications (task 4 in Table 1). All other vehicles (complex transactions) require matching the two classifications and resolving any possible discrepancy. This task was manual with the old system, but it has been automated in the new system. In addition, collectors executed the toll assessment task (task 5 in Table 1) differently for the two types of transactions with the old system. Toll assessment for simple transactions often did not require a table look-up because collectors had memorized the amount of the fare. On the other hand, complex transactions always required a table look-up. The new system eliminated this difference by automating the toll assessment task for both transactions. Given these two changes in the processing of transactions with the new system, we expected a differential impact of IT on the two types of transactions. Consequently, we will focus on measuring the impact of IT intervention on direct labor productivity for each transaction type.

This distinction between simple and complex transactions is similar to prior work by Kauffman [15]. He found that ATM implementation at a bank changed the proportion of complex transactions handled by human tellers because customers used the new technology primarily for simple transactions.

We can now state our research question as: What was the impact of IT on direct labor for simple and complex transactions and indirect labor at the turn-pike?

While indirect labor is concerned with the functions of an interchange, direct labor is defined at the transaction level. How can we then use interchange level analysis to study the IT impact on direct labor? This question should be addressed from two directions. First, if IT indeed altered the direct labor requirement for one or both transaction types, would it be reflected at the interchange labor data? The answer is yes, because each transaction is independent of other transactions, allowing us to aggregate the impact on individual transactions to the interchange level.

Second, if the direct labor at the interchange level did change, could that be attributed to IT? The answer is again, yes, if the service level (average queue length) did not change after IT deployment. There are several reasons for assuming that the service level remained relatively unchanged immediately before and after the deployment of the new system. If the service level deteriorated after IT deployment, the queue length would increase during peak traffic hours resulting in accidents and negative media coverage. Being a public sector organization, turnpike officials are highly responsive to such developments. We verified from the records of public hearings that such issues did not arise after the deployment of the new system. In addition, the presence of a strong union at the turnpike acts as a deterrent to overloading the system at peak hours.

We now discuss four hypotheses on the impact of IT deployment at the turnpike. Our first hypothesis examines the overall impact of IT on the fare collection process. From our discussion of old and new systems, we expect the new system to have an impact on labor productivity. If this hypothesis does not hold, the remaining hypotheses concerning specific impacts would become meaningless. On the other hand, if this hypothesis is supported, at least one of the three other hypotheses must also hold.

Hypothesis 1. The new system would affect the labor requirement for the toll collection process.

Our next hypothesis is concerned with the IT impact on indirect labor. We expect indirect labor to decline with the new system due to its improved reliability and flexibility. The old system was introduced in 1955 and was prone to breakdowns requiring much attention from shift leaders to schedule repair work with a maintenance contractor. Thus the old system often required more than one shift leader for interchanges with high traffic volumes. The new system also makes the scheduling task for the shift leader easier because it does not require entry lanes to be manned. For the old system, the shift leader had to balance the number of hours spent at entry and exit lanes for each collector. The new system has not only reduced the scheduling task in magnitude (automatic entry lanes), it has also simplified this task (ratio of entry and exit lane hours for individual collectors not relevant). By simplifying the scheduling and maintenance tasks, the new system often allows the shift leaders at low volume interchanges and/or during nonpeak hours to directly participate in the fare collection process.

Hypothesis 2. The new system would lead to a reduction of indirect labor.

Next, consider the IT impact on simple transaction processing. The new system provides some advantages, although limited, over the old system in processing simple transactions. The advantages include automating the vehicle entry process and easier methods to deal with exception transactions. However, the new system can not provide any advantage for simple transactions by automating the task of matching entry and exit classifications since passenger cars are not subject to classification problems. Moreover, the labor required for the entry side of the transaction was not eliminated altogether. In most interchanges, one person is still required on the entry side to help any handicapped patrons and other customers who may need help (for example customers from out of state who may not be familiar with the system). The entry side of a transaction is also much less involved than the exit side and, takes much less time. Thus labor savings at entry lanes are limited.

The automatic fare calculation by the new system is, in fact, considered a drawback for simple transactions. With the old system, a collector at the exit point would first look at the ticket presented by the driver of a passenger car to determine if the vehicle came from a nearby interchange. If it did, the collector would verbally tell the driver the toll charge because most collectors remembered the toll charge for passenger vehicles from nearby interchanges due to the high frequencies of such transactions. The new system, however, does not allow this practice by requiring all cards to be read by the machine. It takes a few seconds to feed the card into the machines while the collector must wait. This aspect of the new system design probably reduces labor productivity. We expect the disadvantages of the new system to nullify its advantages with the net result on direct labor requirement for simple transactions being minimal. Thus, we do not expect the following hypothesis to be supported by our data analysis.

Hypothesis 3. The new system would lead to a reduction of direct labor for simple transactions.

Finally, we consider complex transactions processing. These transactions get the full benefits of the new system. The automation of the vehicle entry process, entry and exit classification matching and calculation of the toll significantly reduces worker effort in some of the information processing tasks. With the old system, a collector could make mistakes in assessing the appropriate toll that required looking up a table with 37 rows and nine columns. The new system generates accurate fare calculation and simplifies methods for handling exceptions. Moreover, a significant portion of trucking companies hold charge cards with the turnpike; the new system also automates charge card processing (transaction posting for billing purpose) and thus reduces the time required to process a complex transaction.

Hypothesis 4. The new system would lead to a reduction of direct labor for complex transactions.

We can now summarize our theoretical analysis. It involved (a) using Thompson's typology to identify a relatively independent information process with a clear process boundary, (b) decomposing the selected algorithmic process into specific well defined information processing tasks, (c) identifying the tasks performed by direct and indirect labor, (d) distinguishing between simple and complex transactions, (e) analyzing how the new IT changes information tasks for each transaction type, and (f) hypothesizing the impact on labor productivity based on the fit between IT and information tasks.

## 5. Data collection

We compare labor productivity before and after the IT deployment at the turnpike. As stated before, the new system was implemented in all 38 interchanges in July 1987. The installation and testing of the new system had taken over four months before the switch over. The start of the new system across the turnpike caused some problems and attracted some media attention. However, the number of complaints received by telephone and mail declined rapidly in a few weeks. The new system had some residual technical problems which were fixed before the end of the calendar year. In sum, 1987 represents an unstable period for the fare collection operation at the turnpike and will not be included in the statistical analysis.

Our data for this study consist of the volume of simple and complex transactions for each of the 38 interchanges for 1986 and 1988 obtained from the fare collection system. It also includes labor hour data obtained from the payroll system of the turnpike for shift leaders, permanent and temporary collectors for each interchange for 1986 and 1988.

We use the annual volume as the transaction count for both simple and complex transactions. Although a shorter period such as a quarter can also be used, strong seasonal fluctuations in traffic volume may confound results. Thus we use the annual data for our analysis. The list of variables and their summary statistics appear in Table 2. Note, each variable is defined in terms of annual data per interchange. The advantage of this data is that it incorporates multiple observations; the average number of transactions processed by an interchange in a year is over two million. It is clear from the table that there is a great deal of variability in interchange size. In addition, the average traffic volume increased from 1986 to 1988 by 9.4%.

The labor data, however, can not be easily classified into direct and indirect categories. This is because the shift leaders who perform most of the support tasks may also help in fare collection. However, we have no records of the number of hours spent by shift leaders in transaction processing. To avoid possible problems of measuring indirect labor [7], we use linear regression to measure direct and indirect labor. In particular, we use the following model:

$$
L _ {i} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} * \mathrm{ST} _ {i} + \beta_ {2} * \mathrm{CT} _ {i},\tag{1}
$$

where $L_{i}$ is the total annual labor hours (shift leaders, permanent and temporary toll collectors) at interchange i, $ST_{i}$ and $CT_{i}$ are the annual volumes of simple and complex transactions at the same interchange. Note that $\beta_{0}$ is independent of transaction volume while $\beta_{1}$ and $\beta_{2}$ are driven by simple and complex transaction volumes. Thus $\beta_{0}$ captures the indirect labor at the interchange level while $\beta_{1}$ and $\beta_{2}$ represent the direct labor required for simple and complex transactions respectively. We expect $\beta_{0}$ to be less than the average shift leader hours and $\beta_{1}$ to be smaller than $\beta_{2}$ .

Eq. (1) is also consistent with our knowledge of the production process. This linear equation implies that the marginal productivity of labor is constant and the ease of substitution between the two transaction types is high [11]. For this simple production process, it is expected that the labor required would increase at a constant rate if one type of transaction is increased keeping the other type constant. It is also easy for an interchange to handle more simple transactions and fewer complex transactions for the same labor hours (the increase in simple transactions is probably higher in magnitude compared to the decrease in complex transactions) and vice versa. Our examination of detailed data for all interchanges supports such substitution possibility; the ratio of the two transaction types fluctuated considerably from day to day while the labor hours remained within a tight band.

In summary, the functional form of Eq. (1) is justified on two grounds. First, it allows us to measure indirect and direct labor (for both simple and complex transactions). Second, it is consistent with the characteristics of the production process.

It is clear from both models that complex transactions require much more labor compared to simple transactions. To examine the robustness of our results, we performed the data analysis again by adding 1985 and 1989 data to our original data set. The advantage of the new data set is that it includes a longer time period. The disadvantage is that there is a greater probability that exogenous factors, not accounted for in this study, could influence the results. The extended data set, however, leads to very similar results (Table 5). The results of all four hypotheses do not change.

Overall, the results show that IT has helped improve labor productivity at the turnpike. Given the difficulty of successfully designing and implementing large IT applications, the results are highly encouraging. Our knowledge of the toll collection process and the precise nature of the IT impact on this process enhance our understanding of the productivity impact. Our analysis demonstrates a certain degree of causality between the information technology and labor productivity. This being a field study, there could be confounding factors influencing the results. However, our analysis strongly suggests that the productivity impacts are far from accidental and are most likely due to the IT deployment.

We have identified the impact of a new information system on a structured and relatively uncoupled information process. The results indicate that productivity gains from IT can be achieved only when the design of the application changes the speed and/or quality of information processing tasks within the process. In our example, the new IT improved the processing of complex transactions because the new system speeds up and improves the quality of individual tasks performed by the human operator. On the other hand, no change was observed in simple transactions because the human operator had already developed an effective method that was not enhanced by the new system.

Our research is limited by a lack of a control condition. Thus we may question if the changes in labor productivity can be ascribed to the IT intervention. However, our results indicate productivity gains in one type of transactions but not in the other. Therefore, if there is some other factor that explains productivity gains, this factor needs to explain the differential impact. For example, labor productivity gains may be the result of improved motivation. However, it is difficult to explain productivity gains in complex transactions due to higher motivation while productivity in simple transactions remains constant. After studying the process and the organization carefully, we could not generate another factor that could explain the pattern of the empirical results.

## 7. Discussion

We have proposed that two process dimensions facilitate or hinder the measurement of productivity gains at the application level: (1) the independence of the process with respect to other organizational processes to constrain the measurement problem, and (2) the degree of the structure of the information process to decompose it into well defined information processing tasks. For this research we selected a process that requires an algorithmic transformation of information and that is relatively independent from other processes. This facilitated the selection of productivity measurements (i.e., labor productivity at the interchange level) and to distinguish the impact of IT on two types of transactions within the same process. Obviously, we expect that measuring productivity gains at the application level is more difficult for interdependent and less structured information processes. But we believe the results of this work may provide some guidance. In the rest of this section we extrapolate the lessons learned in this research to the future study of interdependent and less structured processes.

The first lesson from this work is the importance of properly defining process boundaries. This was somewhat easy in our work because the toll collection process is relatively uncoupled from other processes. This step is crucial and more difficult for long-linked or reciprocally interdependent processes. In these cases the researchers have to assess how the process under study is linked to other processes and how the IT may affect these relationships. For example, a prior study by Kraut et al. [16] illustrates the importance of understanding these dependencies. Their study examined the impact of a new computerized record system on the productivity of the customer service process. Their analysis showed that the new system had a positive impact on the regular work performed by customer service representatives but it had a negative impact on the productivity (measured using self-reports) and quality of work performed by their supervisors because the system increased the difficulty of coordinating with other units. Their results suggest improved productivity in simple transactions for the organization (no coordination required) but lower productivity in complex transactions (in which supervisors played an important role outside the customer service process). The researchers clearly defined the boundaries of the process and the interactions with other processes so they could explain unexpected impacts of IT on the organization. We propose that process boundaries and interactions need to be defined before selecting appropriate measurements at the application level.

The second lesson is that predicting the impact of IT on information work is difficult even for algorithmic tasks because of the complexity and flexibility of human information processing. In our work, we were able to describe why the new toll collection system had no impact on simple transactions because we could characterize with relative ease the cognitive and motor tasks taken by the operators before and after the introduction of the IT. Of course, this characterization is more difficult in complex information work such as dynamic decision tasks (e.g., real-time scheduling) or design tasks (e.g., software engineering) because decision makers and designers have large knowledge bases that allow them to adapt to complex tasks. In these examples, new information technology may improve some tasks but either degrade performance in others or alter the usefulness of the knowledge base in coordinating tasks within the same process. Examples of applications that may suffer from this problem are process monitoring systems and CASE tools.

The third lesson is that there is no substitute for good data quality. Impacts of IT technology are usually small in the short-term. In our simple process with an excellent data quality, a massive investment in IT had a 15% increase in labor productivity and a 30% improvement in the time to complete complex transactions. But there were no improvements in processing simple transactions based on annual data. Without good quality data it is difficult to trace IT impacts in the short-term; but short-term assessments are usually the most valuable to managers making investments in IT.

The last lesson is that the linkage among information tasks has an important influence on the effectiveness of IT applications. In our research all information tasks required for processing the two types of transactions were strictly sequential. That is, each task only has an impact on other downstream tasks. For example, calculating the fare affects the collection of the toll, but not vice versa. Therefore, the impact of IT can be decomposed by observing the impact of the technology on each task in isolation. For example, we inferred that, because the new technology improved the operation of calculating the fare for complex transactions but did not change it for simple transactions, the impact would be positive and null respectively.

The above is not necessarily true for many information processes affected by IT applications. For example, Clark et al. [6] have observed that the productivity of product development in the world auto industry is strongly linked to the organization of activities. They identified two critical linkage aspects among activities: the degree of overlap in the timing of upstream and downstream design activities and the pattern of communication between them. The overlap among activities measures the degree by which long-linked activities are performed in parallel, even though these activities were originally thought to be linked sequentially. The authors suggest that product design productivity is higher for those organizations in which seemingly sequential activities have substantial overlap and there is a constant reciprocal communication among these activities. They speculate that identifying and streamlining interactions among activities efficiently have a direct impact on the productivity of the whole design process.

We propose that tracing IT impacts first requires to determine the dependency of the process under study to other processes and to characterize the nature of the information tasks and their inter-relationships. We suggest that a first step to measure the impact of IT at the application level is to study uncoupled processes with well defined information tasks and then proceed to examine interdependent processes with less structure. This strategy generates limited but valuable lessons for tackling more difficult assessments of IT.

Given the controversy existing in the literature about the presence or absence of IT impact, we undertook this study to examine this issue at the application level. Earlier studies have examined this problem mostly at the macro level (economy or specific industries) or at the firm level. Recent studies have suggested that all IT applications in organizations may not have uniform impacts and IT investments should be classified according to functional areas (production, marketing, etc. – e.g., [2]) or management objectives (strategic, informational and operational [25]). We take this process view one step further and focus on a specific IT application. Our research suggests that IT impacts can be measured for structured information processing applications when the boundary of the application and its link to organizational productivity are well understood. The toll collection task has a clear boundary and its contribution to turnpike productivity is additive (not conditional on other processes such as road repair). Given these advantages of the research site, we were able to analyze the precise nature of IT impacts in this case.

This study reinforces the need for process orientation in IT impact measurement. The results of the study stress that the impact of IT is not necessarily positive or negative, but rather conditional on the fit between the information process and the design of the application.
