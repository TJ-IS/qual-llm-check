# Computing services supply management: Incentives, information, and communication

Eric T.G. Wang $^{a,*}$ , Terry Barron $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Information Management, School of Management, National Central University, Chung-Li, Taiwan 32054, R.O.C. $^{b}$ William E. Simon Graduate School of Business Administration, University of Rochester, Rochester, NY 14627, USA

Received 26 May 1993; revised 8 February 1994; accepted 12 July 1994

## Abstract

The management of computing resources and the choice of the organizational form used to govern them is an intricate problem for several reasons. First, the technology itself is subject to externalities, both positive and negative. Delay costs in particular are a significant issue, both from the standpoint of the internal cost-minimizing use of the resources, and the growing importance of time-based competition which requires responsive information systems. Second, the relationship between the IS department and central management is likely to be characterized by a combination of incentive conflicts, asymmetric information in the form of specialized IS knowledge that cannot be readily and fully transmitted to central management (limited communication). Existing principal-agent models do not incorporate this combination of features. Hence in this paper we develop a general, yet tractable, microeconomic model to study the control of an organization's computing resources that incorporates all of these features. Because the model is new, the paper is primarily theoretical. Limited communication is modeled by a finite message space which must be used to communicate a real-valued parameter. A mechanism design approach is then used in this setting to derive the optimal centralized control structure. Limited and costly communication implies that the optimal centralized mechanism need not dominate a decentralized mechanism such as a profit center, in contrast to the result with full and costless communication. A numerical example is also presented, which suggests that it is costs of communication rather than limited communication itself that is most likely to degrade the performance of a centralized control structure.

Keywords: Cost center, Profit center, Limited communication; Mechanism design; Capacity; Order statistics; Revelation principle

## 1. Introduction

In this paper we develop a general, yet tractable, model to study the control of an organization's computing service operations that incorporates delay costs together with incentive conflicts, informational asymmetry, and limited communication between an organization's central management and its IS manager. This is the first model, to the best of our knowledge, that incorporates all of these factors in a single model to address IS management issues. Because the model is new, this paper is primarily theoretical.

Delay costs and other externalities. Although computer systems have become increasingly easy to use, their management still requires considerable expertise. One reason for this is that externalities are a common characteristic of such systems. For example, positive (network) externalities can arise with the selection of a particular hardware or software system as more users adopt and use it. A major source of negative externalities is congestion; one user's decision to join a system will create negative externalities for all other users by increasing their waiting time. Moreover, information systems have characteristics of both private and public goods, so the externalities are not completely non-excludable since each user's decision about using the system is private. Such externalities, both positive and negative, mean that the resources need to be managed to maximize their value to the organization.

Delay costs in particular are of great concern since the ability of information systems to provide timely information has become increasingly critical for firms to compete effectively. Since system performance is affected by the users' usage patterns, their behavior may in turn affect a firm's ability to compete. Thus internal control schemes such as pricing can impact both the efficiency of an organization's internal operations and the effectiveness of its competitive strategies. Consequently, the nature of the internal pricing scheme for computer usage and the scale of system operations, as well as the externalities generated by users' decisions, should not be ignored when constructing a mechanism for controlling computer system operations. Our model links internal control to these various externalities through a general value function for the computer system.

Prior studies of managing and evaluating congestion-prone systems typically utilize queuing models to capture short-run cost behavior, and emphasize deriving the system-wide optimal pricing policy (e.g., Dewan and Mendelson [10]; Mendelson [26]; Mendelson and Whang [29], Whang [37]). Westland [36] uses a similar model to study the effects of network externalities on pricing decisions. These studies ignore objective conflicts and information asymmetry between the central management and the IS manager. However, when these problems are present, the full-information-efficiency solutions of these studies usually cannot be achieved; incurring some “organizational slack” is inevitable (see e.g., Antle and Eppen [1]; Antle and Fellingham [2]; Kirby et al. [20]). Our characterization of computer service systems incorporates the possibility of the presence of congestion and network externalities but is not limited to any specific congestion-prone system.

Incentive conflicts. There is reason to believe that an IS manager is prone to incentive conflicts with central management, particularly by desiring to maximize her department's “slack”. $^{1}$ For example, Mendelson ([28], Chapter 1), cites the “professional syndrome” in which IS staff seek the approval of their fellow professionals rather than being motivated by organizational objectives. Thus the IS manager may over-invest in state-of-the-art technologies that may not be ready for practical implementation or may not be suitable to the needs of her organization. Other sources of managers’ incentives to maximize their department’s slack that are relevant to IS management have been discussed extensively in the agency theory literature. In addition to the professional syndrome, these include consuming perquisites (Zimmerman [38]; Biddle and Steinberg [6]) and hiring an overqualified staff that does not justify its costs (Kaplan and Atkinson [19], p. 531).

Asymmetric information and limited communication. The IS manager can be expected to have private information about her department, such as the true cost of operating it, and the appropriateness of its staffing and technical configuration. When incentive conflicts are also present, the IS manager will be unlikely to reveal her information truthfully without being compensated to do so. Such problems have been addressed extensively in the principal-agent literature in accounting, economics and finance (Antle and Eppen [1]; Guesnerie and Laffont [14]; Harris and Townsend [16]; Jensen and Meckling [18]; Zimmerman [38]). Recently these methods have begun to be applied to IS problems; see e.g., Banker and Kemerer [3], Gurbaxani and Whang [15], Brynjolfsson [7], Richmond et al. [33], and Wang and Barron [35]. These solutions typically rely upon an assumption of full and costless communication, which, in an IS setting, may be unrealistic. One example of this is the result in Wang and Barron [35] that a profit center can never be an optimal IS organizational form since its performance can be duplicated by a centralized mechanism that is inferior to the optimal centralized mechanism. This is at odds with the fact that the profit center is used as an IS organizational form by some firms. Furthermore, the IS department's private information may consist of both “know-how” and “state” information. While some types of the latter may be concise and simple enough to be dealt with by the full-and-costless-communication models, some state information and most know-how information are difficult to communicate. This can be due either to the sheer volume of information, hence the prohibitive time to transmit and receive it, or “bounded rationality” constraints arising from specialized knowledge that central management cannot readily understand. Limited communication also affects the control mechanism designer since the amount of communication required for implementing some mechanisms may make them infeasible. Thus a more general model that recognizes limited communication should be more robust and more widely applicable to the study of IS control problems.

Formally modeling limited communication as it occurs in a real-world environment is intractable. There are, however, many models of limited communication in the economics literature; see e.g., Green and Laffont [11–13]; Kofman and Ratliff [21]; Oniki [32]. Our model is similar to that of Melumad et al. [25]; the communication between the central management and the IS manager is assumed to be governed by a finite message set that reflects the central management's knowledge about IS operations. In addition, we do not allow the central management to use the mechanism itself as a communication device. For a fixed message set, we assume that the central management can only construct a mechanism supportable by that message set. Limited communication may thus merely reflect the extent of the knowledge possessed by the central management about the IS operations. In other words, due to the complexity of the IS operations, the central management cannot prescribe a comprehensive mechanism parameterized by every possible realization of the IS department's costs as is assumed in Wang and Barron [35]. Limited communication then constrains both the central management's ability to communicate and its ability to design a mechanism. This assumption departs from traditional principal-agent models and mechanism design.

Significance and contribution of the paper. Despite apparent trends toward more decentralized computing architectures, the management of these resources may, if anything, become more complex than with centralized systems. Externalities, as described earlier, will continue to be significant, and considerable technical knowledge is required to select and configure such systems. Thus information asymmetries may even be exacerbated by these new architectures. As a result, choosing an appropriate organizational structure to govern the management of computing resources seems likely to remain an important problem. Because of the generality of the model developed here, we expect it to provide an appropriate analytical foundation for studying many IS-management-related questions. For example, as the users and the central management of an organization become more knowledgeable about information technology, how should the control structure for information systems be adjusted? As information systems become more distributed, will it be easier to alleviate the incentive and information problems of system managers? Although we leave the study of these issues for future research, we study two control structures in this paper.

First, centralized mechanisms under which the central management centralizes all the IS-related decisions (e.g., a cost center) are studied. The outcomes generated by a centralized mechanism may approximate the outcomes generated by a bargaining game when the central management has all the bargaining power. However, the set of feasible centralized mechanisms is constrained by the central management's knowledge about the IS department's costs, making it possible that a decentralized mechanism could be better.

The second control structure considered is a profit center, where all the IS-related decisions are delegated to the IS manager. Since the performance of the IS department is simply evaluated by its profitability, minimal communication is needed since the IS manager only communicates one number to the central management, the profit. Naturally, the central management's knowledge deficiency will have no effect when all the IS-related decisions are decentralized. As a result, the performance of a profit center may not be replicated by a centralized mechanism.

The plan of this paper is as follows. Section 2 introduces the modeling assumptions and reviews the queuing model of incomplete information with unlimited and costless communication. Section 3 formalizes the notion of “limited communication” that constrains the central management’s ability to control its IS department. We characterize the set of incentive compatible mechanisms and derive the optimal centralized mechanism in this section. The profit center is discussed in Section 4. A numerical example comparing the two control structures is given in Section 5. Finally, Section 6 contains some concluding remarks.

## 2. Model of the computing service system

## 2.1. Preliminaries

The value of the computing services is represented by the aggregate value function $G(\lambda, \mu | \mathbb{S})$ , where $\underline{\lambda} = (\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$ is the vector of tasks that the system performs per unit of time, $\mu$ is the capacity for serving the tasks, and $\mathbb{S}$ captures the operational characteristics of the system. More specifically, $\mathbb{S}$ represents a particular operational rule for sequencing and scheduling the tasks that need to be performed by the system. The $\lambda_i$ 's and $\mu$ are non-negative real numbers measured in the same unit so that $\sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i \leq \mu$ . This reflects the stochastic nature of most on-line information processing activities such as database queries. For example, $\mathbb{S}$ can represent the queue discipline or priority rule for the jobs to be served when the system can be characterized as a queuing system, and therefore

$$
G (\underline {{{\lambda}}}, \mu | S) = V (\lambda) - \lambda W (\lambda , \mu | S)
$$

for a single-class M/G/1 queue (Dewan and Mendelson [10]; Stidham [34]) or

$$
G (\underline {{\lambda}}, \mu | S) = V (\underline {{\lambda}}) - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} v _ {i} \lambda_ {i} W (\underline {{\lambda}}, \mu | S)
$$

for a multi-class M/M/1 queue (Mendelson and Whang [29]). $^{2}$ For this case, the value generated by the system then is the gross value of jobs served minus the delay cost incurred.

The aggregate value function allows the system to be modeled as a queuing system to reflect the time-critical nature of information systems. This is important because information technologies have increasingly been recognized as competitive weapons or necessities and because engaging in time-based competition has become part of daily life of many businesses. Thus, our model may also serve as a starting point for studying the linkage between the internal control and the strategic side of information system management. Assumption 1 gives the properties of the aggregate value function.

Assumption 1. The value function $G(\underline{\lambda}, \mu)^3$ is twice continuously differentiable with respect to $\underline{\lambda}$ and $\mu$ and concave in $\lambda_i$ for all $1 \leq i \leq n$ and decreasing, convex in $\mu$ . Moreover, $G(\underline{\lambda}, \mu)$ approaches minus infinity as $\sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i$ approaches $\mu$ .

The cost of capacity, $C(\mu, \theta)$ , is assumed to be increasing in both $\mu$ and $\theta$ and twice continuously differentiable, where $\theta$ , the IS manager's private information, is a scalar and parameterizes the costs of the IS department. A higher $\theta$ represents a higher cost required to operate a given capacity. Assumption 2 gives the properties of the cost function. $^{4}$

Assumption 2. For all $\theta$ in $\Theta$ and $\mu \geq 0$ , $C(\mu, \theta)$ is assumed to be twice continuously differentiable with respect to both arguments, and $C_{\mu} > 0$ , $C_{\theta} > 0$ , $C_{\mu \theta} > 0$ , and $C_{\mu \mu} \geq 0$ .

This assumption says that the cost function is increasing in both arguments and convex in $\mu$ . $C_{\mu\theta} > 0$ , called the “sorting condition” in the economics literature, merely requires the marginal capacity cost to be monotone in $\theta$ . We make the following standard assumption regarding $\theta$ .

Assumption 3. The central management's uncertainty about the IS department's costs is represented by the probability distribution function $F(\tilde{\theta})$ , which is twice continuously differentiable with a probability density function $f(\tilde{\theta}) > 0$ if and only if $\tilde{\theta} \in \Theta \equiv [\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]$ . The realization of $\tilde{\theta}$ is the private information of the IS manager.

We note that if there exists an external information service market, the organization's IS-related decisions may be affected regardless of which control structure is employed by the organization (see e.g., Barron and Wang [5]). When the internal IS department's cost is correlated with the market price, the central management may learn more about its IS department's cost from this extra signal. Furthermore, the presence of external competition may in effect reduce the IS manager's informational rent even without actual outsourcing (Caillaud [8]; Lewis and Sappington [22]). To avoid these external market effects, neither the users nor the IS department is allowed to access the market. This assumption is reasonable if the organization's information processing requirements are idiosyncratic and/or if serving the organization requires the service provider to incur a substantial relationship-specific investment.

The users and the central management have the same valuation for the expected gross value and the effects caused by externalities; i.e., the central management's objective is to maximize the aggregate net value provided by the information system. This assumption is typical in most queuing-based studies and consistent with welfare maximization without (tax) distortion. Since the central management is not perfectly informed about $\theta$ , the full-information solution is not achievable with incentive conflicts. With a centralized mechanism, define the IS department's informational rent when the IS manager reports $\hat{\theta}$ and the true cost parameter is $\theta$ as:

$$
S (\hat {\theta}; \theta) \equiv R \left(\underline {{\lambda}} (\hat {\theta}), \mu (\hat {\theta})\right) - C \left(\mu (\hat {\theta}), \theta\right) + T (\hat {\theta}),
$$

where $R(\cdot)$ is the IS department's revenue generated from its computing services and the transfer, $T(\cdot)$ , depending on whether the revenue can cover its costs, can be positive (subsidy) or negative (taxation). It is well-known from the incentive literature that in order to induce the IS manager to reveal the true $\theta$ , $S(\hat{\theta};\theta)>0$ except when $\theta=\overline{\theta}$ ; i.e., the IS manager will usually command some informational rent. Inducing the IS manager's truth-revelation requires:

$$
S (\theta ; \theta) \geq S (\hat {\theta}; \theta), \text {   for   all   } \hat {\theta}, \theta \text {   in   } \Theta .\tag{2.1}
$$

In other words, the informational rent that the IS manager can command by reporting truthfully must be at least as large as that from misrepresentation.

The following assumption captures the incentive conflicts between the central management and the IS manager.

Assumption 4. Given the realized $\theta$ , the IS manager's objective is to maximize $S(\hat{\theta};\theta)$ by reporting $\hat{\theta}$ . The central management puts a “welfare weight” $(1-\xi)$ on $S(\hat{\theta};\theta)$ , where $\xi$ in [0,1].

So $\xi$ measures the degree of the incentive conflict between the central management and the IS manager. When $\xi = 0$ , the parties' objectives coincide, and when $\xi = 1$ , the excess transfer is completely wasteful from the central management's point of view. Alternatively, we may consider that the IS manager is able to "siphon off" a fraction $\xi$ of the excess budget allocation or profit, as in Hart [17]. Thus, from the central management's standpoint, the IS manager's informational rent under the centralized mechanism is:

$$
\xi S (\hat {\theta}; \theta)
$$

when the true cost parameter is $\theta$ and the manager reports $\hat{\theta}$ .

## 2.2. Optimal mechanism with unlimited and costless communication

Since S depends on decisions about $\lambda$ , $\mu$ , T, and the IS manager's true information, $\theta$ , as well as revealed information, $\hat{\theta}$ , the central management must evaluate the tradeoff between operational efficiency and informational rent. This kind of principal-agent relationship with information asymmetry is typically studied using “mechanism design” (Myerson [31]). Based on the results in Wang and Barron [35], the mechanism design problem with unlimited and costless communication can without loss of generality be formulated as the following constrained optimization problem:

$$
\max _ {\underline {{\lambda}} (\cdot), \mu (\cdot)} \int_ {\Theta} \left\{G \left(\underline {{\lambda}} (\theta), \mu (\theta)\right) - C (\mu (\theta), \theta) - \xi \beta (\theta) C _ {\theta} (\mu (\theta), \theta) \right\} d F (\theta)\tag{2.2}
$$

subject to

$\mu(\theta)$ is decreasing in $\theta$ ,

(2.3)

where $\beta(\theta)\equiv(F(\theta)/f(\theta))$ is the (inverse) hazard rate. By extending their result to the current multidimensional case, we have the following proposition characterizing the optimal mechanism with unlimited and costless communication.

## Proposition 1. If

1. The Hessian matrix of

$$
H _ {=} ^ {\text { def }} G (\underline {{\lambda}}, \mu) - C (\mu , \theta) - \xi \beta (\theta) C _ {\theta} (\mu , \theta)
$$

with respect to $\lambda$ and $\mu$ is negative definite;

2. $C_{\mu}(\mu, \theta) + \xi \beta (\overline{\theta}) C_{\theta \mu}(\mu, \theta)$ is increasing in $\theta$ for all $\theta \in \Theta$ , the optimal solution of Eq. (2.2) and Eq. (2.3) is the solution of the following equations:

$$
\underline {{0}} = \nabla_ {\underline {{\lambda}}} G (\underline {{\lambda}}, \mu)\tag{2.4}
$$

$$
0 = G _ {\mu} (\underline {{\lambda}}, \mu) - C _ {\mu} (\mu , \theta) - \xi \beta (\theta) C _ {\theta \mu} (\mu , \theta),\tag{2.5}
$$

where 0 is the vector of n zeroes and $\nabla_{\underline{\lambda}} G$ is the gradient of $G$ with respect to $\underline{\lambda}$ , and the optimal solution is globally incentive compatible.

The revelation principle guarantees that the mechanism given in Proposition 1 will perform at least as well as any other method of controlling an IS department with unlimited and costless communication. Let $\lambda^{*}(\theta)$ and $\mu^{*}(\theta)$ be the solution to Eq. (2.4) and Eq. (2.5). Without loss of generality, we can assume that the central management taxes away all the IS department's revenue and then allocates it a lump-sum transfer $T$ . Then the optimal incentive compatible transfer function:

$$
T ^ {*} (\theta) = C \left(\mu^ {*} (\theta), \theta\right) + S ^ {*} (\theta),
$$

where

$$
S ^ {*} (\theta) = \int_ {\theta} ^ {\bar {\theta}} C _ {\theta} \left(\mu^ {*} (\tilde {\theta}), \tilde {\theta}\right) d \tilde {\theta}.
$$

If the central management can credibly commit itself to a mechanism characterized by

$$
\left(\underline {{{{\lambda}}}} ^ {*} (\theta), \mu^ {*} (\theta), T ^ {*} (\theta)\right),
$$

we know that it is optimal for the IS manager to report $\theta$ truthfully. The IS manager's informational rent is:

$$
U (\hat {\theta}; \theta) = \xi \left\{C \left(\mu^ {*} (\hat {\theta}), \hat {\theta}\right) + \int_ {\hat {\theta}} ^ {\bar {\theta}} C _ {\theta} \left(\mu^ {*} (\tilde {\theta}), \tilde {\theta}\right) d \tilde {\theta} - C \left(\mu^ {*} (\hat {\theta}), \theta\right) \right\}
$$

if she reports $\hat{\theta}$ . The incentive compatibility of $T^{*}(\theta)$ can then be easily checked by observing that

$$
\left. \frac {\partial U (\hat {\theta} ; \theta)}{\partial \hat {\theta}} \right| _ {\hat {\theta} = \theta} \equiv 0.
$$

This condition simply says that, given the mechanism offered by the central management, the IS manager, by performing her own optimization, will find that it is the optimal strategy to reveal her private information truthfully.

## 3. Centralized mechanisms with limited and costly communication

When the central management centralizes the IS-related decisions, its communication with the IS manager is assumed to be governed by a finite message set, and the size of this set depends on the amount of the central management's “learning” about information technologies and the organization's IS operations. We first model limited and costly communication in the next subsection.

## 3.1. Model of limited and costly communication

Let M be a finite message set related to IS operations that the central management can understand, thus feasible for communication. The message set may simply be a set of labels that the central management uses to communicate with the IS manager, as well as to design control mechanisms. Since the revelation principle can fail with limited and costly communication, an optimally designed centralized mechanism may not be able to replicate the performance of a decentralized mechanism such as a profit center. Yet, if we confine our attention to centralized mechanisms, by following the argument used to establish the revelation principle, we can again without loss of generality focus on mechanisms for which the IS manager obeys the provided communication rule $\sigma(\cdot)$ governed by M (Melumad et al. [25]). Consequently, when dealing with centralized mechanisms, we can further restrict our attention to the direct revelation mechanisms defined by the following $n+2$ outcome functions:

$$
\underline {{\lambda}} \colon M \to R _ {+} ^ {n}
$$

$$
\mu : M \rightarrow R _ {+}
$$

$$
T: M \to R.
$$

A centralized mechanism then specifies the decisions $(\lambda(m),\mu(m))$ to be implemented by the IS department through the transfer $T(m)$ for all possible messages m in M. That is, we are seeking the optimal centralized mechanism among the set of central mechanisms that are feasible with respect to the message set M. A mechanism is compatible with M if and only if for every message in M the central management's decisions are the same for all $\theta$ that are associated with a particular message m in M. Then a mechanism with M is incentive compatible if and only if for all $\theta$ in $\Theta$ and all m in M,

$$
T (\sigma (\theta)) - C (\mu (\sigma (\theta)), \theta) \geq T (m) - C (\mu (m), \theta).
$$

For a given M Appendix A shows that there is no loss of generality in restricting attention to interval partitions induced by M. This result allows us to construct the central management's information structure unambiguously. Lemma A.1 says that if there are two cost parameters $\theta_{1}$ and $\theta_{2}$ associated with a particular message m, where $\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}$ , then all $\theta$ in the interval $[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]$ should also be associated with that message. Thus, from Lemma A.1, we know that the set of cost parameters corresponding to a particular message must be a convex set and thereby an interval. Consequently, the message space, or the central management's information structure, can be represented as a partition over $\Theta$ without loss of generality insofar as incentive compatibility is concerned. This characterization of the message space is natural. As a simple example, the message set may consist of only two elements: {high, low}, and the message “low” (“high”) can be viewed as corresponding to the condition that $\theta\in[\underline{\theta},\theta_{1}]$ ( $\theta\in(\theta_{1},\overline{\theta})$ ). This situation is similar to using some “primitive” messages such as bits to communicate the location of the realized $\theta$ on $\Theta$ , as in the monolog algorithms in Kofman and Ratliff [21]. When the correspondence induced by M fails to be convex, some convexification is needed. However, when convexification is needed, it can be done without loss of generality. Limited communication can thus be characterized by a finite set of intervals that partitions $\Theta$ . The resulting partition then represents the central management's information structure about $\Theta$ with each interval representing a “message”.

A mechanism $(\lambda (\cdot),\mu (\cdot),T(\cdot))$ is thus incentive compatible if and only if

$$
T (m) - C (\mu (m), \theta) \geq T (n) - C (\mu (n), \theta), \text {   for   all   } \theta \text {   in   } \Theta_ {m} \text {   and   } m, n \text {   in   } M.\tag{3.1}
$$

To define a feasible mechanism, we need an additional set of constraints so the central management can determine the appropriate tax or subsidy:

$T(m) - C(\mu (m),\theta)\geq 0$ , for all $m$ in $M$ and $\theta$ in $\Theta_{m}$

(3.2)

This set of constrains merely enables the IS department to at least balance its budget after accounting for the tax to or subsidy from central management.

Let $M = |\mathsf{M}|$ , the number of messages in $\mathsf{M}$ . Given Lemma A.1, the central management's information structure can then be represented by a mapping from the set of messages to the possible realizations of $\theta$ . This mapping formalizes the formation of the central management's information structure through learning.

The partition generated by M, denoted by $\mathsf{P}(M)$ , is modeled as resulting from a sampling process performed by the central management. This process then represents the central management's effort spent on learning and understanding information technologies as well as the IS department's local operating environment. We first ignore the cost associated with such a learning process and focus on formalizing the process of generating $\mathsf{P}(M)$ . To this end, we model the message set as a set of order statistics generated by a sampling process performed by the central management from $\Theta$ based on the probability distribution $F(\theta)$ . This sampling process corresponds to a simultaneous search for the realized $\theta$ , and the resulting order statistics form the information structure that constrains the central management's ability to communicate with the IS manager and to design control mechanisms.

Since learning takes time, we assume that the central management has to decide the number of samples that it should take before the outcomes of the samples can be realized. That is, even though samples may be taken sequentially, the central management cannot determine whether or not it should take an additional sample based on the history. Consequently, the central management has to determine the amount of learning (the optimal number of samples) based on the expected gains in the organizational net value.

![](/api/attachments/9BG4CPRM/fulltext/images/df13270645a2a7cbbbeaeadb54a1fffc332a47afd1bd3a24ac4df224b93eee85.jpg)  
Fig. 1. The formation of P(M) through order statistics.

Formally, let $X_1, \ldots, X_k$ be i.i.d. random variables with the distribution function $F(\theta)$ . That is, $k$ is the number of samples taken by the central management and $k = |\mathsf{M}| - 1$ . The $i$ th order statistic of $X_1, \ldots, X_k$ , denoted by $\theta_i$ , is defined as

$\theta_{i}^{\mathrm{def}}$ the $i$ th smallest of $X_{1},\ldots ,X_{k},i = 1,\dots ,k.$

It then follows that $\theta_{1} \leq \ldots \leq \theta_{k}$ with the joint probability density function (p.d.f.) (see e.g., Mood [30], p. 254):

$$
g \left(\theta_ {1}, \dots , \theta_ {k}\right) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} k! f \left(\theta_ {1}\right) \dots f \left(\theta_ {k}\right) & \text { if } \underline {{\theta}} <   \theta_ {1} <   \dots <   \theta_ {k} <   \overline {{\theta}} \\ 0 & \text { otherwise. } \end{array} \right.
$$

Thus, with $k$ samples, the resulting order statistics with probability one will form a non-degenerate partition of $k + 1$ intervals:

$$
P (M) = \left\{\Theta_ {m}: m = 1, \dots , M \right\},\tag{3.3}
$$

where $M = k + 1$ , $\Theta_1 \equiv [\theta_0, \theta_1]$ and $\Theta_m \equiv (\theta_{m-1}, \theta_m]$ for all $m \in \{2, \ldots, M\}$ , $\theta_0 = \underline{\theta}$ , and $\theta_M = \overline{\theta}$ . See Fig. 1 for an example for a partition generated by the sampling process.

By Lemma A.1, we can without loss of generality treat $\Theta_{m}$ as message $m$ . Let $\Theta_{m}(\theta)$ be the interval within which the true parameter $\theta$ lies. With a limited communication system, even though the IS manager intends to report truthfully (so that $\hat{\theta}(\theta)=\theta$ ), the central management can only receive the message $m$ (where $\theta\in\Theta_{m}(\theta)$ ). Incentive compatibility here simply requires the IS manager not to pick a message $n$ such that $\theta$ is not in $\Theta_{n}$ . Again for the case with $\mathbf{M}=\{high,low\}$ , incentive compatibility requires the IS manager not to report the department's cost as “high” if $\theta$ is in $\Theta_{1}$ , and vice versa.

We make the following assumption regarding the organization's costs associated with the mechanism that it employs.

Assumption 5. Compared to decentralized mechanisms, the organization will incur an extra cost of $\Psi(k)+h$ if it employs a centralized mechanism, where k is the number of samples that the central management takes to generate $k + 1$ messages for communication, $\Psi(k)$ is increasing and weakly convex, and h is some non-negative real number.

The cost h represents the fixed component of the costs of employing a centralized mechanism. This can include the central management's optimization costs for deriving a centralized mechanism as well as the costs of setting up a communication channel. $\Psi(k)$ represents the costs of a message set with $k + 1$ messages generated by the central management's learning. Thus, in order to have a larger available set of messages, the central management has to exert more effort on learning about information technologies and its IS operations through more samples. By varying the functional form of $\Psi(k)$ , we can examine, for example, the situation when the information technologies become more user-friendly and easier to understand, or when a knowledgeable IS person is promoted to a position that is responsible for corporate IS planning and control. Similarly, by varying h, we can study the effects of improving organizational communication. For example, if the information related to IS operations is better documented or shared, the communication costs for arriving at an effective IS operational decision may be reduced significantly.

## 3.2. Characterization of feasible mechanisms

We first ignore the costs related to centralized mechanisms, $\Psi(M)+h$ , and derive the expected organizational net value after the central management's information structure, $\mathsf{P}(M)$ , has been fixed; these costs will be reintroduced in Section 3.3. For a fixed information structure, the program for deriving the maximum expected organizational net value can be recast as:

$$
\max _ {\underline {{\lambda}} (\cdot), \mu (\cdot), T (\cdot)} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \int_ {\Theta_ {m}} N V (\underline {{\lambda}} (m), \mu (m), T (m), \theta) d F (\theta)\tag{3.4}
$$

subject to

$$
T (m) - C (\mu (m), \theta) \geq T (n) - C (\mu (n), \theta), \text {   for   all   } m, n \text {   in   } \{1, \dots , M \} \text {   and   } \theta \text {   in   } \Theta_ {m}\tag{3.5}
$$

$$
T (m) - C (\mu (m), \theta) \geq 0, \text {   for   all   } m \text {   in   } \{1, \dots , M \} \text {   and   } \theta \text {   in   } \Theta_ {m},\tag{3.6}
$$

where

$$
N V \left(\underline {{\lambda}} (m), \mu (m), T (m), \theta\right) = ^ {\text {def}} G \left(\underline {{\lambda}} (m), \mu (m)\right) - C (\mu (m), \theta) - \xi [ T (m) - C (\mu (m), \theta) ],
$$

the ex post organizational net value for a given realization $\theta$ . We now characterize the set of feasible mechanisms.

Lemma 1. Given an arbitrary information structure $\mathsf{P}(M)$ , suppose that $\mu(m)$ is decreasing, the IS manager's communication rule is equivalent to requiring $\sigma(\theta) = \theta_m$ for all $m \in \mathsf{M}$ and $\theta \in \Theta_m$ , and the transfer $T(m)$ is incentive compatible if and only if for all $m \in \{1,2,\ldots,M\}$ ,

$$
T (m) = C \left(\mu (m), \theta_ {m}\right) + \sum_ {j = m + 1} ^ {M} \left[ C \left(\mu (j), \theta_ {j}\right) - C \left(\mu (j), \theta_ {j - 1}\right) \right],\tag{3.7}
$$

where by convention

$$
\sum_ {j = M + 1} ^ {M} \left[ C \left(\mu (j), \theta_ {j}\right) - C \left(\mu (j), \theta_ {j - 1}\right) \right] \equiv 0.
$$

Moreover, if

$$
T (M) = C \left(\mu (M), \theta_ {M}\right)\tag{3.8}
$$

Eq. (3.6) is satisfied for all $\theta \in \Theta$ .

Given $T(m)$ satisfies Eq. (3.7) and $\mu(m)$ is decreasing, the IS manager will send a message that she is supposed to send by design. To derive the optimal centralized mechanism for a fixed $P(M)$ , we only need to seek an undominated centralized mechanism in which $\mu(m)$ is decreasing.

In the presence of information asymmetry, regardless of whether or not the communication is limited and costly, the central management will distort the capacity (and thereby the price and the transfer) so as to make a tradeoff between operational efficiency and the IS manager's informational rent. But with limited communication, the resulting incentive compatible mechanism is discrete. Consequently, the central management is not able to distort the capacity continuously as a function of $\theta$ , as in the unlimited and costless communication case in Section 2.1. Since the mechanism is discrete, thus containing gaps, we may consider the resulting mechanism with limited communication to be an “incomplete contract” (Hart [17]).

Given $\theta$ , we see from Lemma 1 that the expected ex post informational rent that can be commanded by the IS manager is:

$$
\xi \int_ {\Theta_ {m}} \left\{\left[ C (\mu (m), \theta_ {m}) - C (\mu (m), \theta) \right] + \sum_ {j = m + 1} ^ {M} \left[ C (\mu (j), \theta_ {j}) - C (\mu (j), \theta_ {j - 1}) \right] \right\} d F (\theta).\tag{3.9}
$$

However, after taking account of the possible occurrences of the cost parameter, the organization evaluates the IS manager's virtual informational rent in Eq. (3.11) as:

$$
\xi \int_ {\Theta_ {m}} \left\{C (\mu (m), \theta_ {m}) - C (\mu (m), \theta) + \frac {[ C (\mu (m) , \theta_ {m}) - C (\mu (m) , \theta_ {m - 1}) ] F (\theta_ {m - 1})}{F (\theta_ {m}) - F (\theta_ {m - 1})} \right\} d F (\theta).\tag{3.10}
$$

Comparing Eq. (3.9) with Eq. (3.10) we see that when $m = 1$ , the IS manager's ex post informational rent is the greatest, but the organization only takes

$$
\xi f _ {\Theta_ {1}} \left\{C (\mu (1), \theta_ {1}) - C (\mu (1), \theta) \right\} d F (\theta)
$$

into account when determining the capacity (since $F(\theta_{0})=0$ ). As a result, the extent of distortion to the decisions is smallest even though the IS manager can earn the largest informational rent ex post. On the contrary, when m=M, the IS manager's ex post informational rent is smallest, but the organization's objective function takes full account of the IS manager's virtual informational rent. Note that, due to the effect of limited communication, the capacity will still be distorted unless $\theta=\theta_{1}$ because the cost parameter is evaluated at $\theta_{1}$ instead of $\theta$ . The usual “no distortion at the bottom” property thus holds if we view $\theta_{1}$ as the “bottom” of the message set. Similarly, the IS manager's ex post informational rent is strictly positive unless $\theta=\theta_{M}=\overline{\theta}$ .

In Lemma 1, we rely upon the assumption that $\mu(m)$ is decreasing. This condition in general is not satisfied for arbitrary probability distribution functions and capacity cost functions. In order for Eq. (3.7) to be incentive compatible, some restrictions on the functions involved are needed. But, first the following lemma is helpful.

Lemma 2. The objective function (Eq. (3.4)) can be written as:

$$
\max _ {\underline {{\lambda}} (\cdot), \mu (\cdot)} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} H \left(\underline {{\lambda}} (m), \mu (m); \theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}\right) \left[ F \left(\theta_ {m}\right) - F \left(\theta_ {m - 1}\right) \right],\tag{3.11}
$$

where

$$
H \left(\underline {{\lambda}} (m), \mu (m); \theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}\right) = ^ {\text { def }} G \left(\underline {{\lambda}} (m), \mu (m)\right) - V C \left(\mu (m), \theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}\right)
$$

Fig. 2. An example of the capacity functions $\mu^{*}(\theta)$ for the unlimited communication case and $\mu^{c}(m)$ for the limited communication case with an equally spaced partition of ten intervals.

is the virtual organizational net value,

$$
V C \left(\mu (m), \theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}\right) = \frac {\int_ {\theta_ {m - 1}} ^ {\theta_ {m}} \left\{C (\mu (m) , \theta) + \xi \beta (\theta) C _ {\theta} (\mu (m) , \theta) \right\} d F (\theta)}{\int_ {\theta_ {m - 1}} ^ {\theta_ {m}} d F (\theta)}
$$

is the virtual capacity cost.

Letting $\mu^{*}(\theta)$ and $\mu^{c}(m)$ be the optimal capacity for the continuous case and the limited communication case, respectively, then for every m there must exist some $\theta\in\Theta_{m}$ such that $\mu^{*}(\theta)=\mu^{c}(m)$ , provided that $C(\mu,\theta)+\xi\beta(\theta)C_{\theta}(\mu,\theta)$ is increasing in $\theta$ . Fig. 2 gives an example of the capacity functions for the cases of unlimited as well as limited communication with an equally spaced $\mathsf{P}(M)$ . The next proposition characterizes the optimal incentive compatible mechanism for a fixed $\mathsf{P}(M)$ .

Proposition 2. Suppose that for all $m$ the Hessian matrix of $H(\underline{\lambda},\mu ,\theta_m,\theta_{m - 1})$ with respect to $\underline{\lambda}$ and $\mu$ is negative definite and, for a fixed $\mu$ ,

$$
C _ {\mu} (\mu , \theta) + \xi \beta (\theta) C _ {\theta \mu} (\mu , \theta)\tag{3.12}
$$

is increasing in $\theta$ , there exists for any partition $\mathsf{P}(M)$ a unique optimal incentive compatible mechanism:

$$
\left\{\left(\underline {{\lambda}} ^ {c} \left(\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}\right), \mu^ {c} \left(\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}\right)\right): m = 1, \dots , M \right\}
$$

which is the solution to the following equations: For $m = 1, \ldots, M$ ,

$$
\underline {{0}} = \nabla_ {\underline {{\lambda}}} G (\underline {{\lambda}}, \mu)
$$

$$
0 = G _ {\mu} (\underline {{\lambda}}, \mu) - V C _ {\mu} (\mu , \theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}).\tag{3.13}
$$

(3.14)

If the capacity cost function is multiplicatively separable so that $C(\mu,\theta)=\tau(\theta)c(\mu)$ , our assumption then reduces to requiring that

$$
\tau (\theta) c ^ {\prime} (\mu) + \beta (\theta) \tau^ {\prime} (\theta) c ^ {\prime} (\mu)
$$

is increasing in $\theta$ , which is satisfied if both $\tau(\cdot)$ and $c(\cdot)$ are increasing, convex, and $\beta(\theta)$ is increasing. For this case, the virtual capacity cost function becomes much simpler:

$$
V C (\mu , \theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}) = \gamma (\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}) = \frac {\int_ {\Theta_ {m}} [ \tau (\theta) + \xi \beta (\theta) \tau^ {\prime} (\theta) ] d F (\theta)}{\int_ {\Theta_ {m}} d F (\theta)} c (\mu).
$$

Thus, when the capacity cost function is multiplicatively separable, $\tau(\theta) + \tau'(\theta)\beta(\theta)$ increasing is sufficient to guarantee that the optimal capacity is decreasing. Hence, when $\tau(\theta) = \theta$ , in order for $\mu^c(\theta_m,\theta_{m-1})$ to be decreasing in $m$ , it is sufficient to have $\beta'(\theta) > -1$ , which can be shown to be exactly the condition required for the unlimited communication case to be globally incentive compatible.

## 3.3. Optimal centralized mechanism with limited and costly communication

To derive the expected organizational net value that can be generated by k order statistics, it is more convenient to consider one interval at a time. The joint p.d.f. of two adjacent order statistics $\theta_{m}, \theta_{m+1}$ for $m \in \{1, \ldots, \theta_{k-2}\}$ is (Mood et al. [30]):

$$
g _ {m, m + 1} \left(\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m + 1}\right) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {k !}{(m - 1) ! (k - m - 1) !} F \left(\theta_ {m}\right) ^ {m - 1} & \left[ 1 - F \left(\theta_ {m + 1}\right) \right] ^ {k - m - 1} f \left(\theta_ {m}\right) f \left(\theta_ {m + 1}\right), \\ & \text { if } \underline {{\theta}} <   \theta_ {m} <   \theta_ {m + 1} <   \overline {{\theta}} \\ 0, & \text { otherwise }. \end{array} \right.
$$

To deal with $\Theta_{1}$ and $\Theta_{M}$ , we also need the p.d.f. of $\theta_{1}$

$$
g _ {1} \left(\theta_ {1}\right) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} k \left[ 1 - F \left(\theta_ {1}\right) \right] ^ {k - 1} f \left(\theta_ {1}\right) & \text { if } \underline {{\theta}} <   \theta_ {1} <   \overline {{\theta}} \\ 0 & \text { otherwise } \end{array} \right.
$$

and the p.d.f. of $\theta_{k}$

$$
g _ {k} (\theta_ {k}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} k F (\theta_ {k}) ^ {k - 1} f (\theta_ {k}) & \text { if } \underline {{\theta}} <   \theta_ {k} <   \overline {{\theta}} \\ 0 & \text { otherwise. } \end{array} \right.
$$

Define the expected virtual organizational net value over the interval $\Theta_{m}$

$$
\overline {{H}} \left(\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m + 1}\right) = ^ {\text { def }} \int_ {\theta_ {m}} ^ {\theta_ {m + 1}} H \left(\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m + 1}\right) d F (\theta)
$$

and the expected organizational net value over the whole support $\Theta$

$$
\begin{array}{l} E \left\{N V ^ {o}; k \right\} = \int_ {\underline {{\theta}}} ^ {\overline {{\theta}}} \overline {{H}} (\underline {{\theta}}, \theta_ {1}) g _ {1} (\theta_ {1}) d \theta_ {1} + \int_ {\underline {{\theta}}} ^ {\overline {{\theta}}} \overline {{H}} (\theta_ {n}, \overline {{\theta}}) g _ {n} (\theta_ {n}) d \theta_ {n} \\ + \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {k - 1} \int_ {\underline {{\theta}}} ^ {\overline {{\theta}}} \int_ {\underline {{\theta}}} ^ {\theta_ {m + 1}} \overline {{H}} (\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m + 1}) g _ {m, m + 1} (\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m + 1}) d \theta_ {m} d \theta_ {m + 1}. \end{array}
$$

The optimal amount of learning is then determined by solving:

$$
\max _ {k \in I} E \left\{N V ^ {o}; k \right\} - \Psi (k) - h,\tag{3.15}
$$

where $I$ is the set of non-negative integers. Since $E\{NV^o; k\}$ is bounded between the unlimited communication case and the null communication case, the optimal number of samples, denoted by $k^*$ , must be finite.

Since the way that the partition is formed impacts the expected organizational net value, it is interesting to see how the optimal partition should be constructed. Note that the communication system is in general beyond the central management's control. However, even when this is the case, investigating the optimal partition supportable by a finite message set is useful, since the expected net value with the optimal partition provides an upper bound on the organizational net value that can be generated by a message set.

## 3.4. Optimal partition

Given an arbitrary partition $\mathsf{P}(M)$ , let $(\underline{\lambda}^{c}(\cdot),\mu^{c}(\cdot))$ be the optimal mechanism with respect to that partition, and

$$
H \left(\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}\right) = G \left(\underline {{{\lambda}}} ^ {c}, \mu^ {c}\right) - V C \left(\mu^ {c}, \theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}\right)
$$

the maximum expected organizational net value attainable is the value of the following program:

$$
E \left\{N V ^ {c}; M \right\} = \max _ {\theta_ {m}} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} H \left(\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}\right) \left[ F \left(\theta_ {m}\right) - F \left(\theta_ {m - 1}\right) \right]\tag{3.16}
$$

subject to

$$
\theta_ {m} \geq \theta_ {m - 1}, \quad \forall m \in \{1, \dots , M - 1 \}.\tag{3.17}
$$

Letting

$$
G ^ {m} \stackrel {\text { def }} {=} G \left(\underline {{\lambda}} ^ {c} \left(\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}\right), \mu^ {c} \left(\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}\right)\right)
$$

the following proposition shows that, for any finite M, the optimal partition is non-degenerate and unique, provided that the assumptions given in Proposition 2 hold.

Proposition 3. Suppose that the Hessian matrix of $H(\underline{\lambda}, \mu, \theta_m, \theta_{m-1})$ with respect to $\underline{\lambda}$ and $\mu$ is negative definite for all $m$ and, for a fixed $\mu$ ,

$$
C _ {\mu} (\mu , \theta) + \xi \beta (\theta) C _ {\theta \mu} (\mu , \theta)\tag{3.18}
$$

is increasing in $\theta$ , there exists a unique, non-degenerate optimal partition for any $\mathsf{P}(M)$ with finite $M$ , and the optimal partition is given by the following equations: For all $m \in \{1, \ldots, M - 1\}$ ,

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & G ^ {m} - C \big (\mu^ {c} \big (\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1} \big), \theta_ {m} \big) - \xi \beta \big (\theta_ {m} \big) C _ {\theta} \big (\mu^ {c} \big (\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1} \big), \theta_ {m} \big) \\ & = G ^ {m + 1} - C \big (\mu^ {c} \big (\theta_ {m + 1}, \theta_ {m} \big), \theta_ {m} \big) - \xi \beta \big (\theta_ {m} \big) C _ {\theta} \big (\mu^ {c} \big (\theta_ {m + 1}, \theta_ {m} \big), \theta_ {m} \big) \end{array}\tag{3.19}
$$

with boundary conditions: $\theta_0 = \underline{\theta}$ and $\theta_M = \overline{\theta}$ .

Proposition 3 establishes the existence of a unique optimal partition and shows that the optimal partition is never degenerate. It also suggests that the expected organizational net value is increasing in the number of intervals induced by the optimally constructed partitions. Consequently, we can obtain the optimal partition for any finite M by solving the unconstrained maximization problem (Eq. (3.16)). Eq. (3.19), an “arbitrage” condition, is a well-defined second-order non-linear difference equation in $\theta_{m}$ with the initial and terminal conditions $\theta_{0} = \underline{\theta}$ and $\theta_{M} = \overline{\theta}$ , respectively. With arbitrary forms of functions and distribution, this set of equations is very difficult to solve even numerically. But the idea of solving the optimal partition is simple. Given the initial condition $\theta_{0} = \underline{\theta}$ , $\theta_{2}$ can be determined uniquely if $\theta_{1}$ is specified, since the right hand side of Eq. (3.19) is decreasing in $\theta_{m+1}$ . Once we have determined $\theta_{2}$ , $\theta_{3}$ can also be determined accordingly. Since $\theta_{2}$ , and thereby $\theta_{m}$ for all $m \in \{3, \ldots, M\}$ , are continuous and monotone in $\theta_{1}$ , the optimal partition is obtained once we have found the $\theta_{1}$ with which the terminal condition: $\theta_{M} = \overline{\theta}$ is satisfied.

## 4. Profit center

With unlimited and costless communication, the revelation principle implies that the performance of a cost center governed by the optimal mechanism is at least as good as a profit center. However, when communication is limited and costly, the central management may be attracted to the profit center, delegating IS-related decisions to the IS manager. Although by evaluating the IS department as a profit center, the central management loses the control of the IS department's decisions, the gain in flexibility by delegating decisions to the better informed IS manager plus the savings on the central management's communication and optimization costs may outweigh the loss of control.

As in the centralized case, the organizational slack still can appear in the form of profit. Therefore, it is reasonable for the central management to put the same weight on the IS department's profit. Since the IS related decisions are decentralized, the IS manager's strategies for determining capacity and prices will not be affected by the weight that the organization puts on the department's profit. That is, the IS manager sets the capacity and price so as to maximize the department's profit,

$$
\pi (\theta) \equiv \pi \left(\underline {{\lambda}} ^ {p} (\theta), \mu^ {p} (\theta)\right) = \max _ {\underline {{\lambda}}, \mu} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \lambda_ {i} M R _ {i} (\underline {{\lambda}}, \mu) - C (\mu , \theta),\tag{4.1}
$$

where $MR_{i}(\underline{\lambda},\mu)$ is marginal revenue of the computing services for task i per unit of time when $\underline{\lambda}$ is served per unit of time with capacity $\mu$ .

For a given $\lambda$ , the IS-related costs borne by the organization when the IS department is organized as a profit center are exactly the same as when the central management is fully informed. However, the IS department's profit maximizing price fails to maximize the organizational net value. In contrast with the revelation mechanism, the distortion of decisions here stems from the distortion of the users' gross value, not the cost, since when maximizing the organizational net value, the users' gross value should be evaluated as $G(\underline{\lambda})$ instead of $\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}MR_{i}(\underline{\lambda},\mu)$ .

When the IS department is evaluated as a profit center, the expected organizational net value is:

$$
E \bigl \{N V ^ {p} (\theta) \bigr \} \stackrel {\mathrm{def}} {=} \int_ {\Theta} \Bigl \{G \bigl (\underline {{\lambda}} ^ {p} (\theta), \mu^ {p} (\theta) \bigr) - C \bigl (\mu^ {p} (\theta), \theta \bigr) - \xi \pi (\theta) \Bigr \} d F (\theta).
$$

Obviously, if the central management puts zero weight on the IS department's profit, the expected organizational net value is simply the expected difference between the users' and the IS department's valuations: $G(\underline{\lambda},\mu) - \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_iMR_i(\underline{\lambda},\mu)$ .

## 6. Concluding remarks

The primary objective of this paper has been to develop and present a microeconomic model for studying IS management and control problems that captures more of the salient properties than have previous models. As a result, the paper is primarily theoretical.

More specifically, the model incorporates externalities, with delay costs in particular being studied, together with incentive conflicts, asymmetric information, and limited and costly communication between the IS manager and central management. A model of limited communication was presented, and the centralized mechanism under limited communication was characterized. Unlike the case for full communication, limited communication implies that the optimal centralized mechanism can be outperformed by decentralized mechanisms, for example, a profit center. The example in Section 5 suggests that the major performance penalty for the centralized mechanism relative to the profit center stems from communication costs rather than from the coarseness of the central management's information. Thus as long as communication costs are not too great, it is likely that a cost center supervised by a moderately informed central management will be preferable to a profit center since the losses from its monopolistic behavior tend to be quite large. It should be noted that the results in this paper were derived under the assumption of no access to an external market for IS services. As was shown in Barron and Wang [5], the threat of outsourcing tends to make the profit center much more attractive since it both reduces the IS department's monopoly power and yields a much simpler outsourcing decision rule than that for the cost center (centralized mechanism.) Thus even though the outsourcing threat will also improve the cost center's performance (through reduced informational rent), the use of profit centers would be expected to rise among firms where outsourcing is a credible option and costs for the central management to become informed about the IS department are high.

## Appendix A. Characterizing the message space as a partition

The following lemma shows that there is no loss of generality in restricting our attention to an interval partition induced by the message set M.

Lemma A.1. If there are two cost parameters $\theta_j$ , $\theta_{j+1}$ in $\Theta$ such that $\theta_j < \theta_{j+1}$ and $\sigma(\theta_j = \sigma(\theta_{j+1}) = m$ , then the incentive compatibility constraints (Eq. (3.1)) imply that $\mu(\tilde{m}) = \mu(m)$ and $T(\tilde{m}) = T(m)$ , where $\tilde{m} = \sigma(\theta)$ for some arbitrary $\theta$ in $(\theta_j, \theta_{j+1})$ . Thus the IS manager's communication rule can be recast as $\sigma(\theta) = m$ for all $\theta$ in $[\theta_j, \theta_{j+1}]$ without loss of generality.

Proof. Suppose there is a $\tilde{\theta} \in (\theta_j, \theta_{j+1})$ such that $\sigma(\tilde{\theta}) = \tilde{m} \neq m$ . Then Eq. (3.1) implies that

$$
T (\tilde {m}) - C \left(\mu (\tilde {m}), \tilde {\theta}\right) \quad \geq \quad T (m) - C \left(\mu (m), \tilde {\theta}\right)
$$

$$
T (m) - C \left(\mu (m), \theta_ {j}\right) \geq T (\tilde {m}) - C \left(\mu (\tilde {m}), \theta_ {j}\right)
$$

$$
T (m) - C \left(\mu (m), \theta_ {j + 1}\right) \geq T (\tilde {m}) - C \left(\mu (\tilde {m}), \theta_ {j + 1}\right),
$$

or

$$
T (m) - T (\tilde {m}) \leq C (\mu (m), \tilde {\theta}) - C (\mu (\tilde {m}), \tilde {\theta})
$$

$$
T (m) - T (\tilde {m}) \geq C \left(\mu (m), \theta_ {j}\right) - C \left(\mu (\tilde {m}), \theta_ {j}\right)
$$

$$
T (m) - T (\tilde {m}) \geq C \left(\mu (m), \theta_ {j + 1}\right) - C \left(\mu (\tilde {m}), \theta_ {j + 1}\right).
$$

The first two inequalities imply that

$$
C \left(\mu (\tilde {m}), \theta_ {j}\right) - C \left(\mu (m), \theta_ {j}\right) \geq C \left(\mu (\tilde {m}), \tilde {\theta}\right) - C \left(\mu (m), \tilde {\theta}\right).
$$

By assumptions that $C_{\mu \theta} > 0$ and $\tilde{\theta} > \theta_j$ , $\mu(\tilde{m}) \leq \mu(m)$ . Similarly, the first and the third inequalities imply that $C\big(\mu(\tilde{m}), \theta_{j+1}\big) - C\big(\mu(m), \theta_{j+1}\big) \geq C\big(\mu(\tilde{m}), \tilde{\theta}\big) - C\big(\mu(m), \tilde{\theta}\big)$

which in turn implies that $\mu(\tilde{m}) \geq \mu(m)$ , since $\tilde{\theta} < \theta_{j+1}$ . Thus $\mu(\tilde{m}) = \mu(m)$ and thereby $T(\tilde{m}) = T(m)$ . Since the IS manager will be indifferent between messages $\tilde{m}$ and $m$ , the message $\tilde{m}$ is redundant and thereby can be deleted without loss of generality.

Proof of Lemma 1. Given an arbitrary $\mathsf{P}(M)$ , assume that the central management's decision on the system capacity $\mu(m)$ is decreasing. Then for any $m < M$ , local incentive compatibility requires, for all $\theta \in \Theta_m$ ,

$$

and thereby $(d \setminus d\theta_m)E\{NV; M\} < 0$ when $\theta_m$ is in a neighborhood of $\theta_{m+1}$ . So, Eq. (B.10) changes its sign from positive to negative at least once, and consequently the constraints in Eq. (3.17) are non-binding and the optimal partition is never degenerate.

We now need to show that the right hand side of Eq. (B.9) is single-peaked in $\theta_{m}$ , so the solution to Eq. (3.19) gives the unique optimal partition. By the negative definiteness of $H(\underline{\lambda},\mu,\theta_{m},\theta_{m-1})$ , we can write it as a function of $\mu$ : $H(\mu,\theta_{m},\theta_{m-1})$ , and $H(\mu,\theta_{m},\theta_{m-1})$ is strictly concave in $\mu$ . Thus,

$$
G (\mu) - C (\mu , \theta_ {m}) - \xi \beta (\theta_ {m}) C _ {\theta} (\mu , \theta_ {m})
$$

is concave in $\mu$ as well. Since

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max _ {\mu} G (\mu) - C (\mu , \theta_ {m}) - \xi \beta (\theta_ {m}) C _ {\theta} (\mu , \theta_ {m}) \\ = G \left(\mu^ {c} (\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m})\right) - C \left(\mu^ {c} (\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m}), \theta_ {m}\right) - \xi \beta (\theta_ {m}) C _ {\theta} \left(\mu^ {c} (\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m}), \theta_ {m}\right) \end{array}
$$

and $\mu^c (\theta_m,\theta_{m - 1})$ is decreasing in both $\theta_{m}$ and $\theta_{m - 1}$ ,

$$
G \left(\mu^ {c} \left(\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}\right)\right) - C \left(\mu^ {c} \left(\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}\right), \theta_ {m}\right) - \xi \beta \left(\theta_ {m}\right) C _ {\theta} \left(\mu^ {c} \left(\theta_ {m}, \theta_ {m - 1}\right), \theta_ {m}\right)
$$

is strictly decreasing in both $\theta_{m}$ and $\theta_{m - 1}$ . Then

$$
\sum_ {j = m} ^ {m + 1} H \left(\theta_ {j}, \theta_ {j - 1}\right) \left[ F \left(\theta_ {j}\right) - F \left(\theta_ {j - 1}\right) \right]
$$

must be single-peaked in $\theta_{m}$ , thus establishing the uniqueness of the optimal partition. ☐
