# An empirical study of the efficacy of a computerized negotiation support system (NSS)

Michael M. Delaney $^{a,*}$ , Abbas Foroughi $^{b}$ , William C. Perkins $^{c}$

$^{a}$ The American College, Bryn Mawr, PA 19010, USA

$^{b}$ School of Business, University of Southern Indiana, Evansville, IN 47712, USA $^{c}$ School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, IN 47405, USA

Received 27 August 1994; revised 28 March 1996; accepted 28 June 1996

## Abstract

This paper presents the results of an empirical study to investigate the effects of a computerized NSS on the outcomes of face-to-face negotiations and on negotiator attitudes. In the study, pairs (dyads) of college students were involved in a simulated industrial bargaining scenario which tested the impact of computer support and conflict level on contract outcomes and negotiator attitudes. Three levels of computer support were compared: a comprehensive NSS (DSS component and an electronic communication component), DSS support only (no electronic communication component), and no computer support. The results showed that the DSS support was similar to the comprehensive NSS in improving the information processing aspects of the negotiation, such as joint outcomes, contract balance, and number of contract proposals. However, the comprehensive NSS had a wider spectrum of positive effects, impacting the socio-emotional aspects of the negotiation such as perceived satisfaction and negative climate as well as the information processing aspects. © 1996 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Negotiation support systems; Empirical study; Contract bargaining

## 1. Introduction

Negotiating is one of the major decisional roles played by managers. In fact, resolving conflict is said to occupy 20% of a manager's working hours [22]. This importance of negotiation, coupled with the increasing complexity of the issues which need to be resolved, makes the possibility of computer enhancement for negotiations very appealing. Implementations of rudimentary computerized negotiation support systems (NSS) in the business world, international affairs, labor law, and environmental and safety disputes, have demonstrated their potential for making negotiation problems more manageable and comprehensible for negotiators. Pioneers in NSS research, however, have expressed their dismay at the lack of rigorous empirical research and evaluation of NSS. In particular, research is needed which will determine how and under what circumstances negotiation processes can be enhanced by NSS support.

One development in the NSS area is the integration of group support systems (GSS) and decision support systems (DSS) in full-featured, session-oriented comprehensive NSS which can support the entire negotiation process [1,4,5,10,11]. Lim and Benbasat [17] have proposed a theory of NSS which emphasizes the importance of evaluating the impact of each of the two subcomponents of an NSS – the individual DSS for each party and the electronic communications channel between the parties – instead of evaluating an NSS as a single entity.

This paper presents the results of an empirical study to investigate the effects of a computerized NSS on the outcomes of face-to-face negotiation and the attitudes of negotiators. More specifically, this study evaluated the relative impacts of the electronic communication component and the DSS component of the NSS by employing three levels of computer support: a comprehensive NSS (DSS component and an electronic communication component), DSS support only (no electronic component), and no computer support.

## 2. Previous NSS empirical research

Formal NSS laboratory studies were conducted by Sainfort et al. [21] and Jones [14]. Sainfort's NSS provided interactive DSS support at each step of a structured bargaining process, and Jones' NSS provided modeling support at one step in the negotiation process (computing and presenting near-optimal solutions). Neither provided both electronic communication and DSS support.

Foroughi et al. [11] combined electronic communication support with a DSS designed to implement Jones' negotiation task. The Foroughi et al. study attempted to provide a comprehensive NSS. First, negotiation process structure was provided by including computer support at each of five stages in an integrative bargaining process (see [15] for a discussion of these five stages). Second, bargainers communicated electronically, with public display of their input. Third, the NSS included the use of group process structuring techniques, such as a computerized form of role reversal and the use of the Problem Centered Leadership Approach by the facilitator [18]. Fourth, a DSS was designed to support alternative generation and analysis. Fifth, the input, display, and refining of negotiating issues and solution alternatives served to document the negotiation process and the final agreement.

Pairs of bargainers played the roles of buyers and sellers negotiating a four-issue, three-year purchase agreement for an engine subcomponent. Conflict level was manipulated by varying the amount of conflict of interest between the bargainers. For low conflict, different weights were assigned to the four issues so that mutually beneficial tradeoffs were possible. In high conflict, the issues were weighted similarly for both parties, creating a situation in which one party's gain was almost equal to the other one's loss.

In addition to varying the conflict level, half of the pairs of bargainers used a computerized NSS while the other half had no computerized support. The computerized NSS consisted of both electronic communication via a local area network and a public display screen and a DSS to support each negotiator in the generation and evaluation of contract alternatives. To ensure that the only differences in the results were caused by either high or low conflict, or by the presence or absence of a computerized NSS, the same integrative bargaining process was used by all groups. This process was clearly spelled out in a detailed script, and the script was closely followed by a facilitator who was present throughout all bargaining sessions.

The Negotiation Decision Support Tool (NDST), a DSS developed for the Foroughi et al. study [11], supported alternative generation and evaluation. Each negotiator had his/her own NDST, which consisted of a spreadsheet with two windows. Window No. 1, the Decision Tool, was used by negotiators to input their own priorities for the issues as well as their perception of the other party's priorities based on what they learned about the other party during the early stages of negotiation. Based on the priorities input by the subjects, the Decision Tool estimated the point structure of the other party, generated all the possible contract alternatives (748 altogether), and ranked them in descending order according to the joint outcome they would give. The Decision Tool then displayed the three contract alternatives which gave the highest joint outcome. Window No. 2 was a Contract Point Evaluator, incorporating an algorithm with the preset preference scores of the negotiator built in. The negotiator could plug in alternative contracts, and the algorithm determined the total score (for his/her side only) that could be achieved with each one.

The results of the Foroughi et al. study showed that computerized NSS support did help bargainers achieve higher joint outcomes and more balanced contracts, and that the NSS support increased negotiation time. Satisfaction was greater for NSS groups in both conflict levels, and perceived negative climate was reduced in low conflict.

## 3. Purpose of present study

The results of the Foroughi et al. [11] study suggested the significant potential value of a computerized NSS, with the primary cost being an increased time commitment. An additional question that arose, however, is the relative value of the two components of the NSS – the electronic communication tool (which was Topic Commentator, a part of the GroupSystems software package) [19] and the decision support system (which was a DSS written expressly for the study using Lotus 1-2-3).

During the Foroughi et al. [11] study, bargainers communicated with each other exclusively via Topic Commentator in order to express their preferences for the issues, engage in role reversal, and to propose/reject contract alternatives. Verbal communication was used only to ask questions about the procedure or the use of the software. Electronic communication was essentially the only channel of communication used in the NSS conditions. On the other hand, the decision support system provided both negotiators a new, easy-to-use tool to aid them in generating and evaluating contract alternatives. Thus, it may have been the availability of the decision support system which led to better performance in the NSS conditions.

These doubts about the role of electronic communication vis-à-vis the DSS component were also reinforced by the lack of consensus among GSS researchers concerning whether computer support increases the quality of decisions made by groups. While some studies have shown that GSS improves the quality of group decisions (e.g., [6,13,16,20,24]), others have shown that GSS support has no impact on decision quality (e.g., [9]) and can even lower perceived decision quality [25]. In their meta-analysis of 31 GSS studies published between 1970 and 1992, Benbasat and Lim [3] found that GSS support did improve decision quality. They also found that a Level 2 GSS (electronic communication plus decision support) improved decision quality more than a Level 1 GSS (electronic communication only).

The present study was designed to answer the following research question: Is the decision support system the reason for the improved performance by NSS pairs? In order to sort out the separate impacts of the two NSS components, a follow-up study was designed to build upon the results of Foroughi et al. [11]. The task and most of the procedures employed were identical to those used in Foroughi et al. These levels included a comprehensive NSS (DSS component and an electronic communication component), DSS support only (no electronic communication component), and no computer support. In the DSS support only condition, the structured negotiation process was retained as was the use of the DSS model for the generation of alternatives and for the analysis of contract offers. Each offer was communicated orally and was also written on a flip-chart that was visible to both parties. With the exceptions noted, the DSS support only condition parallels the other two conditions in all respects, thus permitting an assessment of the relative efficacy of the DSS and electronic communication components of a comprehensive NSS.

The second phase also provided an opportunity to test Lim and Benbasat's [17] theory of NSS, because the theory pertains directly to a two-person, session-oriented, multiple-issue setting such as that used in this study. Lim and Benbasat [17] suggest that the DSS will provide increased information processing capacity and capability, and that this increased capability will result in joint solutions that are closer to the efficient frontier (the locus of achievable joint outcomes from which no joint gains are possible) and closer to the Nash (or fair) solution (see [2]), as well as solutions in which the parties are more confident. Further, they suggest that when electronic communication is added to the DSS, each party will perceive the commitment of the opponent to be greater, and that this greater perceived commitment will result in greater satisfaction with the outcome and a reduced time to settlement. For the second phase of the study, measures similar to those proposed by Lim and Benbasat were adopted, thus providing a partial test of their theory.

## 4. Hypotheses and hypothesis testing

Seven sets of hypotheses were formulated to answer the primary research question. All but two of the sets consist of one hypothesis comparing the dependent variable achieved by DSS-supported dyads with that achieved by comprehensive NSS-supported dyads, and one hypothesis comparing the dependent variable achieved by DSS-supported dyads with that achieved by bargainers with no computer support. The final two sets of hypotheses – for perceived collaborative climate and perceived negative climate – consist of four hypotheses because it is believed that level of conflict will also have an impact on these attitude measures. The dependent variables include four outcome measures (joint outcome, contract balance, negotiation time, and the number of contracts proposed), and three attitude measures (perceived collaborative climate, perceived negative climate, and satisfaction).

Half of the hypotheses are concerned with comparing outcomes and attitudes from dyads using the DSS alone against those resulting from using the comprehensive NSS. It was suggested earlier in this paper that the DSS alone might be as effective as the comprehensive NSS in terms of the outcome measures. The DSS, in comparison to the comprehensive NSS, was hypothesized to result in (1) no difference in joint outcome, (2) no difference in contract balance, (3) no difference in negotiation time, and (4) no difference in number of contracts proposed. Further, it was hypothesized that there would be no difference in any of the attitude measures between the DSS and the comprehensive NSS. The remaining half of the hypotheses are concerned with comparing outcomes and attitudes from dyads using the DSS alone against those resulting from no computer support. Earlier, it was suggested that the DSS would be more effective than no computer support in terms of joint outcome and contract balance. In addition, this study hypothesized that the use of the DSS would result in more contracts proposed and longer negotiation time, and that the DSS would increase satisfaction. In the low conflict treatments, the DSS was hypothesized to result in no difference in perceived collaborative climate and perceived negative climate; in the high conflict treatments, the DSS was hypothesized to increase perceived collaborative climate and decrease perceived negative climate.

Because of the differences in the degree of competition and the solution space between the low conflict and high conflict treatments, a main effect for the level of conflict was expected for each dependent variable, which necessitated testing all hypotheses separately for low and high conflict. The results of the ANOVA confirm that this main effect did occur in almost all of the hypothesis tests.

## 4.1. Joint outcome

Hypothesis 1.1. Bargainers with DSS support will achieve a higher joint outcome than bargainers with no computer support.

Hypothesis 1.2. There will be no difference between the joint outcome achieved by DSS-supported dyads and the joint outcome achieved by NSS-supported dyads.

Hypothesis 1.3. In the high conflict treatments, bargainers with DSS support will achieve a higher joint outcome than bargainers with no computer support.

Hypothesis 1.4. In the high conflict treatments, there will be no difference between the joint outcome achieved by DSS-supported dyads and the joint outcome achieved by NSS-supported dyads.

Joint outcome provides a measure of the total utility of the negotiated settlement. It is determined by summing the number of points reached by each negotiator; the higher the value of the joint points, the higher the joint utility.

In the Foroughi et al. study [11], the dyads with the comprehensive NSS had a higher joint outcome than the dyads with no computer support. This study examines whether this higher joint outcome is a result of the DSS portion of the NSS or of the combined effect of the DSS and the electronic communication component of the comprehensive NSS. These hypotheses assert that the joint outcome differences are due to the DSS portion rather than to the combined effect of the two components of the comprehensive NSS.

The DSS developed for this series of studies, the Negotiation Decision Support Tool (NDST), supported the generation of alternatives and the evaluation of them in an organized manner $[11]$ . The model assists the participants in overcoming the cognitive difficulty of determining a near optimum contract. It also helps participants avoid the tendency toward premature closure and the preference for more available and more salient solutions $[1]$ . The comparison of joint outcomes from NSS and no computer support conditions should accomplish essentially the same purpose as the comparison of joint outcomes relative to the efficient frontier proposed by Lim and Benbasat $[17]$ .

## 4.2. Contract balance

Hypothesis 2.1. Bargainers with DSS support will achieve better contract balance than bargainers with no computer support.

Hypothesis 2.2. There will be no difference between the contract balance achieved by DSS-supported dyads and the contract balance achieved by NSS-supported dyads.

Contract balance is computed as the absolute value of the difference between the number of points achieved by each bargainer. It measures how well the negotiated settlement reflects the needs of each participant. If the difference is zero, neither participant takes advantage of the other. The higher the difference, the more distorted the contract is in favor of one negotiator. The Foroughi et al. study [11] resulted in a better contract balance (closer to zero) with the NSS than with no computer support.

It is asserted that the DSS portion of the NSS provided the greatest contribution to this contract balance. Each participant could use the NDST to quickly, easily, and accurately compute the number of points that he or she would receive from each proposed contract. In addition, the NDST provides a close approximation of the number of points to be received by the other negotiator. Thus, it is possible for each bargainer to find a contract which is perceived to be fair to both parties and not disproportionately advantageous to one side. Contract balance as used in this study should serve essentially the same purpose (e.g., a measure of fairness) as closeness to the Nash solution proposed by Lim and Benbasat [17].

## 4.3. Negotiation time

Hypothesis 3.1. Bargainers with DSS support will take more time than bargainers with no computer support.

Hypothesis 3.2. There will be no difference between the negotiation time achieved by DSS-supported dyads and the negotiation time achieved by NSS-supported dyads.

The total time required for the negotiation was measured in minutes. Foroughi et al. [11] showed that the NSS dyads took longer negotiation time than the dyads with no computer support.

It is asserted that the time involved in keying in input, waiting for the opponent's response to contract proposals (using his/her NDST), and using the NDST for the generation and evaluation of alternatives increases the time required for the DSS over negotiations with no computer support, but would not increase the time over the comprehensive NSS dyads.

Lim and Benbasat [17] hypothesize that the use of a full NSS will reduce negotiation time compared with the use of a DSS only. They predict that the electronic communication support will increase perceived commitment of the two negotiating parties, increase their trust in the negotiation, and thus speed up the negotiation process, resulting in lower negotiation time. In this study, we predict no difference in negotiation time between DSS-supported dyads and NSS-supported dyads.

## 4.4. Number of contracts proposed

Hypothesis 4.1. Bargainers with DSS support will propose more contracts than bargainers with no computer support.

Hypothesis 4.2. There will be no difference between the number of contracts proposed by bargainers with

DSS support and the number of contracts proposed by NSS-supported bargainers.

Each combination of values for the issues that was proposed by either party was counted as a separate contract proposal. If a bargainer suggested two alternatives at one time, it was counted as two offers. Foroughi et al. [11] showed that the NSS dyads had more contract offers than the dyads without computer support. It is asserted that the DSS facilitates the generation of quality alternatives and the efficient analysis of a large number of contract proposals. Thus, the DSS dyads should have no difference in the number of contract proposals compared to NSS dyads, but they should have more proposals than the dyads without computer support. Lim and Benbasat [17] did not suggest the use of this measure.

## 4.5. Post-bargaining negotiator attitudes

## 4.5.1. Perceived collaborative climate

Hypothesis 5.1. In the low conflict treatments, there will be no difference between the perceived collaborative climate for DSS-supported bargainers and that for bargainers with no computer support.

Hypothesis 5.2. In the low conflict treatments, there will be no difference between the perceived collaborative climate for DSS-supported bargainers and that for NSS-supported bargainers.

Hypothesis 5.3. In the high conflict treatments, DSS-supported bargainers will experience a higher perceived collaborative climate than bargainers with no computer support.

Hypothesis 5.4. In the high conflict treatments, there will be no difference between the perceived collaborative climate for DSS-supported bargainers and that for NSS-supported bargainers.

## 4.5.2. Perceived negative climate

Hypothesis 6.1. In the low conflict treatments, there will be no difference between the perceived negative climate for DSS-supported bargainers and that for bargainers with no computer support.

Hypothesis 6.2. In the low conflict treatments, there will be no difference between the perceived negative climate for DSS-supported bargainers and that for NSS-supported bargainers.

Hypothesis 6.3. In the high conflict treatments, DSS-supported bargainers will experience lower perceived negative climate than bargainers with no computer support.

Hypothesis 6.4. In the high conflict treatments, there will be no difference between the perceived negative climate for DSS-supported bargainers and that for NSS-supported bargainers.

## 4.5.3. Satisfaction

Hypothesis 7.1. Bargainers with DSS support will experience higher satisfaction than bargainers with no computer support.

Hypothesis 7.2. There will be no difference between the satisfaction of bargainers with DSS support and the satisfaction of bargainers with NSS support.

In the low conflict treatments, bargainers' preferences for the issues differ, and there is room for tradeoffs. A minimum of non-rational escalation of conflict and negative framing for bargaining dyads will likely occur, regardless of computer support [1,4]. Thus, computer support is not expected to have an effect on perceived collaborative climate or perceived negative climate in the low conflict treatments.

In the high conflict treatments, subjects find themselves in a nearly zero-sum situation in which their interests are pitted against each other $[9]$ . Perceived collaborative climate will likely be greater for DSS dyads than for non-computer supported dyads, and perceived negative climate will be less for DSS dyads than for non-computer supported dyads. The DSS is likely to give negotiators confidence about reaching a good outcome, thus helping them to save face and maintain a positive frame about the negotiation $[1]$ . Expectations are less clear for collaborative climate and negative climate when comparing DSS-alone dyads with comprehensive NSS dyads. The impact of the DSS may be the dominant factor, supporting Hypotheses 5.2 and 6.2 that no differences will be found. On the other hand, the electronic communication component of the comprehensive NSS may help focus the attention of the negotiators on the content of the negotiation instead of on any personal conflict, thus creating a more collaborative climate and minimizing non-rational escalation of conflict [8].

The use of the DSS is expected to increase satisfaction in both the low and high conflict treatments compared to the non-computer supported dyads. It seems reasonable that if negotiators achieve a higher joint outcome and better contract balance, as hypothesized above, they are likely to be more satisfied. No difference in satisfaction is expected between DSS-alone dyads and comprehensive NSS dyads, just as no difference is expected in terms of joint outcome and contract balance.

Lim and Benbasat [17] proposed measuring two additional sociological aspects of the negotiation – satisfaction with the negotiation outcome and confidence in the negotiation solution. The three measures considered in Hypotheses 5.1 through 7.4 relate to these dimensions, especially to the satisfaction dimension.

## 5. Research methodology

## 5.1. Research design

This study employed a $3 \times 2$ experimental design, with 16 pairs of bargainers in each of the six cells, for a total of 192 individual subjects. One independent variable was the type of NSS provided (a comprehensive NSS, DSS support only, or no computer support), and the second independent variable was low conflict or high conflict.

It should be noted that the data for two of these cells – the DSS alone with low conflict, and the DSS alone with high conflict – were collected one year later than the data for the other four cells. Insofar as possible, the recently conducted experiments (gathering data for the last two cells) were identical to the earlier experiments. The same task was used, the same procedures and scripts, the same focus on integrative bargaining, the same two levels of conflict, and the same output variables. The same subject pool was used – student volunteers from the required sophomore-level computer course (entitled “The Computer in Business”) at Indiana University. The only feature which distinguished these experiments from the earlier ones was that the decision support system alone (not the electronic communication tool) was provided to the subjects.

The researchers were aware of the possible threat to the internal validity of the experiment which was posed by adding two cells at a later date. In particular, non-random, systematic blocking could have occurred. This threat was minimized by the careful application of the controls and procedures used in collecting the first four cells. Further, with the exception of the sociological aspects, the results for the last two cells were very similar to those for the comprehensive NSS cells, suggesting that the internal validity of the experiment was not seriously undermined by adding two cells at a later date.

## 5.2. Bargaining task

The bargaining task chosen for this research involved negotiation between a buyer and a seller over four issues of a three-year purchase agreement for an engine subcomponent [14]. The issues were unit price, purchase quantity, time of first delivery, and warranty period. Low conflict treatments were simulated by assigning different weights for the issues, creating an integrative bargaining situation in which mutually beneficial tradeoffs were possible. High conflict treatments featured issues for both parties being weighted similarly, creating a distributive bargaining situation in which one party's gain was nearly equal to the other one's loss. For both low and high conflict levels, point sheets were constructed for buyer and seller using these weights.

## 5.3. Independent variables

The study included two independent variables, type of negotiation support and level of conflict. Type of negotiation support consisted of three treatments (a comprehensive NSS, DSS only, and no computer support), and levels of conflict included two treatments (high conflict and low conflict). Thus the study employed a $3 \times 2$ random factorial design with fixed-effects.

## 5.4. Dependent and control variables

Pre-negotiation attitudes were surveyed to ensure that there were no significant differences between the subjects used for the first four cells of the study and those used for the last two cells. Outcome measures from the negotiations included joint outcome, contract balance, negotiation time, and the number of contracts proposed. In addition, post-bargaining negotiator attitudes (perceived collaborative climate, perceived negative climate, and satisfaction) were measured. Davis' Technology Acceptance Model [7] was administered to the computer-supported groups to measure overall evaluation, perceived ease of use, and perceived usefulness of the software tools used. Several variables were controlled in both phases (see [11]). To control for group structure differences, each negotiating side consisted of one person, with zero history in negotiating with each other. Individual differences were controlled by random assignment of subjects to the role of buyer or seller and to pairs, and of pairs to experimental treatments. The same type of bargaining task was performed by all dyads, the only difference being the assignment of weights to the issues in low conflict or high conflict. The physical environment was similar (Indiana University laboratory rooms) for all dyads, except for the presence or absence of computer support.

Joint outcome was defined as the total number of points achieved by the bargaining dyad. It is highest when the contract is on the efficient frontier. The contract balance was the absolute value of the difference between the buyer's and the seller's points. It is zero for a balanced contract and a higher number for an unbalanced one. The negotiation time was measured in minutes. All dyads completed a contract within the two-hour experimental session. An offer was counted as being proposed each time one of the four contract factors (price, quantity, delivery time, or warranty period) was changed. Post-bargaining negotiator attitudes were measured by a questionnaire [14] administered at the conclusion of the bargaining session. Subjects responded (using a 7-point Likert scale) to questions that were factored into measures of perceived collaborative climate, perceived negative climate, and satisfaction with the bargaining session. The factor loadings for the items on this questionnaire had previously been validated in Foroughi et al. [11].

Several variables were controlled in the study. To control for group structure differences, each negotiating side consisted of one person, with zero history in negotiating with each other. Individual differences were controlled by random assignment of subjects to the role of buyer or seller and to pairs, and of pairs to experimental treatments. The same type of bargaining task was performed by all dyads, the only difference being the assignment of weights to the issues in low conflict or high conflict. The physical environment was similar (Indiana University laboratory rooms) for all dyads, except for the presence or absence of computer support.

## 6. Statistical analysis

Each of the questionnaires (pre-negotiation attitudes, post-negotiation attitudes, and technology evaluation) was analyzed separately. The primary interest in the pre-negotiation attitude questionnaire was in comparing the results for the subjects used for the first four cells and those used for the last two cells. Happily, the results indicated that the two samples were consistent, with the assumption that they were randomly selected from the same underlying population. For the technology evaluation questionnaire, the means for the relevant software tools for each measure (overall evaluation, perceived ease of use, and perceived usefulness) for both the low and high conflict treatments were above the midpoint (4.0) on the Likert scale, indicating a favorable evaluation of the tools. Only one significant difference occurred in these results, so the complete tables are not included in this paper; the single significant difference is discussed in Section 7.2.

To analyze the post-negotiation attitude questionnaire, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for each attitude measure (perceived collaborative climate, perceived negative climate, and satisfaction), using support level and conflict level as the main effects in order to determine if there were any interaction effects. Differences in the mean values were identified. Finally, for each attitude measure and each conflict level, a one-way ANOVA was performed in order to determine if there were any significant differences, followed by a series of planned comparisons among the three levels of support. The analysis of each outcome measure began with a two-way ANOVA, using support level and conflict level as the main effects. Differences in the mean values were identified. For each outcome measure and each conflict level, a one-way ANOVA was performed followed by a series of planned comparisons among the three levels of support.

The planned comparisons of the means were carried out using a t-test with an alpha of 0.05 for a one-tailed test. The alpha was modified to adjust for the three levels of support. As a check, non-parametric tests were also used for these planned comparisons; the results were essentially the same as the t-tests reported below. $^{1}$

## 7. Results

The primary results of the study are presented in Table 1. We will first consider the results from DSS-supported dyads versus dyads with no computer support (Hypotheses 1.1 through 7.1, 5.3 and 6.3), then the results from DSS-supported dyads versus comprehensive NSS-supported dyads (Hypotheses 1.2 through 7.2, 5.4, and 6.4). Finally, we will compare our results with those suggested by Lim and Benbasat's theory of negotiation.

## 7.1. Results for DSS alone vs. no computer support

In comparison to the non-computer supported situation, use of the DSS alone resulted in higher joint outcomes and longer negotiation times for both levels of conflict. These results are consistent with those reported in Foroughi et al. [11] for the comprehensive NSS compared to no computer support. But there are also differences in the results between the two studies. First, the number of contract offers was higher with the DSS compared to no computer support, while there had been no difference in the number of contract offers between the comprehensive NSS and no computer support. Second, the DSS alone improved contract balance compared to no computer support only in the low conflict case, whereas the NSS improved contract balance for both levels of conflict.

## 7.2. Results for DSS alone vs. comprehensive NSS

Comparing the results of the DSS alone and the comprehensive NSS yielded several interesting outcomes. There were no significant differences between the DSS and the NSS for joint outcome, contract balance, negotiation time, collaborative climate, and negative climate. However, more contract proposals were made with the DSS alone than with the comprehensive NSS. $^{2}$ Satisfaction was significantly better with the NSS than with the DSS alone. While not significant, NSS helped negotiators achieve a higher joint outcome than DSS at the 0.07 level in low conflict, and negative climate was lower with NSS than with DSS at the 0.10 level in low conflict. In high conflict, the overall technology evaluation for the comprehensive NSS was significantly higher than for the DSS alone. This result could be attributed at least in part to the cumulative effect of the DSS and electronic communication components of the comprehensive NSS.

## 7.3. Results for test of Lim and Benbasat's theory

This study confirmed several of the hypotheses made by Lim and Benbasat [17]. For instance, DSS support alone did help dyads reach higher joint outcomes than without computer support, as suggested by the theory. However, DSS support improved contract balance over no computer support only in low conflict. The comprehensive NSS did improve satisfaction relative to DSS support alone, as suggested by Lim and Benbasat.

Perhaps the most significant deviation of our results from the Lim and Benbasat theory occurred for negotiation time. Negotiation time was unchanged with the comprehensive NSS compared to negotiation time with the DSS alone, instead of shorter as hypothesized by Lim and Benbasat. These results might be explained by the relative unfamiliarity of the subjects with the computer support system. Ongoing use of the DSS or comprehensive NSS might reduce negotiation time. Alternatively, the interaction with the computer support system may simply add time to the negotiation process even after the system is mastered. Negotiators did propose more contracts in the DSS-supported dyads, and the existence of the NSS may encourage negotiators to analyze more options and consider them in greater depth. Further study is needed to determine which explanation is more accurate.

## 8. Conclusions

This study was undertaken to determine whether interactive DSS support alone could be as effective as this same support coupled with electronic communication. Although the DSS improved some aspects of the negotiation outcomes compared to no computer support, the comprehensive NSS provided a wider range of enhancements to the negotiation. These results thus provide support for Lim and Benbasat's [17] theory of negotiation, which recognizes the separate but important roles played by the two components of NSS – DSS and electronic communication – and hypothesizes that the effects of each component will be different. While the DSS plays the role of improving information processing capacities, the electronic communication component impacts positively on the sociological aspects of negotiator interaction. In terms of Lim and Benbasat's hypotheses about DSS as opposed to no computer support, joint outcomes and contract balance (at least in low conflict) were better with DSS, thus providing evidence of the DSS enhancement of bargainers' information processing capacity. In terms of Lim and Benbasat's hypotheses concerning comprehensive NSS support versus DSS support, negotiation time was not shorter with comprehensive NSS support than with DSS support. Of course, ongoing use of the NSS might tend to reduce negotiation time. The hypothesis that comprehensive NSS support would lead to greater satisfaction with the solution than with DSS alone was confirmed by the results, thus underscoring the idea that electronic communication can impact positively on socio-emotional aspects of negotiator behavior.

Improving negotiator satisfaction is an important outcome: in real-life settings, negotiators who have been satisfied with negotiation processes and outcomes in the past will approach future negotiations with positive attitudes, thus improving their performance in the long run. By effectively enhancing the integrative bargaining process, the comprehensive NSS support makes it more likely that negotiators' performance will improve in the long run $[23]$ . The results also provide evidence in support of the effectiveness of group support systems, in particular a Level Two GSS which combines electronic communication and decision support system components, as in this study $[8]$ .

Further research is needed to examine other types of DSS and electronic communication components by themselves and in configuration with each other. This study has added to our knowledge in the area of NSS, and the ongoing research program of which it is a part will continue research with the purpose of yielding NSS that will be useful and effective in real-life negotiation settings.
