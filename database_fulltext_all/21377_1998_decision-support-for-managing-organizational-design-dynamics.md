# Decision support for managing organizational design dynamics

Anitesh Barua <sup>)</sup>, Andrew B. Whinston

Center for Information Systems Management, Department of Management Science and Information Systems, Graduate School of Business, The UniÕersity of Texas at Austin, Austin, TX 78712, USA

## Abstract

Changes in the business environment and the advent of new Information Technologies IT create new challenges andŽ . opportunities for redesigning the organization. Organizational design encompasses a large number of factors including IT, incentives, business processes, and the scope of decision authority. Further, a design configuration based on a given business and technological environment may have to be modified with changes in the environment. We suggest using an approach called business value complementarity 8 as a systematic basis to ensure that design decisions contribute maximally to <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> bottom-line performance measures such as profitability. It is based on the notion of creating a business value model showing relationships between key performance measures and design decision variables involving IT, incentives, etc. Complementarity between the variables in the business value model provides a basis for choosing the levels of design variables. We derive some results which specify conditions under which the overall performance measure s are complementary in the designŽ . variables. We suggest that a Decision Support System DSS would be appropriate for helping senior management in theŽ . complex process of developing and maintaining a business value model and in choosing a suitable organizational design. We also enumerate high level functional requirements of such a DSS. q 1998 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Organizational design; Business value modelling; Complementarity; Supermodularity; Decision support system; Requirements analysis

## 1. Introduction

1.1. Choosing an organizational design: a critical decision process

More than ever, designing or redesigning organizations for efficiency, flexibility and responsiveness to customer needs has become a high priority item on senior management’s agenda in the 90s. For many organizations, design changes have been primarily internal, for example, the organizational structure is moving towards cross-functional team-based design, where the decision structures are changing from strictly hierarchical to more of an ‘interlocking decision authority’ structure 3 . The eagerness on the <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> part of management to turn the organization around by redesigning its core processes, information technology IT , lines of decision authority and rewardŽ . systems is reflected in a multitude of popular trends such as business process reengineering BPR , re-Ž . structuration, rightsizing, and total quality management. The definitions of these terms are far from being unanimous. To some, reengineering involves employee empowerment by redefining roles, tasks and decision making authority. To others, reengineering has a technical implication, involving migration to a client<sup>r</sup>server architecture from a mainframe environment. We argue shortly that such isolated views of reengineering or organizational design in general lead to undesirable outcomes. In certain industries such as computer hardware, applications and system software, data communications, telephone and cable TV, organizational changes are taking even more dramatic turns to include multibillion dollar mergers and takeovers, whereby an entire industry can get restructured in response to changes in the business environment. While reengineering has clearly been the central management theme in the 90s, it is more useful to collectively think about these internal as well as external change phenomena under the umbrella of organizational design dynamics.

Unfortunately, large investments do not necessarily translate into success; in fact, both the business and academic press highlight a high rate of failure of organizational redesign projects through both anecdotal and systematic evidence. For example, estimates of reengineering failure range from 70% 11<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> 15 to 85% 20 . While there are many documented stories of success, a common theme that appears to run across a majority of the reported surveys is that many of these investments do not pay off. We look upon BPR, restructuration and other rejuvenation efforts as changing the design of an organization. Regardless of the banner under which it operates, organizational design is a major decision making process, where senior management has to make a set of crucial and costly both in terms of opportunityŽ and real costs decisions regarding the states of . design variables pertaining to a variety of organizational and technological factors. The popular prescription of ‘radical change’ based on anecdotal evidence in reengineering provides a useful starting point, but is not operational enough for senior management to make design decisions that contribute to the bottomline payoff. Similarly, the notion of ‘fit’ in the sociotechnical literature is intuitively appealing, but again provides no solid yet pragmatic foundation which can aid senior management in such a major decision process, where design decisions must be made to have maximal impact on performance measures such as profitability and growth 8 .

1.2. A basis for making organizational design decisions

We present a powerful new organizational design paradigm based on developments in operations research 24 , economics 17,25 , and MIS 8 . This<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> approach is called business value complementarity Ž . BVC 8 . The central thesis involves the combina-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tion of factors, objects, processes, people and technologies that have a value synergy among themselves. The presence of synergy implies that when combined correctly, the set of factors will have a significant positive impact on bottomline payoff. While synergy is not new to the world of management, we believe that a synergy-based operational framework provides a strong foundation for making design decisions which maximize bottomline impact. It allows top management to analyze at the planning stage rather than after the fact the directions in Ž . which organizational and technological design factors should be changed in response to changes in the business environment, and the impact of such changes on organizational payoff and other performance measures.

BVC entails a two-step approach to managing organizational design: i the creation of a multilevelŽ . business value model showing the relationships between design variables and performance measures at various levels within the organization, and ii theŽ . identification of complementarity in the relationships of the business value model. The opportunity cost of not being able to discover complementary relationships between various organizational and technological factors is high, since such relationships can significantly increase the value, reduce operating costs, and contain sharp increases in capital costs. Conversely, a set of changes in the design variables in the wrong directions can reduce the value derived from a set of design changes. BVC also has an important implication for the role of senior management regarding an overall vision of organizational design. While the business value model integrates performance measures that span multiple levels within the organization, the exploitation of complementary relationships requires a top level understanding of how all variables eventually contribute to overall payoff. Thus, while decision authority and IT management are being decentralized in many organizations, it is imperative that senior management has a centralized vision of the drivers of organizational performance to be able to steer design decisions along a path of maximum payoff.

## 1.3. ProÕiding decision support for BVC-based organizational design

Making decisions regarding organization design is a complex process, which entails consensus building among different units within the company, enumerating design choices, and identifying drivers of bottomline payoff. We suggest a Decision Support System DSS can provide systematic guidance to seniorŽ . management in developing a business value model with the active participation from managers in various units within the organization, analyzing complementary relationships within the value model, making decisions regarding the levels of design variables as well as actual choices of technologies, decision rights and business processes which lead to such levels, and in periodically testing the suitability of the existing design in the wake of changes in the business environment.

Admittedly, the requirements of such a DSS are demanding, spanning a wide range of capabilities from model building to negotiation and computational support. However, given the criticality of managing organizational design in a rapidly evolving business and technological environment, the benefits are expected to outweigh the cost of developing, maintaining and using the DSS.

## 2. Motivation and prior literature

Organizational design has been a focal theme in the contingency theory and sociotechnical literature Žsee 8 for a brief critique of this literature in the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> context of reengineering . Both theories recognize. the need for a fit between various aspects of organizational design. Unfortunately, contingency theory has been shown to suffer from the lack of theoretical support e.g., see 22 , while sociotechnical or fit Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. theoretic studies do not provide an operational basis for assessing the impacts of a given design configuration on the organizational payoff 8 , and have not<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> found substantial empirical support for its theoretical foundation 12 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

To measure the impacts of IT investments on economic performance, Barua et al. 7 develop and<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> test an approach called business value modeling. They suggest that the economic impacts of IT investments can be assessed through a business value model in which choice variables such as IT and other input investments affect ‘intermediate’ level performance measures such as inventory turnover and capacity utilization, which in turn determine financial performance measures like return on assets and market share.

Based on the seminal work of Topkis 24 in<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> operations research on the optimization of ‘submodular’ functions, Milgrom and Roberts 17,18 used complementarity to rationalize patterns of practice in modern manufacturing firms. In the MIS literature, Barua et al. 8 use complementarity theory along <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> with the business value modeling approach to assess the impacts of changes in various organizational and technological factors upon chosen measures of performance in reengineering projects. This business value complementarity BVC approach calls for theŽ . identification of a set of organizational and technological factors which are drivers of business value, and the coordinated choice of the levels and directions of the design-related factors in a complementary manner. The key idea behind complementarity is that the value of changing two or more complementary factors in the right directions is higher that the sum of the values derived by making the same changes in one factor at a time. That is, for a set of complementary factors, ‘the whole is worth more than the sum of the parts’ 17,18 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

We suggest that BVC is applicable to a wide variety of organizational design problems, and is not limited to reengineering projects. We provide some new theoretical results which are useful in analyzing design alternatives for certain classes of problem settings. We also study the process of developing business value models for two large organizations. Further, we discuss the functional requirements for a DSS to help senior management in developing, using and maintaining a business value model.

Several case studies by Applegate 1–3 have<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> documented business transformations which proved to be beneficial for the organizations involved. Why is it that some organizations are able to manage and obtain significant benefits from organizational redesign, while others find the results disappointing or even disastrous? What systematic patterns if anyŽ . characterize the management of design change in these successful organizations? If any pattern is observed, what is its basis or theoretical foundation, which will enable other organizations to implement design changes that will improve their bottomline payoff? We analyze design change management in two large organizations Frito Lay and Phillips 66 .Ž . We interpret Applegate’s accounts of change management in these organizations from the standpoint of BVC. Our analysis reveals a systematic and correlated pattern of management involving the simultaneous implementation of changes in a large number of technological and organizational factors. We show that the similarity between the decisions in both the organizations to engage in coordinated changes is hardly coincidental, and is in total agreement with our basic premise involving complementary organizational activities.

## 3. Business value complementarity as a basis for organizational design

To illustrate the process of developing a business value model, we first describe key variables chosen by two large organizations, and then proceed to analyze the complementary nature of the relationships in the value model.

## 3.1. DeÕeloping a business Õalue model: a tale of two organizations

The first step in building a business value model is to determine the key financial objective s of the Ž . firm. In the 1980s, Frito Lay faced a market that was saturating with aggressive competitors. Its management determined that in order to succeed in such a competitive marketplace, it must achieve sustained growth 3 . Management also concluded that creating<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> market niches and increasing the rate of product development would enable them to achieve the desired growth. Additionally, micromarketing strategies or regional marketing rather than national Ž strategies would enable Frito Lay to create market. niches through region specific product mix. We interpret market growth as the highest level performance measure, and the extent of market niche and the rate of product development as two intermediate level factors in Frito Lay’s business value model.

The lowest level in a business value model consists of design variables. These are the factors, which when chosen appropriately, will enable the organization to achieve a positive impact on the intermediate and consequently the highest level performance measures. While the specific set of variables depends upon the organization in question and its business environment, in general, we can identify six categories of design variables: IT, decision authority, organizational structure, coordination mechanisms, incentives and business processes. There may be several variables for each of the above categories. For example, IT variables may include the level of intra- and inter-organizational information access and the user friendliness of client applications, while the number of processing steps and the number of organizational units involved in a business process may constitute business process variables 8 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Frito Lay management concluded that flexible information access, decentralized decision authority Ž . which would enable a micromarketing strategy , appropriate management control systems, efficient business processes and incentives would be critical to achieving the intermediate goals of market niche and faster product development. These factors whenŽ properly scaled constitute the choice variables. This. two-level business value model is shown in Fig. 1. In the absence of information on operating and capital costs, only the benefit side of the model is represented. It is interesting to note that management did not come up with the complete set of choice factors pertaining to IT, decision authority, etc., from the very beginning. During our discussion of the complementary relationship between the various factors, we will highlight problems that occurred when management did not make changes in all design variables.

## 3.2. Analyzing complementarity in the business Õalue model

Once the drivers of business value and their basic relationships i.e., hypothesized links between theŽ variables are specified, the next step is to assess and. exploit any complementarity that might exist between various factors at different levels. This is a critical phase in the BVC approach; even after the drivers of business value have been identified, the actual payoff that the organization can expect will depend on the choice of the levels of the value drivers. But how can a manager discover complementary relationships? In other words, how does the manager know if two factors are complements? A qualitative test would involve asking a question which relates to the very definition of complementarity: By increasing the level of one factor, can we increase the payoff from increasing the level of another factor? For example, in a client<sup>r</sup>server computing environment, users need fast response from powerful server machines, while expecting user-friendliness from client applications. The value e.g., in the formŽ of user productivity of increasing the power of the. server side clearly increases with the ease of use of applications on the client side, showing the complementarity between the two design variables, the power of the server and the ease of use of the client application s . The complementarity need not stay Ž . restricted within the IT category. In fact, we expect some of the most powerful complementary relationships between IT and non-IT variables. For example, the benefits of business process integration and simplification can be increased substantially if the information necessary to do perform the steps and make decisions in the business process is made readily available in a transparent manner throughout the enterprise.

The key implication of a complementary relationship is that the levels of the variables in such a relationship must be chosen in a coordinated fashion <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 8 . For example, managers engaged in reengineering or an organizational design project may envision the need to move to a team-based structure as a way to make the organization more flexible and responsive to customer needs. Yet, an MIS group may not recognize the need to complement this change in the decision structure by migrating from a centralized computing environment to a more distributed and flexible information architecture which will enable information access and sharing throughout the enterprise 8 . Even worse, an IT infrastructure which<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> allows information access and sharing may not be complemented with new incentive systems which encourage users in different parts of the organization to exchange information 9 . Complementarity, how- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ever, dictates that making changes in the levels of various factors in an isolated manner will not be effective.

Frito Lay started its redesign project with changes in the decision authority structure and incentive systems. Unfortunately, the changes initially failed to contribute to the target performance measures because the IT infrastructure was inadequate to provide information sharing throughout the organization, and because appropriate management control processes had not been implemented for the new decision structure. According to a Frito Lay manager, ‘‘The organization became dysfunctional because we did not have the management and information systems in place to support the new strategy. . . . Problems surfaced. New products went stale in the warehouse. Information on regional promotions came long after the increased inventory had been shipped to the warehouses . . . ’’ 3 . However, the micromarketing <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> strategy succeeded when appropriate changes in the IT infrastructure and management control processes were implemented. Similarly, in the early phases of the restructuration process, Phillips 66 one refinery Ž . implemented area work, process management and quality management teams. The authority for managing operating and business strategies was shifted from corporate headquarters to strategic business units 3 . However, this empowerment was not ac-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> companied by top management vision regarding the company objectives. In other words, the potential for fragmented strategies and decision making, which would be incompatible with the overall organizational value model, was very high. Over a span of several years, both organizations discovered the need to implement coordinated changes in all the categories of design factors mentioned earlier.

## 3.3. Building consensus regarding Õarious leÕels in a business Õalue model

Which processes or techniques are appropriate for determining the business value model? Given the multiple levels in such a model, it should not be entrusted to just one senior manager or a shop floor supervisor. Determining the drivers of value requires consensus building among managers at various levels in the organization. Note that reengineering is considered to be top management-driven, while the Japanese practice of continuous change Kaizen is a Ž . bottom-up process, where workers play a lead role in suggesting changes 5 . While business value mod-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> elling starts as a top–down modeling exercise, a successful value model will integrate different levels in an organization, and provide a platform for interactions among managers regarding the performance measures. Further, it lets managers at different levels understand their interdependencies and how their actions and decisions contribute to the overall organizational value. In fact, the levels in the business value chain would generally correspond to the managerial levels in the organization. For example, at the CEO level, the key performance measures may be shareholder value and profitability, while it may be percentage defectives or rework statistics for a quality control manager. Of course, because of its ability to understand the overall business, top management has to play a critical role in the formulation of the business value model, especially at the higher levels.

## 3.4. The need for continual assessment of organizational design

Organizations are operating in an economically and technologically turbulent period, undergoing a major structural, technological and social transformation. Rapid advances in IT and economic factors such as globalization and competitive pressure are forcing organizations to embrace major technological and structural changes in an attempt to become more competitive and efficient. Thus, organizational design is not a one-shot process—the design itself has to change in response to a set of stimuli, either internal and<sup>r</sup>or external to the organization. For example, the frequency of mergers and takeovers Žwhich we view as a part of organizational design involving a large number of external factors in a. given industry increases significantly during periods of turbulence in the relevant environment, as witnessed in the health care and IT sectors over the past few years. Thus, organizational design has to be considered as a dynamic process, where small or large changes will have to occur on a continual basis in order to adjust to internal and<sup>r</sup>or external changes.

## 5. Decision support system requirements for BVC-based design management

We suggest that a DSS can help senior managers in building, maintaining and acting upon a business value model. Although it may appear more logical to start with the motivation for developing a DSS to help in design decisions, it will be useful to first enumerate the broad functional requirements for such a system. The complexity of the tasks to be supported will provide direct justification for designing and developing such a DSS.

A DSS for organizational design support must help: i build a business value model; ii identifyŽ . Ž . complementary relationships; iii assess the levelsŽ . of design variables which lead to maximum organizational payoff; iv identify specific technologies,Ž . business processes, etc., which will provide the desired levels of characteristics; and v assess whetherŽ . changes in the levels of decision variables in the business value model are necessary in response to changes in the external environment. Capabilities iŽ . through iv correspond to theŽ . design and choice phases of the decision making framework of Simon <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 23 , while $\mathbf { \Pi } ( \boldsymbol { v } )$ is analogous to Simon’s intelligence phase. The design stage involves enumerating and evaluating alternatives available to a decision maker. In the case of organizational design, senior management needs to specify the key elements of the business value model such as performance measures at high and intermediate levels, decision variables pertaining to IT, business processes, decision authority and incentives, as well as different states of each decision variable. The choice phase involves the actual selection of an alternative; the corresponding task in organizational design is the choice of the levels of decision variables in the business value model which leads to maximum payoff. When the external environment changes, a DSS must alert senior management regarding the need to reevaluate the suitability of the last design decision for the new environment. In the balance of this section, we focus on the areas where a DSS can aid organizational design management.

## 5.1. Helping build a business Õalue model

Since the ultimate goals of a commercial organization involve performance measures such as prof itability or market share, all design decisions must be geared toward such objectives. Accordingly, a top– down analysis which involves various levels within an organization appears to be the most appropriate approach to developing a business value model. At the top level, a DSS can help elicit overall measures of success, e.g., profitability, market share, growth,Ž etc. through a series of questions for senior manage-. ment. This is similar to the critical success factor method developed by Rockart 21 .

Having identified the top level of the business value model, the DSS focuses on intermediate level performance measures which lead to better outcomes judged critical in the previous step. Which intermediate level variables are appropriate will depend on the industry sector as well as the specific organization. For example, price may be critical in a highly competitive sector, while customer service may be the differentiating factor in other settings. If industrywide data is available, such a relationship can be empirically validated as in 7 . Alternatively, a Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. qualitative approach may be adopted where the DSS guides senior management through a series of questions to elicit key intermediate level measures. At this stage, inputs may be obtained from management at various levels and teams within the organization. For example, managers in the production group can help specify what their performance measures are, and how such measures contribute to higher level measures such as profitability.

At the lowest level, management needs to identify relevant characteristics associated with IT and other design factors mentioned earlier. Since different groups or teams in an organization may be responsible for different sets of factors e.g., MIS groupŽ managing IT-related decision variables, human resource handling incentive systems, etc. , it is critical. to get each group involved in completing the lowest level of the business value model. As noted by Barua et al. 8 , a specific group may be unfortunately left <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> out from the reengineering effort for a variety of reasons including oversight or a lack of understanding of the complementarity between different decision variables.

## 5.2. Helping identify complementary relationships

Since complementarity is a critical component of the BVC approach, identifying and validating such relationships is central to making prudent design decisions. If industry-wide data on the variables in the business value model is available, the DSS can help estimate the magnitude of complementarity from the coefficients of a generalized functional form Že.g., a translog production function 13 which<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. accounts for complementarity. Admittedly, the empirical assessment of complementarity has many statistical caveats, many of which are discussed by Athey and Stern 4 . When data is not available,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> senior management can be guided through a series of scenarios to qualitatively assess the joint impact of making pairwise changes in the levels of design variables.

Not every relationship in a business value model will be complementary. For example, even when only those choice sets which constitute chains are considered, and when there are three or more levels in a business value model, supermodularity at each level does not guarantee overall complementarity as seen in Proposition 2. In that event, the analysis should focus on the strong supermodularity property discussed earlier. Barua et al. 10 suggest that complementarity need not be a commonplace occurrence, and that there may be negative effects upon complementary relationships from certain variables in a business value model. They propose the notion of ‘weak submodularity’ which requires such negative effects to be weak enough for the highest level to be complementary in the choice variables.

To summarize, there are many obstacles to the overall complementarity in a business value model. A DSS for this purpose must consider appropriate conditions e.g., the complementarity conditions de-Ž rived in Propositions 1 and 2, the strong supermodularity conditions suggested by Barua et al. 8 , and <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> weak submodularity conditions proposed by Barua et al. 10 and test either empirically or subjectively if <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. such conditions are likely to be satisfied in the design problem under consideration.

5.3. Helping identify alternatiÕes for IT, business processes, etc.

While a business value model contains key performance and design variables, it does not specify actual technologies, business processes and incentives which will lead to desired levels of design variables such as information access and process integration. At this stage, the DSS should help in mapping from actual choices of technologies and organizational factors to levels of design variables. This process involves enumerating choices available to management, and assigning levels of various design variables to each choice. For example, an application based on the World Wide Web may be rated as providing the highest level of enterprise-wide information access and interaction due to the open nature of standards used by the Internet. By contrast, an application built around a proprietary product like Lotus Notes which however works over the Internet as well as with Internet applications like the Web 6<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> may be assigned a lower value for information access. Systems developed with more proprietary components and standards which do not work across the Internet at all may be mapped to even lower levels of information access.

In a similar manner, different organizational and decision authority structures, business processes and incentive systems can be mapped to various levels of corresponding design variables. Of course, this mapping is necessarily subjective, requiring inputs from various managers with expertise in specific areas. The potential for disagreement over the correspondence between a certain technology or organizational structure and their design variables is high; it implies that the DSS must have mechanisms for consensus building as in the case of choosing key elements of the business value model itself.

## 5.4. Helping determine the leÕels of design Õariables

As in the above steps, the analysis involved in the actual choice of levels of design variables can proceed in two ways depending on the availability of data. If management has sufficient data for the business value model, the levels of the design variables may be computationally chosen to maximize the net value or payoff at the top level. Due to complementarity, the optimal investment in one factor will increase if its complements can also be changed in a coordinated fashion 17 . However, the choice of<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> levels of design variables must also take into account preexisting organizational conditions. For example, contrary to the ‘clean slate approach’ prescription of reengineering, few organizations have the luxury of building an IT infrastructure by ignoring potentially vast stocks of IT that they already have. Barua et al. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 8 show that when preexisting conditions relating to IT and organizational factors are unfavorable, the optimal levels of the design variables are lowered due to an increase in the cost of achieving a given level of a design variable. Thus, the DSS must allow management to consider what already exists in the organization, and how such conditions affect the optimal levels of the choice variables.

## 5.5. Helping monitor the optimality of design

Like the intelligence phase in the decision making of Simon 23 , senior management can use the DSS<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> periodically to check the optimality of design. The DSS should help management answer the following question: Is there any external stimulus from the business or technological environment that calls for a change in the current organizational design? For example, an aggressive pricing policy by a competitor may force an organization to become more efficient through restructuring its operations. As suggested by Applegate 3 , a change in the organiza- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tional design can also be based on long term projections of economic and technological trends.

As an example from the IT domain, standards, technologies, and applications based on the Internet can significantly reduce the cost of providing a given level of enterprise-wide information access. It implies that a higher level of information access is now both technically and economically feasible. The opportunity for senior management lies not only in investing in an IT infrastructure which enhances access, but also in assessing parallel changes to the decision authority, control and incentive systems. Similarly, new competition may imply that the organization must increase its customer responsiveness to a level which was not previously considered necessary or optimal. Since technological, economic and competitive factors keep changing very frequently, this reevaluation of the business value model is likely to be an ongoing exercise.

5.6. Justifying a DSS for organizational design-related decisions

While we suggested the need for a DSS to support the process of managing a business value model, it is also evident that the proposed DSS is substantially complex, calling for managerial support that spans various levels and units across the organization, helps build consensus through suitable mechanisms, provides computational support and keeps track of external stimuli that govern the optimality of a given design configuration. It is important that such a DSS be justified based on its value 16 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

As enumerated in the previous subsections, a large number of computationally intensive steps are involved in making informed decisions about organizational design. Holsapple and Whinston 14 sug-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> gest three types of limits—cognitive, economic and time—which restrict the ability of management in making optimal decisions. Consider cognitive limits in the context of a business value model. In a seminal article, Miller 19 suggested that an individual can effectively handle seven pieces of knowledge at the same time. Not only do we expect more than seven variables in a well-developed business value model, one of the key elements is the complementary or coordinated nature of decisions that have to be made regarding the levels of the design variables. In other words, the variables cannot be treated in isolation; their levels have to be determined all at once. Developing a business value model involves eliciting information regarding key performance variables and building consensus among multiple decision makers. Given the potential divergence of objectives of different decision units across the organization, these activities place major demands on time and other resources available to senior management. To sum up, without the support of a DSS which integrates a diverse set of capabilities, the cognitive, economic and time constraints on senior management will be seriously challenged in building, maintaining and acting upon a complementarity-based business value model.

## 6. Conclusion

In a world of rapid economic changes and technological advances, senior managers have to assess the design of the organization on a continual basis to ensure that maximum net value is being realized for the current business environment. Organizational design has generally relied on popular prescriptions such as ‘fit’ and ‘radical change’ with mixed results. We suggested that these prescriptions do not provide an operational basis for making design decisions which contribute to maximum bottomline payoff. Instead, we proposed using BVC as a promising approach for systematizing a complex set of issues and decisions that management must deal with in evaluating organizational design. Business value modeling provides an understanding and assessment of which variables are critical to the overall objective s of the firm, while complementarity between Ž . the variables in a business value model forms the basis for choosing the levels of the design variables.

The idea of complementarity is not unique to organizational design, and pervades almost every aspect of society. Individuals make complementary choices in their day-to-day dealings, career decisions, and financial planning, to name just a few. Similarly, good management involves identifying complementary activities or design factors, and changing the levels of these factors in the right directions by the right magnitudes in response to stimuli in the business environment. Complementarity is particularly suitable for analyzing organizational design because the latter includes a large number of considerations, leading to the possibility of overlooking some critical factors and their interdependencies. BVC has the advantage of being applicable at the planning stage, before significant resources have been committed. It allows top management to visualize the synergy between the design variables of interest. Moreover, it links proposed design changes directly to the business value, and enables different managers to understand how their operations contribute to the overall organizational payoff.

The complexity of developing, maintaining and using a business value model calls for the help of a DSS which provides a variety of support features. We provided a high level overview of the functional capabilities of such a DSS. Future research in this area should focus on an in-depth investigation of the required features and their economic justification.
