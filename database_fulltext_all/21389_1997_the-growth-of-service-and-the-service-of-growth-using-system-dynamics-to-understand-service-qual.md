# The growth of service and the service of growth: Using system dynamics to understand service quality and capital allocation

Erik R. Larsen $^{a}$ , Ann van Ackere $^{b,*}$ , Kim Warren $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Universita Degli Studi Di Bologna, Departimento di Discipline Economico-Aziendali, Piazza Scaravilli 2, 40126, Bologna, Italy $^{b}$ London Business School, Sussex Place, London, NW1 4SA, UK

## Abstract

This paper discusses how system dynamics can help understand a service company's growth potential as well as its limitations. The model discussed here is being built for a European restaurant chain which grew from nothing to over 200 outlets in less than a decade. The model highlights two conflicting pressures: the need to spend on meeting customer expectations and hence build sales versus the need to meet profit targets from headquarters and thus win the capital to fund expansion. We use the model to study how management policies affect the achievable rate of growth. The issues discussed are relevant to any service based company facing the problem of maintaining and improving service quality against the pressure of performance expectations set by shareholders or corporate owners. We also briefly discuss the benefit of using such models for executive training. The model will be used as a basis for educating the emerging generation of managers who will have to cope with the tensions described in the model. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: System dynamics; Team decision making; Scenario; Executive training

## 1. Introduction

This paper discusses how system dynamics simulation can improve understanding of managerial issues. We illustrate how system dynamics can help understand a service company's growth potential and limitations by focusing on a European restaurant chain, which grew to 200 outlets in less than 10 years. Our model studies the impact of service quality and the budgeting process on the acceleration and slowing down of growth. The issues discussed are relevant to any service based company facing the problem of maintaining and improving service quality whilst at the same time having to manage investor expectations. The model will be used as a basis for educating the emerging generation of managers who will have to cope with the tensions described in the model.

Simulation is generally thought of as a tool for forecasting and prediction. This was indeed the case until the early 1980s, when simulation was primarily used to forecast a variety of complex systems, ranging from company cash flow to inflation. Many such models are still in use. However, over the last 10 years a new way of using simulation models has developed. Instead of focusing on predicting the future state of a system, simulation is used as a tool to help a team of managers understand the company's problems and opportunities, both current and future. This use of simulation models, often in direct collaboration with a management team, has been shown to greatly enhance the team's understanding of problems and opportunities [26].

The system dynamics approach was considered appropriate for a number of reasons. The system was known to contain, and be heavily influenced by critical ‘levels’ (number of restaurants, quality of menu, service and environment, and investment support), which were known to adjust slowly over time and to decay. Also dynamic feedback was known to occur, e.g. marketing expenditure drives customer numbers up, this drives crowding up, thus driving customer numbers down again. System dynamics is particularly suitable for capturing such level-changes and feedback in an intuitively simple manner. Another advantage of system dynamics is its ability to take ‘soft’ variables into account. Most system dynamics models incorporate a number of such variables. For instance, the model described here explicitly represents customers’ perception of restaurant crowding and service quality. Other models have focused on issues of morale, customer satisfaction, quality, etc.

System dynamics was founded over 30 years ago [9,29] and grew rapidly during the 1960s and early 1970s. This early growth was primarily driven by the application of system dynamics to the analysis of social and macro-economic problems [10,11] although the method was also used as a management tool [10,14]. During the last 10 years there has been a renewed interest in applying system dynamics to business policy and strategy problems. This interest has been driven by a number of factors. New, user friendly, high level graphical simulation programs (ithink, Powersim and Vensim $^{1}$ ) have been developed, enabling the decision maker (or the model user) to get a better understanding of the model. New popular books describe the use of the method and its results [28,30]. Finally, a process has been developed which allows the user of a model to get actively engaged in its development. This process has been used extensively over the last 10 years and has been shown to be very effective [28].

Applications can be found in a variety of industries, for example in health care [15], energy [7,24], utilities [3,4], chemicals [33], transport [14,33], telecommunications [22,23], bio-technology [25], financial services [6,31], and Business Process Re-engineering [34].

## 2. System dynamics

We briefly discuss the main ideas and concepts of the system dynamics approach. These are illustrated using simple examples, which turn out to be building blocks of the restaurant chain growth model discussed below. For a more complete overview of system dynamics, see $[8,28,30]$ .

To understand complex business situations, system dynamics uses causal loop diagrams to represent relationships between concepts. As these maps are developed, 'loops' are 'closed' to recreate the behaviour observed in the real system. Consider the relationship between customer base and customer satisfaction. A 'linear' way of thinking would state that as customer satisfaction increases, the customer base grows. A system dynamics view of the same problem would close the loop as in Fig. 1. This loop adds the key observation that the growth in the customer base subsequently leads to a decline in customer satisfaction.

An additional concept, waiting time, has been added. More customers implies longer waiting times, leading to lower customer satisfaction. The notation S and O in Fig. 1 is the system dynamics way of labelling the direction of a relationship between two concepts. If the link is labelled S, the two concepts 'move' in the same direction, i.e. if Customer Satisfaction goes up (down), then Customer Base goes up (down). If the link is labelled O the two concepts 'move' in opposite directions, i.e. if Waiting Time goes up Customer Satisfaction goes down. The double bar (\) on the connection between Waiting Time and Customer Satisfaction indicates a delay. Such a loop is called balancing or goal seeking. This is indicated by a B in the centre of the loop. The reason for this can be seen on the graph in Fig. 1 which shows the typical behaviour of a balancing loop. The delay causes the loop to overshoot and undershoot its goal before reaching the equilibrium state.

Fig. 1. Example of a balancing loop and characteristic behaviour.

A second type of loop is known as reinforcing. Consider for instance the relationship between the attractiveness of a restaurant, and its profitability, as shown in Fig. 2. Higher profitability enables management to invest more in maintenance. Spending on maintenance leads to a more attractive restaurant and therefore more customers, which in turn leads to higher profit. Whereas the loop in Fig. 1 (a balancing loop) had a goal seeking behaviour (the goal might be implicit or explicit) the reinforcing loop has no limitations. In isolation this loop exhibits exponential growth as shown in Fig. 2. This is indicated by an R in the centre of the loop. The number of customers keeps increasing and soon exceeds realistic numbers. When there is a reinforcing loop, therefore, there must be one or more balancing loops to prevent indefinite growth.

A number of structures which recur frequently in business situations are commonly known as 'archetypes' [30]. These archetypes are a combination of reinforcing and balancing loops. A common archetype, known as limits to growth, is shown in Fig. 3. This archetype combines the loops from Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 into a single structure. Initially the reinforcing loop 'drives' the development, yielding exponential growth as in Fig. 2. The rapidly growing customer base causes increasingly long waiting times. After a while customers realize this and become less satisfied. Eventually they start shopping around for other restaurants. As customers become less satisfied, the growth of the customer base slows down, resulting in stagnation or even decline.

## 3. The growth of the restaurant chain

The model developed here reflects the history of a real business – the Beefeater restaurant chain – which started around 1980 and grew to dominate the UK market for mid-priced restaurants over the subsequent decade. The first restaurants were developed from a few of the 6,000 pubs owned by the UK brewer Whitbread PLC. Attractive, suburban sites were substantially redeveloped, and equipped with some 60–100 seats and an open kitchen area, all within a radical and dramatic design theme. A simple, but interesting menu was offered, together with intensive and friendly service, and frequent redecoration and restyling of the restaurants occurred. Some sites were so busy that, on Friday and Saturday evenings, each seat might be 'sold' three times.

These first sites, being some of the best available, generated spectacular returns, and Whitbread allocated considerable resources to grow the chain. With this support, and access to more of the company's best sites, Beefeater grew rapidly to over 100 units. However, several pressures began to arise, both from the market and from within the company itself.

As the chain grew, the sites available to it were increasingly of lower quality than the early units, so that more effort had to go into product and service quality to maintain customer numbers. At the same time, the early units were deteriorating. The busier they were, the faster they deteriorated and the more maintenance was required. Meanwhile, consumers were beginning to take for granted the quality of food and service, so money was needed for developing the menu. This spend was also needed to extend the restaurants' appeal to new groups of customers.

All these pressures to spend money on supporting the business tended to hold back the chain's profitability and growth. Head office, accustomed to strong profits growth and good returns, continued to hold high expectations, which the chain's managers strived to meet. The temptation was to cut back on service staff, on restaurant maintenance and on menu development to meet budget targets and thus win the capital needed to drive further growth. Price increases were another possible means of boosting profits. These pressures were exacerbated by the 1985 downturn in consumer spending. However, by thorough discussion of the dilemma faced by the management team, a satisfactory balance was achieved between the performance expectations from head office and the investment in service quality, environment and product that was essential to longer-term success.

Having weathered the recession of the mid 1980s, the business was again in good shape, and growth resumed for the rest of the decade, to the point that the chain was able to eliminate its former major competitor, Berni. That firm was managed in a somewhat different manner, with high expectations and incentives for financial performance. Its managers cut back on staff, maintenance and menu support, and pushed prices upwards, so that customer satisfaction with their restaurants declined and sales volumes fell back. This further damaged financial returns, so that yet more cost reductions occurred, a vicious spiral from which there was no escape.

Although Beefeater recovered strongly and renewed its growth to over 200 units, the late 1980s also saw the upsurge of a second rival, Harvester. This chain benefited from access to good sites, and Beefeater was only just able to maintain a lead in reputation with customers. Harvester was able to grow to some 100 sites, and become a major threat. The year 1990 saw difficult market conditions return, along with the same conflict for both companies between financial performance requirements and business support. Once again, though, Beefeater retained sufficient investment for service, environment and product development to survive this difficult period. Harvester could not match this commitment, and was subsequently offered for sale.

The story graphically illustrates the conflicting pressures many managers face, on the one hand, to ensure they keep meeting the demands of customers even when market conditions are tough, and on the other hand to keep delivering profits for their investors. This pressure is similar, whether the ‘investors’ are true shareholders or corporate owners. A dynamic model of the trade-offs involved should, therefore, be most helpful, both to management researchers and to managers who find themselves in such situations.

## 4. Model development

The model was developed in conjunction with an experienced manager, utilising extensive internal research as to the critical influences. This included among others consumer research data (which included measurements of the relative importance of various aspects of quality), saturation data, known time-delays and scale of response to marketing activities, time and cost of menu enhancement, and information on the extent of pressure for improved financial performance from the parent company. The data was tested by comparing the scale and speed of change in key variables against reality. Some of this data is available in the Beefeater Restaurant Case series [35].

The components and relationships of the model are based on knowledge of the interactions in the real system. The choice of consumer concerns derived from research on the actual business. The selected factors were given equal weighting because of the relatively close known balance between these factors. These factors were measured by using consumers' views on how they actually feel. Assumptions on the delay in customer reaction to changes in quality factors are based on known consumer trial-frequency and validated against the speed of change in consumer perceptions.

The reaction of the parent company to short-fall in profits was bench-marked against the respective responses of management teams for the two rival businesses. For the parent company actually modelled, extensive information was known about such target-adjustments and capital allocation across a number of comparable businesses (pubs, other restaurant chains, hotels, etc.). The assumptions about the impact of a shortfall in profits on pricing decisions is based on actions considered and enacted, both by the company being modelled and some of its competitors, in response to pressure from headquarters when facing decreasing profitability.

Still, the model we build is a known simplification of reality. Issues not central to the model's purpose, although relevant to the overall dynamics, were left out. One example of this is the external environment, specifically the downturn in consumer spending and the performance of rivals. These factors are purposely omitted. The model shows that prospects are largely in the hands of the management - decisions and policies that seem perfectly sensible in isolation (a low-price position or heavy marketing or reacting to parent company pressure with cost-cutting) can lead to failure to grow and/or business failure. It is not necessary to postulate external pressures to produce such outcomes.

Ideally, we could have included exogenous factors such as the economic conditions, changes in consumer behaviour and competitor behaviour. The absence of these factors limits direct comparison with historical data. However, the model is intended to improve management understanding of issues under their direct control. Including these factors would risk making the model less useful as a learning tool, as participants would be able to blame poor performance on such external influences (which in practice are unknowable at any point in time), rather than on their own decisions.

## 5. The model

Our model focuses on two conflicting pressures:

\- Customer-perceived value: how is customer-perceived value managed and what effect might value have on the growth of the restaurant chain?

\- Allocation of capital for growth: how does the parent company decide on capital allocation, and how does the performance of the division in the previous year affect its allocation?

These two aspects of the model are discussed below. This is followed by a brief description of the full model.

## 5.1. Conflicting pressures

Customer-perceived value. Overall customer-perceived value is made up of four main components: service quality, restaurant standard, menu attractiveness and crowding.

Service quality. Are there sufficient staff to deliver high quality service? The number of staff is determined by the fraction of revenue allocated to labour cost. The quality is assessed against a benchmark of the number of staff required to serve a given number of customers. If the actual number of staff is higher, the service will be seen as effective, fast and polite. On the other hand, if the staffing is below this benchmark, the quality will suffer as a consequence of stress, slow service, etc.

Restaurant standard. The standard of the restaurants depends on the amount of money spent on maintenance, which is given as a fraction of revenue. However, this fraction depends on the performance of the division. If profitability is low, the management of the chain tends to cut back on maintenance. There is strong pressure to keep the return on capital employed (ROCE) at a level which will win enough capital from head-office to maintain growth. If the ROCE is high, management may decide to spend somewhat more to keep the restaurants attractive. How fast the restaurants decay depends on the number of customers: the larger the number of customers, the more maintenance will be required. Even with very few customers some maintenance is needed just to keep up with current fashion.

Menu attractiveness. Just like any other business, a restaurant chain needs to innovate, in this case by continuous investment in new menu items. For a small specialist restaurant this may be a straightforward matter – for restaurant chains this is an almost scientific exercise which involves identifying trends in customers' taste, developing new dishes within the constraints of the existing kitchens, testing the items in selected restaurants, etc. This is a costly and time consuming activity.

Crowding. Would you enter a restaurant you do not know, if it is empty on a Saturday evening? Or alternatively, wait a couple of hours for a table? These two effects are captured in the model: low levels of utilization and overcrowding both decrease the attractiveness of a restaurant.

The combination of these four factors yields the overall service quality experienced by the customer. A change in any one of these factors will affect the attractiveness of the restaurant and thus the customer's experience of eating there. However, such a change does not impact the customer's perception of quality instantly. It takes time for a customer to change his or her expectations. Consider walking into a restaurant where you usually receive good service. If you are unsatisfied once you may think they had a bad day, but after repeated unhappy experiences you will conclude that the service quality has declined permanently. It will then take a long time before you risk going back, even if you hear quality has improved. This way of forming expectations, and the delays involved, exist in most service industries (as well as in many manufacturing settings). The model assumes that the longest delay is associated with forming an opinion regarding the change in restaurant standard, while the three other factors have a shorter delay. Because of these delays a change in any one factor, for better or worse, will not be reflected instantly in the restaurant's revenue: it takes time for a customer to notice the improving or declining standards, and adapt his behaviour.

Finally, to determine whether customers actually visit the restaurants or not, the overall perceived quality is compared with the price level that management selects for the meals they offer to arrive at a measure of value. Relatively modest quality may be acceptable if prices are rather low, and exceptional quality may offer the chance to push price upwards. Inconsistency between the two, however, will cause trouble, resulting in either overcrowding (high quality and low prices) or empty restaurants (high prices and low quality).

## 5.2. Capital allocation

The second main theme of the model is capital allocation. Diversified companies often face strong internal competition for resources $[2]$ and one of the main functions of the corporate headquarters is to allocate capital among these competing divisions $[13]$ . In our model the amount of capital allocated to a division depends on the performance of that division in the previous year and on expectations of future growth. There are two criteria for capital allocation: absolute performance (i.e. return on capital employed, ROCE) and relative performance (i.e. actual performance versus expectations).

The allocation based on ROCE is a straight forward process: higher returns result in more capital allocated for the next year. To limit the impact of an exceptionally good or bad quarter, the headquarters consider the trend in ROCE over the last four quarters. Capital allocation is expressed as a fraction of the existing capital, to recognise that head office is unlikely to commit large sums of capital to a relatively small business. If performance is unsatisfactory head office may decide to take capital away. The model assumes a linear relationship between ROCE and the fractional increase in capital, ranging from no increase for an ROCE of 10% (or less) to a 20% increase for an ROCE of 30% (or more). Competitive effects are excluded from this relationship because of the site-specific nature of the market and the ‘mobility barrier’ provided by the choice of out-of-town locations, the only serious rival being locked into town-centres.

When profit performance exceeds expectations, the headquarters uprate the amount of capital provided for expansion. However, the expected return depends on how well the division has performed in the past. As the division improves its profitability, the headquarters' expectations creep up. Thus a performance which was considered satisfactory three years ago, may now be below expectations. In many diversified companies, such expectations are a critical issue for business unit managers. How does one keep presenting better and better results, without creating unrealistic expectations? This is one example of a commonly occurring structure, known as the eroding goals archetype.

## 5.3. The full model

Fig. 4 provides an overview of the main connections in the model. The full model is significantly more detailed. Value for money is a concept which compares Beefeater to customers' normal expectations. If the customer feels that the overall quality (as discussed above) is what one expects for a given meal price, then value for money equals 1. If the overall quality is good compared to the price of a meal, value for money exceeds 1 and more customers will be attracted to the restaurant. On the other hand, if the quality is not good enough for the price asked, value for money will be less than 1 and the customer base will decline. Revenue depends on the customer base per restaurant, the average meal price, and the number of restaurants.

When profits fall below expectations, management is under increased pressure to generate more profit. This can be achieved by cutting costs or increasing prices. In this version of the model we assume that the management will react to a shortfall in profit by pushing up the meal price. This process involves a number of delays: first, the time taken to realize that there is a shortfall in profits, second the time taken to decide to increase prices and finally the time needed to implement the change. This can be done by increasing the price of drinks or by changing the menu to slightly smaller portions etc. Similarly, when there is a higher than expected profit, the pressure on prices tends to lift, and prices will fall.

The quality level that restaurants can afford to provide depends on revenue, since a fraction of revenues is used to fund staffing, to maintain the restaurants and to improve the menu. These fractions depend on a number of factors such as the customer base, the need to improve ROCE, etc.

## 6. The base case

Using a set of parameters which are broadly in line with conditions faced by the real business we are now ready to simulate the model. It is important to re-emphasise that we are not aiming at a statistically precise reproduction of the real case. The model should not be judged on the basis of measures such as $R^{2}$ , but on criteria such as the model's structure and dynamic behaviour over time:

\- Is the structure of the causal links in the model a fair representation of the structure in the ‘real’ case?

\- Is the dynamic behaviour produced by the model comparable to the behaviour in the real case? Are the numbers within a reasonable range of those in the real case? Are the patterns through time similar?

The model structure was developed in a modular fashion, each module being validated and tested against real conditions before being integrated with the others. For example, we started by building the model of consumer response to quality variables and price for a single restaurant. We checked that the model behaved correctly in terms of customer numbers won or lost as these variables changed. We then added the model structure representing the sum of all the restaurants to form a chain, including data on central costs, and representation of market saturation and the corresponding escalation of site acquisition cost. Finally, behaviour of head office regarding targets and capital was modelled and compared with known managerial behaviour.

Fig. 5 shows simulated and actual data for the growth of the restaurant chain over the period 1976–1986, indicating an acceptable fit. Data beyond 1986 (240 restaurants in 1991) is not relevant for our purpose, as further growth was affected by a poor economic condition and the take-over of a competitor. Neither of these factors are included in this model.

For a thorough review of how to evaluate this type of models, see $[8]$ . This does not exclude statistical tests, but for our purposes, as described in the section on the future use of the model, we are not concerned with the fit of our model. If it is necessary to fit a set of empirical data precisely, a much larger and more detailed model is required.

Next, we highlight a few results from the model. Fig. 5 shows the growth of the restaurant chain, ending up with around 170 outlets after 10 years. We observe a classic logistic growth pattern over this period. The model starts with 3 restaurants and grows slowly over the first 5 years to about 35 outlets. A major expansion takes place during the next 3 to 4 years. As the chain expands, the cost of sites goes up. At the same time it is assumed that the most attractive sites, i.e. the sites with the best location, potential customers and price, are bought first. This means that sites for new restaurants become less and less attractive. This is the main reason for the declining growth rate: the price for new restaurants goes up from £500,000 to £1,000,000. The maximum growth rate is reached around 1983, when the chain is growing at a rate of up to 8 new restaurants per quarter.

The growth rate can be seen more clearly in Fig. 6, which shows the amount of capital allocated to the division for expansion. It takes quite a long time before substantial amounts of capital are allocated on a quarterly basis. Because of this, the reinforcing process starts relatively slowly and it is not until quarter 18 that we see a sharp increase in the amount of capital allocated. We then see how fast reinforcing processes work (both ways!). The next 12 quarters show an increase of 800%. This is followed by an even more dramatic decline over the next ten quarters. The decline is due to increasing restaurant costs and service quality problems, especially crowding, i.e the restaurants are too popular. Up to the peak in capital allocation, profits fairly consistently exceed the target profit set by the parent company. However, as soon as the actual profit falls significantly behind the target profit, the capital allocation is cut back (see Fig. 7).

## 7. Scenarios

The previous section described the behaviour of the model in the base case. It is now possible to use the model to ask ‘what-if’ questions to help understand the consequences of various actions. This is one of the key uses of this type of models: a means to challenge one’s thinking and check that one’s expectations about what should happen are in line with what is actually happening in the model. A simulation model of this type is nothing more than a collection of assumptions agreed by the model-building team. If the outcome differs from expectations, this can only be for one of two reasons (assuming that there are no ‘technical’ errors in the model):

\- The expectations were wrong. This can be checked and understood by going back and following the causal links that have lead to the unexpected outcome. This way the user will improve his or her understanding of the ‘system’, i.e. get a better understanding of the ‘reality’ the model aims to represent. This should lead to better decisions being made.

\- The expectations were actually correct, and the model's results are wrong. However, as the model reflects the users' assumptions, this indicates a need to re-examine these assumptions (e.g. the way customers react to changes in service quality). In this case the users learn by having to revise their assumptions – again leading to improved decision making.

Next, we describe two scenarios which illustrate how the model can be used to analyze 'what-if' questions. The first scenario relates to the decision on how much to adapt the meal price as a result of the pressure from profit. As mentioned before, a shortfall in profit leads to pressure to increase the average meal price to make up for this shortfall. On the other hand, if profit is larger than expected, the pressure on prices eases off and the price will fall slightly.

Fig. 10 illustrates two policies: no adjustment to price and faster adjustment. In the first case we assume that management cannot change the meal price (full line), i.e. there is no feedback between profit and meal price. This has a dramatic effect on the rate of growth: instead of the 180 restaurants of the base case, we only achieve a peak of about 35 restaurants after 6 years, followed by a decline. How can this be explained? Consider the reinforcing loop linking profit and capital allocation. The growth never takes off as the price of an average meal is not increased to keep up revenues, which results in low levels of profit. As profits are below the expected level, allocation of new capital stops after an initial grace period.

The second policy on Fig. 10 (crosses) shows a simulation where the adjustment of the meal price is faster than in the base case. The results are even more disastrous, as we end up with a peak of barely 18 restaurants, i.e. half the number reached in the ‘fixed meal price’ scenario, and a downward trend. Again studying the feedback structure helps us understand the cause. The rapid increase in meal price keeps customers away: the meal price more than doubles over the time period, driving down the value for money. This example illustrates that fast adjustment can in certain circumstances make things worse than no or slow adjustment, a phenomenon observed in many system dynamics models. Although initially teams find such results counter-intuitive, further analysis usually enables them to understand why this is the case.

The second scenario relates to under-staffing, a situation often encountered in service businesses: the 'we can get people to work a little harder' syndrome. This has quite dramatic consequences. In the base case, 19% of revenue is allocated to staff, and there is a minimum staff of 17, i.e. whatever the revenue, there will always be at least 17 people employed (this includes cooks, waitresses, cleaners, etc). ## 8. Using models as teaching tools

Over the years, simulation has increasingly been used as an engine for games. There is a large literature on game design, and the impact of using games for team learning. For an overview, see for instance $[16,21,5]$ . These issues have also attracted increased attention in the systems dynamics area, where the central questions raised have been: (1) how can such models be used as more than just a tool to help a small group of people, who might have been involved in the development of the model, and (2) how can one disseminate and get commitment to the insights discovered through the use of such models? System dynamics has struggled with these issues for a number of years. The first breakthrough came in the late 1980s when it was realised that simulation models turned into management games could provide a useful tool for creating understanding and commitment in organisations $[24,31,33]$ . These games, often referred to as ‘management flight simulators’, have now been used in a number of organisations and have proved to be reasonably effective as well as entertaining for the participants. They are also being used increasingly in business schools and for executive training.

The effectiveness of these games is still an open question. Emerging evidence suggests that they may both increase learning and speed up the learning process $[19]$ . Further research is required to confirm these findings, and to gain insight into how the learning from a game can be transferred to the real world $[1,17,18]$ .

Some guidelines regarding the learning objectives and use of games have gained acceptance in the system dynamics community, setting these games apart from other simulation based games. Specifically, system dynamics based games tend to have narrow, clearly defined learning objectives $[17]$ . They also tend to be combined with the case method. This approach leads to a new and more comprehensive way of teaching, which increases student involvement. First students go through the standard discussion phase, and come up with a proposed strategy. They then move on to the implementation phase, where the gaming simulator allows to find out the results of their chosen strategy $[12]$ . Observing the consequences of their decisions and experimenting with alternative options leads to a deeper understanding.

To accompany the Beefeater Restaurants model, we have produced a teaching package which contains a conventional case study, describing the development of the restaurant chain and the issues the growth raised $[35]$ . This conventional case series is supported with an interactive, computer-based game $[36]$ where the participants have to manage the growth of the restaurant chain on a quarter by quarter basis, setting the price of meals, number of staff, amount spent on maintenance, etc.

This interactive game is a useful tool to enhance team learning and team decision making. The model can be used to illustrate that ‘local’ decision-making, in which for instance marketing or staffing decisions are made in isolation from other considerations, is disastrous. This implies that team-negotiation of co-ordinated decisions is critical (e.g. don't do marketing if perceived quality is poor, or if the restaurants are over-crowded). (See for instance [27] for a discussion of this issue in the context of business structure analysis.)

Giving a group of people from different parts of the business the opportunity to run the model as a team enables them to experience these interactions the hard way (by failing to let the business grow), but without having to face the real-life consequences of their actions. This experience becomes a constructive starting point for discussion on how to cope with these interactions, discussion which differs somewhat in the case depending on whether the participants are managers from the business itself (or from closely similar businesses), or alternatively come from completely different backgrounds.

In the first case, key learning concerns the policy choices made by teams in trying to manage the system, and the interdependency between those policies. Further debate focuses on how users' mental models of the business compare with the actual model in the simulator. (For this purpose we 'open the box' and show users the model, at least in outline). Such users can also be asked to consider those issues that the model does not contain, (e.g. staff morale or economics cycles) and debate what difference such factors might make.

Where users come from a different business background, the debriefing needs to include a debate on the parallels and differences between the model and their own business. For example, most businesses need to keep up a good level of customer-perceived value, and most business unit managers know about the problem of delivering expected performance without creating unrealistic expectations of future performance.

## 9. Conclusion

We have illustrated how system dynamics can be used to help understand the growth of a service company. Starting from a map of the inter-connections between a number of factors that relate customer-perceived quality and capital allocation to the growth in restaurant numbers, we developed a simulation model. This model enabled us to reproduce a growth pattern not unlike the one experienced by the real restaurant chain over a 10 year period. The model includes examples of how soft variables, such as service quality, crowding and value for money, can be included in what many perceive as hard models. We illustrated how the model can be used for 'what-if' analysis. Specifically, we discussed the impact on growth of changing prices faster or slower, and making relatively small cuts in resources allocated to staffing. This gave a feel for how the model can be used to explore the impact of important policy variables.

This type of models has a number of advantages, as well as some disadvantages, one should be aware of. We first discuss two of the advantages:

\- Being involved in a model building exercise like this is an engaging activity for a management team. Although it is time-consuming, there is usually general agreement that the return on this time investment is high. As pointed out above, the model building process itself is of significant value. When the management team itself is closely involved in the process, the model can be build relatively fast [20], which helps keep up the interest of the team.

\- The models include soft variables, which are often as important – if not more so – as the hard variables one is used to see in more traditional models (e.g. spreadsheets). We may not be able to get as precise a definition of a soft variable as of a typical financial variable (e.g. cash-flow). However, managers are usually able to describe what factors influence a soft variable, such as morale. Given this verbal description it is mostly possible to include the variable in the model. This is typically done by describing the variable, e.g. morale, as ranging on a scale from 0 to 1, 0 being low morale and 1 a very high morale. Being unable to measure something directly is not a good reason to leave it out. The ‘error’ resulting from leaving out such variables is much larger than the ‘error’ arising from having no precise measurement of them in the model, as this type of variables is often critical to the understanding of a given problem.

The system dynamics modelling approach also has a number of limitations:.

\- The resulting models do not produce forecasts. They are built to improve understanding and learning. Teams tend to focus on the exact numbers resulting from the simulation, rather than on the general behaviour of the model. Most people have been trained to focus on the exact number (as for instance in the use of spreadsheets). To fully benefit from this approach, one must distance oneself from the numbers and focus on the overall picture.

\- This modelling approach is not suited to all situations. A number of conditions must be fulfilled before one can use system dynamics:

1. the problem must have a time dimension, i.e. we are looking at some development over time (e.g. the evolution of the number of restaurants, and the associated variations in the overall level of quality resulting from difficulty in managing growth);

2. system dynamics is most suitable for analysis of dynamic complexity as opposed to detailed complexity, for which other approaches are more suitable (see [30] for further details). For instance, rather than trace every single staff-member individually, staff is treated as a group of x people, and we can see how this aggregate figure x changes over time. Value is obtained from the aggregate view, ignoring information on discrete items. It is not necessary or particularly useful to model individual staff, restaurants or menu items.

Based on the model described in this paper, a management game $[36]$ has been developed for use in education and training of managers in the service industry. We believe that such tools have the potential to increase the rate of learning in management education.
