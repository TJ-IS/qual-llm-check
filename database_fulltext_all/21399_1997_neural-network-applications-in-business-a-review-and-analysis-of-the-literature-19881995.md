# Neural network applications in business: A review and analysis of the literature (1988–95)

Bo K. Wong $^{a,*}$ , Thomas A. Bodnovich $^{b,c}$ , Yakup Selvi $^{d}$

$^{a}$ Department of Finance and Decision Sciences, School of Business, Hong Kong Baptist University, Kowloon Tong, Hong Kong $^{b}$ Computer and Information Sciences Department, Youngstown State University, Youngstown, OH 44555, USA $^{c}$ Administrative Sciences Department, Kent State University, Kent, OH, USA $^{d}$ Department of Accounting, Faculty of Business, Istanbul University, Avcilar-Istanbul 80850, Turkey

Accepted 2 October 1996

## Abstract

A survey of journal articles on neural network business applications published between 1988 and 1995 indicates that an increasing amount of neural network research is being conducted for a diverse range of business activities. The classification of literature by (1) year of publication, (2) application area, (3) problem domain, (4) decision process phase, (5) level of management, (6) level of task interdependence, (7) means of development, (8) corporate/academic interaction in development, (9) technology integration, (10) comparative study, (11) major contribution, and (12) journal provides some insights into the trends in neural networks research. The implications for neural networks developers/researchers and suggestions on future research areas are discussed. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Neural networks; Research

## 1. Introduction

In the last decade, after almost 20 years of stagnation, great strides have been made in neural network technology [108]. This breakthrough has not only led to increasing research on a wide variety of scientific applications, but has also contributed to the development of many different types of business applications. As a result, an increasing amount of published research has appeared, a considerable proportion of which focuses on actual neural network development for business applications. The goals of this survey were to examine the historical trends in published business neural network application research, and to explore the potential neural network research areas in the future. It is hoped that the findings of this study can highlight the importance of neural networks in business and provide insight into current neural network research for both academics and practitioners.

## 2. Research methodology

To identify those journal articles which describe specific neural network applications, an extensive search of the literature was conducted. The scope of the bibliography and the criteria used in the selection of articles are discussed in Appendix A.

## 3. Classifications

A final total of 203 articles or 213 applications were considered to be acceptable for the purposes of this study. As each article was reviewed, it was classified according to 12 categories:

1. Year of publication.

2. Application area (accounting/auditing, finance, human resources, information systems, marketing/distribution, production/operations, or others).

3. Problem domain (structured or semi/unstructured).

4. Decision process phase (intelligence, design, or choice).

5. Level of management (operational control, management control, or strategic planning).

6. Level of task interdependence (personal support, group support, or organizational support).

7. Means of development (programming languages or neural network tools).

8. Corporate/academic interaction in development (independent effort or corporate/academic joint efforts).

9. Technology integration.

10. Comparative study.

11. Major contribution.

12. Journal.

## 3.1. Classification by year of publication and area of application

Each application area is a distinct functional business area for which the neural network was designed.

As shown in Table 1, neural network business research is increasing. We found no business applications published earlier than 1988. Furthermore, major growth occurred in the 1990s.

Taken together, the areas of production/operations and finance represent a majority of the total number of applications published (168 applications, 78.9%). The most heavily researched area is production/operations (114 applications, 53.5%), while the least is human resources.

Table 2 shows the research emphasis within each area of application; e.g. in production/operations, the emphasis has been on developing applications for part family/machine grouping and process control. This is not surprising, since neural networks are found to be more consistent than human judgment in classification and coding and can deal with large part family formation problems/machine grouping more efficiently [97]. Some of the process control tasks that are traditionally reserved for humans can be better performed by neural networks [75], especially those difficult, complex, and nonlinear applications [33].

In the area of finance, relatively more articles on neural network applications have been published in the fields of stock performance/selection prediction and bankruptcy prediction of businesses. Many studies seem to suggest that when it comes to analyzing time-series data, neural networks may perform better than other sophisticated statistical techniques because they are capable of identifying and simulating nonlinear relationships in the data set, with no requirements of multivariate normal distribution or prior probability specification.

## 3.2. Classification by problem domain

Managerial and other business decisions can generally be categorized as being either structured or semi/unstructured. About 83.1% (177) of the applications were designed for semi/unstructured decision analysis, while the rest were designed to address structured problems. Many business decisions are semi/unstructured, partially due to a certain degree of uncertainty in the knowledge. According to Giarratano and Riley [71], the sources of error that create uncertainty include ambiguity, incompleteness, incorrectness, measurement, systematic errors, random errors, and reasoning. Feedforward networks are especially good at addressing some of these problems [177]. Also, techniques such as fuzzy logic and genetic algorithms are incorporated into some neural networks and have greatly enhanced their ability to deal with relatively unstructured problems.

## 3.3. Classification by decision process phase

The neural network applications were also classified by three generic phases in the decision-making process. Approximately 63.9% of the applications (136) have been developed for intelligence, 5.2% (11 applications) for design, and 30.9% (66 applications) for choice.

Our survey results support the notion that most neural network applications have been developed for the intelligence phase (problem identification) of the decision process $[177]$ . A good example is the use of neural networks to diagnose machine faults, or potential malfunctions in a machine. In the area of finance, a neural network has been developed to predict those firms/banks/thrifts that could go bankrupt, so that those institutions with potential financial troubles can be identified.

It is not surprising to find that there are few applications in the design phase, since a neural network is not an appropriate tool for identifying alternative solutions. In the choice phase, neural networks can be used to address some combinatorial optimization problems. Typical applications include job shop scheduling, flow time minimization, and optimization of machine operation.

## 3.4. Classification by level of management

We further classified the applications by year according to their level of management activities, as shown in Table 3. Approximately 46.95% of the applications (100) were for operational control and 46.95% (100 applications) were developed for management control, while only 6.1% (13 applications) were for strategic planning.

It is not surprising to find that more applications are for the management control level, since neural networks are typically designed to optimize the use of capital, people, information, and other corporate resources $[177]$ . The capability of neural networks for distinct classification allows them to support decision making at the operational control level, especially in part family/machine grouping in the production/operations area. Yet, there are only eight applications in strategic planning: commercial loan application analysis $[139]$ , energy forecasting $[219]$ , financial distress forecasting $[38]$ , market response functions determination $[81]$ , plant location classification $[11,67]$ , real estate appraisal $[12]$ , and stock performance/selection prediction $[223]$ .

## 3.5. Classification by level of task interdependence

The neural network applications were also classified in terms of the types of decision making for which they were designed. These can be represented by three levels of increasing organizational task interdependency, as suggested by Thompson [200]. The first occurs on the personal level, which includes neural network applications designed to support a single user or a single class of users in a task that is distinct and relatively independent of other tasks. A second is seen at the group level. This focuses on applications designed to support groups that are engaged in separate yet highly interrelated tasks. Finally, some decision making exists at the organization level, with diverse operations and individuals.

Of the 213 applications reviewed, 61.5% (131 applications) involve personal decision making. About 29.1% (62 applications) are group decision making, while only 9.4% (20 applications) are designed for organizational decision making.

The more common examples of personal support are process control and stock performance/selection prediction. Part family/machine grouping and job shop scheduling are the typical examples of group support. The 11 applications in organizational support are bankruptcy prediction of banks/thrifts [175,197–199], bankruptcy prediction of firms [207], energy forecasting [219], managerial problem diagnosis [95], plant location classification [11], market response functions determination [81], product purchase frequency prediction [60], and salespeople hiring [221].

## 3.6. Classification by means of development

In general, neural networks can be developed either through the use of (1) programming languages such as C or Pascal, or (2) neural network tools such as NeuralWorks Professional II/Plus or NeuroShell. Out of 104 applications for which the means of development were reported, 52.9% (55 applications) were developed using neural network tools, 41.3% (43 applications) with various programming languages, and 5.8% (6 applications) were developed with both tools and languages.

Table 4 shows the distribution of articles on neural network applications by the means of development and year. There appears to be no pronounced increase in the use of one means of development over another.

Neural networks have been developed by academic institutions, business-related institutions, or both with joint efforts. As shown in Table 5, the distribution by the means of development is classified by the type of development institution. The results seem to indicate no preference for academic institutions choosing the means of neural network development. ## 3.7. Corporate–academic interactions in neural network development

During the period covered, 203 articles (213 applications) on neural network business applications were published. These articles were written by a total of 376 authors, of whom approximately 83% (312 authors) were affiliated with different academic institutions and 15.7% (59 authors) with nonacademic or business-related institutions. Five authors did not report their affiliations. Approximately 67.3% (253 authors) were affiliated with U.S. institutions, and 31.4% (118 authors) with foreign institutions.

Our survey found that a vast majority of the published articles on neural networks (201 applications, or 94.4%) were the result of the research efforts of either academicians or practitioners. It is surprising that only 4.2% (9 applications) were the products of joint efforts. Three applications could not be classified because insufficient information was provided in the articles.

Joint research efforts can provide a “middle ground” for neural network applications development; projects that historically may not have been considered “sophisticated” enough for academic research can still be important to organizations. Hence, corporate/academic joint efforts could help produce more “real world” applications. For example, the University of Maryland and Westinghouse Electric Corporation developed a high-reliability, low-cost quality assurance neural network system that was capable of predicting the quality of wire bonds $[217]$ .

By combining resources and uniting academic expertise with corporate knowledge, joint efforts could reduce the costs of development and increase the chance of success of a project. Also, since it can be assumed that the organization has a better understanding of its own functional interdependencies than any outside researcher, joint efforts may also help researchers develop organizational level neural networks. It seems that these advantages have not yet been realized.

## 3.8. Classification by technology integration

The integration of neural networks with other technologies, such as expert systems, robotics, or decision support systems, can improve the applicability of neural networks in addressing various types of problems. Our survey found that 16.4% (35 applications) of the published research describe the development of neural networks that are integrated with one or more other technologies. Some of these include neural networks that are integrated with expert systems [26,28,46,50,53,56,95,125,139,151] [154,164,167,169,180,182,230], robotics [5,90,118]; a robot controller [75]; a decision support system [69]; CAD [59,127]; and a simulation system [136]. Neural networks are integrated with more than one technology in Refs. [83,133,223].

## 3.9. Classification by comparative study

The performance of neural networks in business applications is always an important research issue. Our survey found that 82 applications (38.5%) compared the performance of neural networks against a total of 85 different conventional techniques. As indicated in Table 6, discriminant analysis, regression analysis, logit, and ID3 were the most common techniques. This finding is not surprising, since neural networks are good at forecasting and classification. Typical examples include bankruptcy prediction of banks/thrifts/firms, computer vision inspection, and consumer market segments identification.

A variety of major contributions are mentioned in our surveyed articles, as indicated in Table 7. ## 4. Implications and suggestions for neural network researchers/developers

Our survey reveals that few neural networks have been developed for supporting strategic planning or the design phase of decision making. Developers should be extremely careful when developing systems for these types of decisions. Two inherent limitations in neural networks jeopardize their use in strategic planning [177]: (1) neural learning algorithms are inductive, requiring masses of data and repetitive examples, whereas strategic decision making deals with unique and nonroutine types of decisions; and (2) neural networks do not provide explanations for their decisions. Neural networks are not considered an appropriate tool for the design phase either, since they are not good in constructing or evaluating solutions.

It is surprising to find that only one application integrates a neural network with a decision support system. The possible explanation for this is that the development of business neural networks is still in its embryonic stage, as is reflected by the fact that most applications have been developed by academics. On the other hand, our survey has found that most neural networks have been developed to support the intelligence phase of decision making, whereas most decision support system tools are used to support the design and choice phases; such integration will therefore mean that each phase will supplement the other. It is the authors' belief that many more integrated decision support system/neural network systems will be developed in the near future.

The integration of neural networks with other technologies/techniques also encourages researchers and developers to build new frameworks/architectures for different business applications. The contributions of future research should not only focus on exploring the potential usage of neural networks, or on proving their overall success in applications. Instead, researchers and developers should conduct more research on new application frameworks/architectures in order to gain deeper insight into the applications, to improve the quality of decision making, and to achieve financial/economic improvements. Such benefits can be achieved, especially when they are actually involved in the development of real applications.

Since the results of studies of the performance of neural networks in comparison with other techniques are often conflicting, future research should be conducted to explore the possible explanations for this. Some suggested research areas are as follows: (1) to evaluate the performance of neural network using different paradigms, architectures, and training schemes, (2) to understand how the quality and quantity of the training data can affect the performance of neural networks, (3) to investigate how the nature of the data and the domain of application affect the performance of neural networks in comparison with other techniques, (4) to develop general guidelines for the design of neural network structures, (5) to develop an explicit set of rules to determine whether a given learning algorithm is suitable for a particular application or not, and (6) to reduce the possibility of convergence to local minima using genetic algorithm or stochastic approach. Although some work has been done in some of these areas, as pointed out in Refs. [91,199], the research findings are still inconclusive.

A majority of the neural network applications have been developed by academics. This implies that most companies are still hesitant to use this state-of-the-art technology in business. We believe that companies should explore the potential applications of neural networks and consider the option of joint efforts with academics as a means of developing business neural networks. Our survey has found only a few such cooperative efforts, some of which have already indicated the benefits to developers through the efficient integration of academic and corporate resources. Other forms of joint effort, such as company-to-company research and investment in AI start-up companies, could also be considered.

## 5. Conclusions

The future of neural networks should see increased integration with other existing or developing technologies. As advancements are made in AI technologies and computer-based related systems, there should be new opportunities to apply neural network technology for business research.

## 6. Limitations of the study

Readers should be cautious in interpreting the results of this survey, since the findings are based on data collected only from journal articles. The results therefore do not include all actual neural network applications. Second, due to the lengthy journal review process, the neural networks reported in our surveyed articles are likely to lag behind the actual adoption of neural networks in the real world. Third, we have reviewed academic/professional journal articles only. Conference proceedings and doctoral dissertations are excluded, as we assume that high-quality research is eventually published in academic/professional journals. Also, many foreign journals were not included in our review since they were not within the scope of our computer/manual search.

## Appendix A

## A.1. Scope of the bibliography

The first and most important step in the literature retrieval process was a search of the ABI/INFORM database. The minicomputer-based version of this database was searched for the period 1971–95. This step provided access to abstracts of articles from over 800 different business-related journals worldwide. By using the descriptors “neural network” and “neural networks,” we were able to retrieve over 650 abstracts for review from the specified 25-year period.

Next, we conducted a manual search of the Business Periodical Index (BPI) for the period January 1980 through December 1995. The BPI indexes about 340 business-related periodicals. Only articles from journals not included in the ABI/INFORM database were considered for further review.

The next step involved a reference search of various textbooks on neural networks and related topics. The 21 textbooks examined were: Beale and Jackson [9], Boullart et al. [13], Caudill and Butler [23], Dagli [51], Fausett [63], Gallant [68], Gelenbe [70], Hecht-Nielsen [78], Khanna [104], Kosko [111], Lisboa [131], Lisboa and Taylor [132], Murre [147], Nelson and Illingworth [150], Simpson [181], Soucek [188], Trippi and Turban [203], Turban [205], Wang and Takefuji [216], Wasserman and Oetzel [218], and Zahedi [232]. These books were not very useful for our research since most refer only to scientific applications.

The textbook search, together with the ABI/INFORM and BPI searches, provided articles from a wide variety of journals. However, it was decided that an additional 15 journals should be searched, for two reasons: (1) several journals known to publish neural network articles were either only partially included or not included in the ABI/INFORM database or the BPI, and (2) some of these additional journals are recognized by management information system (MIS) experts as being important to the field of MIS [72,76]. These 15 journals were: ACM DATABASE, AI Expert, Artificial Intelligence, Communications of the ACM, Expert Systems: International Journal of Knowledge Engineering and Neural Networks, IEEE Expert, IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics, Information Systems Research, International Journal of Man–Machine Studies, International Journal of Production Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Manufacturing Systems, and Omega: The International Journal of Management Science.

## A.2. Selection criteria

Every article retrieved through the process described above was carefully reviewed before making a decision regarding its inclusion in this survey. We required each article to discuss the prototype or development of a neural network application for business. This requirement eliminated many of the articles retrieved from the ABI/INFORM database, since the descriptors used produced abstracts from numerous articles that did not describe business applications. In addition, we examined the research methodology of each article retrieved. Only those articles based on rigorous research methods were included.
