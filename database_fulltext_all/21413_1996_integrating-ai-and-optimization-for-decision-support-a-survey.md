# Integrating AI and optimization for decision support: A survey

Amitava Dutta \*

College of Business, George Mason University, 4400 University Drive, Fairfax, VA 22030-4444, USA

Received 15 January 1995; revised 15 June 1995; accepted 17 November 1995

## Abstract

Optimization models have been the workhorses of computer based decision support. Their emphasis is on model structure, quantification and solution efficiency. However, there also are important meta-modeling, analysis and interpretation activities associated with practical decision making. AI methods, because of their aim of emulating human reasoning and thinking activities, have the potential of providing computer based support for these other decision making activities. There has been a growing literature on the integration of AI and optimization techniques for decision support. Much of this body of work describes techniques that are application or problem specific. Others describe more general methods addressing different specific aspects of decision making. In this paper we use a conceptual framework to survey and analyze these efforts. Our survey shows that efforts to integrate AI and Optimization have been focused mainly on model formulation and selection. Other activities such as post solution analysis or solver selection, have received considerably less attention for automated support. The dramatic difference in paradigms between AI and Optimization result in vastly different data structures and control primitives in their respective software implementations. We conjecture that this disparity will continue to thwart the development of general tools for seamless AI/Optimization integration.

Keywords: Optimization; Frames; Rules; Reasoning; Decision support

## 1. Introduction

The area of decision support concerns itself with computer based structures and procedures that augment human decision making capabilities. Mathematical models, particularly optimization models, have been, and continue to be, the workhorses for decision support. The interest in integration of AI and Optimization, for decision support, results primarily from a recognition of the weaknesses of pure optimization and the perceived potential for AI to compensate for the same. Optimization models have well defined structural forms. The compactness of the models and efficiency of solution methods facilitates decision making by allowing computing power to be used to systematically explore a multitude of alternatives and find the best one. The emphasis in optimization has been on developing efficient solution methods. However, it has always been clear that there are important “meta modeling” issues in decision making. For example, decision problems need to be identified, formulated and “solved”. Solutions need to be analyzed and tested for applicability, and perhaps modified prior to use. These meta-modeling activities require human expertise and call for the use of expert heuristic knowledge. Thus it can be readily seen that pure optimization methods support one important but confined phase of decision making. On the other hand, many AI methods are developed with the aim of emulating human reasoning activities. It would seem natural to try and employ AI methods to support some of the meta-modeling activities associated with decision making. The difficulty, of course, lies in integrating the AI and Optimization components to provide seamless decision support. A secondary reason for the increased interest in AI/OPT integration is the dramatic improvement in price/performance characteristics of desktops. AI applications are typically resource intensive, and the required memory/processing capabilities have only recently become economical. Further, many low cost and user friendly AI programming environments have appeared ([34], p. 478), making it easier to experiment with integration methods.

There are many problem specific examples of integration, and we use three of them to highlight the benefits as well as difficulties. The K-FOLIO system [22] was developed to assist with investment management. It integrates the Markowitz quadratic programming risk-return optimization model with an investment expert's personal preferences within a frame based expert system. Within this system, the investment manager's knowledge and meta knowledge supporting investment decisions can be captured to be used along with results from the optimization model. The benefit from integration is a higher and more consistent level of support for the general investment manager. A Florida electric utility was able to cut its Nitrous Oxide emissions in half while still increasing generation efficiency by 2%, all by fine tuning its processes [31]. This was achieved by adding AI Ware's CAD/Chem software system to the existing plant control system. The software combines several AI paradigms with conventional statistical techniques. A distinction of this system compared to the previous one is its real time nature. In [16] a scheduling system called MacMerl is described. The design of MacMerl is based on an analysis of a human expert's approach to scheduling, and it is augmented with various scheduling algorithms. Using this tool, in which the human and machine cooperates, schedules that once took hours to generate now take seconds. While there are many other success stories of AI/OPT integration, it is worthwhile to note that the integration methods are usually problem specific. In other words, the way in which frames, rules or other AI representations are combined with linear programming, quadratic programming or statistical models, depend on the specific problem instance. However, for more widespread utilization of AI/OPT integration, it is necessary to have software environments that contain general tools, procedures and data/knowledge structures to support a range of integration issues. Otherwise one is reduced to a case by case implementation which is expensive, time consuming and error prone. In this paper we survey recent research on AI/OPT integration to examine the scope and generality of various solutions that have been proposed.

In Section 2, we begin with a summary of the relative strengths and weaknesses of optimization and AI methods so as to suggest where the greatest benefits from integration may lie. Section 3 describes a conceptual framework within which integration efforts are examined. The framework identifies characteristics of different activities associated with decision making, and highlights those which have been the major targets of research on integration. As a byproduct, decision making activities which could benefit from AI/OPT integration, but which have not yet received much attention, can also be revealed. Some of the technical and conceptual barriers to integration are mentioned in Section 4. Section 5 concludes with a summary of suggestions for general integrated modeling environments and a few conjectures about future developments in this area.

## 2. Comparison of AI and Optimization

To set the stage for an examination of AI/OPT integration issues, it is instructive to summarize their individual strengths and weaknesses. Used in conjunction with the conceptual framework of Section 3, this will help to identify specific phases of decision making which might most benefit from integration.

## 2.1. Optimization

Traditional optimization models have well established structural forms. These models have the following components.

A set of decision variables.

A set of constraints.

One or more objective function(s).

They differ in the nature of decision variables, the structure of the constraint set, and the type of objective function(s). Solution methods consist of different algorithms for searching the feasible space implied by the constraint set. In one form or another, they all attempt to take advantage of some aspect of the shape of the search space. For example, the simplex method for linear programming examines corner points of the feasible region since an optimal solution must be a corner point. Solution methods strive to achieve exact or near optimality.

The advantages of optimization models include the following: (i) the models are compact, (ii) solution methods are well established and often efficient, (iii) behavior of solution methods is well understood, (iv) quality of solutions can be quantified in many cases, and (v) software is readily available for established solution methods. Their drawbacks include the following: (i) the models are usually unrealistic to some degree or other, (ii) solution methods can get stuck at local optima, (iii) incremental model changes may be cumbersome to make, (iv) model formulation requires specialized skills, (v) interpreting solution values requires specialized skills, (vi) they can only be applied to structured and quantifiable problems. As a practical matter, the expertise of the human modeler has been, and still is, necessary to counteract the drawbacks.

Other optimization methods have also emerged, such as simulated annealing $[17]$ , genetic algorithms $[14]$ and Tabu search $[13]$ . They attempt to overcome search problems such as local optima, and draw their parallels from crystalline cooling in the physical world and mutation in human evolution. These models are somewhat less compact, and their solution methods have several parameters that often have to be set heuristically. Further, the behavior of these solution methods are less well understood and harder to characterize than those implied above. They have had notable successes on several hard problems $[18]$ .

Note that, like traditional linear and integer programming, these newer methods are also concerned with formulations and solution methods for optimization, rather than any meta-modeling activities. The Tabu search method is somewhat of an exception in that it tries to capture heuristic knowledge about the search process/search space in the search algorithm itself. Still, it is easy to see in this method, its primary focus on model solution.

## 2.2.AI methods

For purposes of this paper, we summarize only those AI methods that have been applied in decision support settings with some frequency. The most common types of AI methods that have been employed for decision support fall into the category of symbolic reasoning. They include Rule Based Expert Systems, Mathematical Logic, Frame Based Systems and Truth Maintenance Systems (see [34] for a review). These methods represent knowledge about a domain in some structured symbolic form, such as rules, well formed formulae or frames. These structures may store general facts about the domain as well as solution strategies and other fragments of heuristic knowledge. These AI methods emulate human reasoning, albeit to a very limited degree, through computations on these structures. Backward rule chaining, and resolution proof procedures are examples of such computations and they emulate human reasoning activities such as deduction and classification. The emphasis is clearly not on quantitative modeling as in the case of optimization.

The strength of these AI approaches lies in their ability to represent heuristic knowledge symbolically, and then manipulate them in an automated fashion to emulate reasoning. The knowledge bases can evolve incrementally. The knowledge of human experts is usually fragmented and the data structures of symbolic reasoning lend themselves to storing such knowledge. In some instances, the decision problem itself mostly requires reasoning with relatively little numerical computation. AI methods have been used extensively in such problem settings. For example, United Airlines uses an Expert System for gate assignment to arriving aircraft ([34], p. 704). AI methods can capture qualitative aspects of the decision environment, such as special constraints or re-

quirements. Formalisms such as certainty factors and Fuzzy Logic have been developed to automate reasoning with uncertainty. Nevertheless, there are several weaknesses of symbolic reasoning methods in AI. First, their machine representation is often not compact. Useful rule bases can run into thousands of rules, and frame based systems also require considerable storage space. It is difficult to extract domain and problem solving knowledge from human experts. Even then, it may contain errors and inconsistencies. Thus, knowledge acquisition and validation have been problematic all along. These AI methods also typically do not concern themselves with optimality. They are not geared to find the “best” alternative from a large set of feasible ones. Rather they take a satisficing approach. The first solution that meets feasibility criteria is often the one that is used. The computational properties of many AI reasoning methods are also difficult to describe and less well understood. For example, when a solution is obtained, we often do not know its quality – the best we can say is that it is feasible and looks plausible.

Another AI paradigm, that of Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), has also been employed for decision support [27,29,33]. For purposes of this special issue however, we will limit ourselves to ANN applications where some integration with optimization methods is involved. ANNs are based on a biological model of intelligence. The brain is thought to be a massively parallel processor consisting of millions of neurons connected to each other in a structured manner: The premise is that emulation of low level neuron activity will result in aggregate intelligent activity similar to that exhibited by humans. The power of ANNs comes from their ability to model nonlinear relationships and their ability to deal with poor data quality that is often encountered in real life. In contrast, frames and logic have great difficulty dealing with bad data. ANNs have been particularly successful in emulating the classification abilities of humans. One disadvantage of ANNs is the need for a relatively large training set of examples. Also ANNs, unlike rule or frame based methods, offer no simple and convincing means to assess the credibility of their outputs. They need to be justified on the basis of an empirical track record.

It should be apparent by now that AI and Optimization have complementary strengths for purposes of decision support. Practical decision problems are often semistructured. Optimization models support the structured and quantitative aspects of decision making. Meta-modeling and other more qualitative aspects of decision making are mostly left up to humans. Integrating AI with Optimization could therefore provide increased support for decision making.

## 3. Conceptual framework to examine integration

To examine AI/OPT integration issues, we use a conceptual framework based on the well known Simon framework [30], that identifies different phases of the decision making process. Fig. 1 shows these phases. Specific core activities are identified within each phase. It is possible to categorize and evaluate various integration proposals in the literature based on the characteristics of these core activities. Note that the activities in Fig. 1 are necessary for decision making, independent of any computer based support that may be provided. In the figure, we have also pointed out those activities for which computer based support has been developed.

There has not been much automated support for the intelligence phase of decision making. Examining the real world, recognizing a decision problem and delineating its scope is a very unstructured activity requiring inputs from a variety of sources both internal and external to an organization. Furthermore, it is difficult to articulate the general reasoning process leading up to a problem statement. That would still be difficult even if one is limited to a specific application domain such as transportation or pharmaceuticals. The most common form of computer “support” for this initial phase is to provide convenient access to a variety of information sources, much like an executive information system.

## 3.1. Model formulation

In the next phase, model formulation is one activity where integration has been attempted. The output of formulation is usually one or more optimization models, defined by their decision variables, constraints and objective function(s). Traditionally, formulation is time consuming and requires specialized skills on the part of a human modeler. A skilled modeler has to balance realism of the model against tractability. Basic feasibility checks are necessary and the availability of numerical data is an important consideration. In one of the early efforts to support formulation [26], the authors use AI to help formulate Linear Programming models. The system used an LP generator, IBM's MPSX system, SQL for data management and a tableau solution analyzer. Other modeling languages and knowledge based representation schemes have been developed [2,19] to aid the formulation process. Case based reasoning methods have also been suggested [23]. The idea is to formulate a new model by examining previous models of similar problems from a large library. Past models are stored in a structured manner as prior “cases” and reasoning methods are used to identify those that bear similarity to the current problem situation. This approach is different from [26] in the sense that it views model formulation as one of making incremental changes to an existing but similar model. The case based approach assumes that human experts also use the analogical approach in their modeling activities. Another case based approach is proposed in [24] to structure decision theoretic models. This approach is also based on the idea of matching with prior “similar” cases. A hierarchical frame based method is proposed in [32], to formulate the precedence networks that feed into the line balancing problem. The frame hierarchy captures knowledge about underlying physical requirements, engineering considerations and product details. With this knowledge, precedence networks can be formulated in increasing levels of detail. Based on the foregoing, it appears that the model formulation process has been successfully aided by a structured representation of domain knowledge. This is possible through knowledge representations schemes such as frames, although the difficult part is the acquisition and validation of expert knowledge.

## 3.2. Model selection and sequencing

In Fig. 1, the design phase may also involve model selection and/or model sequencing. For example, there are a variety of forecasting models, and selection depends on a number of factors such as the forecast horizon, amount of data and time bounds. Alternatively, a decision problem sometimes requires the solution of several models in an appropriate sequence. The output of one feeds into, or serves to determine, the next. A frame based method was proposed in [6] to select and sequence models. Since frames are supposed to represent “stereotypical” situations, the preconditions in a frame can be used to store criteria under which it is appropriate to select a given model; i.e. the stereotypical situation in which that model would be used. Also, slots in frames can be used to connect to other frames. This allows for model sequences to be developed. The basic idea here is to represent appropriate conditions for model use using logic or frames. A logic based procedure was proposed in [7] to perform the same functions. Inputs, outputs and preconditions for models are stated as Horn clauses. The resolution proof procedure is then used to select and sequence models in order to solve a given decision problem. A neural network based approach was developed in [4] to select forecasting methods. It views the selection activity as one of classification, and the ANN trains itself to select the best technique based on a history of past cases. Object oriented representations have also been put forth for this synthesis. They use the properties of inheritance and encapsulation to facilitate model synthesis. However, this representation falls outside the realm of the AI structures mentioned in Section 2. A blackboard architecture has been proposed in [25] for model selection and sequencing. A special feature is that if there are alternate model sequences that are feasible, the blackboard architecture attempts to suggest one based on user preferences. An interesting suggestion appeared earlier in [28] where machine learning techniques were employed to refine criteria for model selection based on past experience. Past modeling experiences are stored in the form of schemata and are used to continually refine model representations as well. Note that this approach is different from the case based formulations mentioned earlier in its attempt to self adjust based on experience. In summary, there does not appear to be one dominant AI method being used for model selection and sequencing. From a practical standpoint however, model selection is an important activity often requiring specialized skills. Therefore, any AI based method, which can capture this expertise in some form would be helpful to this task.

## 3.3. Model solution

In the Choice phase of Fig. 1, the “solve model(s)” activity is not as straightforward as it may first appear. There can be multiple variants of a solution method along with specialized computer implementations of the same. For example, there are many linear programming packages available, and they vary in the problem size they can handle, their memory requirements and execution speeds. Some simplex implementations use sparse matrix techniques to sacrifice speed for storage efficiencies. Thus, selection of a solution method may require the careful consideration of multiple factors. Occasionally, some solution procedures will require experimentation to set the values of certain starting or controlling parameters. For example, the use of Lagrangian relaxation to solve integer programming problems may require tests to determine a good starting multiplier value. It will also be necessary to set an appropriate limit to the number of iterations. Some initial experimentation may be necessary for this purpose. Sometimes, optimization solution methods run into local optima. Previously mentioned techniques, such as simulated annealing and genetic algorithms have been developed to get around the problem of local optima. However, they also require some experimentation to set solution parameters. Annealing methods need to use a judicious “cooling” rate. This may be viewed as being analogous to step size in a descent algorithm. In the physical world, excessively rapid cooling will result in a poor crystalline structure with high entropy, where entropy is analogous to objective function value. Thus, excessively rapid cooling in simulated annealing may prevent the procedure from arriving at the global optimum. Genetic algorithms need to set “selectivity” and “mating” parameters that determine which elements of a population survive from one generation to the next. Excessive selectivity results in inbreeding which propagates the “weaknesses” of a species, while low selectivity is computationally inefficient. In other words, excessive selectivity results in the algorithms wandering around in a part of the search space with little promise, while low selectivity means too much search. The experimentation needed to set these parameters are cumbersome and require time. As yet, we have not seen AI based approaches to facilitate these various aspects of “solving” a model.

## 3.4. Solution testing and integration

These two activities in the Choice phase of Fig. 1 usually require considerable manual intervention from human experts and are good candidates for AI support. They are necessary before model solutions can be adapted for use in the real world. Testing a solution has several aspects. A model rarely captures all aspects of a real problem and will usually make simplifying assumptions. The numerical values used as inputs to the model often have uncertainty associated with them. Therefore it is advisable to test solutions for robustness. Sensitivity analysis helps in this task. Also it is necessary to interpret the meaning of solution values. The ANALYZE system [15] is an example of a system that assists various post solution activities. ANALYZE specifically supports linear programming analysis. Using rule based heuristic reasoning, it can provide explanations of dual prices, diagnose infeasibilities, and reason about redundancy. However, solution testing may need to be more elaborate. For instance, it may be desirable to solve minor structural variations of the model and compare solutions.

Sometimes, the results of several models have to be somehow “unified” into one practical solution because each model provides only a partial solution. In [8] for example, the authors address the problem of network capacity planning. Separate models are necessary to address capacity and reliability issues satisfactorily. However, the topology and capacity values proposed by the capacity and reliability models may only be partially consistent. In particular, low cost solutions may have unacceptable topologies from the standpoint of reliability. In contrast, topologies which are reliable usually have higher connectivity, resulting in higher costs. The conflicting solutions have to be reconciled. They suggested a blackboard architecture to unify the results of different optimization models. A truth maintenance system is used to analyze the effects of modifying separate solutions to achieve consistency. This is one example of an application where solution integration requires reconciling conflicting individual solutions.

After solutions from optimization models have been tested, it may be necessary to modify and adapt them prior to actual use. This occurs because there are usually some factors that the model is unable to capture. An interesting problem is described in $[20]$ , which consists of determining the sales mix. A mix, based on short term profits, can be determined through optimization modeling. However, this solution needs to be modified through a consideration of qualitative strategic impacts. General business facts and trend assessments are factored into this post solution modification. Another example which also highlights the need to adapt results from optimization models appears in $[21]$ . There, the quantifiable variables and their relationships are captured in a linear programming formulation, while the subjective variables are handled through a rule based reasoning approach. At present, post model solution activities have not received as much attention for purposes of decision support. However, it is one area of support activity which could benefit substantially from the use of AI methods because of its heuristic and expert nature.

## 4. Barriers to AI/OPT integration

While the benefits of AI/OPT integration have been recognized for some time, their respective data structures and algorithmic paradigms are so radically different that it is difficult to implement seamless integration schemes of reasonable generality. A seamless integration scheme would allow a human decision maker to alternate between qualitative heuristic analysis and structured quantitative computations in a flexible manner as needed during different phases of decision making. Unfortunately, the current state of integration efforts falls far short of this desirable goal, and in this section we mention some of the specific barriers to seamless integration.

AI representations, such as rule bases and frames often use list structures that are very different from the arrays and matrices used by optimization models. Consequently, their programming languages, such as KRL and FORTRAN respectively, have primitives that manipulate different objects altogether. For example, PROLOG is rather clumsy at manipulating matrices while FORTRAN cannot manipulate lists. Other procedural languages such as C or Pascal do not have particular strengths in handling dynamic nested lists. Such incompatibility does not facilitate implementation of general tools for AI/OPT integration. Most AI programming environments do provide the ability to call external routines written in machine or high level procedural languages. However, parameter passing mechanisms are often most primitive and convoluted programming tricks are often needed to exchange complex data objects.

Quite apart from programming incompatibilities, another barrier to AI/OPT integration is the sheer complexity of information that may need to be exchanged for seamless integration. This is because integration may require exchange of not only the obvious data structures such as constraint matrices and heuristic rules, but also a considerable amount of control information. For instance, one may want to intervene in an ongoing branch and bound procedure to change the search based on heuristic reasoning about how the solution is emerging. This would require control information such as current bounds, number of fathomed nodes, etc. to be exchanged with the heuristic reasoning component. Given the huge variety of models, and an even greater variety of solution methods, it is not easy to identify and parsimoniously express the control information that may need to be exchanged. It may only be possible to do so for specific problems or a class of problems.

## 5. Integrated modeling frameworks

It is easy to see from the foregoing, that integration efforts have been somewhat fragmented, in that they have addressed isolated facets of integration. A few authors have suggested ideas for integrated modeling environments, although the interpretation of the term “integrated” varies somewhat. One framework appears in [5], where the objective is to provide support for the definition, manipulation and control of mathematical models throughout the entire modeling life cycle. The focus in [5] is on the model integration aspects of this environment. The author suggests that such a modeling environment should facilitate combining different quantitative modeling paradigms. This view of integration, while still very ambitious, is somewhat different from the view of integration presented in earlier sections. A hyperknowledge framework appears in [3]. As in preceding sections, examples of integrated modeling can be found for specific problems. A system to help plan earthwork operations in road construction is described in [1]. The expert knowledge possessed by planning engineers and construction equipment specialists is stored in a way that it can be used along with a variety of optimization routines that try to minimize transportation costs.

Probably the best known and formal framework for integrated modeling is the structured modeling framework of Geoffrion [9–12]. The three main long term design challenges as seen in structured modeling are: (i) the need to develop a framework for conceptual modeling, (ii) machine executable modeling languages, (iii) software integration. A graph theoretic framework has been developed as an answer to (i). Structured modeling aims to support the entire modeling life cycle, facilitate the evolution of models, and manage key resources. The approach is undergoing conceptual development, and represents an important effort to formalize major aspects of modeling. It is probably fair to say that at this time, its main emphasis is on the model formulation activity. Other meta-modeling activities remain to be formalized.

## 6. Conclusions

In this paper, we have surveyed some of the literature on AI/OPT integration, specifically from the standpoint of decision support. Clearly, the major target activity for integration has been model formulation and model selection. However, there remain important meta-modeling activities, such as model solution and solution testing, which can benefit substantially from integration. The efforts to integrate AI and Optimization are rather fragmented in that they do not usually view the decision making process in its totality. Rather the approach has been to isolate specific activities in decision making and then provide support for them through the use of AI or Optimization. We conjecture that the difficulties mentioned in Section 4 will continue to discourage general tools for AI/OPT integration from emerging. However, as AI software development environments become more user friendly, we do expect to see more AI/OPT integration efforts that are specific to applications or a class of applications.

Transactions, European Journal of Operational Research, Operations Research and IEEE Transactions on Communications. He serves on the editorial boards of several IS journals, and is a member of IEEE, ACM and INFORMS.
