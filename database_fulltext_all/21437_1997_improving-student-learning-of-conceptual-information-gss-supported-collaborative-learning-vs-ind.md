# Improving student learning of conceptual information: GSS supported collaborative learning vs. individual constructive learning

Dorothy E. Leidner $^{*}$ , Mark Fuller $^{1}$

Baylor University, P.O. Box 98005, Waco, TX 76798, USA

## Abstract

A great deal of time in the traditional classroom environment is spent gathering information (taking notes) rather than processing the information and assimilating the information. The traditional learning model goes from the gathering to recall stage without regard for whether the information is actually comprehended (processed and assimilated). The research reported in this paper employed a quasi-experimental design to examine whether technology-enabled collaborative learning involving case analyses is superior to individual constructive learning involving individual case analyses where the goal of both methods is to increase student interest in the course, increase student understanding of the material, and enhance student performance. The study found that students working collaboratively in either small or large groups were more interested in the material and perceived themselves to learn more than students that worked individually but that students that worked individually outperformed students that collaborated in small or large groups before working individually. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Electronic Classrooms; Group Support Systems; Constructivism; Cooperativism

## 1. Introduction

IT researchers have recently begun an introspective process of examining the most effective ways of implementing information technology into their own profession of helping others to learn. This has coincided with a general trend toward improving business education which has largely failed to keep pace with the technological advances in the business world. The focus takes two angles: exposing students to more technology to better prepare them for their role as managers and to use technology to enable new or more effective methods of learning. Initial research has focused on the latter angle, examining how information technology alters traditional teaching and learning [19], how IT can improve certain teaching methods such as the CASE method [14] and such learning methods as collaborative learning [1,3], and how IT can create new learning environments [18,20]. These studies are part of a nascent body of IT in higher education research the cornerstone of which is the belief that the traditional method of teaching and learning has failed to sufficiently engage students in the learning process and that IT can be used to either compensate for inefficiencies inherent in the typical traditional environment or to enable the effective application of alternative methods of learning [20].

Proponents of alternative learning models criticize the traditional method on the grounds that it transmits knowledge to students with little concern for whether the students understand or assimilate the material into their cognitive schema. Alternative models of learning emphasize enabling students to construct the knowledge themselves (constructivism) [27], enabling students to collaborate with other students to expose them to a wider variety of ideas (collaborativism) [29], enabling students to process information in a manner that best suits their cognitive style (cognitive information processing theory) [28], and providing a realistic learning context (social learning theory) [12]. However, of these alternative models, there is not yet empirical evidence to suggest which of the methods leads to the most effective learning and given what context. In fact, a weakness of much of the learning research is that it compares a single alternative learning method to the traditional method and usually finds that the alternative method leads to greater satisfaction and learning. However, little is known about when to apply a particular alternative model.

In terms of the additional factor of information technology, the most one can conclude is that the technology is an enabler of the effective application of the models of learning. For example, research has found that technology-enabled collaborative learning yields superior outcomes to non-technology enabled collaborative learning $[1]$ . While some results (e.g. increased participation) may be related to other factors (such as class size), technology may still serve a role by changing the perceived size of a class without decreasing the true physical size. Given the constraints facing most universities related to class size and teaching loads, technology may be the most feasible and economical way to enable new and creative applications of learning methods.

While alternative methods have been compared to the traditional methods and IT enabled methods have been compared to the same method without IT, there also exists a need to compare alternative methods with and without IT support to begin to understand when to apply the alternative methods of learning and what the appropriate role of the technology is. This paper will examine whether technology-enabled collaborative learning involving case analyses is superior to individual constructive learning involving individual case analyses where the goal of both methods is to increase student interest in the course, increase student understanding of the material, and enhance student performance. The research question is thus: will the use of collaborative learning via electronic case discussions prior to individual case analyses lead to higher perceived learning, higher interest, and greater performance than constructive learning via individual case analyses in the absence of group interaction.

The research reported here is interested in examining the learning that takes place within the same time/same place conventional classroom context. Information technology presents many new opportunities for supporting the educational process outside the conventional classroom environment, including the concepts of virtual learning and forms of distance learning (see $[20]$ and $[16]$ for a thorough discussion of these issues). While research on these new opportunities is important, a parallel stream of research is needed to study how information technology can be effectively applied within the classroom, since we think it unlikely that the conventional classroom environment will become obsolete in the near future. This paper pursues this latter goal, and is organized as follows: Section 2 presents prior research and hypotheses, Section 3 presents the research design and methodology; Section 4 presents the results, and Section 5 discusses the implications of the results, the limitations of the study, and the conclusions.

## 2. Prior research and hypotheses

Various outcomes have been addressed in learning theory research. Among the most common are student interest in learning (in that interest is viewed as a requisite to motivation), student performance, self-efficacy (student belief in their ability to learn which is believed to affect motivation), and student perceived learning [2,15,23]. Consistent with Alavi [1], we are going to examine student interest and student perceived learning. As with Alavi, our concept of "student interest" is limited to the student's short term interest in the task, as opposed to a "vested interest" where learners believe the task increases their long term success. While our second variable, perceived learning, may not completely correlate with actual learning, is still of interest to educators (and thus this research) because higher levels of perceived learning will likely result in greater student satisfaction with the learning experience. This in turn should result in better teaching evaluations, a common measure in assessing instructor performance. Lastly, we are going to examine student performance. Performance is considered distinct from perceived learning because past research has indicated that subjective and self-measures of performance are often inconsistent with objective measures of performance [4,21]. This study will thus compare individual learning interest, perceived learning, and performance for students engaged in individual constructive learning versus students that first engaged in technology-enabled collaborative learning in the form of small (5 students) or large (the entire class, in this case 40 students) group anonymous electronic discussions. Collaborative learning, for the purposes of this study, is broadly defined as peer to peer interaction designed to promote individual learning.

Various learning models can shed light on how to facilitate the outcomes discussed above. Learning research suggests that the traditional method whereby an instructor speaks until interrupted by a student question is inadequate in effectively fostering student interest and active processing of the material $[24]$ . Learning in such an environment becomes a rote process involving information acquisition and recall. The cognitive information processing theory of learning suggests that methods must foster active assimilation and processing of the information in order for meaningful learning to occur $[28]$ . There are various teaching techniques available to foster such learning. One technique is the use of case analyses whereby the burden rests with the student to apply relevant information to the situation being discussed $[14]$ . The goal of the cases is to enable students to process instructional inputs and assimilate the course material. Such cases can be analyzed individually or in the context of a group.

The collaborative model of learning further suggests that the exchange of diverse ideas, as well as feedback on those ideas, is crucial to the effective processing of information. Operationalizing this collaboration can be achieved by placing students in groups which work together to analyze case materials. This group work should serve to expose students to a greater quantity of ideas, more diverse ideas, as well as to increased feedback on their own ideas, with the overall goal of increasing student interest, perceived learning and performance. This group work can be further enhanced through the use of group support system technology (for a complete discussion of GSS technology see [17]). GSS technology has been shown to increase idea generation and exchange, and using this technology in the current study's context should have the effect of enhancing the potential of the student groups. Again, the value of collaborative learning should not be that a group of students outperforms a single student on a given learning task such as a case analysis, but that each student learns more and performs better after having interacted with a group than if they had worked entirely alone.

Based on this reasoning, the following hypothesis is posited:

Hypothesis 1: Compared with students working alone, students participating in collaborative groups for their case analysis (prior to writing their individual responses) will:

1. be more interested in the learning task

2. perceive that they learn more

3. perform better on their individual case analysis

Given that many of the benefits of collaborative learning are associated with increased exposure to a greater diversity of ideas and faster feedback on ideas, at first glance we might expect larger groups to yield more ideas and faster feedback than smaller groups (simply because there are more people involved in the interaction). A look at prior research on group productivity, however, illustrates that this does not occur, i.e. that large groups generally do not outperform small groups in generating ideas $[8,13,22,27]$ . This decrease in the relative performance of large groups as compared to smaller groups is in part explained by two types of group problems, namely production blocking and evaluation apprehension $[25]$ . Production blocking occurs when an individual cannot express his/her opinion because another member of the group is talking. Evaluation apprehension is the reduced likelihood someone will speak because they fear disapproval by other group members. The effect of these two factors may grow as group size increases [30].

Technology can play a role in helping gain the potential benefits inherent in larger groups. Collaborative technology, like Group Support Systems, have been shown to have a significant impact on group performance by decreasing the effects of production blocking and evaluation apprehension. Two features of GSS are responsible for this impact: parallel processing and anonymity $[4,5,9–11]$ both of which have been shown to increase group productivity within the GSS context. In relation to the current research, collaborative technology should allow us to increase the size of the interacting groups in the classroom, which in turn should increase the number of ideas to which students are exposed as well as the feedback they receive on their ideas. Increases in idea quantity and feedback should increase a student's individual ability to process the materials related to the case, subsequently enhancing the student's performance on the individual written case analysis. By using collaborative technology, larger groups should do this more effectively than smaller groups.

The reasoning above leads us to the second set of hypothesis related to group size. We propose that:

Hypothesis 2: Compared with students in small computer supported groups, students participating in large computer supported groups (prior to writing their individual responses) will:

1. perform better on their individual case analysis Not all of the results of larger group size are positive, however. GSS research suggests that as group size increases, free riding (the tendency of some group members to rely on other members to accomplish the task without their contributions) increases, uninhibited behavior increases, and satisfaction decreases [32]. Research has also found that members of small groups rate themselves as more effective than large groups [32]. We suspect this same trend will occur in the classroom, where students will be slightly frustrated by larger group sizes, reducing their interest in the course material, and decreasing their perceived learning.

We thus hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2: Compared with students in small computer supported groups, students participating in large computer supported groups (prior to writing their individual responses) will:

1. be less interested in the learning task

2. perceive that they learn less

## 3. Research design and methodology

This section presents the research design and methodology.

## 3.1. Context and subjects

This research uses a quasi-experimental design to examine the effects of computer supported collaboration in a real educational (field) setting. The context of the study is an undergraduate required Management Information Systems course for all business majors at a large private university in the Southwestern United States. The course is highly conceptual and managerially focused rather than technology or software focused. Two sections of the course taught during the Spring semester, 1995 by the same professor comprise the sample for the current study. Each section had 40 students. The students are mostly juniors with an occasional sophomore or senior majoring in business who have completed a basic computer literacy course but with little other computer experience. The average age of the students taking the course each semester hovers around 20, with very few students having had any prior full-time work experience. The course is currently being taught in a newly remodeled classroom facility which incorporates networked laptop computers available at each student station. The instructor had taught the course several times before, but had never before used electronic case discussions as part of the course requirements.

## 3.2. Task and equipment

Each Tuesday a lecture was given and each Thursday (excluding test days and computer lab days), the students engaged in a case analysis that related to the material covered on previous class period. The cases were all developed by the instructor to clearly reinforce or extend concepts discussed in the prior lecture. A total of seven such cases were used over the course of the semester. In general, the cases required the students to either generalize from the specifics of a case to major points addressed in previous course material or to take generalized points from the lecture material and then adapt it to the specifics of a case (the cases thus fostered either inductive or deductive reasoning). In order to provide an incentive for students to take the cases seriously, the average grade received on those cases completed before each major course exam counted for 10% of the exam grade.

In each condition, 75 minutes were allowed for the case analysis. In the electronic discussion conditions, the students had 45 minutes to discuss the case in the electronic groups and then 30 minutes to write their individual analysis. They were allowed to consult their class notes, to write down comments from other students during the electronic discussion, and to look at the output of the electronic discussion on the screen while writing their individual analyses. For the individual learning condition, the students were given the full 75 minutes to think on their own, look at their notes, and write their analysis. During the electronic discussions, the instructor was the technology facilitator. She handled problems with the computer equipment, but did not enter any comments into the discussion nor in any way guide the discussion. This was to ensure that the instructor was a neutral presence during either the individual analyses or the group discussions.

The first case analysis was treated as a pilot with both sections engaging in an electronic discussion for the primary purpose of technology familiarization and getting them familiar with the grading. These cases were not counted toward their final grade, unless they needed to be substituted for an absence on a later case. The remaining six cases included two individual analyses with no prior group discussion, two individual analyses preceded by a small group electronic discussion using Group Systems Software, and two individual analyses preceded by a large group electronic discussion also using Group Systems Software. Random assignment resulted in the cases being administered in the following sequence across the two sections of the course:

Case: 1 2 3 4 5 6
Section 1 Small Group Small Group Large Group Individual Large Group Individual
Section 2 Individual Large Group Small Group Small Group Individual Large Group

While it would have been advantageous, from an experimental design perspective, to use cases of an equivalent nature and completely randomize their order throughout the semester, this was not possible given the nature of the research. This study was conducted in a real classroom setting, and learning objectives required that the cases be sequenced in a way that corresponded to the order of the lectures, thus preventing their randomization.

## 3.3. Design

The research design is a quasi-experimental 3 by 1 factorial design intended to provide a means for comparing large GSS supported collaborative learning, small GSS supported collaborative learning, and individual learning. We chose to vary conditions within two sections of a class taught by the same instructor rather than varying the conditions across three sections of the course. This within subjects design was done to control for differences in the group (class) composition between courses offered at different times of the day. In addition, given the fact that this technology was new, University authorities were anxious to see it used, and the course instructors wanted to gain as much experience with this technology as possible with as many students as possible. This particular design poses some limitations which will be mentioned in the discussion section.

## 3.4. Environment

This study was undertaken using GroupSystems, a GSS developed at the University of Arizona. GroupSystems is a PC-DOS based GSS which runs on several Personal Computers (PC's) networked over Novell Netware. The collaborative technology classroom used in this study contains 43 PCs embedded in a semi-circular classroom configuration. No front screen or group oriented audio visual support were turned on for this study.

Group Outliner, the GroupSystems program used, is an interactive discussion tool which allows users to read comments from the other group members and add comments of their own. When a comment is added, the participant will see his comment added to the group list. One file is accessed by all members of a group simultaneously. In the large group condition for this research, all 40 members of the class accessed the same discussion area. In the small group condition, the class of 40 was broken up into eight 5-person teams. Each of these teams had their own discussion area within the software that was not accessible by other non-team members in the class. In all conditions, the anonymity setting was enabled (comments were not signed) and comment numbering was turned on (a unique number was assigned to every comment submitted in the discussion).

## 3.5. Procedure

A survey immediately following the completion of the written analyses was designed to assess perceived interest and perceived learning. The items used to assess perceived learning and interest had been pretested in a survey given to a group of 200 students to assess their perceived interest and learning in an electronic classroom at the university. Because the students would be required to complete a survey after each class in which a case analysis was conducted, the survey had to be kept short to avoid students becoming bothered by having to complete it each time. The survey was as follows:

This questionnaire is designed to get your perceptions of today's case analysis. Please be honest and accurate in your responses. Please rate the extent to which you agree with the following questions:

Today's case analysis:

The students responded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from "to no extent" to "to a great extent. The factor analysis on the pretest sample indicated two factors (an interest factor comprised of the items asking about interest, enjoyment, and attention; a learning factor comprised of the items asking about understanding, learning, and acquiring knowledge) both with reliabilities over. 8.

To measure performance, two graduate students both of whom had taken sufficient IS and strategy course work to qualify them for grading the cases were used as graders. The graders were given detailed instructions for the expectations of each case. They were instructed to look for the quality and completeness of the ideas, the organization of the ideas, and the professionalism of the write-up. They were given detailed expectations concerning material that the students should have noticed in the case. They then independently assessed each analysis and compared their evaluations. The graders were unaware of the conditions or of the experimental nature of the cases. Where there was an initial disagreement in the evaluations, the coders were required to discuss the reasons for the given grade and agree on the final grade. To ensure a common frame of reference, the graders were asked to express student performance evaluations on a four-point scale that included "Below Average" (1), "Average" (2), "Above Average" (3), and "Outstanding" (4). The class after the completion of each analysis, the instructor disin the responses. The instructor read and displayed the best two or three cases. This was intended to help the students on subsequent case analysis. It also provided a means of reinforcing student learning and completing a cycle that began with a lecture.

## 4. Results

This section presents the statistical results of the study and the results of hypothesis testing.

## 4.1. Normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics for each variable. The main point indicated by the descriptive statistics is the very high mean across conditions on each of the variables measured in the survey and the low standard deviations. In general, the case analyses regardless of condition seemed very satisfying to the students. Normality is the assumption that each variable is normally distributed. The Shapiro-Wilks test was used to assess normality. The data were significantly skewed and had significant kurtosis. An examination of the descriptive output revealed this was because of the relatively high mean responses to each question (above 4 on a scale of 5 for each item) and small variance. The data were thus transformed into the logarithm before conducting the multivariate statistical analysis.

## 4.2. Construct validity and reliability

Construct validity addresses the question of whether the measures are adequate surrogates for the true constructs describing the event or merely artifacts of the methodology. Factor analysis was conducted on the survey questions. The two anticipated factors are expected to be highly correlated since according to theory interest in learning results in greater learning. For this reason, an oblique rather than orthogonal rotation was used [31]. Eigenvalues greater than 1 and scree plots were used in determining the number of factors. For an item to be considered in the composition of a variable, it had to have a loading of at least .7 on the factor, with no loading exceeding .5 on another factor, had to conform to a priori assignments, and had to add to the variable's reliability.

The mean of the items in each scale was used to combine the items into a variable score. Cronbach's alpha was used to assess the inter-item reliability of the final, multi-item scales. Both factors exhibited high reliability (.87 and .90 for interest in learning and perceived learning, respectively). The factor loadings and the reliability scores for each variable are provided in Table 2.

4.3. Inter-coder reliability for the performance variable

In total, 374 cases were graded by each coder. There were 82 differences in initial grades given, indicating an initial agreement of $292/374 = 78\%$ . While the reliability is less than desirable, all of the differences in initial grades were of only 1 point on the four point scale and were resolved by the coders discussing the reason for the assigned grade and agreeing on the final grade.

## 4.4. Statistical analysis performed

MANOVAs were run to test for an overall condition effect. The results were significant for both the perceived interest and learning variables (F = 3.8, p > .001) and for the performance variable (F = 7.359, p > .000). Because the survey was anonymous, and hence the responses of the subjects were not tracked over time, repeated measures MANOVA was not possible; rather, the effect of time had to be controlled for within MANOVA. MANOVA was run to test for a significant time effect. The result was also significant for the perceived interest and learning variables (F = 3.9, p > .000) and for the performance variable (F = 9.149, p > .000). Further analysis using regression revealed that interest decreased over time (B = -.087) but not to a significant degree (T = -1.6, p > .1053), perceived learning increased over time (B = .09) but not significantly (T = 1.6, p > .1085), and performance increased significantly over time (B = .137; T = 5.346, p > .000). MANOVA was run to test for a significant difference in the two sections of the course. There was no significant section effect (F = .0465, p > .495).

T-Tests were conducted comparing the perceived interest, perceived learning, and performance variables across the three conditions. Table 4 summarizes the results of the t-tests.

## 4.5. Hypotheses testing

Hypothesis 1 predicted that students after having discussed a scenario related to the course material in an anonymous electronic group would be (1a) more interested in the material, (1b) perceive themselves to learn more, and (1c) perform better than students that worked individually. Hypothesis 1a was supported by the data: students working first in small electronic groups (T = -4.28, p > .000) and students that worked initially in large groups (T = -3.13, p > .000) both reported greater interest in the material than students that worked alone: Hypothesis 1b is partially supported by the data: students that worked first in small electronic groups perceived themselves to learn more than students that worked alone (T = -2.42, p > .02) but there was no perceived difference in learning when the students worked first in large groups versus when they worked entirely alone (T = -1.21, p > .23). Hypothesis 1c is rejected: in fact, students working alone significantly outperformed students that had the opportunity to discuss the case electronically within a small group (T = 3.18, p > .000) or within a large group (T = 2.63, p > .01).

Hypothesis 2a predicted that students working in large groups would (2a) perform better, (2b) be less interested, but (2c) perceive more learning than students working first in small groups. None of these hypotheses were accepted. There was no significant difference in the interest (T = 1.07, p > .29), perceived learning (T = 1.12, p > .26), or performance (T = -.69, p > .49) variables for students that discussed scenarios in small groups versus students that discussed the scenarios in large groups before analyzing the scenarios themselves. ## 5. Discussion, limitations, and conclusions

This section discusses the implications of the findings and presents the limitations and conclusions of the study.

## 5.1. Discussion

There are four major findings from this research which will be discussed below. The four findings are: (1) Students involved in collaborative learning using GSS showed higher levels of interest in learning than those who worked individually, (2) Students involved in individual learning outperformed students in either small or large collaborative groups which used GSS, (3) Students perceived their learning to be higher after they had first interacted in a small group discussion than when they worked entirely alone, and (4) There were no differences in either interest or performance levels for students in small versus large group discussions.

## 5.1.1. Level of interest

The findings of this study suggest that collaborative learning via electronic case discussions contributes significantly to student interest in the material of a course. Both small and large group electronic discussions were associated with greater interest in course material than when students did not participate in the collaborative learning. There may be several reasons why the students rated their interest higher following collaborative learning via case analyses versus individual constructive learning via case analysis. One explanation is simply that, as the collaborative model suggests, people enjoy communicating when they are given a non-threatening environment within which to participate. The GSS technology provided such a non-threatening environment. Collaborative research generally compares collaborative learning to traditional learning and finds that collaborative learning creates greater interest. Our findings suggest that collaborative learning also exceeds individual constructive learning in terms of creating interest. Another explanation is that the students were able to use the technology during the group discussion conditions but not during the individual analyses and that the technology itself created interest. The students were allowed to write their analysis using a word processing software during all conditions, but the fact that special communication's software was used during the electronic discussion likely contributed a fascination with the technology. In addition to the fascination effect, the students may perceive value to using technology in and of itself, particularly for a course they associate with computers. Thus, there may in fact be a symbolic value to the technology in the minds of the students [13] - they felt privileged when getting to use the technology and hence were more interested. The study did continue throughout the semester with only a slight and nonsignificant drop in interest. Should the students be using a similar learning method in other classes, perhaps the interest would drop rapidly.

## 5.1.2. Level of performance

Although enjoying the technology-enabled collaborative learning more than the individual learning condition, the students performed better when they worked alone than after they worked in either small or large collaborative groups. This is a major finding that has not surfaced in past research which has looked at group performance rather than individual performance after group involvement. There are several explanations for this. One explanation is that the same amount of time was given for each case analyses regardless of condition, yet the electronic discussions took more time than when the students merely reflected about the case on their own, consulted their class notes, and wrote their response. Hence, it is possible that the students were too hurried when they first engaged in an electronic discussion. This could be easily ameliorated by providing more class time for the discussion and analysis. Another more serious explanation is that the students were so anxious to input their comments into the system that they spent little time thoughtfully reflecting on what either they were writing or on what others were writing. This in fact would suggest that though collaborating, they were not processing and assimilating the information; though exposed to a diversity of ideas, they did not incorporate the ideas into their own cognitive framework. In essence, they may have become so focused on contribution that they did not seriously think about the material nor seriously evaluate the ideas contributed by others, but merely rushed to make sure they included everything in their analysis that was mentioned during the discussion. Again, this may be remedied by allowing students time to reflect on their GSS conversation. These concepts merit further research given that a primary goal of using the technology enabled collaborative learning was to help the students assimilate and process information from the course.

## 5.1.3. Level of perceived learning

Another finding is that students perceived their learning to be higher after they first interacted in a small group discussion than when they worked entirely alone but that their performance was significantly higher when they worked entirely alone. Hence, consistent with Connolly, Jessup, and Valacich [4], self-ratings of effectiveness were in direct opposition to the measure of actual output. This poses a dilemma for the instructor contemplating incorporating the technology: if the students are more interested and think they are learning more when using the technology but are actually performing worse, should they incorporate the technology method or not? Interestingly, the students did not perceive greater learning after having interacted with a large group than when they conducted the analyses entirely alone. A possible explanation for this finding is that the large group discussions, while interesting, seemed to pose an information overload problem. There were times when gasps of frustration could be heard following the appearance of a sudden onslaught of messages on the screen. It was also hard for the students to follow the logical flow of arguments because if they were responding to a particular point addressed on the screen, by the time they finished typing and sending their response, there were 5 or 10 other responses to something else in between their response and the intended comment to which they were responding.

## 5.1.4. Size of the group

Another finding of the study is that the students neither perceived greater interest, learning, nor performed better when they engaged in small group electronic discussion versus large group discussions. The statistical findings in this case are contradictory to the written comments students were asked to make on the final survey completed for the course. The written comments largely indicated that students preferred smaller groups - with the entire class of 40 there were too many comments and students complained of offensive or digressive comments from other students. With large groups, there was a higher perceived anonymity (particularly after a case discussion the topic of which focused on conditions of anonymity in the context of using GSS) resulting in a greater amount of digression and sometimes offensive comments $^{2}$ . Students also showed annoyance in the large discussions when no one responded to their comments but merely inputted comments with no relation to previous statements (they indicated their annoyance by inserting a comment asking for feedback in a rather forceful way: "WILL SOMEONE PLEASE RESPOND TO THE COMMENT ON LINE 57??? I'M WAITING.").

At the same time, the groups of five seemed too small for two reasons. First, the university population used in this study is fairly homogeneous. As suggested by Dennis, Nunamaker, and Vogel [6], drawing subjects from a narrowly defined population may lead to the formation of groups whose members have the same basic knowledge domains and hence with a group size that is logically smaller than the physical size. In addition, in the small group condition, students were annoyed when there were not enough comments and sometimes students requested, but were denied, the capability to switch into another group. Thus, our observations of the discussions in progress and student written comments led us to conclude fairly early that five was in fact too small but that forty was too large for groups. Neither size was ideal. After the six cases that were part of the study were completed, the instructor for the remainder of the course used group sizes of 20 which seemed manageable and the most pleasing to the students.

## 5.1.5. Instructor observations

Several observations were made during the course of the study that are worth mentioning. The pilot study used a case at the end of the chapter in the textbook and in fact the intention was to use the two-page cases and questions at the end of each chapter covered as the basis of the case discussions and analyses. While the pilot was characterized mostly by technological familiarization and the accompanying problems when forty individuals use a software for the first time, it was noticed that the discussion itself was less interesting to the students than the technology. Part of the reason was that there was little controversy or even debatable material in the case. The questions asked in the text were so broad as to leave the students dumbfounded regarding what to discuss. Another problem was the case length. Despite being told to read the case in advance of coming to class, few of the students actually did and they had to spend ten minutes just reading through the case. The instructor thus custom created the six used in the study. The cases each involved a topic of a conceptual nature (thus, where there was no clear right or wrong answer), of a slightly controversial or at least debatable nature, and tied very closely to the material covered in class. The cases were short enough to be read in several minutes before the discussions began, required no extra outside of class work for the students, and included very specific questions to be addressed in the analysis.

An observation made by the instructor of the two sections used in the study was that the nature of her own style of teaching in the traditional environment was altered: knowing that the majority of the Thursday class would be occupied by a case closely tied to the material covered on Tuesday, the instructor felt pressure to cover the material. Whereas in previous semesters, the professor's style involved asking a lot of questions, after postponing some of the cases for having failed to cover required material, the instructor began lecturing without questions for the sake of sticking to the syllabus and completing the material before the associated case. Thus, the professor became even more traditional in style during traditional teaching. Yet during the collaborative sessions and individual constructive sessions, the role of the professor was one of observer and technology supervisor. Future research can examine various roles of the professor during electronic discussions. For example, the professor could act as a confederate injecting critical comments aimed at encouraging students to evaluate the ideas of others rather than just accepting them. Connolly, Jessup, and Valacich [4] found that the simple injection of a stream of either critical or supportive comments had substantial effects on group performance. The professor can take a more active role in the discussion than was designated in our study.

## 5.2. Limitations

Several limitations to the current study warrant mention. One limitation relates to the way learning conditions were varied within each class, rather than between classes. This design characteristic, however, is both an advantage and a limitation at the same time. On the positive side, having the same group of students exposed to varying learning conditions reduced any effects resulting from differences in the group composition across the different classes used in the study. In addition, interest declined slightly over time but not significantly. It is possible, and we feel likely, that had one section done individual case analyses the entire time, one done small group discussions the entire time, and one group large discussions the entire time, that their interest level would have declined significantly over time. Several times on the surveys following individual case analysis, students wrote comments stating that they would have preferred using the electronic discussion technology. Nevertheless, they rated the case analyses as very beneficial. Thus the mere variation of conditions may help maintain interest. This could be tested in future research.

On the negative side, this within class design also presents certain disadvantages. The variation itself (changing conditions within each class) may have mitigated effects that would have occurred over time. This reduces our ability to draw firm conclusions regarding the effects of repeated use of the technology, especially where a group of students would be exposed repeatedly to the same learning environment.

Another limitation is that we made no attempt to establish the same group during each small group discussion. Mennecke, Hoffer, and Valacich [26] suggests that established groups should have greater cohesion and therefore be more satisfied than ad-hoc groups. Possibly if the students engaged consistently in electronic discussions with the same members, they would begin to be more critical and serious in their analyses than seemed to occur in our study. In addition, the GSS technology used in this study had the anonymity feature enabled (i.e. participants were not identified during the group interactions). These group interactions, however, could have been designed in several different ways (anonymous repeating groups, non-anonymous non-repeating groups, or non-anonymous repeating groups), each of which may result in very different learning outcomes.

In addition, we did not have a means to determine if in fact all students were participating during the electronic discussion. While the instructor was observing the students during their electronic discussions and interest and participation appeared to be high among all students, it is possible that a few students may not have engaged in the discussion, but merely observed others. The potential for free-riding, in this instance, seems also likely to be related to GSS anonymity settings (where non contributors would be more easily identified in non-anonymous conditions) and established repeating groups (where other group members could, over time, apply pressure on a free-rider to increase participation).

Finally, the special nature of the task used in this research should be noted. We choose to study the effects of group interaction via GSS technology on individual learning. However, other forms of collaboration, and its outcomes are possible. Classroom tasks can be designed in such a way that the final outcome is attributable to the group, rather than the individual. The impact on learning outcomes may be different in these instances, and warrants further research.

## 5.3. Conclusion

This paper has presented the results of an experiment examining whether collaborative learning using electronic discussions for case analysis creates higher interest, higher perceived learning, and greater performance than learning in an individual constructive environment for the same task. The study found that students working collaboratively in either small or large groups showed more interest in the material and perceived themselves to learn more than students that worked individually but that students that worked individually outperformed students that collaborated in small or large groups before working individually. The findings of this study are at once sensical and suggestive. It makes sense that students enjoy collaborating more than when they work alone, but it also makes sense that, if given the same amount of time, they perform better when they work alone by virtue of the fact that they do not have to analyze and critique the ideas of others.

This research has important implications for the field of technology-enabled learning. Much of the recent focus of technology-enabled learning has been on supporting collaborative learning and the benefits of collaboration to the learner. The results suggest that an equally important aspect to consider is individual learning and, in particular, whether individuals (as opposed to groups) truly benefit from collaboration. In addition, the study suggests that students have difficulty discerning which ideas presented by peers in collaborative groups are better than others. In essence, the collaboration reduces the cognitive effort of developing unique ideas but increases the effort required to sift through a myriad of ideas from peers, some of which are much better than others. Lastly, the study implies that technology has a certain symbolic value to students $[7]$ , increasing their interest and perceived learning while potentially not increasing their actual learning. Educators may feel pressured to incorporate technology into their teaching even in cases where the technology does not benefit the individual learner directly. Clearly, there is a marked need for more research into the effective uses of technology in learning.

A great deal of time in the traditional classroom environment is spent gathering information (taking notes) rather than processing the information and assimilating the information. The traditional learning model goes from the gathering to recall stage without regard for whether the information is actually comprehended (processed and assimilated). This study was an examination of whether collaborative learning and constructive learning via case analyses can be used to supplement the traditional method and encourage the processing and assimilation phases of learning. As an early study in an emerging field, the findings from this research (as with all research) requires replication and extension before making prescriptive comments. It is our hope that this research will be helpful in building an understanding of the appropriate and successful implementation of technology and learning models within the classroom context. ## A.1. Case discussion: EIS

The new president of Southwestern University has hired your consulting company to develop an EIS for him. You are a junior consultant with the company and have been assigned to the project team. Your job is to deal with the issues related to deciding what information to incorporate into the system rather than the issues related to how to establish the technical linkages among various data sources. The President has indicated that he saw an EIS for the President of Southeastern and the President of Midwestern and he thinks he needs one too. Because he is very busy, you only can meet with him for two hours on one occasion before submitting a paper-based equivalent of what the computerized EIS would look like. When you ask him what information he needs the system to deliver, he seems to respond in an ambiguous manner stating that he wants to get a feeling for student satisfaction, for faculty performance, for departmental budgeting practices, for a comparison with other universities on dimensions such as crime, student graduation rates, student job placement rates, student recruitment (SAT scores etc.), minority enrollment and other things he should be looking at. He basically insists that you as the IT expert should provide him with all information of relevance to the university in an easily displayed manner. That's why he hired you - if he knew in advance everything he needed he could have had a staff member build the system!

Some specific things he mentioned were that he would like you to set up an anonymous list service for students, faculty, and staff to enter anonymous comments (an "electronic suggestion box"), he asks for summaries of faculty evaluations to be provided, for financial data from each department detailing how they spent their budgets (recruiting, entertainment, conference travels etc.), and for grade summaries for each faculty in each department. He also is concerned with the image of the university and is concerned that with widespread access to the Internet, students will be able to send messages considered against the culture of the university as well as access pornographic material. He therefore asks for random access to student computer accounts whereby he can occasionally examine email messages as well as comments sent to lists (a list is an electronic discussion group on the Internet that often reaches 500 + persons that "subscribe" to the list). He thinks this will help avoid the possibility of future lawsuits pertaining to abuse of the Internet.

In order to develop a prototype of the system, you need to gather some basic data so you go to the departmental chairmen and deans. You meet with unanticipated anger and refusal to give you any information concerning budgets, faculty evaluations and even student job placement. Students object strongly to the president having carte blanche to examine their email and Internet messages which they consider private. Faculty object strongly to their evaluations being made available to the president, fearing that anytime they try something new in a course and it doesn't work out as well as planned, that they will come under fire from the administration - they feel this violates their academic freedom. This is your first EIS project and you find yourself in the middle of a messy situation. What is going on and what are you going to do?
