# Research opportunities in electronic commerce

Michael J. Shaw \*, David M. Gardner, Howard Thomas

Department of Business Administration, College of Commerce, University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign, Urbana, IL, USA

## Abstract

The Web technology will move the whole economy to be information-based because of the greater efficiency it can help achieve. The rate of technology change is so rapid that emerging electronic commerce is already making fundamental changes in the economic landscape, affecting every aspect of how business is and will be conducted. The Web has made the need for decision support ever more ubiquitous because of the vast amount of business information made available by the global Web, which helps bring together the information passed between businesses, between a business and its customers, and among different departments of a business. We, who are entering a new era of enterprise computing and research in the decision support systems field, must consider this ubiquitous network business model. This paper lays out the dimensions of electronic commerce and develops a framework for identifying the important research issues associated with electronic commerce. The emphasis is on taking an interdisciplinary view that integrates technology and business modeling. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Electronic commerce; Decision support systems; Internet; Web strategy

## 1. Introduction

We are witnessing a revolution in the business world due primarily to an explosion in information technology and the resulting rapid emergence of electronic commerce (EC). Commercial activities for information gathering, shopping, trading, brokerage, banking, accounting, auditing, financing, negotiating, collaborating, marketing, supplying, partnering, training, meeting, scheduling, manufacturing, distributing, servicing, and retailing will all be changed thanks to the capabilities of the new information technology. In short, much of what we know about doing business will change. All companies, large and small, will face the inevitable challenges brought about by these technological developments. However, change creates not only risks but also opportunities. To minimize the risks and maximize the potential pay-offs, carefully thought-out research in the area becomes important.

The information revolution is drastically reshaping the business world and pushing the US toward an information-based economy. We are entering a new era in which the majority of the value-adding activities in the economy will be shifted to cyberspace through globally connected electronic networks. According to International Data, by the year 2000, overall Web-initiated sales will rise to US\$117 billion from a mere US\$1 billion in 1995 [1]. Separately, Forrester Research, an IT consulting firm, estimated that total Internet-related business will grow to US\$196 billion by the year 2000 [2]. Web technology will move the whole economy toward being information-based because of the greater efficiency it can help achieve. The rate of technology change is so rapid that electronic commerce is already making fundamental changes in the economic landscape, affecting every aspect of how business is and will be conducted. However, most companies are unclear about Web technology and how to capitalize on the potential it offers. Just putting up an electronic storefront on the Web is a first step, but an effective EC strategy requires a great deal more.

As opposed to the mainframe data processing model of the 1970s and the PC-based end-user computing model of the 1980s, the Internet and the global Web have provided opportunities for developing new business models. The Web has made the need for decision support ever more pronounced because of the vast amount of business information made available by the global Web, which helps bring together the information passed between businesses, between a business and its customer, and among different departments of a business. We are entering a new era of enterprise computing characterized by an emphasis on networking, knowledge sharing, and channel integration. Some people refer to this as the ‘ubiquitous network’ business model for its focusing on a high degree of connectivity between a company and its customers, suppliers, and channel partners. As we enter into this new era, it is imperative that researchers in the decision support systems field consider this new paradigm.

Because of their complexity, electronic commerce research issues should be addressed from two directions. First, information technology and business models need to be integrated, so that they are mutually enhancing. Second, we envision a fundamental transformation of the overall economic infrastructure of industrial organizations as a result of the continuing information technology revolution, especially in the business world. This industrial structural change will lead to enormous needs for innovative applications of new information technologies.

A key feature of electronic commerce research as a distinct discipline is its multidisciplinary perspective. In that light, the research community needs to focus on developing new managerial and engineering insights to create managerial and technological road maps of business opportunities made possible by EC. The opportunities available for research in electronic commerce include both the development of new business models for electronic commerce based on the recent technological transformation of the global

Web, and the development of the technology and business systems to facilitate the realization of EC.

## 2. Research framework

Since electronic commerce can include a wide variety of different sub-areas, it would be useful to identify a structure within which electronic commerce activities take place. In general, it is common to think of electronic commerce in terms of three sub-areas: (1) the interface between businesses to consumers, which includes electronic shopping, product marketing, information retrieval, entertainment, and customer service activities; (2) the linking of a business to its channel partners (e.g., suppliers, distributors, or retailers), which includes purchasing, order fulfillment, logistics coordination, and inventory replenishment activities; and (3) the coordination within a business, which focuses on supporting corporate activities and the integration of departmental activities.

These electronic commerce activities can be summarized into two levels: the enterprise level and the channel/customer interface level. On the enterprise level, where the Web technology is used for internal coordination and integration, the main research issues should include: managing the global networked organizations, managing core processes, such as product realization with the Web, linking producers and suppliers electronically, mass customization, business process coordination, and Web-based business development.

On the channel and customer interface level, where the Web is transforming the industrial and market structure, electronic commerce technologies can be used as digital links to connect and manage all aspects of value-added chains in the market. Business and technological issues concerning electronic banking, pricing of digital products, consumer behavior concerns of on-line shopping, security, virtual enterprises, interfirm coordination, supply-chain management, and strategic partnership are just a few of the issues to be addressed.

The scope of electronic commerce is depicted in Fig. 1, where in addition to linking with suppliers (EC5) and distributors (EC4), it also includes the interface with consumers (EC3), and the management within the enterprise (EC1). Finally, electronic commerce also needs to address the infrastructure issues (EC2), such as payment systems, network security, human-computer interface, and the information infrastructure.

One research complexity in electronic commerce research is the need for new business models suitable for the new type of products (e.g., digital), new industrial organizations (e.g., virtual organizations), and new economic structure (e.g., information based). The business models useful for electronic commerce, for instance, should be able to identify the characteristic of products especially marketable over the Web. The understanding resulting from such an analysis can then subsequently be used to design the most effective human-computer interface for marketing purposes. On the other hand, interorganizational information networks using electronic data interchange (EDI) have helped achieve better channel integration and thus make the supply chains of the traditional goods more efficient too. This point is well illustrated in the channel partnership established between Proctor and Gamble and Wal Mart.

Another example of integrating the business model and the technology is to evaluate the value-added chain for distributing digital products over the Internet. The resulting analysis can be used to design distributed transaction management systems, electronic payment systems, and security management systems for integrating the development, promotion, and distribution of digital products. In addition, the model will support the integration of agent-based technologies, intelligent human-computer interfaces, and data mining.

By its very nature, the scope of electronic commerce research is broad and encompassing. In the following sections we explore four potential electronic commerce research areas that combine business modeling and technology development. These four areas are: (1) human computer interactions in Section 3; (2) consumer behavioral analysis in Section 4; (3) enterprise and channel integration in Section 5; and (4) Web-based business strategy development in Section 6.

## 3. Human computer interaction

It is commonly recognized that how human beings interact with computers to access information over the Web still presents one of the biggest barriers for the implementation of electronic commerce because of the limited ways available for us to communicate through the Web. The human computer intelligent interaction (HCII) research area is concerned with creating more natural and effective ways for humans and computers to interface. This goal can be achieved by making it possible to exchange information between the computer and the human users through multimedia displays. One example to enhance the interface is by incorporating virtual reality (VR) [3] with 3D visual and audio displays. Other possible ways to achieve HCII include the use of natural language understanding capabilities, or getting control signals from human users to the computer through the use of speech and bare-hand gesture commands.

Some of the techniques and tools developed in this area can be useful in implementing commercial transactions between humans and computers. For instance, a good HCII design using VR technology can enrich the Web shopping experience and make it fun for the consumers, thus making electronic commerce in general and Web shopping in particular more viable.

In particular, while many people attribute the popularity of the Internet to World Wide Web browsers such as Mosaic or Netscape Navigator, there is still quite some way to go before computing, communications, and consumer electronic technologies truly support a user-friendly information appliance. Consequently, a potential candidate for the next ‘killer application’ might be the application of virtual reality to electronic commerce. Imagine sitting in your living room browsing the global Web from the VR Web TV. You search and retrieve a direct merchant’s catalogue of winter clothing. Different styles of coats are displayed by putting their image on a three-dimension digital model of you. You then can decide the specific color combination and adjustment to fit your taste. While in the VR environment, you may also want to walk around with it, getting into your car with it, and wearing it to the office, just to test its quality and suitability under various circumstances. In this fashion, a good VR interface can lead to the implementation of mass customization. You can interact with the VR display of the goods until you are satisfied with it and place the order through the Web. When the merchandise is delivered, it is highly customer-specific.

On the enterprise level, the VR product model can be used to link a wide range of business processes. For example, the VR display of the same winter coat, once finalized, can be used to specify job order sent to the company's supply-chain networks (SCNs) and schedule all the delivery of components as well as making the final products on the production lines. Similar virtual reality modeling can be used in automobile, furniture, real estate, and consumer appliance markets. The technology not only helps display products and link them to their production processes, it can also be used to coordinate the internal product development process. Instead of each team working on different product components and putting them together in a prototype, the virtual reality model can be shared among component teams, allowing simultaneous modification and reconfiguration. Simulation of this kind should incorporate the concept of telepresence. For example, a simulation model can be executed from a remote site, managers of difference units of a company will run the simulator representing the factory, and use it to make decisions related to scheduling, ordering, and purchasing. While progress toward the linking business processes is now feasible, at least technically, issues related to system integration will become increasingly important.

## 4. Consumer behavioral analysis on electronic commerce

Electronic commerce will create many opportunities to establish new electronic channels for marketing, distribution, business-to-business transaction processing, and enterprise coordination, one of the most critical aspects influencing the success of electronic commerce will be the effectiveness of the interface interacting with the consumers. To date, it is still not clear what the deciding factors will be that draw people to shop on the Web. Questions about electronic commerce strategy are still left mostly unanswered. Therefore, such questions as the following are relevant: What payment schemes will fit the behavioral characteristics of most consumers? What security level will be the best balance between risk and convenience? What are the behavioral and demographic traits in virtual communities? What are the characteristics of products that can best take advantages of a virtual reality interface? What makes Internet shopping different from mail-order catalogues or TV shopping? These research questions will be addressed by teaming commerce and engineering researchers to integrate the technological, business, and behavioral perspectives. Specifically one of the major issues confronting those concerned with electronic commerce is the issue of consumer acceptance. There are many anecdotal stories about how user/consumer resistance slowed acceptance or led to ultimate market defeat, especially for products and services that might be described as high technology or truly new, such as electronic commerce. These issues affect the future of on-line shopping [4] and product promotion on the Web [5].

An important component of the research under girding electronic commerce is the issue of user/consumer involvement with the new product and service. There are at least two research issues: The first is the perception of the product/service by the targeted user/consumer. Clearly, perception is more powerful than fact in instances covered by most products and services that fall under the broad umbrella of electronic commerce. The second issue is usage, i.e., how does the targeted user (not the technical person) actually use the product/service and what aspects of that usage hinder and/or encourage adoption and usage of the product/service? Fortunately, there is a well-established literature available to give direction to these and related questions. The broad discipline of consumer behavior has developed both the concepts and tools to address these issues. However, it should be noted that these tools and concepts are less well developed for high technology or truly new products/services. In addition, there is within consumer behavior and social psychology a research stream that specifically addresses both the issues of perception and usage, i.e., diffusion of innovation. The discipline of consumer behavior and the diffusion of innovation research stream form the basis to address these issues. More accurate measurements on the relevant perceptions that influence the adoption of electronic commerce and digital products will be needed.

Accordingly, questions such as the following are relevant. (1) How is the perception of a product and service influenced by the various elements of electronic commerce? (2) How do specific types of consumers use electronic commerce? (3) What types of electronic commerce hinder its acceptance and use by consumers? (4) What strategies will be most successful in overcoming resistance to change by consumers toward various forms of electronic commerce?

An example study includes members of various occupational and/or social/demographic groups and their usage and perception of various forms of electronic commerce. Current studies that rely primarily on self-selected responses solicited on the Internet itself do not generate valid or reliable data. There is a clear selection bias in the sample. A cross-sectional study of targeted groups with a well-drawn sample and questionnaire will not only produce better insight but generate a base line for future studies in this area.

## 5. Enterprise and channel integration

Increasingly network organizations of specialized units coordinated through electronic networks will replace the traditional hierarchical organization [6]. Because of their agility, these network organizations can be configured and reconfigured rapidly to exploit windows of business opportunities. The Web also provides new ways to coordinate workflow, manage documents, and enhance group work through the Intranet. Issues of interprocess coordination, client–server computing, and the design of firewalls are important.

While increasing use of Internet and Intranet will provide an unprecedented amount of information, it then becomes important to have means to screen all data and focus on only the most useful aspects of the information. Data mining refers to the process of sifting through a large amount of corporate data [7] (usually archival data stored in a ‘warehouse,’ as opposed to current operating data) to find nuggets of information that will provide the company with some competitive advantage. The classic example is Wal Mart’s marketing successes based on applying data mining to point-of-sale (POS) data. Wal Mart’s huge investment in the technology is not one that most companies can afford to duplicate in the current environment. Research questions include: How to best organize data in the enterprise data warehouse for efficient search; How to develop efficient and effective tools (software) for mining. A variety of machine learning techniques can be used to analyze business data and develop a better understanding of the underlying information that might be useful for decision-making purposes.

While data mining is useful for ‘sensing’ the market and the customers’ demands, the results of data mining must be directly used by the enterprise so that good, timely decisions can be made. Through combining data mining technology with vastly improved data collection, and communications capabilities, it is clear that an enterprise-wide ‘sense and response’ system for rapid deployment is emerging as the new paradigm for best utilizing new capabilities made possible by the availability of market data. The analytic outcomes will trigger the necessary product and process decisions. This requires the full integration of data mining and information retrieval operations on the one hand [8], with well-coordinated supply-chain networks on the other hand. The ability of a company to develop a globally wired business network and the related implementation as well as managerial components will determine the firm’s competitive position. An example of this may be found in the market situation where demands for a certain new product suddenly surge and the company making the product needs to be able to put together a supplier network quickly to meet the demands. This involves the capability to integrate the underlying information infrastructure and business processes quickly. For sourcing potential products and suppliers, Web-based search engines and software ‘agents’ [8] can be used to assist the search, collection, and documentation of information.

Security is another critical issue in EC. It is important that customers placing orders via the Internet be able to pay for their purchases. Customers require secure systems if they are to accept EC payment processes. Cryptography should be used to ensure network and transactional security [9]. Some experimentation with electronic banks and digital money has occurred. At this time the most convenient payment method is still by credit cards. However, just as one should be cautious about transmitting one's credit card number on an insecure phone line (e.g., a cellular phone), one should realize that at the present time the Internet is far from secure. The various types of digital cash $[10,11]$ and network payment systems $[12]$ that have emerged in recent years have made system integrity and transaction security even more important issues. How to integrate these on-line payment schemes into the financial and banking infrastructure will also be crucial in bringing forth a greater volume of electronic commerce activities.

## 6. Web-based business strategy development

The emerging global information infrastructure is creating a major transformation of many industrial organizations. Since the Web provides new channels for marketing and distribution, fewer layers may be needed in the economy's value-added chain and thus a 'delayering' effect may occur in industrial organizations [6]. At the same time, a new virtual value chain will appear and new sectors of the information-based economy will be developed. The new sectors may be typified by emerging businesses in information brokerage, electronic trading, retailing through the Internet, electronic publishing, etc., as exemplified by such new start-ups as E\*Trade, Amazon.com, and Virtual Vineyard. Of particular interest is the development of the business strategy for digital products and electronic markets [13] and how/whether information technology will be a key source for differentiation in these new markets.

New business models will also be needed to price digital products $[14]$ , such as electronic publications, videos on demand, software, and information services. These products delivered by the Internet are radically different from traditional goods in terms of their market dynamics, sensitivity to prices, perception by the consumers, economies of scale and scope, competitive conditions, and cost structures. For instance, these digital products may be characterized by increasing returns in the market place (that is, the more a product is sold, the higher rate of return it enjoys), instead of the ‘diminishing returns’ behavior (that is, when a product is sold more and more, the rate of return will start to drop because of increasing competition) demonstrated by the more traditional goods $[15]$ . A number of companies have started to charge ‘micro-payments’ for their digital products, which raise a whole new range of pricing and consumer behavior issues. The recent turmoil in the on-line user community caused by a sudden shift to the flat-rate policy by one on-line service provider (i.e., America On Line) showed how important it is to understand consumer behavior in developing new electronic markets and digital products.

Another major transformation taking place now in industrial organizations is the increasing interorganizational coordination made possible by information technology. Electronic connections linking suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, and retailers provide the basis for information sharing between channel partners to facilitate the flows of goods and to reduce the costs incurred because of inventory needs. Direct communication with channel partners, i.e., suppliers and distributors, will greatly improve the quality and responsiveness in carrying out business processes. Web technology can be used to redesign business processes that require interorganizational coordination. Example applications include: order fulfillment, sourcing, purchasing, management of accounts payable and receivable transactions, and parts servicing. Research in this area requires an integration of the Internet, Intranet (internal to the enterprise), and Extranet (linking with channel partners). Using the Web as the basis for process automation and improvement, the corporate security, integrity, and accounting soundness can be ensured by applying a structured systems analysis methodology that has been used for the management of software engineering as well as business auditing. Yet new information technologies developed for electronic commerce, such as digital signature, raise new issues for accounting and auditing research. Moreover, the incorporation of Web technology in the management of entire supply-chain networks can further enhance the integration of products and services channels, leading to the potential formation of virtual corporation [16].

## 7. Conclusion

As with all previous technological breakthroughs that change the social fabric and the life style of human beings—be it the telephone, the railroad, or electricity—Internet and the global Web have already led to many innovations and major transformations of the world economy. Although some of the changes in the economy have already taken place, this revolution, the shift to electronic commerce, is still in its infancy.

The Business School research community has been through a number of paradigm shifts in the IT area, moving from focusing on database management, model management, knowledge management, to the emerging electronic commerce based on the Web technology that is under way. This paper summarizes the important research issues from both behavioral and technological standpoints. We also argue that a host of strategic issues need to be examined. A wide range of research opportunities are identified and discussed, with special emphasis placed on four of them: (1) human computer interactions, (2) consumer behavioral analysis, (3) enterprise and channel integration, and (4) Web-based business strategy development.
