# Viewing the Web as a marketplace: the case of small companies

Gina Colarelli O'Connor $^{a,*}$ , Bob O'Keefe $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Lally School of Management and Technology, Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, Troy, NY 12180, USA $^{b}$ Department of Computer Science and Information Systems, Brunel University, Uxbridge, UB8 3PH, UK

## Abstract

Companies have rushed to set up presences on the World Wide Web. In most instances, they are advertising their products and services, and in some instances, are actually delivering them over the Internet. Many of these companies are small, and there is at least anecdotal evidence that small companies are at the forefront of commercial use of the Web. In this paper, we consider various perspectives for assessing the role and use of the Web. We argue that most small companies will view the Web as an electronic marketplace. We consider the attraction of the Web as a marketplace for small companies, and the key strategic drivers that are moving small companies to join the Web. We also suggest a number of inhibitors that will make the Web less attractive for small companies in the future. The result is a model that can be used to understand the position of small companies with respect to the Web. Despite the youth of this new medium, some marketing paradigms common to many entrants are emerging. We discuss these paradigms and give examples of their use. We briefly review some successful cases. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Web; Small companies; Marketplace

## 1. Introduction

In 1849, thousands of families joined the California gold rush and moved to northern California in the hopes of striking it rich. Some did make it rich, but most did not. However, the families that decamped there, and their descendants, had a huge impact on the subsequent development and economy of the area. The year 1995 was the World Wide Web gold rush; despite the overuse of this analogy [7,28,33], it adequately portrays the frenzy that has occurred. Thousands of companies have set up Web pages in the hope of advertising, marketing or retailing their products and services. The listing of commercial Web sites maintained by Open Market is growing at a rate of 7% per week, and grew from fewer than 2000 Web sites at the end of 1994 to nearly 20,000 at the end of 1995 [31]. (The exact number of sites listed as of January 1, 1996 was 19,940.) Some companies will prosper because of their involvement with the Web, but many will not. Like the gold rush of 1849, the 1995 goldrush will define the economic future of the Web, and shape the Web as an electronic marketplace.

Retail today is unrecognizable from the retail landscape of 20 years ago. The rapid changes in retailing have been enabled by Information Technology (IT). The use of advanced logistics and communications by Mass Merchants such as Wal-Mart [5,8,18,19], the analysis of vast quantities of scanner data [10] and the provision of store specific credit cards [9] are just three examples. Information to aid in inventory planning and control now flows through the value chain at a speed that allows retailers to act quickly upon changing customer requirements. As an example, stores that would stock for a season (such as winter clothing) and then analyze the success of sales after the season can now replenish and alter items in response to consumers on a, say, weekly basis.

The newest part of this ever changing IT-enabled retail landscape is on-line marketing, and particularly marketing through the Web. Companies ranging from two-person software developers to some multinational corporations have scrambled to set up shop. In this paper, we analyze the rapid explosion of Web-based marketing and retailing. In part, due to the low barriers to entry afforded by the Web, small companies are at the forefront of developments, and thus our focus is on small companies. We assess the pull of the Web for small companies, the factors pushing small companies to get involved, and the changes in traditional marketing paradigms resulting from the use of this medium as an electronic marketplace.

We briefly review some cases of successful use of the Web by small companies. We analyze how their marketing techniques vary from those of other retailers, and comment on how these have contributed to their success or, alternatively, not been appropriately exploited. Prior to all this, we need to consider why and how the Web is viewed as a marketplace.

## 2. The Web as inter-organizational IS versus the Web as a marketplace

A number of authors $[1,12]$ have chosen to view the Web as a form of inter-organizational IS (IOS). Following from the work of Johnston and Vitale $[17]$ , Malone et al. $[23]$ and others, it is clear that commercial use of the Web exhibits many of the traits of IOS (such that benefits must be shared for the IOS to be successful) and some of the strategic advantages (such as a reduction in the importance of physical location).

A second perspective is to consider the Web as a global Information System (IS) architecture $[22]$ . Since any computer can, in effect, be connected to any other, companies can build globally distributed Intranets based upon Web technology. Suppliers and customers can be included in some aspects of the system, similar to IOS, but typically the provider of the Web-based system integrates suppliers and customers in a way that is dependent upon their use of the system as desired. Unlike EDI relationships, usage is not (at least yet) mandatory. A good example of this is the system that Hitachi has built for its suppliers, allowing them to access Hitachi product data at will.

As Lu and Song [21] discuss, the notion of IOS does not fully translate to systems where users (particularly users than can not be pre-identified) interact with organizations. Our position is that understanding the development and success of such ‘extra organizational systems’ (using Lu and Song’s phrase) can be aided by taking a marketing perspective. A large proportion of companies on the Web are small. These firms are not building systems to maintain and strengthen relationships with current suppliers and customers through increased interaction; rather, they are building Web sites to attract new customers [26] and as a way of delivering deep information about services and products and (in some instances) the services and products themselves. Using a marketing perspective, we can identify the strategic factors associated with entering and supplying a market. While strategic approaches such as value-chain analysis can be appropriate (see, for example, Ref. [3]) for determining the survival strategy of an organization, this does not address the competitive advantage of operating in any particular market medium. Small companies (and particularly start-ups) face particular challenges in finding and keeping customers. The literature on market entry strategy offers a number of important factors to be considered as a new product is launched into a conventional market environment $[11,20]$ ; Ref. [13] additionally considers the factors that deter entry into a market.

Drawing from this research, we offer three categories of factors that can be used to analyze small business entry onto the Web. Where we differ is in the application of these factors. Previous work has focused on the launch of a single product into the physical market place; here we focus on the launch of products and services into a different exchange medium. Using these concepts, we see the Web has a number of attractors that tempt, or pull companies to enter the marketplace. Companies also have strategic drivers that push them onto the Web. Finally, the Internet can be characterized by certain dimensions that inhibit entrants, or cause entrants to withdraw. Fig. 1 lists these three influences (attractors, drivers, inhibitors), and identifies instances of each that we will consider in this paper.

## 3. Attractors

Low barriers to entry and the existence of supporting logistics have made the Web particularly attractive to small companies. We define a small company using the definition of the National Institute of Standards, namely, less than 200 employees and US\$50 million in revenue. Despite previous evidence that small companies tend to be followers in the application of IT, there is now growing evidence that IT is vital to some small companies who, unburdened by legacy systems, are innovators in a position to take advantage of IT developments [29].

As part of our research in this area, we downloaded and visited all 2966 commercial Web sites that were listed in the Open Market directory on March 18, 1995. We found that over 600 were immediately identifiable as small companies in the US that are selling products, and a similar number were marketing information and services. Another indication of this small company prevalence is that by the end of 1995 only 127 of the Fortune 1000 had Web sites [16]. Following is a description of the characteristics that we consider as attractors, or pull factors, for these organizations.

## 3.1. Large and growing market

Viewed as a marketplace, the net offers more than 30 million global consumers. Further, a recent CommerceNet/Nielsen study [4] estimates that more than 2.5 million people have already purchased a product or service through the Web. Despite criticisms that this study overestimates Web usage [15], it is clear that purchasing through the Web is growing.

Companies want a piece of what is predicted to be a very large growth area. One estimate by Forester Research puts on-line retailing sales in the US at US\$4.8 billion by 1998 [30], another by Jupiter Communications US\$73 billion by the year 2000 [6] (at present retail volume this works out at about 6% of all US retail). Predictions in the UK are even more optimistic. Others have suggested that as much as 15% of all US retailing could be performed via the net [28]. Many companies feel that they have to start working out how to gain some of this market now, although sales volumes may not be appreciable for a few years.

The costs of advertising to gain exposure to such an immense group are beyond what all small, and many medium-sized firms can bear. Further, users of the Web are a highly educated, computer-literate group, which is an attractive market for vendors of specialty, high-tech products: a typical offering of many a small firm.

## 3.2. Global presence

The call for companies to become more international has persisted for years, and the net aids this.

Existing multinational corporations like General Electric, IBM and Toyota can provide a uniform set of information and a single image that is available globally. Companies that serve small niche markets with highly specialized products now have access to potential consumers around the globe, for very low cost. Without the Web, costs of locating and selling to enough customers to make business profitable would hamper the probability of these niche companies' long-run survival. Now, a critical mass of a customer-base can be accessed. The Web provides an easy venue for very precise market targeting efforts.

## 3.3. Supporting logistics for physical delivery

The lure of the Web should not be considered in isolation from other advances in direct retailing. While many services can be delivered on the Web, most products must still be shipped. There has been a quiet revolution in distribution and logistics where companies like Federal Express and UPS can efficiently ship small quantities. Airborne Express will even manage a company's inventory, so that shipping a product may be as simple as informing your logistics company (electronically, of course) where to send a particular item.

## 3.4. Regulatory issues

Many companies need to avoid the regulation of their product and service that occurs in physical retailing. The most obvious example here is pornography, where local laws can ban distribution of material that is federally legal, or limit the way in which it is made available. There are also some perfectly legal products that are difficult to distribute because of attitudes and the association they have with morality; a good example is condoms.

## 4. Strategic drivers

What are the strategic concerns that are driving companies onto the Web? We present five key drivers that are influencing companies. We label them as drivers, or push characteristics, because they emanate from conditions of competitive intensity and a sense of not wanting to be left out.

## 4.1. Drive toward cost reduction and competitiveness through direct distribution

Many companies have entered on-line marketing so that they can ‘leapfrog’ the channel of distribution [2]. Many barriers to entry exist to small firms attempting to get their new products on store shelves. Slotting fees have arisen as a new revenue source among retailers over the past ten or fifteen years. Because of the intense competition for shelf space in retail outlets, manufacturers must pay monthly allowances that vary depending on the amount and location of the shelves that their product will occupy. Products shelved at eye level or on end-aisle displays must pay prime slotting fees. Those that occupy the bottom shelf in a grocery store, where buyers do not tend to glance, pay lower fees. Similarly, other forms of push money are required to ensure that one’s product gets attention from retail clerks, or is not stored in the back of the retail location.

The Web market is (at present) relatively unbiased—anyone with net access and a share of a Web server can set up shop. Slotting fees and push money are not required $[26]$ . Companies can offer their products and services at a cheaper rate to consumers by avoiding the problems of dealing with distributors. Costs of communication, training and positioning the product to the distribution channel are a considerable addition to overall costs, and are frequently passed on to consumers. These cost reductions allow Web retailers to increase their margins. (It must be noted, however, that new sorts of costs are incurred. Computer hardware and software, network access, and Web skills require some level of investment. At present, however, these are minimal in comparison to conventional distribution and communication costs.)

## 4.2. Reducing reliance on channel partners

Aside from the issue of cost, a very real threat to a firm's strategic positioning occurs when it must rely on intermediaries to represent, promote and handle it's products in the marketplace. Necessity requires this for most small companies, who cannot afford to send salespeople to every physical retail store, or conduct store audits to ensure that the product is properly merchandised. Now, however, the Web venue allows the manufacturer to retain control over his product until it reaches the purchaser.

## 4.3. Efficient use of advertising dollars

Given the heavy advertising costs that many small companies incur reaching local markets, on-line marketing is a dream. For as little as US\$30 a month, a company can have its Web pages hosted by an Internet Service Provider (ISP). Advertising a new virtual shop may simply involve getting listed in Yahoo, Lycos, and other on-line directories. Compare this cost to approximately US\$1000 for a half-page print advertisement in a local newspaper, or as much as US\$50,000 for a similar advertisement in a nationally recognized paper.

4.4. Direct access: responsiveness and learning about markets

The Web takes direct marketing one stage further —companies can have complete control of their catalog and marketing without being reliant on printers or postal services. Further, in a technology where they can observe the visitors to their catalog, on-line marketers can track which products are gaining the attention of most shoppers, which ones are being shopped but not bought, which ones are not comprehended, and which ones are purchased outright. This allows the retailer to vary his product mix, and to change and update his catalog's text to better match customer needs and inquiries, in a real-time environment. Physical catalogers, on the other hand, make changes to their catalogs in a batch mode. And, while they have become expert at understanding what buyers buy, they cannot track what buyers look at and question but do not buy, or what buyers bypass immediately in the catalog's pages.

## 4.5. Physical distribution opportunities

Where products and services can be distributed over the Web, the reduction in distribution costs allows companies to lower transaction costs (possibly passing some savings onto the consumer) and provide better service. The obvious example here is software retail, which is rapidly moving toward netbased distribution. The vendor does not have to produce and mail the disks containing the software, and the consumer does not have to struggle with decompression and physical installation of the code. Other products that can be distributed over the net include company reports, electronic art, and photographic images. The distinction between information-based and physical products is becoming blurred as physical goods such as photographs can exist in digital form, and many products (such as automobiles) are searched for and purchased based in part on information [32].

## 5. Inhibitors

Despite the excitement of Web-based marketing and retail, and the success of some small companies (examples to be reviewed later in this paper), there are a number of cautions that small companies would do well to consider.

## 5.1. Limited bandwidth

Bandwidth is a present and future problem with the Internet which has been exasperated by the Web. In the same way that software developers find new ways to use up the growing amount of disc space available on personal computers, Web site developers find new ways to use up bandwidth. The time to download multimedia, particularly video and sound, can be considerable. While this is a demand problem for the consumer, who does not want to wait for pages to be downloaded, it is equally a supply problem for businesses trying to locate themselves on the Web. Companies who pay for large bandwidth connections, or locate in a mall or with an ISP that can provide this, are the equivalent of ‘just off the highway.’ Companies that locate with low bandwidth connections or unreliable service providers find themselves ‘up a back road.’

In the same way that companies have relocated or set up divisions globally to take advantage of physical sea or air routes, companies will locate to take advantage of telecommunications infrastructure. For example, a company physically located in a highly regulated telecommunications environment (say, France) wishing to retail to the rest of Europe may find it expedient to locate its server in the US, where communications links to Europe are often better than between European countries. Small companies are moving to take advantage of this—a new Web-based jewelry start-up in the Northeastern US will locate its pages on a server in the mid-west where telecommunications are less expensive and access times from all parts of the US are reasonable.

## 5.2. Biases in the Web as a marketplace

Companies will have to contend with creeping biases in the Web as a marketplace. On-line services such as America On-line provide their own marketplaces, more easily accessible to AOL users than the Web. Users of Netscape now find links to certain directories and services already part of their browser. Local phone companies, who in the US are still considering their reaction to the growing use of the net (and Baby Bells are presently kept out by regulations covering long distance traffic), could provide very low cost net connections with added services such as electronic yellow pages. In the UK and other parts of Europe, Teletext is well established and many companies (particularly in the travel industry) use it as a direct marketing channel. These may be more attractive to consumers than a global entity like the Web. In the future, just setting up shop on the Web may not be enough to attract customers.

## 5.3. Technical advances as barriers to entry

HTML 2.0, the present standard, is relatively easy to use by computer literates, and is simple enough that conversion from word processors such as Word and Word Perfect is relatively trivial. The Web, however, is rapidly moving toward the much more complex HTML 3.0 and the programming language Java as de-facto tools. As time progresses, more companies will become increasingly sophisticated in their technical prowess at formulating interesting Web pages. Thus, a small company that had the skills to develop a Web site 18 months ago now finds itself lacking the new skills, and the cost of hiring such skills becomes a barrier.

## 5.4. Market segmentation

It is already apparent that the size of the Internet user community is illusionary. Small companies trying to pitch their product or service to an audience of over 30 million have found, in some case, angry reactions. The size of the market does not relinquish the vendor from understanding the market segments they want to target and who their potential customer is. Moreover, attempts to sell globally will not succeed unless cultural differences are understood. What looks like an appealing service and Web site in the US may be considered overly complex, simple, or insulting elsewhere in the world.

## 6. On-line marketing paradigms

For a company establishing a Web presence, the content and structure of that presence is paramount. Despite the youth of the medium, some marketing patterns common to many entrants are emerging, as are some categorizations of these paradigms [14].

At the simplest level, a Web page can be considered as an electronic billboard or electronic advertisement that provides information on products or services plus contact information for interested consumers. The majority of present Web pages probably fall into this category. Such advertising may be useful in niche markets where just getting the word out is important, or where a company wants to provide deeper information than can be contained in regular advertising. Consumers can also be enticed to order physical catalogs or samples.

Beer and alcoholic beverage companies, have taken this type of advertising to a new level. Molson's site, for example, provides entertainment, including the ability to take part in discussions and e-mail other visitors. Users have to register for the site, providing Molson with demographic data. Sites for specific brand names are also prevalent, including Zima and Captain Morgan Rum. (The numerous examples of advertising alcohol on the Web can be understood by the regulation driver discussed above.)

Virtual catalogs typically couple information on products or services with facilities for ordering, much like a physical catalog. However, the ordering can be done through the Web, with the user completing a form on-line. Other advantages that accrue to virtual catalogs compared to their physical counterparts include the following: (1) The catalog can be linked to inventory data, so that the user can see if an item is immediately available or not. (2) A company can immediately add new items to a catalog, without waiting for the next catalog printing. (3) Items which sell out and cannot be replenished can be removed immediately. This is especially useful for items whose supply is naturally limited, such as antiques or rare automobile parts. (4) The consumer can be provided with search facilities to quickly locate items. This is especially useful for producing catalogs with thousands of items, such as compact discs or books. Numerous examples of virtual catalogs now exist.

In a typical catalog, the product is put ‘up-front,’ and associated information about the product line or its derivation may be available elsewhere or very limited. The Web, with its cultural history of providing free information, allows catalog marketers to ‘invert’ their catalog. In this paradigm, relevant free information and services are provided to the consumer who visits the page, and it is not always apparent that a product is being sold. The user, searching for more information, may then be told about a book, or compact disc, or a financial product.

Perhaps the earliest and best example of this approach is The Real Beer Page, a start-up company which provides its users with information on microbreweries and their locations. Valuable as an information source in its own right (users can get lists of breweries in geographic areas that they define), the page also markets beer associated memorabilia such as tee-shirts.

For a business that does not want to manage a Web server itself, locating in a virtual mall is a viable option. Like a physical mall, the mall owner rents out space, draws traffic to the mall through advertising, and sometimes manages orders and order transactions. There are now over 400 established virtual malls worldwide. Like physical malls, a shop can actually locate in more than one mall.

Virtual malls, unconstrained by physical space, are becoming multifaceted venues that provide information and entertainment as well as shopping. Virtual bazaars take this a stage further—they allow members to sell, buy or trade with each other. Hence the bazaar becomes a focal point for trade and interaction between members. IndustryNet is a very successful example focusing on business-to-business relationships between small and medium sized manufacturers. Members can buy or sell, for example, a used injection molding machine.

It is a small step from running a bazaar to running an electronic market, where the retail outlet acts as an intermediary between consumers and suppliers. InsWeb is one such example—visitors can specify the necessary information for an insurance quote, and InsWeb then obtains the quotes. InsWeb presently has relationships with ITT Hartford and Blue Cross/Blue Shield of New Jersey, plus some specialist insurers. A variation of the electronic market is electronic auctions. Cathay Pacific has experimented with auctioning the seats in entire airline flights. Small companies that auction niche products are moving on-line with virtual catalogs with a view to replacing phone auctions with on-line real time auctions in the future. One such company, UK-owned but based in the US, is Teletrade, an auctioneer of rare coins and sports memorabilia.

Finally, Fig. 3 arrays these paradigms based on the level of information content and transaction processing to be managed. These two dimensions can be considered representative of total market maintenance costs. High information content market venues include catalogs, inverted catalogs, and malls. These market venues require the constant turnover of information that is relatively rich in description, detail, and perhaps graphics and even video demonstration. Low information content venues, such as billboards, remain static in response to customer orders, and only carry information about a single (or few) product or service offerings. They are used more as reminders than as education or awareness building advertising tools.

The transaction processing dimension characterizes the level of exchange activity that the market chaperones. Thus, even though catalogs give rich information about a wide variety of products, and buyers may order via the Web, the venue only allows for a single seller, and transactions may be processed on or off-line. In contrast, within bazaars, the market is a constant churn of numerous buyers and sellers seeking matches. Malls have to provide transactions facilities for numerous retail outlets and buyers. An electronic market has (potentially) the highest level of transaction management, since buyers have to be connected with market providers, perhaps using EDI or other IOS methods.

## 7. Interactive marketing

Well-organized virtual stores do not just employ a paradigm; they adapt it for their own use and provide associated facilities that result in true interactive marketing where consumer and retailer interact before, during and after the sale.

The most common tool in interactive marketing is giving consumers direct access to human know-how. Kaleidospace is a virtual art gallery that allows visitors to e-mail the artists of the work they are displaying, and have reported that most purchasers of the art have an e-mail conversation with the artist first $[25]$ . Virtual Vineyards gives tips for wine selection by a wine expert, and allows visitors to e-mail the expert. The responses are collected into a FAQ (Frequently Asked Questions) file that is often a convenient way of providing deeper information that can be driven by consumers.

Like regular physical shopping, free samples and trials are popular as part of interactive marketing. Software companies such as Oracle now let users download versions of software for a 30-day trial period. Record companies provide audio clips of their discs, and larger companies have set up entire sites for a single disc that contain clips or even extra tracks not available in store. In the future one can imagine virtual coupons that can be used at virtual stores, or printed out for physical stores.

In some incarnations of interactive marketing, a consumer or client can interact with the company to shape the service or product. Such personal marketing has existed for as long as there has been commerce, but until recently has been associated with high priced goods (such as boats or automobiles) or goods which can result in fee income for the seller or an intermediary (such as stock purchases or life insurance). The customer can inquire, specify and 'sign off' on the service or product, and do all of this within a limited time frame that is determined by the customer. Ideally, the customer can also make payment electronically. For information or digital products, the service or product can be distributed to the customer over the net. Where distribution is not possible, enough imitation material and information can be provided to have the consumer accept the physical product.

Software, financial analysis, text books, and electronic art are examples of areas where this level of interactive marketing can now be achieved. For example ESI, based in England, allows subscribers to receive real-time stock information and make stock trades though a partner called Share Link. Stocks, of course, can be traded with the physical identification of the stock changing hands after the time of the deal. Publishers can now customize text books for specific courses, and there is no reason why all or part of these can not be delivered over the net. Consumers can browse and agree on a version (in this case an electronic version) of the text, and may not need to see the physical copy prior to purchase.

Small start-up companies provide fascinating examples of new variations of products that are based on this highly interactive approach. Build-A-Card lets a user build a greeting card at its Web site; the card can then be made available as a Web page to the receiver. While rather simple at present, technological advances may make this and other efforts the preferred way of sending cards. The Cyrano Server writes love letters; the user specifies the basic elements of a letter, which is then crafted by the service and e-mailed to the user. (The service did e-mail letters anonymously for users, but has had to discontinue this.) In an interesting example of learning about markets, the Cyrano Server has found that there is a considerable demand for letters that break off relationships.

The sale of personalized apparel can be managed through this interactive approach. A consumer can submit various versions of a graphic image that they designed, see pictures of simulated shirts with the designs on them, choose a design, agree on quantity and price, and then wait for the printed shirts to arrive. This can be done now with some apparel specialists, but the Web allows this process to happen in a very short time frame between parties that may not even be aware of each other's physical location.

## 8. Small company cases

Nine Lives Clothing Store [27] is a small company in Los Gatos, California, that sells high quality used clothing, particularly designer labels. It's Web site, established in late 1994, uses agent technology to allow visitors to generate personal shopping assistants. These are used to query the inventory database, and will e-mail the user when an addition to the database matches the query stored as a shopping assistant. There is clear evidence that the Nine Lives Web site has been valuable in helping to grow the business—trade has increased every month since the Web service was established, the store is making a profit, and three local competitors have come and gone.

In terms of the factors in Fig. 1, Nine Lives is very much a case of a small company that was attracted onto the Web by the low costs and barriers to entry, coupled with the technical skills of one of the proprietors. It took nearly a year for the owners to determine the strategic value of the Web server—the ability to reach a local market. Putting the store database on-line and the personal shopping assistants allow people to browse electronically; they can then visit the store when the inventory matches their requirements. Thus, visitors to the store are more likely to make a purchase since they have already determined the availability of product.

Other small companies have been similarly attracted onto the Web, with the advantages of being on the Web only becoming clear at a later stage. Golden [12] presents Kennys Bookshop and Art

Gallery, one of many bookstores on the Web. Based in Galway, Ireland, they service a niche market—books by Irish authors, or books on Ireland, including rare and used books. They have sales of approximately US\$1.5 million per annum, and employ 22 staff. They signed a contract for e-mail with an ISP, and the provider encouraged them to move onto the Web. A virtual catalog was developed, and this is partly inverted with book reviews and news being foremost. The result has been increased sales abroad, and faster conversion of inquiries into actual sales. They now view their page as an important tool in reaching the global market for their niche product.

Other successful cases of small business use of the Web, however, do exhibit preconceived drivers. Harley-Davidson of Stamford, Connecticut (a Harley-Davidson dealership) designed a Web site with the sole intention of selling parts beyond its geographic area. Its Web page provides information on parts and an ordering form (it is not connected to any parts database; all parts are either in stock or can be back-ordered very quickly). It now sells over US\$1000 per week of parts to customers as far away as Russia and Singapore [24]. This is not an insubstantial amount to the business, and the cost of developing the Web site was recouped in a matter of months.

Software.Net was created purely to distribute software electronically. Users can download purchased software, and then receive a 'key' via e-mail that can be used to make the download file self-extract. Electronic distribution of software eliminates some mailing costs (although documentation must often be mailed) and the costs associated with preparing the disc-based copy of the software. Moreover, Software.Net provide corporate accounts where software can be downloaded by individuals and the corporation is billed. Their strategy is to share the reduction in transaction costs with buyers, and hence they provide software at comparatively low prices. Their site is a vast virtual catalog comprised of over 35,000 pages and 9000 products. They ship over 5000 piece of software a week.

## 9. Conclusions

We have argued that small company presence on the Web is best understood by viewing the Web as a marketplace to which small companies are driven and/or attracted. We have presented a simple model that is derived, in part, from previous work in marketing. A number of on-line marketing paradigms have emerged, ranging from simple on-line billboards through virtual catalogs to the establishment of bazaars or markets. We have enumerated these paradigms, and identified information content and transaction processing as two dimensions that can be used to categorize on-line marketing.

Brief analysis of some cases suggests that there is no one path to success: small companies take to the Web for varied reasons with varying marketing paradigms. What paradigm to adopt is a present and future challenge for small companies, especially in the face of growing inhibitors such as the increasing technical sophistication of Web technology. Table 2, however, suggests that use of technology can be highly innovative; Nine Lives may be the first commercial use of agent technology resident on the Web, and Software.Net is one of a number of companies reinventing the distribution channel for software.

The future of the Web as a marketplace that continues to attract small companies is not guaranteed. The sheer volume of companies on the Web may make the marketplace inefficient. Customers will resort to search tools and guides, and thus be in a position to be influenced by the advertising and links provided by such tools. Local phone companies may yet effect the marketplace through widespread adoption of electronic yellow pages. The challenge for the future will not be how to set up shop on the Web, but how to use the right strategies to gain, attract and keep customers.

Numerous research issues arise from the work presented here. First, it is clear that the definitions of various types of IOS, and their major characteristics, is now highly varied due to the rise of open systems and the Web. The rate of innovation and diffusion appears to be outstripping our ability to comprehend and analyze these systems. Second, as technology becomes an ever increasing part of product delivery and IT-based services, perspectives from marketing can provide novel insights. The research interaction between IS and marketing has been limited to this point in time. Third, the innovative nature of some small company use of the Web raises question about our traditional view of small companies as IS followers. At least in the area of on-line marketing, there are some innovative leaders.
