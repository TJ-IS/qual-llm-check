# A framework for the classification of DSS usage across organizations

Frederic Adam <sup>a,)</sup>, Martin Fahy <sup>b</sup>, Ciaran Murphy <sup>a</sup> ´ ´

<sup>a</sup> Dept. of Accounting, Finance and Information Systems, UniÕersity College Cork, Cork, Ireland b Dept. of Accountancy and Finance, UniÕersity College Galway, Galway, Ireland

## Abstract

The research project reported in this paper constitutes an attempt to build upon existing research in the DSS area, namely the well-known framework for DSS developed by Gorry and Scott Morton. The authors put forward a revised framework which researchers in the field can use to classify DSS applications meaningfully and make comparisons across large samples of systems and organizations. The findings of a three year action research study were used to develop a framework for the classification of DSS usage which was then tested empirically. The organizations studied were classified based on the extent to which they used DSS for different decision situations using two specific measurements: DSS spread and DSS complexity. The results obtained suggest that the framework which was developed in this research is a useful vehicle for categorizing the degree of maturity of organizations regarding their usage and development of actual DSS applications. q 1998 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Decision making; Decision support systems; Classification of information systems; DSS applications; Action research

## 1. Introduction

Researchers in information systems have been concerned with the apparent increasing fragmentation in IS research 34 . The reasons for this frag-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> mentation stem from the multi-disciplinary and multi-issue nature of the IS field which results in the existence of many diverging strands of research 27 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> This has not helped to establish the IS field as a coherent discipline either academically or in practice <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 5 . Indeed, some researchers have even suggested that the fragmentation of the IS field might be more voluntary, almost self-inflicted:

The authors we reviewed seemed almost compelled to distinguish their work from related work rather than emphasize areas of continuity and cumulativeness 16 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

This preoccupation with differentiation and with investigating the most recent phenomena has made it difficult for the IS field to build up a cumulative body of research that is characteristic of other fields such as economics and physics 17 . Lucas has sug- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> gested that, as a field, we need to think about interesting problems and look for underlying issues rather than focus on today’s ‘hot topic’ to keep up with the latest IS fashion 19 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

This paper attempts to contribute to the accumulation of research in the DSS area by investigating the Gorry and Scott Morton framework for DSS 7 —one<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> of the most well established and widely referenced frameworks in the IS field 10,15,28 , and develop-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ing it in the light of the research and practice that has been carried out in DSS since its introduction 8 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> This paper is based on an action research project of implementation of DSS applications in one organization and the extension of these findings to a survey of 17 organizations. Results indicate that our revised framework is a useful vehicle to analyze the level of sophistication of organizations regarding their usage of DSS systems 1 and to make comparisons be- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tween organizations as opposed to just between DSS applications.

## 2. A framework for the analysis of decision making

The DSS area is probably the most widely researched in the Information Systems field 34 , and is<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> one that continues to be a focus for information systems researchers 9 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Many of the concepts and definitions of DSS are based on the pioneering work of Anthony Gorry and Michael Scott Morton. In their seminal article ‘A Framework for Management Information Systems’,

Gorry and Scott Morton developed a framework that has become the foundation stone for much of the research work in DSS 8,7 . In developing their<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> framework they made use of the work of Anthony on the three levels of managerial activity and Simon’s work on programmed and non-programmed decisions 3,29,30 . Gorry and Scott Morton used the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Anthony framework to show effectively how the total systems approach could not work in organizations because the information requirements of the three different activities were sufficiently diverse.

Having analyzed decisions in terms of Anthony’s categorization of managerial activity and Simon’s classification of programmed and non-programmed decisions, they defined structured decision systems as those used to support fully structured problems where it is possible to specify algorithms or decision rules that allow the problem to be found, alternative solutions to be designed and the best solution to be selected. They compared these with decision support systems which are designed to support unstructured or semi-structured problems defined as problems where little or nothing is known about the problem situation 7 . The well-known framework they put <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> forward is shown in Fig. 1.

The Gorry and Scott Morton framework has been and continues to be the fundamental concept underlying most definitions of DSS. As Kirs et al. described it

The Gorry and Scott Morton framework is perhaps the best known, most durable and most frequently cited in the IS field 15 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Keen argued that, as a bridging framework between research and practice, the framework of Gorry and Scott Morton made a real and continuing contribution 14 . The importance of the Gorry and Scott <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Morton framework has also been recognized by Wood and Wood Harper who argue that the literature on Information Technology IT and managerial Ž . decision-making has always been dominated by Anthony and Simon, the two axes of the Gorry and Scott Morton framework 35 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Gorry and Scott Morton suggested that a framework such as the one they were proposing was essential for IS:

A framework which allows an organization to gain perspective on the field of information systems can be a powerful means of providing focus and improving the effectiveness of the system efforts 7 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Their framework was seized upon by researchers of DSS as a key to accurately defining the concept of DSS. As a result, DSS researchers have used their framework from the seventies right through to the nineties; with Kirs et al. offering an empirical validation of the framework as recently as the late 1980’s <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 15 .

However there has been some criticism of their work 1,2,14,22,23,33,35 . Stabell 33 noted that the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> degree of structure of a given decision was affected by two important contingencies: the structure of the environment of an organization and the nature of the relationship between means and ends—i.e. the important variables—within a particular industry. Moore and Chang further argued that

DSS scholars have attempted to apply this structured<sup>r</sup>programmed distinction as a criterion for classifying potential DSS problem areas—the conventional wisdom being that a DSS is appropriate for semi-structured decision problems. . . . As we see Ž . it, a problem can only be considered more or less structured with regard to a particular decision maker, or group of similar decision makers, and at a particular point in time 22,23 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

They concluded that it was both incorrect and misleading for DSS designers to speak of structured or programmed problems in the general case when approaching a DSS design situation.

This view was endorsed by Keen. He stated

the Gorry Morton ‘model’ of structured, semi-structured and unstructured tasks which played a key role in launching the decision support systems movement is simply wrong 14 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Keen’s contention is that the ‘famous’ framework wrongly treats structure as an inherent characteristic of the task or problem tackled by the manager, whereas any task can be made more or less structured by redefinition of the objectives pursued or the social context of the decision 14 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Other researchers attempted to provide alternative classifications of decision types based on a number of attributes such as the stimuli that evoked them or the type of solution they required. Mintzberg et al. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 21 summarized much of this research. They distinguished between opportunity decisions ‘‘those initiated on a purely voluntary basis, to improve an already secured situation’’ 21 and crisis decisions<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> which are essentially reactive to intense pressure from uncontrollable factors. Between these two extremes, they identified problem decisions which are evoked by milder pressures.

Mintzberg et al. 21 also distinguished between<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> decisions based on the nature of the solutions that addressed the problem. They identified the four following types:

1. decisions that are addressed by fully developed solutions that are available at the start of the process,

2. decisions for which ready made solutions are discovered during the process,

3. decision that required specific solutions developed specially for the circumstance and

4. decisions where ready made solutions can be adapted to fit a particular situation.

Hickson et al. 11 have also carried out extensive<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> empirical studies of decision making processes. They noted that a unique model of decision making is unlikely to cater for all decision topics and all organizational situations. They put forward a model of strategic decision making which uses complexity and politicality to describe the specificities of particular decisions, but argued that organizational specificities should equally be taken into account. Hickson et al. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 11 concluded their study of 150 decision making process with a warning for researchers in the area of managerial decision making:

. . . rather than eye-catching organizational styles of decision making, there seem to be differences due to the topic under decision, the complexity of the problem, and the interests being accommodated 11 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

But the results of all this research have not, so far, been considered in the context of DSS research and very little empirical work has been conducted that was aimed at adapting this work for the study and classification of DSS applications. As noted by Philippakis and Green, IS research has failed to provide a comprehensive enterprise wide view of decision support activities and:

A conceptual gap exists in identifying, let alone planning or managing, the total DSS function in the organization 26 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Thus, the Gorry and Scott Morton framework stands as it was originally developed in 1971.

## 3. Research aimed at developing the framework

The objective of our research was to develop a framework which, while building on the work of Gorry and Scott Morton, would take account of the specific situation of an organization and would allow an assessment to be made of the extent to which DSS personnel had impacted and could impact significantly on their organization.

Much of the reported empirical DSS research has focused on the development of a once-off system rather than on a number of systems over a period of time in one organization. Reporting on one system does not give an insight into how DSS is actually being used within an organization nor does it help in developing a framework for DSS. Indeed, only practice in a real organizational context could support such a goal for, as was stated by Leavitt 18 : <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ‘‘management is not a one-off activity’’ and neither is DSS. We therefore need to understand DSS as an integrated aspect of organizational life. In addition, relying on research into individual systems makes it more difficult to make comparisons between DSS applications developed in different organizations.

To overcome this problem the approach taken in this study was to undertake an action research pro-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>1</sup> ject 4,36 in the New Ireland Bank . The project involved the development of four decision support systems over a three year period. In essence the researchers became the DSS personnel within the organization. The decision support systems and the issues associated with their development are summarized below. The experience and understanding gained were used to guide the formulation of a framework which was developed for the classification of DSS applications. The relevance of the framework was then tested by investigating the DSS systems in use in a sample of 17 organizations which all had several years of experience with DSS.

## 4. The action research

The action research was undertaken within the New Ireland Bank. This is part of a large financial services group, First Ireland Financial Services Company. The group is organized on a geographic basis, with Ireland divided into five areas which are in turn split into a number of regions. Each region has a number of branches, typically between twenty and thirty. While the majority of policies are formulated at Group Headquarters in Dublin considerable autonomy is given to the areas in running their business. The New Ireland Bank is one of the areas and is headed by a General Manager to whom four Assistant General Managers report directly. These, in turn, have a large number of managers reporting to them.

The work with the New Ireland Bank commenced when the management approached the researchers suggesting that a number of problems that the bank faced could be addressed more thoroughly if they had better quality information.

Following initial discussions it was agreed that the researchers would first develop a system known as ‘The Company Database System’ to expand the analysis capability of the company’s executives and support staff in monitoring the company’s branches.

In the discussions with senior management a number of new performance measures were identified which were more market oriented than their existing measures.

During the development of the system the managers were committed but cautious. As far as they were concerned the system that was being developed was not a once-off system. They were more interested in establishing a long term relationship with the researchers with a view to they developing systems or giving advice as appropriate. The principal implementation role of the researchers was one of advocacy, i.e. explaining, in managerial terms, the possibilities management had for using the system to address different problems.

Six years after it was implemented, senior managers were still using the system with some modifications. The strategy of developing a reasonably ‘safe’ system worked well and it allowed the mutual understanding between the research team and the management personnel to develop.

The second system developed was technically similar to the first in that it was a flexible reporting system that allowed management to specify their information requirements and the format in which the information was to be presented. It was developed for the Credit Department of the New Ireland Bank which is run by a Controller and has responsibility for the issuing of major loans and monitors the lending performance of branch managers. The Credit Controller, when asked for the type of system he wished to have, replied ‘‘I want the type of system that was developed for the Company Database with the requirements that I have specified’’. Following further iterations, the requirements that the Credit Controller specified were to provide:

1. a system with the capacity to perform exception reporting

2. a system with the capacity to perform trend analysis

3. new information which would allow the profile of a branch to be assessed and analyzed over the past thirteen months;

4. a graphic capability

5. a flexible report writing system.

As in the first system, the emphasis was on providing managers with the option to specify and easily change the type and format of report and analysis that they required.

The first two systems which were developed were ones for which much of the data and the analysis required could be specified after initial investigation. The next two systems came from processes which were quite different.

The third system was developed for one of the assistant general managers. It emerged out of a process where the researchers interacted with the assistant general manager and his staff to identify problems which needed to be addressed and established how these might be tackled. The manager’s requirement was to be capable of assessing the performance of the branches under his control. He required that this assessment should be a weighted view of a number of factors. The system was developed using a prototype approach. Its main features were the following.

1. An exception report detailing the branches performing poorly within the region based on a criterion that could be imputed.

2. A ranked branch performance analysis calculated by assigning a set of weights to a number of performance measures. The weights are entered by the branch staff and reflect the relative importance of these measures.

3. A summary analysis containing a six year profile of branch ranking based on overall weighting performance scores.

4. A graphic facility which provided a generic graphing capability.

The problems addressed by the system were serious issues that had confronted management for some time and ones that they had difficulty resolving.

The fourth system was of a fundamentally different type to those previously outlined. It evolved out of senior management’s needs to implement a new market segmentation program. Under this program, the various products and services which New Ireland Bank were offering were to be differentiated largely on the basis of customer age and occupation. Management were particularly concerned with planning for this program and felt that they had no reliable efficient way of accessing information relevant to a segmented marketing strategy. The researchers spent considerable time determining an appropriate way to address the issue. The process followed was very much an exploratory one with both researchers and managers required to come to grips with a difficult problem for which there was no obvious solution or relevant internal information.

One of the suggestions which was studied by the development team was that use would be made of external information such as that available from census data. It was thought that this information, coupled with managers’ experience, would enable them to make a good assessment of their needs. It was agreed to develop a computer based system which would provide demographic information on target markets.

M.A.P.S. Market Analysis and Planning SystemŽ . is the computer based system which was developed to support such strategic market analysis and planning. It uses computer mapping facilities to enable managers to define and analyze specific target markets using demographic data derived from the census of population. M.A.P.S. allows the managers to call up maps of various geographical market regions to outline branch catchment areas using the directional keys on the keyboard or a light pen. The manager can then access a range of demographic data and graphs for the area outlined including population of the catchment area with breakdown by age, sex, economic status, socio–economic group and education levels.

For the New Ireland Bank, M.A.P.S. is particularly useful in comparing the total population of a defined area with the company’s customer numbers to assess its overall penetration of the market, identifying new target markets and assisting with new product development.

## 5. A framework for the problems addressed

The four systems which were developed in the organization studied were found to be broadly similar in the terms of the Gorry and Scott Morton framework. Firstly, the systems in question, were used by all levels of management and secondly, all of the problems which were addressed were ones that were viewed as important by senior management and they would have fallen in between the semistructured and unstructured decision areas of the Gorry and Scott Morton framework. Thus, this framework was not very helpful in differentiating between the problems. Yet, the managers were convinced that the help given by the researchers was fundamentally different for the different applications. In particular, they viewed the help given in developing the third and fourth systems as being of much greater importance than the first two.

When we reviewed these problems with management, two characteristics of the problems facing managers were identified which had an influence on the nature of the contribution made by the systems to the management of the company and the way the development of a DSS could be envisaged. They were the novelty of the problem and its specificity.

Novelty in this instance refers to the prior experience which the management in the company had with this type of problem. March and Olsen have suggested that novelty is not a property of a situation so much as it is our reaction to it; and the most standard organizational response to novelty is to find a set of practices that can be used 21 . March 20 has put forward a number of observations that explain how organizations, while relying on a limited set of ‘stable, routine processes’, are able to tackle a wide variety of new situations and give the impression that they changed themselves drastically to adapt to very significant changes 20 . This factor and its<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> description by March and Olsen can be seen to be consistent with the objectives set for DSS by Gorry and Scott Morton and later researchers 7,12,31 ,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> namely to increase the abilities of managers to react faster and with more certainty to a variety of situations.

In addition, using novelty as a factor enables us to overcome the criticisms that Moore and Chang had made of the Gorry and Scott Morton attempt to clarify problems generically in terms of sheer structure. Thus the first system, the Area Database System, was used to address a problem with which managers had considerable previous experience, whereas the MAPS system was used to address issues which were new for the managers. In this last case, they had no specific prior experience of the kind of information required for the support of the strategic market analysis which was envisaged.

The second factor we identified as influencing the nature of the contribution made by DSS systems to the management of a company, the specificity of the problem, is partially related to the first. Specificity, in this instance, relates to the ability of management to identify the variables that play an important role in a problem and to explain how they operate. This, then, enables them to set out the appropriate specifi cations for the problem and guide the DSS designer.

Drucker has suggested that the specifications must include specific answers regarding the objectives that the decision must achieve, the minimum goals it has to attain and the conditions it has to satisfy 6 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Besides these goal-type specifications, there are issues relating to the ability of management to identify the information that they need to address the problem. In the case of the New Ireland Bank, management were able to specify fairly completely the parameters of the problems which the Area Database System and the Credit Analysis System were designed to address. The Branch Performance and MAPS systems were used to address problems which management could only partially specify. This was obviously partly because of the nature of the problems and partly because of their novelty.

## 6. Generalizing the framework

The framework which was developed for the New Ireland Bank was evaluated in 17 other organizations. This was done by means of interviewing senior managers and DSS personnel in each of the companies. The managers were those who participated in the major decisions of their organizations and hence were in a position to assess the extent to which their DSS staff helped in the decision making processes of their organizations. The DSS personnel were those identified by the managers as having helped them in tackling some of their important decisions. Eighty percent of the DSS staff came either from the Finance area or the IS department. All companies in the sample claimed to have experience of using decision support systems for at least five years. ## 6.2. Complexity of the problem

A further examination of Table 4 shows that there are also differences between the companies in terms of the complexity of the problems which DSS personnel were asked to help address. For example in organizations B, D, F and J, the DSS staff are used to address the most complex problems on a regular basis.

To codify this information, the following approach was taken. Table 5 was used to differentiate between three fundamental types of DSS applications. This gave an indication of the relative complexity of the different types of problems we investigated. Type 1 problems were regarded as problems of low complexity due to they being at least partially specifiable and<sup>r</sup>or to they occurring relatively frequently in the organization. By contrast, Type 3 problems were regarded as problems of high complexity because of the difficulty of attempting to specify them and<sup>r</sup>or the infrequency at which they had occurred in the organization.

If an organization was present in two of the three Types 3 cells, then it was classified as a Type 3 organization. The results for the remaining organizations were then analyzed to see if they were present in two of the three Type 2 cells and, if so, they were classified as Type 2 organizations and so on. ## 7. Discussion

The analysis of the findings for the seventeen organizations suggests that the framework which we have evolved from the work of Gorry and Scott Morton Table 1 is a viable and valuable one. LikeŽ . the Gorry and Scott Morton framework, it allows the classification of potential DSS problems and the applications developed towards solving them. However, it has a number of advantages over the Gorry and Scott Morton framework. The factors that are used for the two axes of the framework, viz. novelty and specificity of problems, allow for it to be applied to the particular circumstances of an organization unlike the generic framework put forward by Gorry and Scott Morton. In particular, it enables researchers and practitioners to assess the degree to which DSS has been institutionalized in any specific organization—that is to say to identify the extent to which the involvement of DSS personnel in solving the problems facing an organization has become an accepted procedure just like the involvement of accountants is required in justifying an investment or putting together the budget of a department 24 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> This is important because the degree of institutionalization of information systems in modern organizations is an important basis for the establishment of new directions for the future of the IS field 24 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Furthermore, the use of objective scores for the spread of DSS usage and the complexity of the problems tackled by DSS personnel in the organizations studied makes the assessment of their overall level of expertise with DSS much more reliable and refined. It enables a more reliable comparison across a large sample of companies.

Our results also show that the framework which was developed in this study is a useful vehicle for categorizing the degree of maturity of organizations regarding their usage and development of actual DSS applications. Unlike the Gorry and Scott Morton framework, it is not based on the premise that there are generic types of decisions but rather it allows for the uniqueness of each organization’s experience and expertise with the problems that they encountered in specific decision situations. Furthermore, unlike the Gorry and Scott Morton framework, it is organizationally oriented rather than focusing on a particular type of problem. As such it enables the meaningful comparison of the success of decision support personnel and the complexity of decision support systems use across organizations, which was put forward by a number of authors as an essential condition for the progress of the DSS field 25,26 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Based on such an approach to evaluating the maturity of organizations with DSS, it may now be possible to analyze DSS usage across wide samples of organizations and to establish with some accuracy how far the DSS movement has actually gone. A number of phases might be identified in the learning process of organizations with DSS. The comparison between different companies at the same stage might reveal the problems that the various organizations are faced with when attempting to generalize the usage of DSS either in relation to the spread of DSS usage or to the complexity of the problems tackled. Based on these findings, researchers can aim at providing guidelines that will enable organizations to progress with DSS and at taking existing research on the development of DSS one step further 13,26,32 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

## 8. Conclusion

We believe that the framework we developed and tested in this study is a significant development of previous work in the DSS area. In particular, it puts forward a viable alternative to the Gorry and Scott Morton framework; the most widely cited framework in the IS literature. Our framework suggests that focusing on the role of DSS personnel in organizations is, potentially at least, a much richer vein for DSS research than focusing on specific types of problems or individual decision support systems.

Further research should attempt to verify that, as organizations use DSS applications to support their problem solving, their knowledge of these problems increase to such an extent that these problems be come increasingly specifiable for the organization. Some research suggests that organizational learning takes place when DSS becomes institutionalized in organizations 24 , but the application of our frame- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> work to longitudinal studies of DSS usage could confirm this finding and give a more accurate indication of the extent to which organizations can benefit from the implementation and refinement of decision support applications.
