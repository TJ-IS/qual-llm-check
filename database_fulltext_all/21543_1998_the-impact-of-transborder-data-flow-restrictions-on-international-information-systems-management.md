## Abstract

This research investigates the effects of transborder data flow TDF restrictions on the information systems ISŽ . Ž . management strategies and costs of multinational corporations. An empirical study was designed to investigate several research hypotheses dealing with the impact of TDF restrictions on IS structure and strategies across different industries. Statistical results indicate that TDF restrictions will significantly increase IS costs and influence MIS decisions overseas. To alleviate the problem, MNCs are adopting distributed approaches to manage their international IS. These findings allow some important implications to be drawn for both practitioners and researchers dealing with this pressing issue. q 1998 Elsevier Science B.V.

## 1. Introduction

In an international business, it is possible for business activities to be dispersed among several countries or consolidated within a few selected countries. The idea that information technology should support the business strategy of an organization is not new to the domain of international business 3 .<sup>w x</sup> To successfully establish an international information system to facilitate transfer of data across borders, multinational corporations MNCs must realizeŽ . that they are operating 1 within and among differ- Ž .

ent sovereignties, 2 under different social, eco-Ž . nomic, cultural, and legal climates, 3 with people Ž . of different value systems, and 4 in national mar-Ž . kets varying in structure, population, and area 16 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Although recent advances in information technology IT have made the exchange of transborder dataŽ . flow TDF technically feasible, MNCs performing Ž . such exchanges are frequently impeded by laws and regulations of the countries involved. Compounding the situation is the lack of uniformity of these laws and regulations among nations. For instance, some countries do not allow outbound TDF of information on citizens unless the destination countries have better or equivalent data protection laws 29 . Other <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> countries require that at least some data processing be done within the country before the data can be moved out of the country through private, leased lines 15 . Additionally, many countries have en-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> forced regulations to restrict all outbound TDF subject to approval by government-recognized security officers 20,23,24 . In general, the US favours the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> free flow of information across its borders 22 while<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> the European and some third world countries prefer regulation of TDF 9,22,25 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

TDF restrictions can have many detrimental implications for the MNCs. These include: impeding organizational strategic decision making, increasing IT costs due to duplication of IT resources, boosting opportunities for data privacy violations during transmission, and adding staff to ensure compliance with existing TDF regulations 25,29 . The issue of<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> TDF is heightened as the impact of converging communications and computer technologies become more pervasive. MNCs no longer assume that they can take advantage of economies of scale and cost efficient telescoping of time and distance through telecommunications and IT. As studies such as Ref.Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 21 indicated that TDF fostered social development. and economic growth, the issues for international debate on TDF have been extended from cultural integrity, national sovereignty, and privacy protection, to economic advantage.

## 2. Background

In 1970, the Data Protection Act of the State of Hesse was passed in West Germany. It was the earliest law that dealt with the privacy and security of data prepared and processed by computers 23 . In<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 1980, this legislation became the basis on which the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development OECD developed guidelines for protecting Ž . privacy of the individual across national borders. This was the first multinational action of its kind to restrict MNCs in the transmission of data internationally. Later, more intergovernmental organizations were formed to define security regulations concerning TDF. Such organizations include the Council of Europe COE , the Intergovernmental Bureau of In-Ž . formation IBI , the International Telecommunica- Ž . tion Union ITU , the United Nations Educational,Ž . Scientific, and Cultural Organization UNESCO , theŽ . European Community EC , and the InternationalŽ . Telecommunications User Group INTUG 20 .Ž . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

The issues falling under the TDF classification are both broad and vague. Generally speaking, the issues have developed along three tracks—political, privacy, and economic 2,6,13,29 . Political track re-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> strictions concern the flow of information pertaining to national resources, economic development plans, government operations, and national security. Privacy track restrictions concern the transmittal of personal data and the creation of corporate databases of competitor information. Economic track restrictions concern the flow of information that might impede a country’s social development and economic growth.

Although the effects of TDF restrictions have been widely discussed and debated, very few empirical studies were conducted to investigate their impacts on MNCs. In a survey conducted by Kane and Ricks 12 on 138 US MNCs, only 30.9% of them<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> believed TDF restrictions were a problem. However, a majority of them 62.5% considered the restric-Ž . tions to be more of a potential problem than a current concern. Sambharya and Phatak 22 chal- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> lenged the findings of Kane and Ricks and believed the magnitude of the impact of TDF restrictions on MNCs were not fully investigated in the empirical study. In recent years, the extent of TDF concerns were intensified as more international organizations were formed to oversee global information exchanges, more regulations were established by host countries to protect their economic rights, and additional advancements were made in IT to support TDF. These developments have prompted the need to again investigate if, in fact, current TDF restrictions are affecting MNCs in managing their IT investments.

## 3. Hypotheses

## 3.1. Industry type

Literature in international studies indicates that not all organizations are equally influenced by TDF restrictions. Service MNCs depend more on international information and are expected to be more affected by TDF regulations. Manufacturing and nonservice MNCs, on the other hand, have less experience with TDF and are less concerned with the potential limitations of TDF restrictions on international data transmission 4 . However, other theory<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> explains that the difference may be related to MNC’s direct investments overseas 8 . Manufacturing MNCs<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tend to have more foreign direct investments than service MNCs, even though services may operate in various different countries. Consequently, such manufacturing MNCs demand that more information be communicated to support their management processes and are thus more vulnerable to TDF restrictions.

H1b: The impact of TDF restrictions is greater on service MNCs than non-service MNCs.

Not only did theoretical research suggest conflicting perspectives on the impact of TDF restrictions on MNCs, but empirical research has also produced contradictory findings in this regard. However, others such as Refs. 11,22 did not find any signif- Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. icant difference in the assessment of the impact of TDF regulation on service and non-service firms. ## 3.2. IS costs

Conforming to the myriad of TDF regulations will significantly increase MNC’s IS operating costs <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 5,11,25 . To ensure transborder data meet the basic principles of the OECD guideline, MNCs have to hire independent consulting firms or certified security officers to perform thorough EDP audits 20,23 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> This is a very costly procedure because of the amount of data that needs to be transmitted on a regular basis. Additionally, TDF restrictions prohibit nonhost country processing of data and require MNCs to maintain databases within the host countries. Such restrictions can translate into significant costs as MNCs respond by creating additional IS departments, investing additional IT resources, and hiring extra data processing personnel. Another serious impact from TDF restrictions results from the requirement that IT equipment utilized within the host countries’ borders also be manufactured within its borders. Countries like Brazil, Ireland, Canada, and Germany are on the complaint list for requiring MNCs to use locally made equipment and software <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 7 . This restriction often forces multinational companies to invest in more expensive and potentially lesser quality locally produced equipment 10 . The <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> consequence can again translate into significant costs not only with initial set-up but also with respect to ongoing operation and maintenance. Currently, little is known about how TDF restriction might impact an MNC’s data processing costs in their affiliates. The following hypothesis is postulated to provide an insight to this issue:

H2: TDF restrictions increase the cost of managing international IS.

The effect of TDF restrictions on an MNC’s IS structure has been discussed widely. Several researchers such as Refs. 1,6,8,20 stated that the Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. formal IS structure of the organization is a key factor in determining the success of TDF management. Ahituv et al. 1 suggested that IS structure can be<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> classified into three categories—centralization, decentralization, and distribution. Centralized IS refers to the centralization of all computing activities and resources such as hardware, software, databases, Ž and IS personnel in one location, normally at the. MNC’s registered native country. Decentralized structure is the least centralized, with the control of data, application development, maintenance, and all other computing activities decentralized to the host country. Distributed IS is designed with the same basic configuration as centralized systems, but with local IS units having their own computing capabilities, storage devices, and databases. With distributed structure, the headquarters IS unit is ultimately responsible for the control and the coordination of processing activities at all corporate levels.

## 3.3. IS structure

Currently, empirical findings on the impact of TDF restrictions on IS structure are inconclusive and somewhat contradictory. Some researchers 11,17<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> have indicated that a decentralized IS structure can accommodate TDF regulations more easily than other IS structures. A decentralized system can also better accommodate cultural, social, political, and economic differences among the host countries’ legal provisions. Other researchers 13,19,27,28 suggest<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> that a centralized system is preferable over other IS structures as centralized IS allows MNCs to strive for global efficiencies and the opportunity to harvest worldwide economies of scale toward a global IS solution. However, there are other researchers who found distributed structure more appropriate due to its ability to foster quicker response time, lower operating costs, improved data integrity, increased reliability, and resource sharing.

Due to the conflicting findings in the correlation of TDF restrictions and IS structure, we postulate the following hypothesis to explore whether:

H3: TDF restrictions have unequal impact on MNCs with different IS structure.

## 3.4. Decision structures

Sambharya and Phatak 22 reported in their study<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> that TDF restrictions did indeed affect the strategic, administrative, and operational IS decisions. Waples and Norris 29 also noted this concern and believed<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> such effects could damage an MNC’s competitive position in overseas countries. However, little has been done to explore empirically how TDF restrictions impact the effectiveness of strategic, administrative, and operational decisions.

TDF restrictions have also affected the pattern and distribution of decision making processes. Organizations were reported to have adjusted the distribution of decision making processes overseas to deal with the restrictions imposed on TDF 1 . In practice,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> three major strategies of distribution of IS decision making were employed: centralized, distributed, and decentralized. Centralization implies the concentration of decision making power totally within a centralized IS unit at the corporation’s native country; decentralization implies that decisions are made within various IS units in different countries and at various levels in the organizational hierarchy. Distribution of decision making power lies in the middle of the decision continuum, with major decisions made at the corporate central IS unit in the nativeŽ . country and other decisions made locally at the IS units overseas 1,5,14 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

The researchers appear to have agreed on the trade-offs of IS decision structures: centralization affords greater efficiency and standardized controls as well as organizational integration, decentralization provides local decision makers greater control and responsiveness to local business decisions, while distribution offers greater flexibility and encourages cooperation between decision makers of different countries 5 . However, some researchers argue that<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> centralization, though efficient in terms of time and effort, can cause information overload in the communication channels when decision structure decreases <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 28 . Additionally, centralized problem solving structure relies on information imported from overseas, which may affect the quality of decisions due to delay and<sup>r</sup>or restrictions of data transmission. Decentralization and distribution, on the other hand, are less susceptible to the influence of TDF restrictions due to their flexible and local-oriented decision making approach. To investigate how TDF restrictions affect IS decisions and impact IS decision distribution, the following propositions are postulated:

H4a: TDF restrictions affect the strategic, tactical, and operational IS decisions of MNCs;

H4b: TDF restrictions have greater impact on MNCs with centralized IS decision making structure.

## 5.1. Seriousness of TDF restrictions

To probe the perceived seriousness of TDF restrictions by MNCs, the assessment method of Kane and Ricks 12 was reapplied to allow measurement <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> of trends and changes of the same issue after a time lapse of 7 years. Although the studies in 1988 and 1994 used different sample sizes and samples, their survey results were very compatible as they both studied the biggest corporations in the United States with international involvement. Hence, the findings in these studies do complement and supplement each other. One general conclusion that can be made from these two studies is that the seriousness of TDF restrictions, as perceived by MNCs, has not changed much in the last 6 years. More MNC executives now believe that TDF related issues can be considered routine problems as opposed to potential problems.

## 5.2. Industry types and TDF restrictions

A t-test was conducted to validate the relationship between the impact of TDF restrictions and industry types. To<sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> further investigate the magnitude of impact of TDF restrictions on industry types, one-way analysis of variance was performed across the six industry groups identified in Table 4. The results indicated that there were no significant differences among industry types with respect to the impact of TDF restrictions. This is possibly due to the heavy financial investment of all MNCs overseas, which compels them to rely heavily on local data to effectively manage their organizations, irrespective of industry type.

## 5.3. IS costs and TDF restrictions

The MNCs were requested to evaluate the impact of TDF restrictions on the costs of their international IS operations. A list of relevant IS costs was provided. With a Likert scale from ‘1<sup>s</sup>no increase’ to ‘5<sup>s</sup>significant increase’, the mean scores and standard deviation for each cost are given in Table 7. An analysis of the listed costs suggests that the cost items can be categorized into: hardware, software, network, data processing, and management. On the top of the list is data processing cost, indicating that the restriction of having data processed locally can significantly increase MNC’s IS costs. Such costs include, but not limited to, additional investment in coordinating host sites, replicating redundant resources, creating and managing additional local IS systems, and resolving incompatible processing platforms at different countries. Trailing data processing cost are network and management costs. Software-related costs attributable to TDF restrictions are assessed by the respondents to be of less significance in effecting overall IS costs.

Simple regression analysis was employed to investigate the significance of the relationship between TDF regulations and IS costs. The postulated hypothesis is that TDF regulations can significantly increase MNC’s IS costs. Residual analysis was initially conducted to establish the appropriateness of the sample data for regression analysis. The results did not signify any violation of regression assumptions. Consequently, the TDF data was regressed against IS cost data. The resulting p-value 0.0001Ž . indicates that IS costs are positively related to TDF restrictions. In other words, an increase in TDF restrictions will cause an increase in IS costs.

## 5.4.1. IS structure

The surveyed MNCs were requested to specify their IS structure to allow examination of the impact of TDF restrictions on the design of the IS function. As different MNCs had different IS structures implemented to manage their global IS operations, it would be interesting to investigate the susceptibility of each of these three IS structures to the influence of TDF restrictions. ## 6. Limitations of study

The analysis of this research is based solely on the responses from the MIS executives of the surveyed organizations. There is no way we can determine how adequately their responses represent the entire organization. Although these top executives are critical in influencing IS decisions, their perspectives cannot adequately describe the organization’s perception of TDF restrictions. Additionally, results reported in this study only represent the US MNC’s perspective. Organizations in others countries may have different evaluations of TDF restrictions on their IS operations. For future research, it is recommended that a multiple-respondent, multiple-country data collection approach be followed.

## 7. Summary and implications

This study represents a significant step in going beyond the anecdotal literature on TDF, by capturing data from organizations that have exposure and experience in TDF. The results indicate that TDF restrictions have now evolved from a potential issue to a more pressing current issue for MNCs. Statistical analysis of our survey data also indicates that TDF restrictions will significantly increase IS costs and influence MIS decisions overseas.

## 7.1. Implications for practitioners

The study’s most important implication for practitioners is the formal verification of the fact that TDF restrictions can affect multinational operations and lead to a wide range of IS design and operations issues. Despite this fact, practitioners must still recognize the legitimate concerns of all nations exercising TDF restrictions and conform to each country’s TDF guidelines. MNC’s should realize that competitive gain should not be achieved at the expense of the basic rights of personal or national privacy. Additionally, they should also realize that transborder data is not a free resource, but a commodity that has to be acquired at a cost.

Within this study, evidence is provided that TDF restrictions can significantly impact IS decisions at the strategic, tactical and operational levels. Currently, these restrictions are not so serious as to dominate IS decisions and damage the competitiveness of MNCs in overseas countries. Practitioners should, however, investigate the possibility of adopting effective strategic planning policies that consider the influence of TDF restrictions on their IS decisions.

Identifying the best way to organize the IS functions within MNCs has been a critical IS management issue. Although no evidence is provided in this research to indicate whether centralized, decentralized, or distributed IS structure is more effective in dealing with TDF restrictions, it is believed that IS structural issues will continue to be of major importance to organizations competing in the international arena. Hence, organizations need to consider designing robust IS structures with enough flexibility to increase the effectiveness of IS decisions, decrease the costs of IS operations, and minimize the impact of TDF restrictions.

## 7.2. Implications for researchers

This study is perhaps among the very few studies that have examined TDF restrictions from both a theoretical and empirical perspective. A number of interesting issues have surfaced from this study that could be considered for future research. First, our study indicates that TDF restrictions can have serious and often adverse effects on international business operations. However, further research is needed to investigate the impact of these restrictions on the foreign-investment decisions of multinational corporations.

Second, it is somewhat counter-intuitive to find an absence of a significant relationship between TDF restrictions and IS structures. The result may be deceiving as the influence of TDF restrictions was longitudinal; but this research only recorded the state of MNCs’ IS structures as these companies were surveyed. It may be the case that MNCs may have reconfigured their IS structures to accommodate the impact of TDF restrictions. It would then be interesting, from a research perspective, to track the evolution of MNC IS structure, as influenced by the constant challenge and threat of TDF restrictions.

Third, most research studies on TDF restriction were conducted from a multinational corporation’s perspective. This perspective provides valuable insights in learning the impact and significance of TDF on business operations. However, this perspective is biased in that the viewpoints of countries imposing restrictions are totally neglected. Researchers should investigate the political, economic, and societal motives and objectives behind the implementation of TDF restrictions. Having a better understanding of such motives and objectives will allow practitioners to make more effective decisions with respect to TDF related issues.
