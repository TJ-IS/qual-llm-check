## Abstract

Globalization, virtual corporations, telecommuting, empowerment of teams, reduced cycle time and the need to frequently make decisions quickly makes it necessary for groups to work together while the participants may be in different locations. Distributed Group Support System DGSSŽ . is a technology that can help groups to overcome some of the difficulties associated with being in different places and sometimes in different time zones. The purpose of this paper is to propose a research framework that addresses the major issues involved in the implementation of DGSSs. These issues were identified in a literature review and in a small scale survey of practitioners. The proposed framework extends the traditional decision room GSS framework by adding relevant topics in all major parts. In addition, a new set of characteristics input Ž . variables related to communication issues while being in different places, was added. q 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Distributed group support systems; Electronic meeting systems; Group support systems; Group decision support systems; Distributed work; Internet; Intranet

## 1. Introduction

In a modern organization, people frequently work and make decisions in groups. Lately, there has been an increased emphasis on team-based organizations and autonomous teams e.g., Ref. 24 as part of Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. business process reengineering. Also, globalization, telecommuting, virtual corporations, reduced cycle time and the need to frequently make decisions very rapidly, sometimes in real time, increases the use of group work. Further, the rapid growth of the intranets and the internet and the development of supportive software, such as Netscape Communicator, are likely to accelerate the trend of people working in dispersed groups.

However, working in groups whose members are in different locations may not be simple. In addition to the regular problems that may limit the performance of group work, there could be limitations due to the distance and the time difference. Thus, the ability of dispersed groups to communicate and make decisions may be constrained as compared to a faceto-face group. Information technology greatly helps the communication and decision making processes in a decision room. Therefore, it is logical to try to improve these processes with the same or similar technologies, in the distributed environment. The technologies developed for supporting face-to-face groups, such as GDSS, could be the base for what Microsoft calls distributed workgroup computing systems, also known as distributed group support systems Ž . DGSS or D-GSS .

To do so it is necessary to study and analyze the manner in which people work in distributed situations with and without technological support. Limited studies have been conducted in the past, but now, due to the magnitude of group work, the need for such studies becomes very important. Because of the large number of influencing variables it is helpful to organize the research as a framework, similar to what was done in the decision room environment. Such a framework will show the major relationships among the relevant variables so it can guide researchers in generating hypotheses, designing experiments, collecting data and studying the literature.

Because DGSS is a new research area, there is very little theoretical or empirical research on the topic. The purpose of this paper is to identify important issues of DGSS and then propose a framework which will facilitate DGSS research.

The issues for the framework were compiled from two sources: first, published DGSS research was reviewed; and second, frameworks for decision room-based Group Support System GSS were ex-Ž . amined.

The paper is divided into six parts: first, a brief introduction is provided, in Section 2 an overview of the traditional GSS research framework is presented and compared with that of DGSS. A literature review of experimental DGSS studies is provided in Section 3. Section 4 organizes the research issues gathered from the different sources into a framework. Section 5 examines several research DGSS issues in greater detail and proposes detailed topics for future investigation. The paper ends with conclusions.

## 2.2. Distributed GSS

New sets of issues and challenges faces researchers interested in spatially separated groups. The framework in Fig. 1 and its detailed derivatives were found to be useful for GSS research in a decision room context for which they were designed. However, they do not take into consideration some of the research issues that are particular to a distributed environment. Thus, it would be useful to develop a DGSS framework to address such issues for both synchronous and asynchronous situations.

## 2.2.1. Synchronous DGSSs

Synchronous DGSSs allow distributed participants to interact with one another in a ‘real time mode, i.e., they interact with one another at the same time. The participants are distributed across multiple sites linked by various communication technologies. Some of the supporting technologies are screen sharing, whiteboard, audio-conferencing, and various types of video-conferencing. These technologies can be carried on the Intranet, Internet, corporate or public networks, or VANs. Some major issues here include: the loss of face-to-face contact and the ability to manage the group process.

## 2.2.2. Asynchronous DGSSs

Asynchronous DGSSs allow distributed participants to log into the same meeting but at different times. Participants can log in and catch up with what is going on in the meeting, enter comments if necessary, and log out of the meeting at various times. E-mail, voice-mail, and messaging<sup>r</sup>workflow management systems such as in Lotus Notes are someŽ . of the supporting technologies. The issues here are more than just time and distance barriers. For example, the control of the participants and the participation, and the delayed response time could play an important role. Therefore, attention must be given to the coordination of group members to ensure that they stay on task and track and meet the decision deadlines, as well as it is necessary to encourage timely participation by everyone.

## 3. Experimental research on distributed group support systems

DGSS research whose major findings are summarized here, is fairly limited as compared to the decision room research. According to Turoff et al. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 36 , distributed group support systems are created by the combination of the characteristics of computer-mediated communication systems and group decision support systems, to support the full range of tasks involved in projects. Thus, not only decision making but also the planning, budgeting, gathering of information, and resolving conflicts are supported. The researchers predicted that distributed group support systems would be widely used in the future as means for dispersed groups of people to work together through computer networks such as the Internet. Based on five case studies, their research found that for different distributed group systems, design requirements and the associated research issues for group support systems would be very different in the distributed environment as compared to the decision room approach. Their conclusion supports the need for a separate research framework for DGSS.

Looking at DGSS past research, we find only a small number of experiments because DGSS is still in its infancy. ## 5. Selected research issues suggested for distributed GSS

The research issues addressed here were divided into five categories

## 5.1. Distributed group structure issues

Two issues are discussed, group membership and the role of the facilitator.

## 5.1.1. Group membership

Members in a distributed meeting have reduced social interactions compared with members in a faceto-face meeting. Therefore, in a distributed environment, it may be beneficial for the participants to ‘know’ or ‘know of’ one another before the meetings commence. As a matter of fact, members often belong to the same work group or they may already know each other prior to entering into the meeting. If, however, group members do not know one another before the meeting commences, the conduct of the meeting may differ from a face-to-face meeting as participants will not be able to ‘get to know’ the other group members socially as the meetings go on. Using the Intranet, or Lotus Notes, members can see resumes and pictures of other members and learn about their activities. Some companies show short videos of the members. Yet, a face-to-face contact may not be possible. Group members who know each other may behave differently from ad hoc group members Research has shown that the behavior of ad hoc groups differ from that of groups whose members worked together in the past or are expected to work together in the future 6,26 . Thus, the nature<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> of group membership in the distributed environment could play a role in the success or failure of the meetings and it merits further investigation. A related issue is the level of trust. Iacono and Weisband <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 17 found that the level of trust among dispersed members can reach a similar level as face-to-face if sufficient interaction time elapses.

## 5.1.2. Role of a facilitator

In the decision room environment, the facilitator’s role of technology expert as well as that of the process facilitator is extremely important. The former role requires the facilitator to start up and control the system so that the participants need not possess the technical knowledge of the system. The latter requires the facilitator to implement the meeting protocols, gauge the group’s procedural problems, and keep the group focused on the task 1 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

In a synchronous DGSS session, the need for a facilitator may not be so critical. However, in a dispersed asynchronous meeting, the requirement for a human facilitator becomes a major system limitation 11 . When a meeting lasts days, weeks, or even<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> months, participants may interact whenever it is convenient for them—possibly asynchronously. Often, there is no facilitator or even a coordinator for such situations and the role of the facilitator is assumed by one of the participants. The facilitator’s role needs to be specified for different DGSS scenarios. A possible area for investigation is the use of an intelligent agent that will assist in managing the asynchronous meetings. Some groupware software Ž .such as Lotus Notes offer features which are intended to ease the coordination problems. Coordination of synchronous meeting is somewhat easier, yet the role of the facilitator is important and need to be researched.

## 5.2. Task issues

The major task issue relevant to DGSS is the life cycle of a task.

## 5.2.1. Life-cycle of a task

Most face-to-face GSS research did not take into consideration the fact that face-to-face meetings may not be needed throughout the entire life-cycle of a task. At different stages of the task, there may be different requirements. For example, the face-to-face mode is preferred when immediate feedback is needed for goal clarification, trust building, role clarification, and group commitment. In other cases, however, there may be a need to communicate in a non-face-to-face mode. Participants, especially when they are in different locations, may use only asynchronous communication. They log into a asynchronous shared-screen work space, evaluate changes, make comments, and exit.

The determination of what stages in the task’s life cycle are more suitable for a face-to-face mode and what stages do not require such environment may depend on factors such as the nature of the task, the urgency of arriving at a solution and the culture of the organization. These factors and their relationships need to be further investigated.

Another suggestion for research is to identify the variables and factors that may affect a preferred technology meeting configuration and its supporting technologies for a specific type of meeting. Research in this area will allow users of groupware to select the right technology for a given task and meeting environment.

## 5.3. Contextual issues

Several contextual issues peculiar to DGSS need to be investigated; notably are partitioned meetings and the temporal nature of work.

## 5.3.1. Partitioned meetings

There may be occasions where it becomes necessary to break a large group into smaller groups for handling sub-tasks during meetings. In a face-to-face meetings this is handled by creating on premise sub-groups and coordinating various smaller groups’ output or efforts. In distributed groups, the integration of multiple distributed smaller groups’ comments, may cause a problem, especially when the resulting comments from the sub-groups are inconsistent or offending. It will take more time and effort before the sub-groups can meet again, unlike in the face-to-face situation. In addition, Gavish et al. 12<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> pointed out that distributed participants may become disconnected from a synchronous meeting due to a failure in a communication link. Depending on the activity, it may or may not be desirable to permit disconnected participants to reconnect to continue work on the problem. Thus, the methods to support these kind of activities in distributed group meetings requires further investigation. Research issues may also include coordination and integration of work done by sub-groups for both synchronous and asynchronous meetings. Operational concerns on disruption to an ongoing meeting for synchronous group meetings is another potential research issue.

## 5.3.2. Temporal patterning of work

Face-to-face and synchronous distributed groups may differ from asynchronous distributed groups in the temporal pattern of work in four ways: production, reception, transmission and simultaneous composition <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 26 . Participants in a single site decision room vary from those with distributed group support in terms of the quantum and speed of the information produced by them. The production of information is different because most people can talk much faster Ž . in the decision room than even a very skilled typist can type. In addition, face-to-face groups utilize spoken communication and visual cues. There is no spoken communication or visual cues in the distributed group unless audio and video technology is added e.g., by using Internet telephone and desktop Ž teleconferencing , and even then it is not of the same. quality and magnitude as that of face-to-face. Participants have access to the same amount of text information, but the visual and nonverbal cues and bodylanguage signals are missing from the discussions. Another issue is that in multiple sites, the reading load is likely to increase because text may be the only way to share information.

One possible consequence of the lack of cues and the lower typing speed for the distributed environment is the longer time taken for the decision making process compared to the face-to-face environment. Thus, one pertinent research issue is whether the addition of audio and<sup>r</sup>or video technologies to the distributed environment as compared to the Ž face-to-face environment will lessen the time taken. for the decision making process.

Other temporal patterning of work—reception, transmission and simultaneous composition—remains similar for both the synchronous and asynchronous environments. In both environments, people can read the shared text. The communication systems allow fast transmission of information whether the recipient is a few feet or several miles away, and the groupware supports simultaneous composition of text information in both environments.

## 5.4. Communication issues

Of the several potential communication issues in DGSS, the nature of the regulatory function is important.

## 5.4.1. Regulating communication flow, expressing emotion, and transmitting subtle meanings

Nonverbal and verbal cues play at least three important roles in group communications: they help regulate the flow of communication; they express emotion; and they transmit subtle meanings.

McGrath and Hollingshead 26 believe that a<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> regulatory function Žthe use of cues to regulate meetings help group members regulate the succession of. speakers, and determine the timing of turn taking. The lack of these cues in the distributed environment may result in an increase of the turbulence of the flow of communications.

The emotiÕe function Žthe use of cues to express emotions suggests that subtle meanings in spoken. speech may be partly replaced by written speech. While written speech includes jargon and more complex syntax, the writer can add emphasis through the use of punctuation and redundancy. Special symbols and conventions can be used to replace the expressive functions of verbal cues. More research is needed to identify tools that can be added to a computer conferencing environment to provide standard cues for regulating communication and supporting an emotive function. Participants need seamless ways to show emphasis, commitment, disagreement and other expressive feelings. Many software packages allowŽ for expressions such as a happy face<sup>s</sup>I am happy ..

Richer media, such as desktop videoconferencing and increased audio capabilities, supports the transmission of nonverbal and verbal cues. Media richness research is complicated because different types of media can be grouped in different configurations to support distributed groups 31 . As audio and <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> video technologies become cheaper and easier to use, and the networks bandwidth capabilities are expanding, there will be more and better communication technologies and methods available. The research in distributed groups is supported by the findings of the research in the media richness studies, such as those examining E-mail applications 26 . Thus, there is a<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> need for more studies of distributed groups using combinations of communication technologies. The effectiveness of meetings, in terms of creativity, quality, and satisfaction was investigated by Ocker et al. 28 . They examined four modes of communica-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tion as the independent variable. They found that creativity and quality of solutions produced by groups that used both face-to-face and computer conferencing was higher than in groups that used an individual communication mode.

## 5.5. Technological issues

Many technological issues may be identified with respect to the design, development, maintenance and operation of DGSS. Only several representative issues are discussed here.

## 5.5.1. Group coordination methods

One of the problems with asynchronous meetings is the increased need for coordination of group activities. Turoff et al. 36 adapted Thompson’s 35 <sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> classification of organizational group activities into four coordination methods that groups use. They are: Parallel, where individuals approached the problem independently; Pooled, same as parallel except a standard is utilized to formulate a group result such as group vote; Sequential, where all group members undertake the problem-solving phases in a sequential manner and; Reciprocal, where changes made in one part of the problem can force other group members to reconsider other parts of the problem, such as in a case where consistency relations are imposed.

Turoff et al. 36 suggested that a group in a<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> synchronous environment usually relies on a self-imposed sequential strategy for various segments of the problems. This is because agendas are often used in GDSS sessions, thereby forcing groups to go through one stage of a problem-solving process before proceeding to another.

A ‘concurrency of transmission’ problem is sometimes found in the asynchronous meetings when messages that are sent to each participant are received in a different order than sent. Thus, it may become unclear to which version of an issue or to what message a participant is responding. An optionŽ in e-mail that allows the respondent to send the message received together with the replay may solve such problems . Managing the delivery of messages . often becomes an important activity 11 , and also an<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> important research area for the design of tools for asynchronous meetings.

## 5.5.2. Group memory and knowledge base

Group memory is important for any GSS since it can increase process gains 14 . Groups need to have an access to the documentation group memory ofŽ . the past meetings that is dynamic over space and time. In distributed meetings this can be even more important since members do not have any access to paper documents. In some decision making situations, the completion of a task may take multiple steps, lasting months. New members may be added to the group. Hence, there are numerous incentives for the development of dynamic and accessible group memories to support ongoing group processes. For a discussion of the information in group memory see Hoffer and Valacich 15 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Specialized information possessed by group members is a scarce resource 26 . Distributed group<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> members may possess more expertise since they are closer to decentralized operations. Thus, the DGSS can serve as a tool to solicit such information as well as to form a dynamic group knowledge base for problem solving. A group may not have all the expertise needed to solve all problems. The group may want to identify and communicate with corporate experts that may be in different locations. The group may also want to search various databases. Groupware software and the Intranet<sup>r</sup>Internet may be useful in dealing with such situations. For example, hundreds of experts and specialists can be reached via Notes at Price Waterhouse 23 . Group-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ware- and Knowledge-based development software is being developed by major vendors to permit similar capabilities for the Internet and the Intranet.

## 5.5.3. Security and Õerification of identity

The distributed environment requires special security measures in addition to the usual controls , suchŽ . as limiting attendance, using passwords for joining a group, and isolating the communication network 11 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> In a distributed environment, intruders may masquerade as either a valid participant or as the system coordinator. In the decision room environment, participants have face-to-face interactions, and they receive acknowledgement of their participation in the meeting, either explicitly or implicitly. In distributed computer conferences, there may be some difficulty in controlling attendance. For example, participants in a distributed session may not be sure if an individual is present in a synchronous meeting and<sup>r</sup>or participating, just present, or has left the session, because the anonymity feature prevents identification of specific user actions. Hence, one research area for the distributed environment is to find ways to maintain the security of the distributed communication channels, including the verification of identities of participants, and a quick encryption of highly confidential files and messages.

## 5.5.4. Design of distributed GSS enÕironments

Some shared presentation aids for decision room GSS such as flip charts are not available for the Ž . distributed environments. Interface design of the DGSSs becomes critical because of the lack of such presentation devices. Without special channels of communication, participants are restricted to sharing text and graphical information using screen sharing software and whiteboards that may not be sufficient for easy and fast completion of tasks or for reaching a consensus.

Groups in the decision room often make use of a public projection screen. The display contrast, background lighting, and font size of the text or graphics can make the shared information attractive. In the distributed environment, the ‘representation’ issue becomes more acute because other ‘cues’ may not be available. The wrong form of representation may negatively affect the conduct of work, and the extent of participation. Also, presentation may influence the opinions of the participants more than in face-to-face meetings. Further research on the presentation of information for distributed groups is thus needed.

Sometimes a DGSS is used to ‘connect’ dispersed sub-groups, all of which are supported by groupware. The DGSS allows the various sub-groups to communicate with each other: exchanging ideas and collaborating on their work. It also allows the subgroups to function as a whole, to make decisions, or to vote. The study of such configuration merits further research.

The spatial arrangement of participants in a single site decision room may affect performance and determine status. In the distributed environment, spatial arrangement is no longer an issue. People who participate individually in different sites cannot move together physically to work in sub-groups although they can have electronic meetings of sub-groups. They cannot meet face-to-face informally outside the meeting to discuss issues. Research on the design of DGSSs is needed to look into multiple site decision room configurations i.e., in each distributed site Ž there is a decision room . ‘Informal’ contacts may be . necessary for group members to know one another better, and for building group trust and cohesiveness.

In a distributed environment, especially in an asynchronous one, participants reported that they are no longer in full control of the changes introduced to their system. For example, information from coworkers may be added to a shared database, perhaps without the knowledge of all of the group members. Information in various document versions may appear and disappear during real time group editing sessions. A monitoring system could convey the progress or changes made to an ‘object’ since the last time it was accessed. There must be a way to smooth coordination between joint work and individual work. People need to be able to control who sees their work in progress, and protect the portions of the document which are private, to ensure that the public cannot view these portions. The design of DGSSs need to take into consideration these and other control issues.

## 5.6. Outcome issues

Most outcome issues in DGSS are similar or even identical to any other GSS. We discuss here only one issue, highlighting its relationship to a distributed environment.

## 5.6.1. Effects of GSS on organizations

Huber et al. 16 made the following propositions <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> regarding GSS:

a. availability of GSS leads to GSS use;

b. use of GSS leads to increased information accessibility;

c. increased information accessibility leads to changes in organizational design;

d. increased information accessibility and changes in organizational design lead to improvements in the effectiveness of intelligence development and decision making.

The impact of distributed workgroups on organizational structure and culture could be significant if Huber’s theories on GSS and the organization’s nature will be verified in real life settings. This is because several organizational units, or even several organizations in an interorganizational DSS, may be impacted. The use of GSS in organizations has not been prolific. However, this situation is changing rapidly due to the increased use of the Internet and Intranet and the availability of groupware such as Lotus Notes, workflow software, desktop screen sharing, and desktop videoconferencing. One major question is: What will be the role of a DGSS with all the above technologies in organizations?

The availability of DGSS technology does not automatically create the demand for it unless other conditions are favorable for its use. First, there must be an awareness of the types of distributed groupware available and their purpose. Often, an influential person in an organization will be the champion for the initial adoption of the technology. The champion may set an example and begin to develop applications for the technology. As the applications become useful, then there is an adoption and diffusion of the technology within the organization. Currently, the practitioners have too many questions that have no answers. More research is needed to find the answers to such questions and help to develop the support tools that will make the groupware even more useful to distributed groups.

## 6. Conclusion

As the time to make certain decisions becomes shorter, the travel expenses escalate, and as globalization spreads, it may be impossible, expensive, or impractical to meet face-to-face. For multi-site organizations, dispersed meetings using a variety of communication channels may be a necessity in order to complete tasks in a timely and efficient manner. Similarly, organizations may need to work together with their business partners, with the government, or with any other organizations. The wide spread of Internet and Intranet infrastructures and groupware software will facilitate the work of dispersed groups and DGSS software and methodologies can be of a great help. Future studies are needed, however, to look at how people adapt to using computer mediated support and to identify the types of environments that will successfully support dispersed meetings.

The framework proposed in this paper and the specific issues listed in Section 5, cover the major topics perceived by the researchers as warrant further investigation. As our DGSS knowledge base increases, more research issues will undoubtedly be added.

The next phase in our research could be the refinement of the framework and testing its value by researchers and practitioners. Also, the identification of software tools that will enhance the multi-site and interorganizational meeting process, and the development of procedures and training methods that will help organizations take advantage of the benefits of using a DGSS need to be researched.

The DGSS environment offers many potential areas for investigation, several of which are discussed in this paper. The research framework and issues raised in this paper are intended for researchers and practitioners who are interested in looking at the impact of the DGSS environment on individuals, groups, and organizations.
