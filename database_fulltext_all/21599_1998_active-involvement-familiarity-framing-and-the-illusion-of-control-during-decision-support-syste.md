## Abstract

Results of a laboratory experiment on Decision Support System DSS use indicated that a active involvement in modelŽ . Furthermore, higher expectations of success were associated with greater user satisfaction, better mood, and lower preference for thinking more before making the final decision. ## 1. Introduction

Research in the area of Decision Support Systems Ž . Ž . DSS suggests that an illusion of control IOC may be operating during DSS use 8,20 . Langer defined<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

IOC as an unwarranted inflation of expectations of success in probabilistic situations 21,23 . Langer <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 21 found that when elements that generally control success in skill situations are present in a purely probabilistic situation, where they do not contribute to success, people behaved as if they could control success and inflated their expectations of success. Examples of these elements include choice, stimulus or response familiarity, passive or active involvement, and competition. Langer 21 argued that the <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> IOC may be stronger in partially controllable probabilistic situations, where skill plays a role, because of greater plausibility of attributing performance to one’s skill.

DSS are used in semi-structured situations, which are probabilistic and partially controllable. Semistructured tasks, such as cost-benefit analysis, have both a ‘structured’ component and an ‘unstructured component. The ‘structured’ component is characterized by the ability to apply one’s knowledge or skills Že.g., determining gross profit for a given level of sales, selling price, production costs, and other costs .. The ‘unstructured’ component is characterized by a lack of perfect cause and effect knowledge and uncertainty about input conditions e.g., deciding Ž whether to launch a new product for which the demand is not yet clear . Decision-makers engage in . information processing assisted by a DSS in an attempt to increase the probability of making a successful decision but because of the unstructured component, control over success is limited.

It is important to understand IOC during DSS use because inflation of expectations of success has important effects. Inflated expectations of success accompanying DSS use could lead to lower quality decisions by causing a decision maker to ignore additional sources of information 13 or continue<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> using DSS while avoiding potentially superior alternatives, such as decision heuristics 8,20 . Inflated<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> expectations of success may increase a decisionmaker’s commitment to implementing the decision <sup>w x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 30 and improve the decision-maker’s mood 14,25 . Mood is related to how decision-makers process information 32 , to organizational attempts to im-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> prove employees’ quality of work life 15 , and to<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> fostering cooperation in the workplace 16 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

The limited research on IOC during DSS use prompted Davis and Kottemann 8 to call for addi-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tional research to verify the existence of IOC in a DSS context and to understand its determinants and ramifications. In accordance with this call, the current study investigated a the effects of active in-Ž . volvement, familiarity, and consistency of a normative decision with the alternative encouraged by the problem frame on unwarranted inflation of expectations of success, i.e., illusion of control, during DSS use, and b the relationships between expectations Ž . of success and a decision-maker’s satisfaction, mood, preference for additional processing, and decision performance. Justification for studying these effects and relationships and development of pertinent hypotheses is provided below.

## 2. Involvement and the illusion of control

Langer 21 found that both active and passive<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> involvement in tasks with probabilistic outcomes led to an IOC. Langer 21 considered active involve-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ment as engaging in physical activity and any mental activity necessary to complete the physical activity during task execution. She considered passive involvement as engaging in a purely mental activity during task execution. Langer’s 21 rationalization<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> for involvement’s effect was based on the premise that people are motivated to control their environments and that they have a genuine difficulty in distinguishing between probabilistic and skill situations. Therefore, they tend to confuse probabilistic situations with skill situations. Langer 21 argued<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> that because involvement in a skill situation determines success, the confusion between probabilistic and skill situations may cause individuals to inflate their expectations of success when they are involved in a probabilistic situation, even though involvement may not influence the chances of success.

Involvement during DSS use may be active, passive, or a combination of both. A DSS user is actively involved when the user builds a decision model, enters the input for a decision scenario, and executes the model. A DSS user is passively involved when he or she conceptualizes a decision model or the decision scenarios to analyze and examines the output of the DSS. Kottemann et al. 20 and<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Davis and Kottemann 8 found that involvement<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> during DSS use in the form of ‘what-if’ analysis of a production-scheduling task led to an IOC. They jointly manipulated both active involvement the en-Ž try of input of decision scenarios and passive in-. volvement the conceptualization of one or moreŽ ‘what if’ scenarios and examination of the output . It. is therefore difficult to determine whether active involvement or passive involvement or both cause IOC. Knowledge of the sources of IOC is important for designing appropriate interventions. For example, if active involvement causes IOC and a reduction in IOC is desired, IOC may be reduced by having a staff person rather than the decision-maker build the model, enter data into it, and execute it.

This study examined the effect of active involvement with a probabilistic DSS model on IOC. The focus on active involvement is important due to an increasing trend in system building by end users 1 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Specifically, the current study examined the effects on IOC of active involvement in ‘model building and ‘data entry’ conditions relative to a ‘model execution’ condition. In the model building condition, users built a component of an incomplete DSS model, entered data into it, and executed it. In the data entry condition, users entered data into a given model and then executed it. In the model execution condition, users simply executed a given model. As shown in Table 1, the active involvement components of a user’s task in the model building condition subsumed the components in the data entry condition, which in turn subsumed the components in the model execution condition. Thus, relative to the model execution condition, the model building and data entry conditions represented higher levels of active involvement.

The effect of active involvement on participants expectations of success was examined independently of variation in model input, which can influence the objective chance of success. Therefore, objectively speaking, greater levels of active involvement in the data entry and model building conditions relative to the model execution condition should not influence participants’ chances of success. However, Langer’s arguments presented earlier suggest that users of a probabilistic DSS model may confuse their ability to make a correct decision with their skill in active use of the DSS. Because of this confusion, active involvement may lead to an IOC, i.e., it may lead to an inflation of expectations of success although it is unrelated to participants’ chances of success. Therefore,

## 4. Framing and the illusion of control

Kahneman and Tversky 19 have shown that <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> whether a problem is framed in terms of potential gains or losses can influence risk-taking behavior. Dunn and Wilson indicated the potential for framing to influence IOC when they stated: ‘‘we suspect that there are situations in which people are susceptible to the illusion of control . . . because of the manner in which people frame the potential gains and losses’ <sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> 14 , p. 321 . This study investigated the effect of consistency between a normative decision and how the problem is framed on unwarranted inflation of expectations of success that characterizes IOC.

Subjects were placed in the position of a manager of an existing business who had to choose between staying in business and not staying in business. Subjects were instructed to make this decision entirely on the probability of making a profit in the upcoming year, for which projections were being made by the DSS. Subjects were assigned three scenarios of this problem, of which two are relevant here. In one scenario, there was a large chance Ž . 80% that the business would make a profit and a small chance 20% of a loss. According to subjectsŽ . instructions that they should make their decisions based on the probability of making a profit in the upcoming year, the normative decision in this scenario was to stay in business and it had an 80% chance of being correct. In the other scenario, there was a large chance 80% that the business would Ž . make a loss and a small chance 20% of a gain. TheŽ . normative decision in this scenario was to not stay in business and it, too, had an 80% chance of being correct.

The problem assigned to the subjects in the current study was framed in terms of a sure loss option Ži.e., the loss of business if the decision is to not stay in business and a risky option i.e., the decision to. Ž stay in business, which could lead to a financial gain or loss . Highhouse and Yuce 18 suggest that the. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> framing of the problem would incline the subjects to choose the risky option of staying in business because it may be perceived as an opportunity to avoid a sure loss of the business. This inclination is in conflict with the normative decision of not staying Ž in business in the high probability of losses scenario. but in accord with the normative decision of stayingŽ in business in the high probability of profits sce-. nario. Consequently, participants are likely to experience greater cognitive conflict when deciding on an option and, therefore, be more uncertain about their decision’s success in the low probability of profits scenario than in the high probability of profits scenario. This difference in expectations of success is unwarranted, i.e., it reflects an IOC, because it is not due to a difference in objective chances of success. Because the expected difference in expectations of success is due to consistency between the normative solution and the decision alternative encouraged by the problem frame, the hypothesis is stated as follows.

Hypothesis 3: The consistency between the normative solution and the decision alternative encouraged by the problem frame is expected to inflate expectations of success.

## 5. Relationships of expectations of success to outcome variables

The above hypotheses pertain to the effects of involvement, familiarity, and framing on the IOC, defined as an unwarranted inflation of an individual’s expectation of success in a probabilistic situation. It was stated earlier that it is important to study this unwarranted inflation of expectation of success because changes in expectations of success have important effects. This study investigated the relationship of expectations of success to participants’ satisfaction, their mood, their preference for additional information processing, and their decision performance. Hypotheses for these relationships are stated below.

## 5.1. Effect on satisfaction

Expectations of success in a DSS supported task are likely to influence a DSS user’s satisfaction with the task. Satisfaction with a DSS supported task is important because it contributes to the DSS user’s job satisfaction 24 . Langer 21 argued that people<sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> are motivated to succeed in their encounters with the environment. Because expectations of success in a DSS supported task fulfill this motivation, they are likely to be positively related to a DSS user’s satisfaction 21 . Therefore,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Hypothesis 4: Higher expectations of success are likely to be associated with higher levels of satisfaction.

## 5.2. Effect on mood

Expectations of success in a DSS supported task are likely to influence a DSS user’s mood 25 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Mood refers to a transient feeling state with no specific target. Although an attitude, such as satisfaction, is also a feeling state, it is different from mood in that it is more stable and is directed at a particular object, behavior, person, or idea 16,26 . Managers<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> are likely to be interested in learning how expectations of success influence mood because mood can have both positive and negative effects in the work place, some of which are described below.

On the positive side, good mood improves both the physical and mental health of decision-makers <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 2,22,31 , an outcome sought by organizations attempting to reduce employee stress and improve quality of employee work-life 15 . Good mood has<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> also been found to make people kinder see Ref. 16Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> for a summary with references related to this effect ,. an important outcome sought by organizations as they increasingly adopt team-based structures, which rely on cooperation among team members. On the negative side, ‘‘mood could affect memory retrieval and probability judgments resulting in distorted analyses, unintended decisions, and serious negative effects for the welfare of the decision-makers and individuals impacted by the decision’’ 32 , p. 277 .<sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Higher expectations of success are likely to be associated with a better mood 25 . An individual’s<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> mood is described in terms of positive and negative mood states 10–12 . Therefore, higher expectations <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> of success are likely to be associated with higher levels of positive mood state and lower levels of negative mood state, as stated below.

Hypothesis 5: a Higher expectations of success areŽ . likely to be associated with higher levels of positive mood state; and b Higher expectations of successŽ . are likely to be associated with lower levels of negative mood state.

## 5.3. Effect on preference for additional information processing

Individuals process information to reduce uncertainty and improve their chances of success. According to the law of diminishing marginal utility, marginal utility of additional information processing will reduce with increasing expectations of success. Therefore,

Hypothesis 6: Higher expectations of success are likely to be associated with lower preference for additional information processing.

## 5.4. Relationship to decision performance

Kottemann et al. 20 suggested that a disturbing<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> aspect of illusion of control is that expectations of success may be independent of actual decision performance. They found that in the case of subjects in whom they induced an inflation of expectations of success by making them perform ‘what if’ analysis, expectations of success were not correlated with decision performance. Based on this finding, the following is hypothesized.

Hypothesis 7: Variations in expectations of success induced by sources of illusion of control are likely to be unrelated to decision performance.

## 6. Method

## 6.1. Subjects

A total of 115 undergraduate management students, 46.1% of which were males, enrolled in an introductory Management Information Systems course at a Northeastern public university participated in the study for course credit.

## 6.2. OÕerÕiew of the experimental task

Subjects were assigned three ‘stay in business’ vs. ‘not stay in business’ decision situations faced by an orange juice bottler. The bottler faced uncertain profits in the upcoming year due to uncertainty in volume and unit variable costs. Subjects were instructed to only consider the chances of making a profit in the upcoming year while making their decision. A Monte Carlo Simulation model based on the risk analysis approach outlined by Hertz 17 and created<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> in Lotus 1-2-3 Release 5 produced a distribution ofŽ . profit estimates that the subjects used to make their decision. Depending on subjects’ active involvement condition, they i executed a given model, ii en- Ž . Ž . tered given data into a given model and executed it, or iii completed an unfinished model, entered givenŽ . data into it, and executed it. During two weeks before the study, subjects completed an assignment involving the creation and use of Monte Carlo Simulation models in Lotus 1-2-3.

## 6.3. Research design and procedures

Subjects were assigned three tasks corresponding to three out of nine combinations of three involvement conditions model execution vs. data entry vs.Ž model building involvement conditions and three. profit conditions low vs. medium vs. high probabil-Ž ity of profits conditions . In the model execution. condition, subjects executed a given business model to generate possible profit outcomes and summary statistics. In the data entry condition, subjects entered given input data into a given business model by choosing appropriate values from dialog boxes that appeared on their computer monitors. Subjects then executed the business model as in the model execution condition. In the model building condition, subjects first created a part of the commands required to run the business model. Subjects then proceeded as in the data entry condition. To prevent the subjects from disabling the computer model by entering ‘incorrect’ commands, the computer model used correct commands that were hidden from the subjects.

In the low probability of profits condition, 20% Ž . 80% of the outcomes generated by the computer model were cases in which the business made a profit loss . In the medium and high probability ofŽ . profits condition, the percentages of outcomes in which the business made a profit loss were 50%Ž . Ž . Ž . 50% and 80% 20% , respectively. The probability distributions of outcomes in the low and high probability of profits conditions were mirror images of each other about the vertical axis. The normative decision, based on consideration of chance of making a profit, in the 20% profit condition is to ‘not stay in business’ whereas in the 80% profit condition it is to ‘stay in business’. These two conditions are normatively equivalent in that the chance of the normative decision being correct in both the conditions is 80%. However, with only these two profit conditions, it would not be clear whether the results were peculiar to the particular probability of the normative decision being correct. To increase the generalizability of the results, the 50% profit condition was also included in the research design.

A subject was assigned only once to any of the involvement and profit conditions to avoid repetition. Subjects performed their three tasks during two sessions one week apart. To the extent possible, the different ways in which the assignment of the study’s profit conditions could be sequentially ordered was balanced. Due to scheduling constraints, subjects were assigned to a the model execution and dataŽ . entry conditions during the first session and the model building condition during the second session, or b the model building condition during the firstŽ . session and the model execution and data entry conditions during the second session. Thus, subjects were never in the model building condition during their second task. Half of the subjects were assigned to be in the model execution condition before the data entry condition. ## 8. Discussion

Results did not indicate a difference in expectations of success between the model execution and data entry conditions. The difference in the level of active involvement across these two conditions may not have been large enough to cause a significant difference in expectations of success. A larger difference in the level of active involvement across the model execution and model building conditions was able to produce significantly higher expectations of success in the model building condition. Because the model building and model execution conditions differed in terms of data entry and model building components, results suggest that active involvement due to model building and data entry is likely to have inflated expectations of success.

A surprising finding was that familiarity with the data entry component was associated with lower expectations of success. A potential reason for this relationship is what Langer 21 , p. 327 calls as ‘an<sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> intrusion of reality’. This occurs when an individual’s focus shifts from skill cues toward the uncontrollable aspects of a task. This could possibly mitigate the illusion of control 21,27 . When subjects entered<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> data into the model for the second time, the data were different from the data entered the first time. This difference in data could have made the subjects aware that the current decision situation was different in some respect from a previously encountered situation. This may have caused them to judge that their familiarity with a previously encountered decision situation was not relevant in some respect, thereby leading them to revise their expectation of success downward.

Higher expectations of success were associated with lower preference for thinking more before making the final decision. Though higher expectations of success were less likely to be associated with preference for creating another model, they were more likely to be associated with making more model variables probabilistic. Perhaps, the subjects who had higher expectations of success attributed their performance to the model’s ability to capture the probabilistic nature of some of its variables. Consequently, they may have believed that refining the simulation model by making more variables probabilistic could help them improve their performance further, leading them to show a preference for such refinement.

Subjects with higher expectations of success did not show a preference for increasing the number of times the Monte Carlo model looped or was made to run, potentially because these actions would have not helped improve performance. The model that the subjects used looped i.e., sampled from the proba-Ž bility distributions of probabilistic input variables. 250 times. Subjects were likely to have been aware from their training about Monte Carlo simulation that increasing the number of loops beyond a few hundred makes little difference in the results produced by the model, and consequently, their performance. Subjects may have reacted similarly to the action of running the Monte Carlo model multiple times because running the model multiple times is equivalent to increasing the number of loops.

## 9. Conclusions

The objective of this study was to verify the existence of IOC, i.e., unwarranted inflation of expectations of success, and to better understand its determinants and ramifications in a DSS context. Results of this study demonstrated the occurrence of unwarranted inflation of expectations of success during DSS use and that this inflation of expectations of success is related to important outcomes. Results of this study extend past research in at least three ways. First, Davis and Kottemann 8 and Kottemann et al.<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 20 had found that the joint manipulation of both active and passive involvement in the form of ‘what if’ analysis led to an IOC. This study found that active involvement per se may cause an IOC during Monte Carlo Simulation for risk analysis. This finding is significant in that it provides evidence that the IOC caused by involvement during DSS use generalizes beyond involvement in ‘what if’ analysis. It is also significant in light of the increasing trend in system building, a form of active involvement, by end users 1 and that IOC, which may be caused by<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> active involvement per se, can lead to problems and also provide opportunities as discussed later.

Second, this study examined additional determinants, beyond active involvement, of IOC during DSS use. Specifically, it found that familiarity with the model execution component of a DSS supported task caused an unwarranted inflation of expectations of success. However, familiarity with the data entry component was associated with a reduction in expectations of success, potentially due to data entry’s ability to draw attention to differences in the current situation compared to previous decision situations. This study also found that consistency between the normative solution and the solution encouraged by the problem frame led to an unwarranted inflation of expectations of success.

Third, this study investigated the relationship of expectations of success to participants’ satisfaction, their mood, their preference for additional information processing, and their decision performance. Higher expectations of success were associated with higher levels of satisfaction, higher levels of positive mood state, lower levels of negative mood state, and lower preference for thinking more about the decision. Higher expectations of success were less likely to be associated with a preference for creating another model but were more likely to be associated with a preference for refining the model by making more variables probabilistic. Variations in expectations of success induced by active involvement, familiarity, and the normative decision’s consistency with the alternative encouraged by the problem frame were not associated with decision performance.

Results of this study have several implications. Expectations of success reflect beliefs about performance and beliefs about performance have been linked to user acceptance of computer-based systems <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 7,9 . Because active involvement, familiarity, and consistency between the normative decision and the problem frame during DSS may inflate the expectations of success independently of actual performance, DSS users may continue using a relatively inferior DSS while avoiding potentially superior alternatives, such as decision heuristics or another DSS model 8,20 . The potential for avoiding another DSS model due to inflation of expectations of success was evidenced in the results of this study: higher expectations of success were less likely to be associated with preference for creating another model. Once adopted, inferior DSS models may continue to be used because users may have difficulty learning from their experience about any degradation in performance 8 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

If reducing a DSS user’s expectations of success is desired, results suggest reducing a DSS user’s active involvement or reducing familiarity with executing the DSS model and the decision situation. A user’s active involvement may be reduced, for example, by assigning the model building and data entry activities to another individual. A user’s familiarity with executing the model may be reduced by having the other individual execute the model as well and providing the results to the user. These recommendations for reducing a DSS user’s expectations of success are likely to be useful for altering the behavior of a user who tends to focus excessively on ‘tweaking’ the DSS model in use and thinks less about the decision or tends to avoid creating another model. Results of this study showed that a DSS user with lower expectations of success is less likely to prefer refining the DSS model by making more variables probabilistic. Such a user is also more likely to prefer creating another DSS model and thinking more before making the final decision.

If an organization has ascertained that using a particular DSS model leads to better performance than alternative decision aids, the likelihood of its acceptance as well as commitment to the decisions made may be enhanced by increasing a DSS user’s expectations of success 30 . Results suggest that a <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> DSS user’s expectations of success may be increased by having the user actively involved in building a model and entering data into it. In addition to increasing the DSS user’s likelihood of accepting the DSS model for future use and commitment to decisions made using it, an increase in expectations of success is likely to be accompanied by an improvement in the user’s satisfaction and mood. The recommendation to increase a DSS user’s active involvement may also be employed to counter a decrease in expectations of success that may result when the normative decision for a situation is opposite to the decision encouraged by how the problem is framed.

There have been reports of computer users getting ‘carried away’ by a computer model 29 . Results<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> from this study may account for this phenomenon. Results indicated that decision-makers may derive their expectations of success from model building and data entry activities. Results further suggested that these expectations of success in turn may cause the user to refine the model, which may further elevate their expectations of success, and so on, thereby trapping the user in an ongoing cycle.

To extend the study’s findings, future research should address the effects of passive involvement on expectations of success and compare these effects to the effects of active involvement. In order to maintain control over the experimental conditions in the current study, participants were not provided the opportunity to make additional refinements to the decision model beyond those included in the experimental conditions or to seek information from sources external to the DSS model before making a decision. Future research should consider extending this study by providing such opportunities to participants and determining their association with conditions that influence IOC. Future research should also consider examining additional determinants of IOC in a DSS environment.

It was found that consistency between the normative decision and the decision alternative encouraged by a problem framed in terms of a sure loss option Ž . i.e., loss of business vs. a risky option inflated expectations of success. Future research should examine whether this effect generalizes to a problem framed in terms of a sure gain option vs. a risky option.

A limitation of this study was the use of students as surrogates for managers. However, this limitation should be tempered by the fact that the subjects were management students who were training to be managers. A study conducted to assess the suitability of using management students with no managerial experience as surrogates for managers showed no difference in decision performance between student and manager groups 28 . Moreover, for the most part, <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> the situation that was assigned to the subjects required them to consider the information produced by a DSS model rather than managerial work experience. Additionally, manipulation and other checks supported that, in general, participants approached their tasks by considering the information produced by their DSS model. Another limitation was that subjects had an opportunity to interact with each other during the week-long gap between their two experimental sessions. This may have influenced the results of this study. Future studies should address these limitations.

In summary, this study showed that active involvement, familiarity, and consistency of the normative decision with the problem frame during DSS use may bias a user’s expectations of success. Managers ought to be sensitive to the production of this bias during DSS use because it is likely to be associated with a DSS user’s satisfaction, mood, preference for additional information processing, acceptance of the DSS, and commitment to decisions made using the DSS.
