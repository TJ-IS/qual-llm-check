## Abstract

Experimental research on Group Decision Support Systems GDSS has generally focused on idea generation and choiceŽ . tasks. The experiments have typically consisted of groups whose members share the same objectives and do not have a formally designated leader. This paper reports on the results of an experiment in which the groups worked on a mixed-motive task. A key feature of the study is that the participants did not have the same information informationŽ asymmetry . The experimental study consisted of a 2. <sup>=</sup>2 factorial design. The two factors were communication channel Ž . Žface-to-face vs. computer mediated communication and group leadership mode group with a leader vs. groups without leader . The results indicate that there are differences in perceived as well as actual performance between groups meeting. same place<sup>r</sup>same time decision room and groups meeting same timeŽ . ## 1. Introduction

The Information Systems community has produced a rich body of research on Group Decision Support Systems GDSS and Electronic Meeting Systems EMS . Researchers have extensively studied the effects ofŽ . Ž . GDSS and EMS on group performance, group behavior, and individual behavior. Comparisons of groups using and not using a DSS<sup>r</sup>GDSS have been reported 2,12,18,26,27,34 . Likewise, the effects of communication channel and task type have all been widely examined 5–7,14,16,17,20,24,36,37 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Prior research on GDSS exhibits a number of characteristics in common. Typically, the tasks used are idea generation and choice tasks 28 . Members of the group have the same stake in the outcome. Group members<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> have had little or no prior interaction as a group and will have little or no interaction after the exercise. With few exceptions 21,22,26,27 , the groups studied do not have a formal leader.<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

It is important to broaden the context within which GDSS is studied and to build on the solid foundation that has been provided by previous research. In an organization, groups often confront complex tasks that involve uncertainty and equivocality. Increasingly, teams and groups are geographically dispersed. The objectives and goals of each group member may differ. Not all members will have the same information informationŽ asymmetry . Often, group participants have worked together previously. Finally, groups may or may not have a. formally appointed leader group authority . Ž .

We report on an experiment that studied the effects of communication medium and authority on group and individual performance and behavior for groups supported by a GDSS under the following environmental conditions:

Ž .a members of the group had conflicting objectives,

Ž . b not all members of the group had the same information,

Ž .c members of each group had worked together and will work together in the future, and,

Ž . <sup>1</sup> d all groups were supported by the same GDSS.

We designed a mixed-motive task in the form of a production planning problem to meet the above conditions. A company manufactures four products and receives orders for these products from its customers. Each order consists of some combination of the four products. The group meets to decide which orders to fill subject to capacity constraints. Each member of the group represents a different business function e.g., Ž marketing, production, purchasing . Task interdependence arises since marketing cannot sell a product that. production does not produce and production cannot produce a product if purchasing does not purchase the raw material. Hence, the group has to negotiate on a common set of orders. Each member has information regarding the costs his<sup>r</sup>her department will incur to fill an order. All members are given the revenue that is generated by each order. Each member can have a personal objective that differs from the organization and the other group members. Under these conditions, finding a solution that is acceptable to all members requires the group to work on a task that contains both ambiguity and equivocality.

There is information asymmetry in the experimental setting as each group member has knowledge only about his<sup>r</sup>her department’s costs and payoff. This is further compounded by the fact that the decision-maker, i.e., the leader is dependent on the group members for cost information.

Information asymmetry causes ambiguity as the identification of the best set of orders for the group lends itself to being understood in more than one way by the group members and the leader. The task that the group faces is equivocal because it is represented by ambiguity about the correct answer and uncertainty about the information that is exchanged. The uncertainty arises as each group member and leader are not sure if the other members are communicating information truthfully. We study the effects of leadership and communication channel on decision-making in such an environment.

In what follows, we first present a series of hypotheses, derived from prior research and theory, that are to be tested experimentally. Next, in Section 3, we describe the methodology, the task, and the GDSS developed to support the experiment. Finally, in Section 4, we discuss the results along with their implications.

## 2. Hypotheses

## 2.1. Group performance

Performance of groups can be affected by a number of factors. Two such factors are communication medium and group authority structure. A number of theoretical perspectives relating to these two factors form the basis for the experiment reported in this paper.

Media Richness Theory MRT , proposed by Daft and Lengel 8,9 , asserts that one should choose aŽ . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> communication medium that matches features of the task. They identified two key features of tasks: degree of uncertainty and degree of equivocality. In addition, communication media were classified along a continuum of low to high ‘richness’ using four criteria: feedback, multiple cues, language variety, and personalization. Channels with high information richness are ‘personal’ while channels with low information richness are ‘impersonal’ 35 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

In contrast to MRT’s use of uncertainty and equivocality as task classifiers, McGrath and Hollingshead 29 <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> used the task typology first proposed by McGrath 28 . McGrath and Hollingshead 29 have proposed a<sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> relationship between medium and task types based on information richness. Task types include idea-generation, negotiation, and intellective tasks. Negotiation tasks are further categorized into cognitive conflict and mixed-motive tasks. Cognitive conflict tasks are those in which group members have conflicts of viewpoint and mixed-motive tasks are those in which group members have conflicts of interest. Both task types exhibit Daft and Lengel’s equivocality and uncertainty phenomena. McGrath and Hollingshead 29 argue that for cognitive <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> conflict and mixed-motive tasks, computer mediated communication provides a poor fit while face-to-face communication provides a good fit.

Computer mediated communication may promote de-individuation by reducing the number of channels that are used for personal interaction. The phenomenon of de-individuation can affect the performance of groups. De-individuation is defined as the process whereby submergence in a group produces loss of identity for the individuals. The member’s obligations to the group norms often leads to the weakening of social norms by relaxing social constraints on the individual 13,39 . When computer mediated communication is used,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> anonymity which also leads to de-individuation has been found to improve performance in idea generation tasks. It makes it possible for the individuals to offer both popular and unpopular ideas potentially resulting in an improved set of ideas. De-individuation, however, may have a negative effect on negotiation tasks as it can foster the view of negotiation as a win–lose situation 33 . Negotiators with a win–lose orientation may not be able to find a solution that makes all negotiators winners.

The theories of task-medium-fit and de-individuation lead us to expect that given a mixed-motive task to perform, groups using face-to-face FTF communication will outperform groups using computer mediatedŽ . communication CMC . This is captured in the following two testable hypotheses:Ž .

H1A: For mixed-motive tasks, members in FTF groups will perform better than those in CMC groups.

H1B: For mixed-motive tasks, organizational profit will be higher in FTF groups than in CMC groups.

Leadership and its influence on small groups has been studied extensively 1,38 . Classic studies of <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> leadership in small groups have generally found that groups with leaders can be more effective than groups without leaders. For example, Borgatta and Bales 4 found that a leader was necessary to achieve task-oriented<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> goals. French 15 found that groups with leaders are less likely to split into subgroups and factions. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Leadership has not received wide attention in the GDSS literature. GDSS research has focused on the role and impact of an independent facilitator 11,19,30 . A few studies, however, have examined the role of leader.<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Hiltz et al. 21 examined the interacting effects of elected leader and feedback to members of groups supported<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> by a GDSS. They found that designated leadership improved levels of consensus.

Ho and Raman 22 used a preference task to study the effect of leadership and type of decision support no<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Ž support, manual support, and GDSS support on post-meeting consensus, equality of influence, and influence of. the leader. They found that leadership did not increase post-meeting consensus and that group decision aids that support task structure undermine leadership in small group meetings. Further, leaders of GDSS groups were less influential than their counterparts in groups with no GDSS support.

Lim et al. 27 viewed leadership as the exercise of influence and used a resource allocation task to study ‘the<sup>w x</sup> interaction effects of GDSS and elected leadership on influence attempts in a decision-room setting.’ They found that in groups with a leader, the GDSS had no effect on the leader’s influence. In groups without a leader, influence was more uniformly distributed if the groups used GDSS than if they did not use GDSS.

In the three studies above 21,22,27 , a leader did not have the authority to override member decisions. Group<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> leaders in organizations, however, have the authority to override member decisions or to select the best decision among the alternative choices. In our study the designated leader had the authority to override the consensus recommendation of group members or to select among competing recommendations. The leader did not have the typical supervisory prerogatives of promoting group members, changing rewards, and terminating membership in the group. The leader could influence the group using his or her power of personal persuasion.

The leader’s reward is based on the performance of the organization. In maximizing the payoff to the organization, a leader may make decisions that result in lower payoffs for members relative to their respective optima. Since the primary means of control the members have is information, they will attempt to manipulate it and may not be forthright in communicating their private information. On the other hand, because the group members have worked together previously and will have to work on future projects together, the leader and members may accommodate each other to achieve a mutually beneficial solution. Results of previous research on leadership in small groups 4,15 supports the latter case. Hence, we expect that groups with leaders will<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> outperform groups without leaders. This leads to the following formal hypothesis:

H2: For mixed-motive tasks, organizational profit will be higher in groups with a leader than in groups without a leader.

## 2.2. Performance Õariability

For groups whose members have different objectives, the variability of the performance of the members can be an indicator of the degree of positive social behavior, such as, cooperation among group members and the sharing of private information. Siegel et al. 36 suggest that groups communicating electronically exhibit more<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> antisocial behavior than face-to-face groups. Furthermore, the effect of de-individuation in the CMC group may lead to the members viewing the problem as a win–lose situation, we expect the variance among member performance will be smaller in FTF groups than in CMC groups. This suggests:

H3: The performance variability among members will be lower for FTF groups than for CMC groups.

The leader acts as a cohesive force 15 , helps achieve task-oriented goals 4 , and improves the level of <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> consensus 21 by trying to be fair to every member. Thus, we expect groups with leaders will exhibit less<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> variability in member payoffs than groups without leaders. However, the leader does not posses all the necessary information to make a decision that is fair to all members. To be fair the leader needs to accurately gauge the cost information of each member. Because the leader has control and the members may fear that he<sup>r</sup>she will exercise it to maximize his<sup>r</sup>her own payoff, the members may not communicate cost information to the leader truthfully.

In groups without a leader, members have to agree on a mutually acceptable solution. One expects two factors to affect an individual’s decision to agree on a solution. The first factor is how much lower than optimal is the proposed solution. The second factor is whether his<sup>r</sup>her payoff is close to what is perceived to be the payoff for the other members. Furthermore, as the group members need to work together on other projects in the future one expects that they will cooperate to achieve a solution that provides similar payoffs to each other. Hence, we expect that the members will be ‘fair’ to each other regardless of whether there is a leader or not. The impact of a leader on performance variability in such an environment should not be significant. This suggest the following hypothesis:

H4: The performance variability among members will be similar for groups with a leader and groups without a leader.

## 2.3. Performance expectations

For tasks that require negotiation among group members and for which each member will receive a payoff based on the group’s overall performance, a member’s actual and expected payoff may be different. Each member’s expected payoff will be equal to his<sup>r</sup>her actual payoff if the members communicate accurate cost information to one another. Media Richness Theory 8,9 and Task-Medium-Fit 29 suggest FTF medium is <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> more appropriate, for a negotiation task, than CMC medium. FTF medium may facilitate the exchange of information between members in negotiation groups. In addition, members of CMC groups may be less likely to share privileged information with other members because of the dysfunctional effect of de-individuation. This will result in a larger deviation of a member’s actual and expected payoff in CMC groups compared to FTF groups. We propose the following hypothesis:

H5: The difference between a member’s actual and expected payoff will be smaller for members in FTF groups than for members in CMC groups.

The influence of a leader as a cohesive force 15 helps improve levels of consensus 21 and may facilitate<sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> the exchange of information in the group. On the other hand, members working on a negotiation task may try to bias the leader in their favor by exchanging information that may not be completely truthful. Given these opposing factors, we expect that the deviation between a member’s actual and expected payoff will not be significantly affected by the existence of a leader. This suggests the following hypothesis:

H6: The difference between a member’s actual and expected payoff will be the same for members in groups with a leader as for members in groups without a leader.

## 2.4. Truthfulness of information exchange

For situations in which the knowledge necessary to solve a problem is distributed among different group members, the truthfulness of the information that is exchanged may influence decision outcomes. For example, since each member wants to influence the leader as well as the other members to the set of orders that maximize his her payoff, a member may report inflated or deflated cost information to decrease or increase the Ž . attractiveness of an order to the leader and the other group members.

The impact of communication channel and leader on the degree to which members share information and distort the information that is exchanged is important. It is important because the truthfulness of information that is exchanged may influence decision outcomes. If a member can convince others to agree with a solution that is most desirable to him<sup>r</sup>her, then his<sup>r</sup>her payoff will increase. FTF medium is a ‘rich’ medium which allows for the exchange of facial and other non-verbal cues. These cues may convey information that is useful to gauge the truthfulness of others in negotiation tasks 29 . In CMC groups the members do not have face-to-face contact<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> and may tend to be less forthcoming when sharing information. Siegel et al. 36 suggest that groups <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> communicating electronically exhibit more antisocial behavior than FTF groups. Hence, the degree of truthful information exchange may be affected by the communication channel. We propose the following hypothesis:

H7: The members of FTF groups will reveal their information more truthfully than members of CMC groups.

There are several factors to consider with respect to the impact of leader on truthfulness of information exchange. Because a leader can be a cohesive force on the group 15 , the leader may be able to positively<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> influence members to reveal their information truthfully. On the other hand, with the authority to impose a solution, a leader may have a negative influence on truthfulness. The group members may have an incentive to be untruthful if they feel that the leader will exercise his<sup>r</sup>her authority to maximize organizational profit. Since each member’s optimal may be different from that of the organization, the leader’s solution may not be best for the members. In an attempt to maximize their individual payoff, members may misrepresent information they disseminate to others. We expect that, on average, the positive influence of the leader on truthfulness will offset the negative influence that results from conflicting objectives. Hence, groups without a formal leader will be as truthful as groups with a formal leader. This suggests the following hypothesis:

H8: The members of groups without a leader will reveal their information equally truthfully as members of groups with a leader.

## 2.5. Satisfaction with the group solution

We define the term ‘satisfaction with the solution’ to mean how close a member believes that his herŽ . solution has come to the best solution that he she could achieve. Prior GDSS research investigating satisfactionŽ . with the solution indicates that satisfaction is higher in FTF groups than in CMC groups 23,32,33 . Of these studies, only one 33 considered a mixed-motive task. Based on this prior research we expect that in a situation<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> in which the group is faced with solving a complex mixed-motive task, the degree of satisfaction with the solution will be greater for the FTF groups than for the CMC groups.

H9: Members of FTF groups will be more satisfied with the final solution than members of CMC groups.

The impact of leader on member satisfaction with the solution has not been studied for groups using a GDSS on a mixed-motive task. Given the lack of research in this area, the impact of the leader on the group’s satisfaction with the solution is difficult to predict. Because a leader has the power to impose a solution on the members, the members may feel resentment and become dissatisfied with the solution. On the other hand, the leader can act as a cohesive force 15 on the group and foster cooperation among members. In this case, the leader’s impact on the group members satisfaction with the solution is positive. In the absence of substantive research favoring either of the two opposing tendencies, we propose the following hypothesis:

H10: Members of groups with leaders will be equally satisfied with the final solution as those without leaders.

## 2.6. Frustration with the process

We distinguish between satisfaction with the solution and frustration with the process. Whereas the former reflects an individual’s self-assessment of achievement, the latter reflects an individual’s assessment of the process. For a mixed-motive task, McGrath and Hollingshead 29 and Daft and Lengel 8,9 suggest that<sup>w x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> electronic communication channels are restrictive and hence not a good fit compared to FTF channels. An electronic channel delays feedback and filters the cues that can be conveyed in face-to-face or verbal communication. Prior research has reported lower frustration with the process in FTF groups than in CMC groups 33 . Members that have to negotiate may feel frustrated with the process in a constrained communica-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tion environment. We propose the following hypothesis:

H11: Frustration with the process will be lower for members of FTF groups than for members of CMC groups.

The authority invested in the leader to select among competing solutions or to override a consensus solution of group members can lead to frustration with the process among group members. In groups where members have studied together, worked on group projects together, and were in the same department of the university, the experience of working together can lessen the impact of what might otherwise be viewed as authoritative behavior of the leader. In groups without a leader, any inability to reach consensus and the absence of a mechanism to break a stalemate could lead to frustration. Again, however, groups that have worked together may have developed informal mechanisms for reaching consensus. On balance in both groups with and without a leader, no one compelling factor outweighs all others leading to the following hypothesis:

H12: There will be no difference between groups with leaders and groups without leaders in the degree of frustration with the process.

## 3.2. The task

The task used in this experiment was a production planning problem. A company manufactures four products. Although each product has a set of basic characteristics, they differ in some features depending on customer requirements and specific orders. A customer order consists of some combination of all four products, each tailored to the customer’s specification. Associated with each order is a total revenue value which depends on the number of products and the complexity of modifying the products to satisfy the specific requirements. For example, an order might consist of 200 units of product one, 450 units of product two, 200 units of product three, and 400 units of product four. Total revenue generated when this order is filled might be US\$865. The information available to the subject is the details of the order and the total revenue the order generates. A decision to fill an order means that the order is satisfied completely; no partial orders are shipped.

The group represented by three department managers—and a leader for some groups will meet to decideŽ . which orders to fill. Associated with each order is a departmental projected cost that is an estimate the organization has regarding the departmental cost incurred if an order is filled. This information is available to all departments all group members . It represents an estimate one member department has of the costs of theŽ . Ž . other members. Each department, however, has information about its internal costs, the Actual Departmental Cost ADC , that is not available to the other departments unless the department wishes to reveal these costs. Ž . ADC is not necessarily equal to the projected departmental cost. Further, each department has information about Uncompensated Departmental Effort Cost UDEC which represents the intangible cost the department incursŽ . for filling an order for a specific level of effort. The UDEC may be viewed as an individual’s opportunity cost of extra time and effort. In general, the harder the employees work, the lower the ADCs become. However, the harder they work, the more personal resources they will provide. The UDEC captures the extra cost of such personal resources as expending more energy due to a more intense workload. In essence, the ADC is a decreasing function of effort while the UDEC is an increasing function of effort.

The real payoff reward to eachŽ . Ž . member, however, is the member’s percentage of organizational profit minus the Uncompensated Departmental Effort Cost. Thus, the reward each member receives depends on the Actual Departmental Costs of other members as well as his<sup>r</sup>her own. Due to the interaction between a member’s reward and other members’ costs, when a member lowers his her effort level, the corresponding ADC goes up and hence other members’ bonus Ž . decrease too. Each member of the group receives the same percentage of profit as a bonus. The reward is the bonus minus the cost of extra effort UDEC . Recall that a standard projected cost is assigned to each order, and Ž . this is an estimate a member has regarding other member costs of filling each order. Each member may update the default projected costs if he<sup>r</sup>she wishes to communicate actual cost information to other members and the leader. The leader only has information about default projected costs at each department. The leader’s reward increases as the departmental costs decrease. The problem for the group is to decide which subset of all orders to fill in order to maximize the reward each person receives without violating product capacity constraints.

The leader has the authority to impose his her solution on the managers. The leader uses these as the initial values of ADCs. The department managers may send the leader updated information about their ADCs. The reward for each member is computed by substituting the solution—a set of orders—adopted by the group into the objective function of members. Member and leader performance is measured by the reward points they achieve. Because the objective of each participant is to maximize his or her individual reward, the higher the value of the objective function, the better will be the individual’s performance.

## 3.3. Subjects

Ninety junior and senior undergraduate business students enrolled in a business decision-making course served as subjects for the experiment. Solution of a case that involved solving the type of problem described above was a course requirement and an integral part of the course. Students were required to work in ‘management teams’ to solve the problem.

Fifteen percent of the course grade was allocated for the experiment. Each student’s grade for the experiment was based on the reward points he<sup>r</sup>she received on the exercise. Once the group agreed on a solution, the reward obtained by each individual was compared with that of others in the same treatment condition. The reward distribution within each treatment condition was then used as the basis for allocating grade points. Grade points could range from a maximum of 15 points to a minimum of 0 points out of the total 100 points allocated for the course grade.

We conducted the experiment early in the term. Completion of the experiment was one of the first components for which a grade was earned by the students. Consequently, students had no grade ‘cushion’ which might lead to their not treating the experiment seriously. The grade on this experiment was a substantive contributor to the student’s final grade. It provided strong motivation to do well in the experiment.

Each student was randomly assigned to a role and to either a three person no leader or four person leaderŽ . Ž . group. The groups were formed the first week of class and were given multiple group assignments during the term before they were given this case. Each group was asked not to discuss the experiment with other groups and was asked to apply the university ‘honor’ system to this project. This study was a part of a larger set of experiments and the subjects did not know what experimental condition they would be assigned to prior to the experiment. To alleviate the problem of unfair comparisons across treatment conditions and roles, each role was compared to the same role in the same treatment condition. Student grades were determined with respect to how well others with comparable roles and treatment conditions performed. These deterred students from discussing the project with other students.

## 3.4. GDSS features

A GDSS was designed to support the problem solving and communication needs of the groups. The GDSS ran in a PC windows environment on a Novell Netware LAN. These results have implications for groups working on mixed-motive negotiation tasks. In addition, it is important to closely examine the extent to which the use of a GDSS on mixed-motive tasks exacerbates or ameliorates the effect of information asymmetry. Because the decisions that are guided by the GDSS may only be as good as the information that is available, the issues of information sharing should be central to GDSS research.

## 5. Summary

As indicated earlier, the results of this experiment indicate that groups, faced with a mixed-motive task, using a face-to-face communication channel outperform groups using a computer-mediated communication channel. These results tend to support the predictions of Media Richness Theory and the assertions of McGrath and Hollingshead. The results, however, may be tempered by the degree of untruthfulness observed in the information exchanges among participants. This latter phenomenon highlights a crucial issue that must be researched further. The issue has great bearing on how one constructs and manages small groups working on mixed-motive tasks and on the use of a GDSS by these groups.

In our setting, we observed that the authority of the leader to override member solutions may be hampered by the information received. However, the leader is better able to influence members to exchange truthful information in a FTF environment than in a CMC environment. This may suggest that in CMC environments and in the face of information asymmetry, a leader may not be able to facilitate truthful information exchange.

As is typical of studies of this nature, one cannot generalize the results of this study beyond the scope of this experimental setting. Also the use of student subjects limits the generality of results. Although the students in this study had significant stakes in the outcomes and were very familiar with the task, they, nevertheless, constitute a subset of the larger decision-making universe. Replications of the experiment with other subgroups of decision-makers or a field study should broaden the generality of the results. Finally, the number of groups per cell 6, 6, 6, and 8 was less than optimal. Although the sample size of each cell is of the order of otherŽ . controlled studies on groups, <sup>5</sup> larger cell sizes would provide stronger statistical results.

The results clearly call for further research of information asymmetry’s impact on group members and the group leader. The effect of truthful and untruthful information exchange on group decisions, group performance, and group dynamics is of great importance and significance in environments where the goals and objectives of group members and group leaders may not be aligned.

Future research should also focus on the effect of other factors including the effects of different reward mechanisms, the effects of group composition, and the effects of different GDSS types on the negotiation process and negotiation outcomes. All of these factors are important organizational variables, controllable by organizational designers, that need to be studied as CMC groups, using distributed GDSS, become widespread in organizations.
