# Managerial intuition and the development of executive support systems

Feng-Yang Kuo <sup>a,b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Business, UniÕersity of Colorado at DenÕer, P.O. Box 173364, DenÕer, CO 80217-3364, USA b Department of Information Management, National Sun-Yat-Sen UniÕersity, Taiwan

Accepted 3 September 1998

## Abstract

Managerial intuition is a well-recognized cognitive ability but still poorly understood for the purpose of developing effective Executive Support Systems ESS . This paper reviews research on cognition involved in intuition. The reviewŽ . shows that, in everyday decision making, executives are not passive choice makers but are active sensemakers who rely on perception, action, and imaginistic reasoning to arrive at solutions to problems. The combination of these cognitive resources appears to be seamless. Furthermore, the knowledge needed for problem solving is distributed between the manager’s mind and the surrounding world. As a result, managers’ intuition can be effective in handling dynamic, ill-structured problem situations. To develop an ESS that fits this perception–action ecology of the manager’s life in a hectic world, we must go beyond the emphasis of the tasks’ functionalities. The author proposes an ecological model of managerial intuition and recommends methodologies that focus the analysis on the interplay between the manager and their environment. He also suggests guidelines to improve the ESS development such that the powerful intuition of managers and the analytic capability of the computer may be combined. q 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. ## 1. Introduction

The concept of using computer-based information systems to support decision making has existed since the 1970s 31 . Over the last 20 years, many systems <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> have been developed to aid corporate managers. Today, Executive Support Systems ESS are in-Ž . creasingly becoming necessary for knowledge workers to collect, process, and disseminate information in a constantly changing, global world in which speedy decisions are key to business competitiveness <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 30,66,67 . The premise of ESS to support executives is that their use may improve executives’ effectiveness in conducting intelligence, design, and choice work 56 . However, researchers of ESS have shown<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> that their effectiveness can be limited 10 . Elam and <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Leidner 11 , for example, discover that the concep-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tual executives may choose not to use ESS, as opposed to analytical executives who rely on ESS for identifying problems. More recently, Vandenbosch and Higgins 62 conclude that executives are <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> most likely to use the system to support their existing beliefs and, as a result, the business continues as usual.

Indeed, the real world in which executives operate rarely permits them to enjoy a peaceful, uninterrupted session with a computer system. In a classical study of managerial work, Mintzberg 41 finds that<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> managers spend most of their time interacting and communicating with other people. Their thinking seems to be abstracted from the past experience instead of based on the here and now. Yet, despite frequent interruptions, they are quite successful in using the myriad of information to make decisions. They can ‘forge understanding out of some very limited and ambiguous cues, . . . by and large, these people are very successful at dealing with information, constructing a workable world, and taking effective action’ 17 , p. 2 . The success is attributed to the manager’s skillful use of intuition to envision possible future scenarios that may cause problems and generate new ideas to respond to the problems <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 29 .

How can ESS assist executives who rely on their intuition in a hectic, fragmented world? The present author attempts to address this question by reviewing recent research in human cognition. This work reveals opportunities for innovative approaches to analyzing managers’ work and to improving ESS design. In Section 2, a review of cognition involved in intuitive decision making is presented. It includes recent research findings in situated cognition, ecology of human–machine systems, and distributed cognition. The review shows that knowledge for intuitive decision making is often socially constructed and that perception and action play a critical role for problem solving in real world settings. What appears to others intuition is actually a display of well-trained cognitive ability to handle ill-structured problems. Therefore, the focus of ESS development should reside in the interplay between the problem solver and the problem environment. The review leads to the proposal of an ecological cognitive model of managerial intuition, discussed in Section 3. In Section 4, many guidelines for ESS development suggested by the model are presented. Section 5 concludes by presenting future research issues.

## 2. Intuition in action

Intuition is the psychological function that allows knowing to occur by means of transmitting perceptions in an unconscious way 29 . It enables individu-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> als to ‘grasp the meaning, significance, or structure of a problem without explicit reliance on analytical apparatus’ hence, it can ‘synthesize disparate ideas, achieving serendipity as it senses combinations which did not appear to be related in the past’ 25 , p. 919 .<sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> Intuition and analytical thinking are not mutually exclusive, but rather work in tandem in problem solving. Intuition permits executives to instantly form a judgment when confronted with a problematic situation, while analytical thinking can help executives probe the structure and rules that may underlie the observed phenomenon.

Managerial intuition is trained 50 ; years of expe-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> rience are needed for individuals to develop the capacity to apply it effectively. It is found mostly in dynamic, unstructured real world settings. Occasionally, it causes great harm when intuition dominates the decision process. In the past, researchers have shied away from studying intuition, probably because of the lack of well-accepted scientific method for researching this issue. But it has gained more recognition more recently by researchers of ecological psychology 14,19 , situated cognition 20,34 ,<sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> mental models 26–28 , distributed cognition 24 , <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> human–computer interaction 16 , and naturalistic <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> decision making 32 . In general, the research has<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> shown that intuition has the following characteristics.

Ž . 1 Effective intuition is trained and is domain specific 50,51 . It works both subtractively and addi-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tively. Subtractively, it enables the individual to reduce a complex situation to a few abstract concepts; additively, it allows him<sup>r</sup>her to fill in the details to grasp the meaning of a situation even when only a few clues are given. Intuition is fundamental to managerial sense making that ‘entails intricate and often unconscious processes of attending, comparing, attributing, relating, reflecting, retaining, and so on <sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> 18 , p. 61 .

Ž . 2 Managerial intuition occurs in dynamic, illstructured environments that have constantly moving and competing goals. In such environments, tasks are distributed over different times and spaces, and individuals have little time or information to conduct detailed, stepwise analytic reasoning. Hence, their success relies on skillful intuition that leads to immediate assessment such that they can find a quick, intermediary decision for one particular situation. Moreover, there may be multiple solutions, but managers often consider only a plausible one and improve on it over time 36 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Ž . 3 Perception is key to reaching immediate situation assessment. While traditionally perception is treated as a psychological function that registers and recognizes sensory information to provide input to the central cognitive processor 1 , researchers of<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ecological psychology 19,14 have shown that experts can extract the meaning of a situation directly by perception. For example, experts of aerial photo interpretation can see things that are nowhere to be found in the picture, implying that elaborate inferencing is involved in making the interpretation; yet, ‘what appears to the outsider or the novice to be elaborate inference making is largely perceptual and is immediate if not direct’ 22 , p. 289 . In other<sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> words, to these experts, meanings arise directly from perception. ‘Perceiving is psychosomatic act, not of the mind or the body but of a living observer’ 19 ,<sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> p. 240 . With repeated practices, individuals are so<sup>x</sup> attuned to perceive a situation’s invariants which may be physical e.g., intensity of lights and color ,Ž . interpersonal e.g., expressions and gestures , cul-Ž . tural e.g., the acts of a ceremonial ritual , or organi-Ž . zational e.g., office layout for executives and em-Ž ployees that they can immediately arrive at an as-. sessment. More importantly, these invariants are perceived as a whole, making the situation assessment appear to be direct. Hence, expert perception cannot be separated from the environment or the problem context. ‘Information is not a thing-in-itself, but is rather a relation between the data, the world the data refer to, and the observer’s expectations, intentions, and interests. As a result, informativeness is not a property of the data field alone, but is a relation between the observer and the data field’ 71 , p. <sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> 176 .<sup>x</sup>

Ž . 4 While perception directly gives an individual the meaning of a situation which she<sup>r</sup>he is in, actions must follow, not as a result of consideration, but as a way of being in the situation 70 . Hence,Ž . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> intuitive decision making can be seen as a seamless combination of perceptions and actions, among which interpersonal perceptions and actions are probably the most critical. Managers in work ‘are not subject to information input; they are actively asking questions to the environment based on their perception of the context’ 52 , p. 132 . As a result, managers act <sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> thinkingly. ‘Thinking is inseparably woven into and occurs simultaneously with action. When managers tour, read, talk, supervise, and meet, those actions contain managerial thought, they do their thinking for managers, they are substitutes for thinking, and they reduce the necessity for separate reflective episodes. Connected ideas, which are the essence of thought, can be found outside the mind, with relatively little assistance from the mind’ 69 , p. 222 .<sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Ž . 5 Successful intuition requires coordination of multiple cognitive processes to support the nonstopping interactions between the individual and the environment. ‘How can I know what I think until I see what I say?’ 68 , p. 133 . Reasoning triggered<sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> by intuition involves induction and deduction based on mental models that relate the individual to the surrounding world 28,32,33 . ‘The mind builds <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> models, and the structure of the models is the basis of human conceptions of the structure of the world . . . In brief, mental models<sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> are knowledge depicting the relation between a person and the structure of a situation. These models are imaginistic, enabling the expert to envision him or herself situated in various problematic scenarios. Mental models are, never-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> theless, almost always incomplete. ‘Mental models seem a pervasive property of humans . . . Ž . 6 The imaginsitic reasoning is a significant contribution of intuition to creative problem solving in a dynamic environment 28 . In encountering a novel <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> situation, people can construct a new model by combining parts of one or more existing models that are perceived to be relevant to the current situation <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 57 . Typically, this selection of relevant aspects involves identifying imaginistic similarities between the known situation and the new one. Thus, without conscious knowing, people may transfer knowledge in the form of visual phenomena known as either analogies or metaphors. Intuition involving analogical or metaphoric models may then serve as the genesis for a larger mental model of a new problem domain.

Ž . 7 The cycles of perception, action, and the imaginistic reasoning can be useful partly because the knowledge for decision in a dynamic environment is socially-constructed 24 . That is, much of <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> the knowledge available to handle a problematic situation is distributed between an expert’s mind and his<sup>r</sup>her environment. As a result, ‘connected ideas . . . can be found outside the mind’ 69 , p. <sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> 222 . Perception, action, and imaginistic reasoning <sup>x</sup> collectively allow the individual to intuitively integrate this distributed knowledge with only limited memory resource. ‘With disembodied intellect, isolated from the world, intelligent behavior requires a considerable amount of knowledge, lots of deep planning and decision making, and efficient memory storage and retrieval. When the intellect is tightly coupled to the world, decision making and action can take place within the context established by the physical environment, where the structures can often aid as a distributed intelligence, taking some of the memory and computational burden off the human <sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> 47 , p. 6 . Hence, while facing difficult decisions, managers find themselves in situations perceived to be relevant either by intention or by accident. Relying on the knowledge in the mind and in the world, they act relentlessly. Their actions are frequently interrupted by the uncertainty and dynamics of the situation. Nevertheless, they may reach some new situation assessment that in turn allows creative solutions to emerge without conscious knowing.

Ž . 8 Managers may employ both intuitive and analytical thinking in making decisions. The issue is not which type of thinking is valid, but which has priority for managers in their everyday life. Linguistic type of problem representations can be constructed for analytic, propositional reasoning when the individual chooses to do so consciously. But translating the observed situation into some formal, abstract representation is difficult and time consuming; consequently, people avoid it. Intuitive thinking that involves mental models simply fits the ecology of everyday decision making for managers. Conversely, analytic thinking may replace intuition when intuition fails and when the individual realizes this failure. Also, analytical reasoning may prevail when the individual is motivated for reasons such as the desire to obtain a certain outcome and peer pressure 12 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Ž . 9 Intuition involves inherent personal values, intentions, and goals 28 . Also, stress, fatigue, and<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> emotional factors can influence judgment reached by intuition 12,13 . Finally, intuition may be con-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> strained some anchoring information of the environment that receives most attention 60 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Ž . 10 Overreliance on intuition can lead to systematic biases and errors undetected by the individual. People are satisfiers 44 . Given limited memory <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> resource, it is both simpler less effort and moreŽ . efficient less time to rely on intuition, leading to Ž . phenomenon known as ‘cognitive lock-up’ 43 , <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ‘cognitive economy’ 18 , and ‘cognitive miser’ 13 .<sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> In brief, ‘our processing of information is error prone. As a result, mental models,<sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> despite their efficiency, may fail although people may be unaware of this weakness and engage in a series of fruitless attempts to make effective decisions.

## 4. Implications to the development of ESS

Structurally speaking, an ESS environment includes the system itself, the people e.g., executives,Ž operating staff, and vendors , and data internal and. Ž external 66 . Its development involves many stages,. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> of which determining information requirements is probably the most important; it also requires a highly easy-to-use interfaces and a high-level performance to satisfy its users 67 . Yet even with these features, <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> executives may not use ESS. Some may argue this is because that information technology, with its limited modality and intelligence, is not suited for supporting multiple senses needed for managerial intuition. But with great advances in graphics, multimedia, and virtual reality, information technology may soon be a viable medium for human intuitive perception and action. Also, progress is being made in understanding the managerial cognition involved in intuition. The research in both the technology and cognition collectively suggest opportunities for determining the manager’s needs and developing ESS that fits the perception–action ecology of managerial work. According to the ecological model Fig. 1 , one of theŽ . emphases should be on how the ESS may co-exist with other world knowledge in the manager’s environment and how the entire world knowledge may correspond to the knowledge possessed by the manager. The other is the ESS’ support for perception and actions. Consequently, self–environment interplays must become the focus of needs analysis and self–ESS–environment interfaces must be the focus of design.

## 4.1. Self–enÕironment interplay as the focus of needs analysis

The world surrounding managers is comprised of a constant ebb and flow of events, people, and objects, afloat on a vast amount of information which is continuously generated, updated, and disseminated. Their tasks are fragmented and distributed in different times and spaces, creating various decision situations and placing enormous burden on the cognitive capacity of the manager Fig. 2 . To under-Ž . stand managers’ needs, we collect data on these events, people, and objects, with the help of instruments such as interview and questionnaire. We may also examine the procedures of tasks and the overall business processes in which they are involved. We then study task characteristics and attempt to achieve a cognitive fit 64 by matching the presentation<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> format to the tasks. The analysis is exhaustive and time consuming. Still, at the end many ESS do not play a critical role in executives’ lives, simply because they do not support managerial intuition.

What can be done? The ecological cognitive model of managerial intuition suggests that the most critical aspect of executives’ cognition is the interplay between the manager and their environment, including, of course, interactions with their associates. The articulation of information needs is often an afterthought, a justification arisen after the manager has already made the decision. Furthermore, this articulation may not reveal the total picture of the manager thinking. In particular, the articulation may ignore two facts: the knowledge used in intuition is socially constructed and the mental models will almost inevitably be incomplete.

Hence, the ESS designer must expand the analysis to include the examination of the managers’ environment, their acts, and the consequences of these acts. The rich information exchanges, verbal or nonverbal, and their related actions must be observed and analyzed. This helps the designer to gain insight into individual and environmental properties useful to the design of ESS Fig. 3 . Important individual proper- Ž . ties include intention, goals, plans, and effectivities, while the environmental properties include those related to when, where, how, and affordances. Effectivities and affordances are two important concepts in ecological psychology 19 . Effectivities are the <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> action capability of the organism in that environment, and affordances are what the environment provides or furnishes to an individual. ‘The possibilities of the environment and the way of life go together inseparably. The environment constrains what the animal can do, and the concept of a niche in ecology reflects this fact. Within the limits, the human animal can alter the affordances of the environment but is still the creature of his or her situation’ 19 , p. 143 . The analysis should also reveal<sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> managers’ mental models useful to problem solving. These models are knowledge about the situation in relation to self and are best understood, not by asking the individual to reflect, but by observing the individuals acting out in problematic situations. In the following discussion, we will review several approaches that are useful for this purpose.

## 4.1.1. Symbols, semiotics, phenomenology, and ethnography

Corporate symbols are an important repository of corporate culture and signify important routines that influence the perception of and convey understandings and meanings among members of an organization 18 . They include visual data, such as drawings <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> used in exchange of ideas, forms used for handling transactions, and graphs showing the organization hierarchy or work flow. They can also be signs, such as logos, metaphors, slogans, stories, and myths that stand for some significant organizational experience. The symbols are important means through which to study managerial thoughts because they may reveal the interpretations and beliefs underlying a certain managerial action. Hence, Meyer 39 advocates the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> use of these corporate symbols for managerial studies.

Semiotics 5 is the study of signs to research<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> systems of meaning that undergird occupational cultures. The objective of a semiotic analysis of a situation is to understand the expressions used by respective individuals to communicate with one another, the content of the expression, the rules for coupling the expressions to the contents, and the set of alternative responses. Thus, this analysis can help clarify how managerial perception of a situation is arrived by means of interpersonal communication.

Phenomenological interview 38 , a methodology<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> to observe and interpret executives’ on-going activities, accepts that the thinking of executives is manifest in their experience. In phenomenology, experience can be cognitive or affective, verbal or nonverbal, conscious or unconscious, and structured or undefined. The methodology begins with the interviewer defining the boundary of activities to be studied, while the interviewee chooses the entry point at which both the interviewee and the interviewer can focus on some topic. This act of focusing is followed by a constellation process of observing, questioning, discussing, reflecting, and revealing shared by both the interviewee and the interviewer so that, collectively, both can reach a deep understanding of the interviewee’s experience. The details of this process are recorded, by either transcript or videotaping, for later analysis. The process is completed when both the interviewee and the interviewer sense that they have amply explored the topics within the boundary and that they have reached a closure.

Recently, ethnography has been proposed as a means to analyze user requirements for defining human–computer interfaces and groupware 61 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Ethnography, similar to phenomenology, emphasizes that the designer must become involved in the organization’s daily routines in order to develop an understanding of the organizational knowledge. The designer, as an ethnographer, is ‘a translator, a culture broker, seeking to achieve an equivalence in value among ways of life by allowing us . . . to see ourselves in them’ 2 , p. 160 . In doing so, the <sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> designer is more than an outside observer who is detached from the target to provide some objective judgment. She<sup>r</sup>he, instead, must allow him or herself to be integrated into the daily routines and experience subjectively the manager’s on-going activities. Note that our knowledge of applying ethnography to information systems design is still in its infancy. Much research is needed for us to fully understand its use for ESS design.

## 4.1.2. Situations, eÕents, contexts, and scenarios analysis

The mental model theory prescribes that knowledge is a relation between the situation and a person. Hence, the analysis of knowledge underlying decision making requires us to study environmental perception and the individual actions in problem-solving situations that are both representative of and critical to the success of the manager’s activities. In essence, the phenomenological study tries to accomplish this analysis.

While the phenomenological interview relies on observation of on-going activities, it is rather expensive due to the time and effort needed, and it could become unfriendly because managers’ hectic schedule may not permit them to conduct such interviews. An alternative is to use some hypothetical events in which managers’ decisions can be simulated outside their regular work schedule. This approach may not yield the same depth of insight as the phenomenology interview, but it can at least enable the ESS designer to grasp the essence of the managerial knowledge for use in conjunction with iterative prototyping.

Simulations can be constructed from samples of historical events, near events that almost, but did not, happen to cause a disaster, or likely future events hypothesized by the manager 37 . To ensure rich<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> understanding, a scenario-based approach can be used <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>8 . Different scenarios are constructed for different perspectives of each event so that a comprehensive range of situations can be studied and the results applied to real-life work situations. Furthermore, by asking the manager to act out in each situation, the designer can identify the goals and intentions of the individual, critical objects of the environment, their perceived representations, and actions taken to interact with these representations to achieve goals. A closely related approach is context analysis 7,23 <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> based on the information flow among activities, the context in which various activities occur, the steps taken for each activity, and the physical layouts of the working environment. The results of analyzing scenarios and<sup>r</sup>or contexts can then be used to design the ESS.

## 4.2. Self–ESS–enÕironment interfaces as the focus of design

The introduction of ESS to the manager’s work will inevitably change the individual, the environment, and the relations between the manager and the environment. The ESS contains an abstract representation of the world’s past and current affairs. This representation is symbolic, typically in some digital form such that computational functions can be performed and useful information can be generated to facilitate managers to perform all sorts of tasks. In an ideal world, the system may do so in a way compatible to a human, as depicted by the computer Hal in the movie 2010 so that the powerful intuition of the manager and the analytic capability of the computer can be combined. The intuitive computers, however, are still far from the reality given today’s technology although combining the people’s intuition and the computer’s analytic capacity remains the goal of the ESS. This goal poses the greatest challenge to ESS designers Fig. 4 . On one hand, we can designŽ . sophisticated programmable systems such that all things are possible but nothing is easy, while on the other, we can design push-button systems such that all things are easy but nothing is possible. This dilemma can be resolved by developing ESS that fits the ecology of managerial work. By ecological ESS we mean one that interactively facilitates visualization, exploration, interpretation, reflection, understanding, and eventually sound decision making, all of which take place while the manager acts thinkingly. It is an integral part of the manager’s perceptual and action fields where managers can function with ease based on their mental models of the environment in which the problem is situated.

Making user interface representation the center of ESS design: If direct perception and thinking actions are what make managerial intuition effective, a sound interface representation to support both must become the center of ESS design. The call for making representations the center of the ESS design activities is not new. Simon, for example, did so by saying ‘‘solving a problem simply means representing it so as to make the solution transparent’’ 54 , p. 77 and<sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> later reiterated it by stating ‘‘attention to the limits of human rationality helps to understand why representation is important’’ 55 , p. 132 . A good problem <sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> representation, though not a complete depiction of the knowledge distributed between the mind and the world, reveals multiple aspects of this knowledge and offers the individual many ways to intuitively interact with it so that immediate situation assess ment is possible. For example, a map of London was used by Dr. John Snow in his attempt to stop the cholera epidemic that broke out in London in the fall of 1854 59 . ‘Dr. Snow, a physician subscribing to<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> the disputed theory that cholera was transmitted by contaminated drinking water, . . . Starting with a London map, Snow drew black dots to plot the addresses of the cholera victims, and crosses to plot the location of public water pumps. The map showed that nearly everyone who had died lived near the Broad Street water pump. Snow had the handle of the contaminated pump removed and halted the epidemic that had claimed over 500 lives’ 39 , p. 218 .<sup>ww</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>x</sup> Another example is the use of blocks to represent job requirements in a factory flow for job scheduling which, in this case, is transformed into a Chinese puzzle 35 . A third example is the visual decision<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> making 3 , in which a complex decision tree is<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> mapped into a two-dimensional array that allows one or a group of individuals to easily perform tasks like resource allocation and organizational planning. These representations differ in the way they are designed, e.g., a map is an analogical miniaturization of the city while the puzzle is an innovative, abstract depiction of the job scheduling task. But they are all imaginistic models of the problem, allowing the solution to be derived by direct perception and iterative interaction with the representation. According to the ecological cognitive model, a good representation is one that allows different properties of the problem to be easily perceived as a whole. The map of London affords to Dr. Snow not only the streets but also much implicit knowledge of everyday life in London such that his reasoning of cholera transmission could be aided by perception. Resource allocation may involve complicated social, political, and economical factors, but these factors are reduced to series of simple acts, e.g., changing the cell size, of which the impact of these factors can be easily perceived as a whole. Note that the easiness of the representation to a person is not necessarily based on the aesthetics of the representation, but on how natural it is for the person to map the representation to his<sup>r</sup>her mental models of the problem situation and how difficult it is to recover from accidental errors 46 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

An essential feature of an effective ESS, therefore, must be the capability to maintain constant communication with people critical to the manager’s work. The ESS can anticipate conversation and monitor its progress when needed <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 70 . All of these will increase the reach of<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> the ESS, which in turn broadens the manager’s perceptual and action range. They are critical to managers who must conduct work over time and space.

Stimulate deep thinking by eÕents<sup>r</sup>scenarios simulations: Collaboration networks in essence broaden the perceptual and action range of the manager to arouse deep thinking. Another useful approach is by events simulation that exposes the manger to different situations. A broad range of events can be extracted from the analysis of self–environment interplays, organized in different temporal and spatial sequences, and classified in different contexts or perspectives. From a long-term perspective, simulation by means of these events can enrich the meanings and lead to more complicated thinking because it allows the seemingly disconnected situations to be comprehended as a whole. For an immediate purpose, such simulation may cause the manager to conduct individuating perception that is needed to reach a comprehensive problem assessment because it exposes the manger to potential failures, which provides the incentives for the manager to go beyond the initial intuition.

Facilitate deep thinking by supporting direct manipulation: Managerial actions involve thinking and cannot be separated from perception. For example, in the visual decision making 3 , any adjustment of the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> array reflects the individual’s attempt to consolidate different factors into a workable solution; the adjusted array, as perceived, in turn lead to further assessment and more actions. Thus, the actions to adjust the array, like those in the real world talkingŽ and touring , must be easy, or otherwise cognitive. resources must be directed to address how to use the computer instead of how to solve the problem. In other words, problem-related thinking actions are possible only because actions are intuitive, requiring no conscious effort and thus available cognitive resources can be used to address problems at hand. Supporting intuitive interactions with the ESS, therefore, is imperative. Intuitive interactions with the ESS means direct actions and immediate responses. In the case of Dr. Snow, his acts marking the mapŽ . and the response the concentration of black dotsŽ . enabled him to be confident of his theory that some water pump is contaminated. Had Dr. Snow been unable to find a map and resorted to walk around the area, he would have needed a significant amount of time to discover the location of the faulty pump. In the visual decision making, the two-dimensional array along with two direct-manipulation operators, hook for altering sibling cells’ weights and pumpŽ . The consequence of each act is always immediately visible, allowing the individual to intuitively read the situation and decide on the subsequent acts.

Changing cognitiÕe gear by alternating representations: While the theory of cognitive fit prescribes that information presentation must match the task <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 64 , predetermination of task needs is difficult for ill-structured problems. One way to address this problem is for ESS to support multiple information presentations. Furthermore, Social Judgment Theory <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 21 prescribes that different reasoning styles can be induced by manipulating presentation attributes. For example, a large number of cues, continuous distribution of cue values, and brief time will induce intuitive style; a small number, discrete values, and long duration would yield analytical style. As a result, deliberately manipulating the presentation attributes can be useful to change cognitive gear—from intuition to analytical thinking and vice versa. For example, while a pie chart is obviously an intuitioninducing representation, individuating perception of such charts can be accomplished by highlighting different parts of the pie with different colors in a timed sequence.

PreserÕing situational cues, especially those re-Õealing the constraints to the problem: In solving problems, managers, guided by available mental models, can quickly identify problem constraints and eliminate unproductive courses of actions. This powerful, intuitive mechanism of the mind is possible because problem constraints are directly perceivable. Using the map, the areas with few black dots are quickly eliminated; using the puzzle, the configurations of the blocks immediately show which cannot be stacked. In a representation, facts are important; but visible constraints make intuitive problem solving possible. Note that the constraints are not limited to physical ones. They can also be semantic, cultural, or logical 46 . Semantic constraints rely on the person specific knowledge of the problem. Cultural constraints are commonly-accepted conventions. Logical constraints are those imposed on the different parts of a representation based on some functional, spatial, or temporal relationships that can be obtained logically from the representation’s configuration.

Supporting multi-modal interaction and categorizing the user interfaces: When well designed, a representation that supports multi-modal interaction can be more intuitive than those do not 48 . Speech, typing, and pointing<sup>r</sup>clicking can be combined to reduce input errors. Auditory icons 15 can be used <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> as an object to represent some real world events; a text label or description coupled with the picture allows a person to quickly grasp the picture’s semantics and reduce the likelihood of misinterpretation. In addition, categorization is fundamental to intuitive thinking. Accordingly, a representation’s objects must be classified to match a person’s knowledge. Related objects are grouped so that the mind can process them in chunks. found to be useful 1,58 . Finally, perception can be <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> attuned by enhancing the vividness of a part of the display. A creative use of graphics and sound can compensate for the limitations of the human inspecting ability.

Keeping history and collecting thoughts: What have I done? Reflection of a person’s unconscious acts allows skillful intuition to be developed. Maintaining a history of a person’s interactions with the computer, therefore, can be useful to facilitate intuition. In addition, creative insights often arrive without warning. Hence, the computer must allow a person to record his or her thoughts at the moment of revelation.

## 5. Conclusion and future research

Managerial intuition is a well-recognized cognitive ability but still poorly understood for the purpose of developing effective ESS. This paper has reviewed research on cognition involved in intuition. The review shows that, in everyday decision making, executives are not passive choice makers but are active sensemakers who rely on perception, action, and mental models to arrive at solutions to problems. The combination of these cognitive resources appears to be seamless. Furthermore, the knowledge needed for problem solving is distributed between the manager’s mind and the surrounding world. As a result, managers can rely on their intuition to handle dynamic, ill-structured problem situations.

To develop an ESS that fits this perception–action ecology of the manager’s mind in his<sup>r</sup>her hectic world, we must go beyond the emphasis of the functionalities of their tasks. The analysis must include the interplay between the manager and their environment. For this, many methodologies are recommended. Other recommendations are also made. The interfaces among the manager, the system, and the overall environment must be considered. Training has to be integrated into development as early as possible. The ESS can enrich managerial thinking by well-designed computer–user interfaces, collaboration networks, event simulation, and direct manipulation that makes direct perception and thinking actions easy. As a result, the powerful intuition of managers and the analytic capability of the computer may be combined. Certainly, modern technologies in both computer hardware and software provide great opportunities for innovative ESS design. The limit will be our own imagination.

Still, much research is needed towards the development of ecological ESS that interactively facilitates visualization, exploration, interpretation, reflection, understanding, and eventually sound decision making, all of which take place while the manger acts thinkingly. What constitutes a good design? How do we create abstract representations of a problem situation e.g., blocks for job scheduling ? HowŽ . do we map the constraints of real world problems into those in the representation? When is a concrete analogy e.g., the London Map preferred over an Ž . abstract depiction? How can analytic and imaginistic representations be integrated to unify rationality and intuition? Is a good design always a product of sheer creativity or can it be an output of some software engineering approach? How do we know that we have a good design and when do we stop improving the design? The making of an ecological ESS that is intelligent, interactive, multi-modal, and multimedia requires not only the computational, procedural discipline of software engineering but also the artistic creativity of graphics designing, play writing, film editing, and directing. Currently, the ESS community has not yet developed expertise in the area.

The many unanswered questions and the complexity of ESS development leads to a question regarding the role of ESS designers. In the quest to understand managerial cognition and to develop ESS, the system is both a decision support tool and a research instrument. Accordingly, the designer must bear the responsibility not only for engineering the ESS but also for designing a research experiment. This new role will impart great difficulty on current system developers who are neither trained nor given the time and budget to conduct cognitive research. But we lack the option of ignoring this approach, since we cannot separate the study of managerial cognition from the actual use of ESS in their daily life. This imperative, in turn, calls for the need to educate ESS developers and to develop new research methodologies.

The current state of ESS research can be described as a transition stage as it is shifting from the computational paradigm that has governed the research in the past 30 years to a relatively unknown ecological paradigm. The transition is taking place in part because of the advance in modern computing and communication technologies, and in part because of the new discovery made in ecological psychology, situated cognition, and distributed cognition. The challenges are unprecedented, and years could go by before useful theories can be discovered. Still, integrating research and development can be fruitful, as demonstrated by Konrad Lorenz, an ethnologist and Nobel Laureate, who in 1973 points out naturalistic observation is a legitimate scientific activity that should precede formalization, quantification, and controlled experimentation. Accordingly, by integrating research and development, ESS researchers can position themselves as the front runners in the race to study managerial cognition and the development of ESS that supports managerial intuition.

Professor Feng-Yang Kuo holds a BS degree in Management Science from National Chiao-Tung University, Taiwan and a PhD degree in Information Systems from University of Arizona. He has been a faculty at University of Colorado at Denver since 1985 and is currently on leave to be a professor of Information Management in National Sun-Yet-Sen University, Taiwan. Professor Kuo’s research interests include managerial cognition, human–computer

interfaces, and information economics. He has published articles in Communications of ACM, MIS Quarterly, Information and Management, Decision Support Systems, and Journal of Systems and Software.
