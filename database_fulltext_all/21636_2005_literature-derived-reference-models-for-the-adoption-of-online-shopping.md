# Literature derived reference models for the adoption of online shopping

Man Kit Chang<sup>a,\*</sup>, Waiman Cheung<sup>b</sup>, Vincent S. Lai<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Finance and Decision Sciences, Hong Kong Baptist University, Kowloon Tong, Hong Kong, PR China <sup>b</sup>Department of Decision Sciences and Managerial Economic, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, PR China

Accepted 2 February 2004

Available online 20 June 2004

## Abstract

The aim of the authors of this paper was to identify areas that would aid in developing a better understanding of the dynamics of a customer’s decision to shop online. To accomplish this, a review of the empirical studies on the antecedents of online shopping was performed. From an extensive literature search, a total of 45 relevant articles were identified. The factors that have been investigated in these studies were classified according to their similarity and patterns of their findings were identified and analyzed.

## 1. Introduction

Forecasts in the past (e.g. [12]) predicted that the value of goods and services purchased online could increase rapidly. In addition, research studies (e.g. [31]) revealed that profits from online shopping would be high, such as a three-fold increase in 5 years. Yet these, which once portrayed online shopping spreading like wildfire across country boundaries, were not realized: online shopping failed to live up to its promise and has not even reached the take-off point of the diffusion ‘S’ curve [67].

However, there have not been many empirical studies that attempt to establish the relationship between the antecedent factors and the intention to use or actually use online shopping. In the period after the downturn of the dot-com businesses, it became even more important to understand these factors. Moreover, analyzing the existing studies and identifying the areas that needed improvement could contribute to the better design of e-business sites. Thus, the objective of our survey was to identify the studies investigating the factors that affected the adoption of internet shopping, analyze them, and identify the areas that required further investigation. More specifically, this study was designed to:

1. study online shopping and evaluate research efforts on it;

2. identify the major antecedent factors significant to online shopping; and

3. derive reference models of online shopping adoption that may contribute significantly to knowledge of online shopping.

## 2. Literature search

Two methods were used to identify relevant studies. First, a number of index databases of academic journals were searched and the titles and abstracts scanned to identify valuable papers in the topic area. The databases included ABI/INFORM Global (1986– 2001), Social Science Citation Index (1996–2001), Sciences Citation Index (1996–2001), PsycINFO (1984–2001), and Sociological Abstract (1986– 2001). The keywords and phrases used in searching these included online shopping, online and shopping, internet shopping, internet and shopping, online buying, online purchase, electronic commerce, and online and consumer behavior.

Second, eight IS journals were searched manually to ensure that no major online shopping articles were ignored. These journals were MIS Quarterly, ISR, Journal of MIS, Information and Management, Decision Support Systems, Communication of the ACM, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, Journal of Organizational Computing and Electronic Commerce. As e-commerce and online shopping are new technologies developed after 1990, only articles published between 1990 and 2003 were evaluated.

Conceptual papers and surveys attempting to track the development of online shopping were discarded. In fact, all articles were carefully screened to meet two criteria:

1. All articles had to involve empirical research.

2. They had to investigate online shopping adoption, considering attitude, intention, or actual use.

## 3. Classification of research variables

A total of 45 studies were found relevant to our study. In these, a large number of independent variables that affect online shopping usage and intention were identified. Except for a few variables, most were unique and had only been investigated in one study. Subsequently, the unique variables were categorized according to their similarity. For rigidity reason, Jarvenpaa and Todd’s [40] scheme, which included product perceptions, shopping experience, customer service, and consumer risk, was used and adapted into a categorization framework (see Figs. 1 and 2) composed of three categories, with a sub-category in each. The three major categories were (1) perceived characteristics of the web as a sale channel, (2) characteristics of the customers, and (3) characteristics of the website or products. The details of these categories are summarized in Tables 1–4.

3.1. Perceived characteristics of the web as a sale channel

## 3.1.1. Perceived risk of online shopping

As indicated in Table 1, risk factors have been investigated extensively. Of the 14 studies that explored perceived risks, 9 examined the general perception of risks, while others analyzed specific aspects of risk. The general risks were measured by asking respondents to assess whether buying goods online was risky; whereas, specific risks were concerned with privacy infringement, system security, fraudulent merchant behavior, credit card fault, and product risk (in not getting what was expected). Not surprisingly, the studies proved quite controversial. For generally perceived risk, six out of the nine studies found that it has a significantly negative impact on intention and actual online purchasing behavior. However, the other three found no such relationship. For the perception of specific risk, one study [8] concluded that risks associated with not getting what was expected and credit card problems could negatively affect online shopping intention. However, another study [55] found that privacy infringement, system security, and fraudulent behavior of the merchants did not have influence on intention. Some authors [42,54] attributed these inconsistent results to a narrow definition of the risk and, therefore, called for specific measures.

Although the impact of risk perception on intention or actual usage were mixed, six studies, as shown in Table 2, found that risk perception had a significantly negative influence on the attitude towards online shopping. As attitude towards online shopping has been found to have a positive impact on the intention to use it, risk perception should have an indirect effect on intention or usage through attitude. Thus, risk perception should be carefully investigated and the validity of the measure must be assessed.

## 3.1.2. Relative advantage of online shopping

A large number of online shopping advantages were identified from the 14 articles that focused on this topic. Some stated these advantages in a general way, including variables such as perceived consequences and relative advantages. Other studies were more specific using: time saving, product value (price and quality), easy to order, and decreased transaction cost, etc. In general, most of the suggested advantages were found to have a positive impact on online shopping intention and behavior but price and transaction cost were both found to have a negative impact.

## 3.1.3. Online shopping experience

Shopping experience, according to most studies, concerns the user-friendliness and aesthetics of online shopping websites. The experiences include the effort required to use a website, its interactivity, and the fun of shopping online. Furthermore, website accessibility was found to be a determinant of online shopping intention [45]. As for the level of domain specific knowledge required to use the website, no research showed any conclusive relationship [19].

Quite a few other shopping experience variables were explored, including evaluated effort, compatibil ity, and playfulness [33,50,86]. The findings of the studies were mixed. Two studies found these variables to have significant positive impact on intention while the other two did not. Huang [39] also included novelty in his study but found no significant direct relationship with desire to shop, though it did have a positive impact on desire to explore, and this, in turn, significantly affected desire to shop. Aesthetics of the website was not found to have an effect on online shopping intention. On the other hand, if customers were satisfied with the website, their intention to purchase increased.

## 3.1.4. Service quality

Service quality can be defined as global judgment about the superiority or excellence of the service provided [58]. A perception of service quality results from a comparison of expectations and performance. The widely known scale for measuring service quality is SERVQUAL, developed by Parasuraman et al. [59]. It identifies five dimensions of service quality: tangibility, reliability, empathy, responsiveness, and assurance. The findings on the impact of service quality are mixed. Three studies investigating general customer service found a significant positive relationship with the intention to shop online, while the other did not.

## 3.1.5. Trust

Lack of trust on the online transactions and the eretailers has been identified as one of the major obstacles in the adoption of online shopping in a large number of discussions [24,37,68,71,78,83]. However, it only recently have more empirical studies appeared to address it. Table 4 summarizes the literature on trust in the area of online shopping.

Trust has been defined and operationalized in a number of ways. It has been defined from a functional point of view: that it reduces complexity in situations of uncertainty [35]. A number of studies conceptualized trust as an overall construct: the trustor is willing to be vulnerable to the action of the trustee [27,44,85] or a general belief that the trustee can be trusted [15,36,41]. Trust has also been seen as a set of specific beliefs, including ability, benevolence, integrity, and predictability [5,9,28,29,69]. Some researchers combine the two kinds of definitions to provide their own trust constructs [53,78]. As can be seen from Table 4, one study’s trust is another’s antecedent to trust. As pointed out by Shankar et al., most of the studies do not make a clear distinction between the underlying dimensions and the antecedents, even though this problem has been pointed out by Mayer et al. [51] in 1995.

Six studies empirically tested the impact of trust on the adoption of online shopping. All six found a significant positive impact of trust on intention to purchase online. One study [18] reported that trust was positively related to intention, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use. Four studies [43], as shown in Table 3, found trust to have an impact on risk perception reduction with a positive influence on attitude towards online shopping, as shown in Table 2.

Aside from overall trust, studies have also investigated the effect of specific trust beliefs on intention to purchase online. Integrity and familiarity have been found to increased purchase intention. However, belief in the ability and benevolence of the vendor has been found to have no significant effect.

Understanding the impact of trust on online shopping is obviously important, however, it is also important to understand how to build trust online. A number of empirical studies examined this critical issue. Overall trusting beliefs, integrity, and benevolence have been found to positively influence overall trust. However, belief in ability, third-party recognition, and previous online purchasing behavior did not have significant effect. Other factors with positive influence were familiarity, structural assurances, perceived reputation, transaction security, and site quality. Individualism has been found to have a negative impact, contradicting what was hypothesized. The findings for perceived size are inconsistent.

Studies also attempted to investigate the antecedences of trusting beliefs. Calculative-based trust builder, structural assurance, situational normality, perceived ease of use, perceived reputation, trust mechanisms, and site quality all increase the level of trust. However, deception was found to have no significant impact. The findings for familiarity were inconclusive.

## 3.2. Online consumer characteristics

## 3.2.1. Consumer shopping orientations

This is a general predisposition toward acts of shopping. Researchers have identified a variety of shopping behaviors, such as the economic, store-loyal, recreational, convenience, price-oriented, brand-loyal, name-conscious, quality, brand conscious, and impulse shoppers [74].

Four studies attempted to link the shopping orientation of the customers to their intention to adopt online shopping. It was found that the convenience oriented and impulse consumers were more inclined to buy online, but, surprisingly, time conscious customers were not [72]. Customers, who prefer experiencing products tended to avoid online shopping. The recreational oriented shoppers, who treat shopping as a fun activity, were mixed. Swaminathan et al. [77] found a positive impact, while the other two found it insignificant [21]. Price-oriented and brand conscious customers were not found to have a preference for shopping online.

By observing the pattern, it seems that if the shopping orientation of the customers matched the advantage provided by online shopping, customers were more inclined to use it.

## 3.2.2. Consumer demographics

The findings on demographic variables were quite mixed. Gender, age, education, and income were found to have a positive relationship with online shopping in some studies, but not in all. Probably demographic variables do not generate the relationships, but they are caused by other deeper structure variables. For example, Li et al. found that males are inclined to buy more online than were females. Thus, it is important to investigate the variables.

## 3.2.3. Consumer computer/internet experience

The factors here are related to the knowledge of the customer on the computer and the internet, as well as the frequency and length of his/her usage. Internet usage has been found to impact positively on the intention to use and the actual usage of online shopping in six studies; only one found the relationship to be insignificant. Similarly, internet purchase experience was found to increase purchase intention. The amount of computer training [48], computer experience [75], and knowledge of online shopping were also found to be positively related to adoption behavior. Since some technology adoption concerns are more psychological than technical, a better understanding of the technology and its operating processes will definitely alleviate concerns and lead to higher usage of online shopping.

## 3.2.4. Consumer innovativeness

Innovativeness is the degree to which an individual is receptive to new ideas. It is regarded as a personality characteristic. Contrary to this, domain specific innovativeness (DSI) measures how innovative a consumer is in a specific product field [32]. DSI was developed because innovative behavior was not apparently consistent across domains. Since online shopping can be viewed as an innovative way of shopping, innovativeness should have a bearing on its adoption.

Of all literature reviewed, seven focused on innovativeness. Four concluded that DSI had a significant positive relationship with intention and usage of online shopping. However, the results for general innovativeness were mixed. While Limayem et al. [49] and Donthu and Garcia found a positive relationship between general innovativeness and intention, Citrin et al. and Sin and Tse found the relationship to be insignificant.

Understanding the effect on adoption behavior of online shopping is important for targeting the right customers. DSI construct seems to be more useful than general innovativeness.

## 3.2.5. Social psychological variables

These variables are derived from a number of psychosocial theories. Several social psychological variables are critical determinants for the adoption of online shopping. Based on our reviewed literature, the doctrines are generally supported. Five of the studies concluded that favorable attitude toward online shopping had a positive impact on the intention of its use. Favorable attitudes towards direct marketing and advertising were also found to have positive impact on the use of online shopping.

One important social psychological variable is perceived behavioral control (PBC). It is defined as an individual’s perception of his/her ability to perform a behavior. This, along with the subjective norm of an individual, was found to have a positive impact on online shopping intention, which, in turn, had a positive influence on the actual use of online shopping. These findings were consistent with the theory of planned behavior [2] and the Triandis Model [81], which could be used as guiding psychosocial theories for research.

One personality variable that was found to influence online shopping behavior was consumers’ risk aversion. Customers with less were more likely to buy online. This was consistent with the fact that online shopping was seen as riskier than traditional shopping. On the other hand, self-confidence was found to have no significance. Two other variables with impact on the intention to use online shopping are intention to use the internet for information search [70] and commitment to the website [60].

## 3.3. Website and product characteristics

## 3.3.1. Risk reduction measures

These, such as a money-back guarantee policy, can be seen as a process-based trust production mechanism. Although some customers may not consider this to be a risk reliever, it is still perceived as an effective measure to act against the unreliable online transaction environment. In addition to a money-back guarantee, buying a well-known brand is another effective way to reduce product risk. Of all articles, one had investigated whether the provision of risk relievers would increase the likelihood of purchase online [84]. It showed that three risk relievers (money-back guarantee, offering a well-known brand, and selling at a reduced price) all had a significant positive effect on the likelihood of purchasing online. Moreover, security and privacy measures also increased the purchase intention of online customers.

Two other studies investigated how other website features affected the intention to shop online; good information content and website design had a positive impact on purchase intention.

## 3.3.2. Product characteristics

It is always important to find out what products are more likely to sell online. This motivated Phau and Poon [61]; they found that low cost and frequently purchased products that are intangible or informational and highly differentiable were more likely to be purchased online. However, another study found no effect of cost on purchase intention [87].

A product’s asset specificity, based on the transaction cost economic [90], was another product characteristic that had been studied. Asset specificity is to the extent to which assets (such as physical, human, or location) are special to a given transaction and used only at lower value in any alternative application [17]. No significant relationship was found between asset specificity and online shopping adoption.

## 5. Discussion and research

As shown in Figs. 1 and 2, many antecedents of the adoption of online shopping have been investigated, but most have only been studied once. For those that have been studied more, their impact has often been inconclusive. Most studies have included variables on an ad hoc basis. The Technology Acceptance Model [20], the theory of planned behavior [1], and Triandis’ Model [82] may be used to structure new investigations. Moreover, Mayer et al.’s model of trust can be referenced to establish the relationship between risk, trust, and the antecedents of trust.

Risk perceptions seem to be the most researched variables. However, the findings of their impact are mixed. Existing studies either use a highly general concept of risk or concentrate on one very specific aspect, the transmission of credit card information. The researchers also realized that there was a need to have a better conceptualization of the perceived risk concepts. Analysis of the literature revealed that there were two categories of risks in online shopping: product and transaction process risks. A consumer may be uncertain whether the purchase will match or satisfy his/her acceptance levels in buying goals or may assume that adverse consequences stem from a failure to satisfy his/her buying goals when a purchase is made [38] (product risk). Perceived transaction process risk is the uncertainty and possible adverse consequences resulting from engaging in the transaction process of purchasing a product or service. Process risks include such items as non-repudiation by the merchant and lack of confidence in the information [65]. Safety of the data transmitted over the internet is only one kind of process risk.

Trust is another factor that is worth further study. As mentioned, trust has been defined and operationalized in a number of ways in the studies. As can be seen from Table 4, one study’s trust is another study’s antecedents to trust. Because of the difficulties in defining trust, some studies may confuse other concepts with trust. For example, in one study, the operationalization of trust in internet shopping has been overlapped with perceived risk as well as perceived integrity, which is an antecedent of trust in the model in the study [16]. Another study mixes the trust in internet shopping and the internet vendor in one trust scale, and the trust measure also overlaps one of its antecedents.

Thus, it is difficult to compare the findings of various studies and their results must be interpreted carefully. Most empirical studies [92] have tested the direct effect of trust on the intention to purchase.

However, whether this is mediated by other variables has not been explored. Only four studies have tested the effect of trust on perceived risk. Moreover, the conceptualization of risk is limited to the overall risk assessment in all studies. As Sitkin and Pablo’s model [73] suggested, perceived risk may mediate the effect of trust on intention and behavior.

A large number of determinants of trust have been proposed in models, however, only a few have been tested empirically. The findings are inconsistent with the predictions of the models or inconsistent among the studies. There are even fewer studies that place trust in a theoretically driven framework. The excep tions are studies by van der Heijden et al. and Gefen et al. who have combined the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) with trust. Unfortunately, their findings are quite inconsistent. van der Heijden et al. tested a model in which perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, trust in the online store, and perceived risk were postulated to have an influence on attitude towards online purchasing intention. However, only perceived risk was found to have a significant negative influence on attitude, and trust in the online store was negatively related to perceived risk. On the other hand, Gefen et al. found that trust, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use all had significant positive effects on intention to use online shopping. Bhattacherjee has pointed out that one of the limitations of his study is that trust explains only 13% of the users’ willingness to transact and 19% of the trust is explained by familiarity with the trustee.

Some studies use traditional scales and apply them directly without modification to the study of online shopping; this can create a validity problem. Adaptation may be needed and/or a new set of dimensions may need to be developed. Also, there has been no validation of the measures and how the questionnaires were designed in some studies [63]. In order to better understand the relationships among the constructs, it is crucial to improve the psychometric properties of the measurement scales.

Since product category affects the intention to purchase online, studies should carefully control the characteristics of the products used and choose products that the customers buy online as their target; otherwise, non-use of the WWW to shop may just be disinterest in the products sold.

## 6. Conclusion

In this study, empirical research of online shopping was analyzed. Two reference models, based on this review, were derived. It was found that many important variables had not been fully investigated. In order to support the development of online transaction and attract customers to buy online, effort was put into the understanding of the dynamics of the adoption of online shopping. A better understanding of the dynamics of the adoption decision of online shopping of the customer will greatly benefit its promotion and help in the transition to a society more heavily involved in electronic commerce.
