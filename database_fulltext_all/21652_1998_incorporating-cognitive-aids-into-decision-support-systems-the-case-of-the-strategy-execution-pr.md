# Incorporating cognitive aids into decision support systems: the case of the strategy execution process

## Abstract

This paper emphasizes the need to support decision makers in their execution of decision-making strategies. In particular, it discusses the specific cognitive requirements of the strategy execution process and uses the insights generated as the conceptual basis from which to guide the design of cognitive aids to be incorporated into decision support technology. The impact of such cognitive aids is then empirically tested. The results indicate that computerized cognitive aids can successfully be designed into DSS to support decision makers’ strategy execution process and that such aids have a significant positive impact on both decision-making efficiency and effectiveness. q 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Decision support system; Cognitive aids; Strategy execution process; Complexity; Feedback

## 1. Introduction

A decision support system DSS is a computer-Ž . ized aid designed to enhance the outcomes of an individual’s decision-making activities. Historically, the primary focus of DSS research has been to study the impacts of various decision aids on decisionmaking efficiency and effectiveness 38 . An exclu- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> sive focus on decision-making outcomes, however, does not foster an understanding of why, and how, particular DSS applications provide better decision support than others. Such lack of understanding, in turn, seriously limits our ability to advance research <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 49 and design computerized aids whose applicability and usefulness transcends the particulars of a given decision setting. As first advanced by Todd and Benbasat 49 , and since echoed by a handful of <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> researchers cf. Refs. 25,26 , there is a definiteŽ <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. need to ‘open up the black box’ and shift our attention from the outcome of decision-making activities to the processes and mechanisms that explain a decision maker’s overt behavior and performance. This paper presents research aimed at opening up one critical dimension of this black box.

In particular, while DSS researchers have long acknowledged the importance of understanding the cognitive make-up of decision makers, very little progress has been achieved in effectively incorporating this knowledge into improved DSS applications <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 11,13 . Performance on various decision-making activities, however, depends upon a number of specific cognitive mechanisms, only a subset of which can be made the object of any given study. In this study, our emphasis is on cognitive mechanisms and cognitive aids for a decision maker’s strategy execution process. Strategy execution is defined as the step-by-step implementation of the various activities that make up a formulated decision-making strategy. It is the second step in a two-stage model of the decision-making process, where the first step is referred to as strategy formulation 37,40,41 . Since even the best<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> laid decision strategy will fail if improperly implemented, the need for supporting a decision maker’s strategy execution process is apparent.

The present focus on the strategy execution process is explained by the fact that the cognitive dimension of the latter has mostly been ignored. It would seem as though there is an implicit assumption according to which once cognitive resources have been expended in order to formulate a decision strategy, the execution of this strategy now depends exclusively on logistical and environmental conditions. This paper argues that not only is such an assumption quite unfounded, but also that it is possible to design computerized decision aids that will substantially enhance a decision maker’s strategy execution process.

Accordingly, based on research from the fields of cognitive psychology and cognitive engineering, this paper first develops a model of some of the cognitive mechanisms at work during the strategy execution process. This model is then used to guide the development of appropriate cognitive aids to be embedded in a DSS application, based on the premise that DSSs should be designed so as to capitalize on individuals’ particular strengths and compensate for their inherent weaknesses 13 . A laboratory experi- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ment was then conducted in order to gather empirical evidence testing the hypothesized impact of such cognitive aids on relevant process variables.

The remaining portion of this paper is organized as follows. An introduction to the basic cognitive mechanisms of the strategy execution process is presented in Section 2. This discussion allows for the development of our specific research model and hypotheses, which are presented in Section 3. Section 4 describes the particulars of the research design, and Section 5 discusses the major results of the research. Our final conclusions are presented in Section 6.

## 2. Strategy execution as a cognitive process

Strategy execution is concerned with the actual implementation of the information-processing and problem-solving activities that make up a formulated decision strategy 37,42 . As such, a successful strat-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> egy execution process is predicated upon the extent to which individuals actively monitor their every activity in order to detect potential deviations between their intended and actual implementation. This activity is referred to as solution process monitoring <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 48 . The particular relationship that exists between solution process monitoring and strategy execution is established through the mediating influence of an individual’s level of cognitive control. This relationship has been conceptually modeled in Fig. 1 and is explicated in the following paragraphs.

Specifically, the exercise of solution process monitoring leads to the generation of on-going feedback information regarding a decision strategy and the extent to which it is being applied accurately, as originally intended 29,30 . This source of informa-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tion is referred to as cognitive feedback e.g., Ref.Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 39 because it provides individuals with the mental. cues necessary to determine the appropriateness of their actions, in the context of the goal s to beŽ . achieved. In turn, the generation of cognitive feedback raises the level of control exercised over one’s strategy execution process by increasing the understanding of the decision-making strategy and the knowledge of when, and how, one is deviating from it. This concept is referred to as cognitive control <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 35,39 and defines the level of mental awareness with which individuals actively control and manage the execution of their decision-making processes. In sum, the exercise of solution process monitoring results in the generation of feedback information which, in turn, contributes to an individual’s overall level of cognitive control.

However, as modeled in Fig. 1, the exercise of solution process monitoring is itself highly dependent upon the particular cognitive make-up of an individual, especially as pertains to one’s level of monitoring ability and attentional resources. First, monitoring has been identified as one of six metacognitive abilities, where metacognitive abilities are defined as high-level executive processes which monitor and regulate the exercise of lower-level cognitive activities 43,44 . In that capacity, <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> metacognitive abilities are deemed central and necessary to the exercise of any intelligent behavior, with differences in such abilities having a direct impact on performance. As such, individual differences in monitoring ability, whether innate or acquired, will impact the quality of one’s solution process monitoring activities.

The proper exercise of solution process monitoring is also highly dependent on an individual’s level of attentional resources, which are limited 16,17,33 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Indeed, all mental activities compete for a single pool of attentional resources, the availability of which constitutes an upper limit on the amount of mental processing that can take place at any one time 16 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> As such, when the monitoring demands of a decision-making task surpass the available attentional resources, solution process monitoring activities may be curtailed, leading to a potential loss of control during the strategy execution process and the unintended generation of deviations between planned and actual actions. Such deviations, which are similar to the concept of dysfunctional behaviors 50,51 are, in<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> a sense, unavoidable, since they are the product of the inherent variability of human action 15 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Additionally, again with reference to Fig. 1, two external factors which have an amplifying effect on the demand for solution process monitoring are strategy and environmental complexity. Strategy complexity pertains to the coefficient of difficulty of a particular plan of actions, as determined by such dimensions as the number of actions and their level of interdependency; whereas environmental complexity refers to the barriers, such as repeated distractions and delayed feedback, raised by the decisionmaking environment within which the strategy execution process takes place. Both factors are especially relevant in light of the fact that they constitute the reality of today’s managerial environments— which are characterized by a heightened sophistication of decision-making activities taking place in increasingly complex decision-making environments.

In particular, there are reasons to believe that the monitoring of one’s strategy execution process becomes increasingly more demanding of individual resources as the complexity of the strategy that needs to be monitored increases 36,46,47,56 . In order to<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> reduce the amount of effort required to solve a specific problem, individuals have been seen to prefer less effortful strategies, often at the expense of accuracy 5,46,50,51 . As such, there is a clear cog-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> nitive cost associated with one’s strategy execution process, and this cost rises with the complexity of the strategy to be implemented 2,31,32 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

A similar argument can be made regarding the impact of environmental complexity on decisionmaking performance 9,14 . In particular, the envi- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ronment within which managers operate has been consistently characterized as being plagued by repeated interruptions and distractions 19,20,23,45 , a<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> situation which leads to high levels of task fragmentation 27 . By interfering with the flow of decision-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> making processes, task fragmentation makes monitoring the progress achieved in resolving any given situation highly demanding 20,28 . Distractions have<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> also been found to negatively impact strategy execution by reducing the extent to which decision makers can make adequate use of the informational evidence at their disposal. Under conditions of high distraction, this negative influence has proven to be such that, at times, individuals’ resulting behavior was found, effectively, to have become erratic 57 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Based on the previous discussion, strategy execution is, clearly, a process whose successful deployment is largely dependent upon the cognitive attributes of a decision maker. Moreover, the importance of such cognitive attributes is magnified by the nature of today’s managerial climate which simultaneously increases the demands placed on individuals’ cognitive resources while effectively acting to decrease their very availability. As such, failing to recognize the importance of these cognitive factors can only restrict our ability to understand, and ultimately improve, the quality of one’s strategy execution process, both in terms of efficiency and effectiveness.

First, efficiency is improved by reducing the risk that some planned activity be unintentionally repeated or that some inappropriate activity be unintentionally engaged in-actions which all contribute towards unnecessarily increasing an individual’s workload. Second, effectiveness is increased by minimizing the likelihood that a planned activity, essential to the problem’s resolution, be unintentionally only partly performed, or altogether overlooked, thus effectively preventing an individual from exploring an essential part of the problem space.

## 3. Research model

The particular operationalization of the conceptual model which is tested in this study is illustrated in Fig. 2. The exact nature of the experimental manipulations and measured variables are discussed in detail in Section 4.

First, characteristics of the decision maker were not directly manipulated in that no attempt was made, through training or otherwise, to increase individuals’ level of monitoring ability or attentional resources. Instead, the objective was to investigate the extent to which computerized cognitive aids can be designed which would compensate for such limitations. As such, two cognitive aids were developed, respectively referred to as memory support to com- Ž pensate for limited attentional resources and strat-. egy support to compensate for limited monitoringŽ ability ..

Second, the respective impacts of the decisionmaking environment and of the decision-making strategy were investigated through the use of two experimental manipulations. Specifically, conditions free of interruptions were contrasted to those where the strategy execution process was repeatedly interrupted. Similarly, the impact of a strategy’s level of complexity was investigated through comparing the implementation process of two strategies varying in their degree of complexity.

Third, the impact of the experimental interventions on individuals’ strategy execution process was investigated, with an emphasis on the level of effectiveness and efficiency of the strategy execution process. Specifically, three particular measures were used to evaluate the quality of one’s strategy execution process. First, unnecessary activities, which are defined as the repetition of planned activities or the execution of activities which lie outside the scope of the original strategy, were used as a measure of the level of efficiency of one’s strategy execution process. A second measure of efficiency was the time taken to execute a strategy. Finally, an analysis of those activities required by the formulated strategy, but which were not executed, was used to assess the effectiveness of the strategy execution process. As such, deviations are used as a surrogate measure of an individual’s level of cognitive control, under the premise that higher occurrences of such deviations are indicative of lower levels of cognitive control Ž . see Fig. 1 .

## 3.1. Computerized aids: memory support

Memory support was specifically designed to compensate for a decision maker’s limited attentional resources. Such resources are drawn upon throughout the entire strategy execution process as individuals must pay attention to and remember not only those activities which have been completed inŽ order to evaluate the extent to which their implementation is faithful to that suggested by the original strategy , but also the particular results of such activ- . ities as such results are used to determine whichŽ portion of the formulated strategy should be executed next . When this mechanism fails, some ac-. tions might be unnecessarily repeated and inappropriate ones might be invoked, while other planned actions might be omitted altogether. Unnecessary actions decrease the efficiency of the strategy execution process by increasing an individual’s workload and the time needed to complete the task. Similarly, omitted actions impact effectiveness by increasing the risk that the path towards a better solution does not get explored.

H1: The efficiency and effectiveness of the strategy execution process of individuals provided with computerized memory support will be the same as that of individuals who do not receive such support.

## 3.2. Computerized aids: strategy support

The exercise of solution process monitoring is also dependent upon an individual’s inherent level of monitoring ability. Such an ability is particularly associated with one’s capacity to detect the occurrence of unintended deviations between planned and actual actions. In cases when an individual’s monitoring ability is low, some actions might be repeated, some unplanned actions might be performed, while some planned ones might be overlooked, thus leading to a decrease in process execution efficiency and effectiveness.

H2: The efficiency and effectiveness of the strategy execution process of individuals provided with computerized strategy support will be the same as that of individuals who do not receive such support.

## 3.3. Computerized aids: interaction effect

Monitoring ability and attentional resources are not independent cognitive processes, if only because the former is responsible for activating appropriate levels of the latter. Indeed, as discussed in Section 2, as a metacognitive ability, monitoring is used to regulate the exercise of lower-order cognitive processes, which include the level of attentional resources devoted to the task at hand. As a corollary, a cognitive aid designed to support one’s monitoring ability will also offer some support to help compensate for one’s limited attentional resources.

H3: The provision of both computerized aids offered together will impact the efficiency and effectiveness of the strategy execution process to the same extent as the sum of the individual impact of each aid provided separately.

3.4. External factors: repeated interruptions: main and interaction effects

As discussed in Section 2, task interruptions impact the strategy execution process by fragmenting thought processes and forcing individuals to switch their attention away from the main task towards the source of the disruption, and back. Such disruptions make it difficult for individuals to keep track of the level of completion of any particular strategy, in addition to affecting their ability to use the informational evidence at their disposal. As such, interruptions overburden both individuals’ limited attentional resources and monitoring ability, resulting in a negative impact on the efficiency and effectiveness of their strategy execution process.

Moreover, as repeated task interruptions increase the need for monitoring and attentional resources, it is expected that the importance of providing computerized cognitive aids designed to compensate for the limited availability of these resources will increase in the presence of such disruptions.

H4: The efficiency and effectiveness of the strategy execution process of individuals subjected to repeated task interruptions will be the same as that of individuals whose process is not disrupted.

H5: The impact of providing computerized cognitive aids on the efficiency and effectiveness of the strategy execution process will be no greater for those individuals whose process is repeatedly interrupted than for those whose process is not subjected to such interruptions.

3.5. External factors: strategy complexity: main and interaction effects

The level of complexity of a decision-making strategy impacts the execution process through raising the level of monitoring ability and attentional resources necessary to ensure its successful implementation. First, it is particularly difficult to stay on track when a strategy has a complicated structure, including, for instance, many feedback loops and decision points. Similarly, the effort placed on one’s attentional resources increases when the size of a strategy is such that it requires one to recollect a large number of activities and the order in whichŽ they must be executed or to deal with a large. quantity of information. As such, strategy complexity increases the level of cognitive resources required to ensure successful strategy execution.

Moreover, as strategy complexity increases the need for monitoring and attentional resources, it is expected that the importance of providing computerized cognitive aids designed to compensate for the limited availability of these resources will increase when individuals are faced with the task of implementing a complex decision-making strategy.

H6: The efficiency and effectiveness of the strategy execution process of individuals faced with the task of implementing a complex decision-making strategy will be the same as that of individuals faced with the task of implementing a simple strategy.

H7: The impact of providing computerized cognitive aids on the efficiency and effectiveness of the strategy execution process will be no greater for those individuals faced with the task of implementing a complex decision-making strategy than for those faced with the task of implementing a simple strategy.

## 4. Research methodology

The objective of this study was to investigate the impact of computerized cognitive aids on individuals’ ability to successfully implement a decision strategy as originally intended, given the contingent presence of two external factors: use of strategies of a varying degree of complexity and presence of repeated task interruptions. The focus being on the strategy execution process only, individuals were instructed into the use of their respective decisionmaking strategy at the onset of the research. It is their respective ability to implement their pre-determined strategy, in the context of a given decision situation, which is reported in the current paper. Details of the research design, including a description of the computerized aids, decision strategies, interruptions manipulation, measured variables, and decision-making task are presented in the following sections. Finally, given the nature of the phenomenon under investigation that of individualsŽ internal cognitive processes , and the exploratory . nature of our conceptual framework, the high level of internal validity afforded by a laboratory setting was considered invaluable. A description of how the experimental sessions were designed and conducted, and how research participants were secured, is provided in Section 4.7.

4.1. Computerized cognitiÕe aids: memory and strategy support

Research participants were required to interface with a customized WingZe application in order to execute their strategy execution process. The DSS application consisted basically of a spreadsheet-type interface allowing for the conduct of sophisticated what-if simulations. A decision maker’s every interaction with the system—that is, essentially, every tested what-if scenario—was automatically captured by the system, in sequence, time-stamped, and logged for future reference. As such, an individual’s entire strategy execution process could be automatically captured, unobtrusively and without visible interference, thereby eliminating many of the drawbacks of other process tracing techniques such as verbal protocols which can interfere with thought processes by Ž forcing individuals to think aloud and verbalize their every move and videotaping or eye movement . tracking which can modify individual thought pro-Ž cesses due to their obtrusiveness 7,26 .. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Two computerized cognitive aids were also designed into the software. Both forms of support were aimed at supporting individuals’ solution process monitoring activities, the first by offering direct monitoring support and the second by decreasing the overall attentional requirements of the decision task —thereby freeing attentional resources for the strategy execution process.

In the first instance, the various activities making up the decision-making strategy were graphically represented on the computer screen using a flowchart-type representation. Individuals could, therefore, directly consult the decision-making strategy as illustrated on their computer screen when uncertain of their up-to-date progress or of which activity to perform next. Moreover, the software application itself was programmed with a logical understanding of the decision-making strategy. This knowledge was used by the software to warn individuals of their deviations between actual and intended strategy—thereby compensating for individuals’ limited monitoring ability. Essentially, the software automatically generated some of the cognitive feedback required to maintain a level of cognitive control sufficient to ensure a successful strategy execution process. This form of support is referred to as strategy support.

The second form of support was specifically designed to augment the level of attentional resources individuals could devote to their strategy execution process; and that by minimizing the overall memory requirements of the decision-making task. Indeed, memorization requires the use of significant attentional resources in order to actively commit information to memory e.g., Ref. 21 , leaving fewer atten-Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. tional resources available to monitor one’s implementation of a decision-making strategy. In order to address this situation, the software application included a bar chart graph which kept track of the various what-if scenarios simulated by an individual, and of their respective results—thereby minimizing the drain on the attentional resources needed to actively commit such information to memory. Essentially acting as an external memory aid, this form of support is referred to as memory support.

## 4.2. Strategy complexity

As discussed in Section 2, there are reasons to believe that the inherent complexity of a decisionmaking strategy has a direct impact on the extent to which an individual successfully monitors his or her execution process. In order to generate some data to this effect, the study participants were instructed into either one of two decision-making strategies which differed in their level of complexity as defined using Wood’s 54 framework of complexity. In particular,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> the two strategies differed in their respective levels of component and coordinative complexity. Component complexity refers to the number of distinct actions and information elements that make up a solution strategy; while coordinative complexity is defined as the quantity, nature, and strength of the interrelationships between the information cues and between the several actions that make up a strategy <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 54,56 . Simply stated, component complexity relates to the sheer ‘size’ of the decision-making strategy, while coordinative complexity is a measure of the intricacy of its ‘structure’.

Component and coordinative complexity were manipulated both at the level of the specific user actions that make up a decision strategy and the various information cues upon which such actions are based. While both suggested strategies were equally suited to solve the decision-making task, one was significantly more complex than the other in that it involved a greater number of distinct user actions, which themselves required the manipulation of, and resulted in the generation of, a greater number of information elements. The overall structure of the latter strategy was also more complex as it contained a larger number of feedback loops and decision nodes. For the sake of simplicity, the two strategies are referred to, respectively, as the simple and the complex strategies.

## 4.3. Task interruptions

Interruptions, which are defined as the occurrence of disruptions during an individual’s decision-making exercise, were generated using a special computerized version of the Group Embedded Figures Test Ž . GEFT 53 . The GEFT requires that an individual <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> visually locate and then trace a simple geometrical figure located in a distracting, complex background field 37,52 . It has been used extensively in MIS<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> and DSS research 3,4,24,34 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Three exits into the GEFT test were programmed into the computer software at pre-determined points. These exit points were the same for all participants, with each exit corresponding to one of the three sections of the original paper-based test. While in the process of solving the decision-making task, participants were suddenly confronted with one of the sections of the GEFT which they had to complete before being allowed to return to the decision-making task. Since the participants were not told when, or how often, such a distraction would occur, these interruptions were truly unpredictable, as are most real-life distractions.

4.4. Experimental measures: process efficiency and effectiÕeness

This study is concerned with the impact of computerized cognitive aids, strategy complexity, and task interruptions on the efficiency and effectiveness with which individuals can implement a given decision-making strategy. As previously mentioned in Section 3, while efficiency is most affected by the performance of unnecessary activities, effectiveness is impacted by a failure to execute planned activities. As such, data collection included an extensive analysis of the deviations found between individuals’ intended and actual strategy execution processes.

Deviations were identified by comparing each individual’s actual strategy execution process to that which should have been observed had the individual followed exactly the decision-making strategy he or she was instructed in. Each deviation was further categorized as either an unnecessary action or as an omission. An unnecessary action is defined as the repeated execution of an action which is part of the original strategy, or the performance of an action which is not part of the formulated strategy. Similarly, an omission is defined as the failure to execute an action part of the original strategy.

A second measure of efficiency was also collected. Specifically, the time needed to perform each user action was automatically recorded, as was the total execution time. Total execution time is defined as the time required to complete the experimental task only and, as such excludes, when applicable, the time needed to perform the interruption task.

## 4.5. Research design

All put together, the research allowed for an investigation of the specific impacts of two computerized cognitive aids memory support and strategy Ž support , two decision-making strategies of a varying . degree of complexity simple strategy and complex Ž strategy and two types of decision-making environ- . ments with and without repeated interruptions onŽ . individuals’ ability to implement a decision-making strategy as originally intended.

In order to avoid confounding the specific impacts of environmental complexity with those of strategy complexity, individuals in the environmental complexity conditions were instructed into the use of the simple strategy only.

## 4.6. Decision-making task

The decision-making task consisted in determining the respective value of three independent variables selling price per unit, advertisement budget,Ž and number of salespeople that will maximize a. company’s total net earnings decision variable .Ž . Other variables which were relevant to the decisionmaking situation, but over which the decision maker had no control, included the company market share, the overall market demand, and the sales volume. The relationships between the problem variables were determined in such a way as to be representative of those found in real-life settings. Such relationships, many of which were mathematically complex and non linear, were implemented using either WingZe scripting language, or through cell formulas programmed directly on the spreadsheet interface.

As implemented in this research, this decision situation confronted the decision maker with a multidimensional search space defined by a complex set of non linear functions. In this context, both decision-making strategies were designed so as to afford individuals with an organized, systematic way of testing various combinations of the independent variables until such time as the solution could not be improved upon. Similar decision-making tasks have been frequently used in previous MIS and DSS studies 8,10,22,34 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

The choice of this task is especially appropriate to this research because its successful resolution is predicated upon constant and active solution process monitoring on the part of a decision maker. Indeed, in their exploration of the problem space, individuals who do not sufficiently monitor their execution process may likely lose cognitive control, either by trying the same what-if simulations more than once Žthat is, may enter the same combination of the three decision variables more than once , or by uninten- . tionally ignoring some other simulations which could prove essential to the problem’s resolution that is, Ž may fail to test for a potentially important combination of the three decision variables, in effect bypassing an essential area of the solution space . In other . words, this task is particularly conducive to strategy misexecution, thus affording an ideal research platform.

## 4.7. Research participants and training protocol

A total of 144 individuals participated in this research, equally balanced across all twelve conditions. The required number of participants was determined using a power analysis, under the assumption of a small to medium effect size. The study participants were mostly business students enrolled in a BBA or MBA program. In the context of an information processing task such as the one used in this research, the use of students as surrogates for practicing decision makers is considered perfectly adequate <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 1,18 . Participation in the study was voluntary; the only incentive used was extra course credits. Individuals signed up for a particular session on the basis of their availability, with each session lasting a maximum of two hours. The assignment of conditions to sessions was randomized. Each session contained an average of four to six participants.

Each session began with an extensive group training which provided detailed explanations about the decision-making task, the software, and the proposed strategy. The training session concluded with researcher and participants resolving together an example decision-making problem in order to ensure that participants understood the decision-making task, were comfortable with the decision-making strategy, and knew how to utilize the software application. The group training was followed by an individual practice session during which participants were encouraged to rehearse their newly acquired skills. Once all participants declared themselves ready, a new decision-making task, similar to that presented during the training and practice sessions, was initiated. It was their performance on this task, which participants completed individually, which is the object of the analyses presented in this paper.

Finally, no deception was used. In particular, throughout the training and practice sessions, participants were repeatedly told that the objective of the exercise was to assess their respective ability to faithfully implement the recommended solution strategy. They were also told that their decision strategy was formulated with the express objective of solving their particular decision task. As such, faithfully implementing the strategy would allow them to successfully solve the decision task.

## 5.2.2. Learning effect

This analysis was carried out to explore performance variability as a function of level of progress. That is, was individual performance consistent throughout the entire strategy execution exercise or are there indications that performance varies as a function of one’s level of progress? Moreover, in such instances where variability in performance is indeed observed, what appear to be the contributing factors?

In order to answer this question, each individual problem solving protocol was divided into two equal portions at the median point, based on the total number of actions executed. The performance of individuals during the first half of their strategy execution process was then contrasted to their performance during the second half, both in terms of total number of deviations generated and time delays in execution see Table 4 .Ž .

Time delays in execution were identified and tabulated as follows. First, for each individual, the average time required to perform an individual action was computed step 1 . Second, time delaysŽ . were identified by denoting each individual action whose time elapsed was at least one and a half 1.5Ž . times greater than the average duration, as calculated in step 1 the ‘1.5’ multiplying factor is based on the Ž distribution of execution times and allows for the identification of those execution times which are more than one standard deviation away from the mean . Such delays correspond to those instances . when individuals had to pause in order to determine what action to take next and are indicative of implementation difficulties.

An analysis of Table 4 indicates that individuals in the simple strategy conditions who received some form of computerized support performed a larger percentage of their deviations and time delays during the first half of their strategy execution process, and that they did so regardless of the presence of repeated task interruptions. Such an improvement in performance is characteristic of a positive learning effect. This is to be contrasted with the performance of those individuals in the simple strategy groups who did not receive any type of computerized support and who generated less delay in execution during the latter part of the exercise, but executed, on average, just as many deviations as they did during the first half of their strategy execution process. That their performance failed to improve indicates that the provision of computerized support tools was instrumental in enabling individuals in the simple strategy groups to experience a positive learning effect.

## 5.2.3. Strategy reintegration

A final analysis was conducted in order to identify the potential presence of long sequences of deviations, that is, of instances where deviations feed upon one another. Such long sequences are indicative of difficulty experienced in resuming strategy execution following a misexecution. As such, the objective was to determine whether a breakdown in cognitive control was more likely to occur at such times when individuals were attempting to resume strategy execution at the onset of a deviation. A positive answer would indicate the existence of particularly vulnerable states and suggest the need for providing additional support at such points.

The results of this analysis are presented in Table 5. This table shows, for example, that for those individuals in the simple strategy condition with no interruptions and memory support only, a total of six instances were observed when an individual required at least three steps to successfully reintegrate his or her strategy execution process at the onset of a misexecution. Among these, one particular individual took as many as 12 steps to reintegrate into the strategy, a clear demonstration of a temporary state of cognitive breakdown during a given segment of his or her execution process. The table also indicates that a total of 30 deviations occurred in a sequence, a number which represents 51% of the total number of deviations committed by individuals in this particular condition.

Overall, the results presented in Table 5 indicate that the ease with which individuals successfully resumed strategy execution at the onset of a deviation diminishes with a decrease in the level of computerized support provided. Moreover, strategy complexity appears to have the most negative impact on performance, especially for those individuals who did not receive computerized strategy support. As indicated in Table 3, it was also found that only half of the individuals in these same two conditions successfully reached the best solution. Taken together, these results indicate that the absence of strategy support increased these individuals’ difficulty in recognizing and correcting deviations from the intended strategy. In turn, this failure to follow the strategy as planned led such individuals to omit some important decision-making activities, omission which ultimately led them to settle on lower quality solutions.

In contrast, the performance of individuals in the interruptions conditions was generally equivalent to that of individuals in the simple strategy with no interruptions conditions. One exception is the performance of individuals in the no support group, who committed more than twice as many long sequences of deviations than their counterparts in the no interruptions condition. Clearly, interruptions have an impact on the strategy execution process, an impact which appears to be mitigated by the presence of the computerized support tools.

According to Table 5, a little over half of the total deviations were the result of long sequences of misexecutions. This percentage, while relatively constant across complexity manipulations, substantially increases as the level of computerized support provided diminishes. The data also indicates that almost half of the deviations were not the result of strategy reintegration difficulties, but rather resulted from occasional misapplications, misapplications which were at once detected and appropriately corrected. Taken together, these results indicate that while misexecutions often feed up on one another, leading to a temporary breakdown in cognitive control, the provision of computerized cognitive aids can effectively help in alleviating this problem.

## 6. Conclusion

Some years ago, Todd and Benbasat 49 con-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> cluded that an exclusive focus on the study of overall performance indicators seriously limited our capability to generate valuable insight into managerial decision-making. They called for the need to ‘open up the black box’ and analyze the reasons and processes which lie behind individuals’ overt behaviors and performance. The research presented in this paper is an attempt at accomplishing just such a task as it presents an in-depth analysis of some of the cognitive mechanisms at work during the execution phase of the decision-making process. In this context, it looks not only at the performance exhibited by individual decision makers, but provides some detailed analyses of the causes underlying such performance.

Such analyses indicate that the complexity of a decision-making strategy has the most negative impact on individual performance. Specifically, our findings indicate that individuals in the complex strategy groups were the least efficient, unnecessarily performing a large number of what-if analyses and taking, on an average, three times as long as individuals in the simple strategy conditions to complete the decision-making exercise. This result is especially relevant given the importance put on efficiency in most business settings. The level of effectiveness demonstrated by individuals in the complex strategy conditions was also the lowest, especially for those individuals in the no strategy support groups, who omitted the greatest number of prescribed what-if simulations. Such omissions help explain why only half of these decision maker’s ultimately resolved the decision-making task successfully, generating a net earnings value which was, on average, approximately US\$31000 or 3.7% less than the best possi-Ž . ble solution—a result which would not reflect well on a manager’s rated performance.

A detailed analysis of individual decision-making protocols in the complex strategy conditions shows the lack of a positive learning curve, with individuals actually committing more deviations as they progressed through the exercise—a situation which may have been the result of an increase in their overall level of mental fatigue as they progressed through the strategy. Moreover, the presence of a large number of deviations occurring in a sequence indicates that the relatively poor performance of these individuals may in part have been the result of their inability to resume the strategy execution process at the onset of a deviation. That is, individuals in the complex strategy groups appear to have experienced undue difficulty in correcting their behavior at the onset of a deviation, a situation which indicates a low level of cognitive control. Interestingly, however, the negative impact of strategy complexity was reflected most in the performance of those individuals who did not receive strategy support, as those individuals exhibited the overall lowest performance level. This result indicates that while the ability to exercise a sufficient level of solution process monitoring is most taxed in the presence of more complex decision-making strategies, it is possible to design support tools which can offset such negative impact so as to minimize a breakdown in cognitive control and in overall decision-making performance.

The impact of environmental disturbances on individual performance was mixed. When measured in terms of the number of deviations committed in-Ž cluding both unnecessary and omitted actions , the. performance of individuals in the interruptions conditions was generally at par with that of their counterparts in the no interruptions conditions. However, when measured in terms of the time required to complete the decision-making exercise, performance was significantly impacted. Clearly, there is a performance cost associated with the presence of repeated distractions in one’s decision-making environment, as time is of the essence in most managerial settings.

Overall, the impact of interruptions on effectiveness appears to have been mitigated by the presence of the computerized tools, as performance degrades mostly for those individuals who did not receive any computerized support. Similarly, a study of individual protocols further indicates that interruptions impacted mostly those individuals in the unsupported condition, who generated a relatively larger number of sequences of deviations than unsupported individuals in the no interruptions group. As opposed, the level of cognitive control exhibited by supported individuals is equivalent to that exhibited by individuals in the no interruptions conditions; just as is their learning trend which exhibits a clear improvement as individuals progressed through the exercise. As such, the negative impact of interruptions was mostly felt as pertains to overall decision time, with computerized support succeeding in mitigating the effects of such environmental disturbances on most other dimensions.

While most of these results confirm our a-priori assumptions regarding the cognitive requirements of the strategy execution process and the specific impact of strategy and environmental complexity, the minimal overall impact of task interruptions on performance remains intriguing. A possible explanation comes from research performed using other forms of environmental stressors, such as high temperature or noise for an excellent review on this topic see Ref.Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 14 . Such research indicates that, by raising their. level of arousal, environmental stressors at times actually force individuals to pay better attention to the task at hand and, thus, reach higher levels of performance. As such, it may be that the presence of interruptions forced individuals to effectively raise their level of attention, thereby enabling them to exercise a level of solution process monitoring sufficient to allow them to perform at a level equivalent to that of the non interrupted subjects.

However, it is also known that there exists a curvilinear relationship between arousal and performance and that the optimum level of arousal is inversely related to the level of complexity of the task—such that more difficult tasks will be impaired at lower levels of arousal 14 . In this context, it may<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> be that given the relative simplicity of the simple strategy, the presence of interruptions did not cause arousal to raise over the threshold level after which performance is negatively impacted. However, the presence of interruptions in the context of the more complex strategy might have raised arousal across the threshold point, thereby leading to a significant decrease in performance. Further research is clearly needed in order to validate this hypothesis.

This research provides strong empirical data in support of Todd and Benbasat’s 51 discussion of<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> dysfunctional behaviors and their impact on decision-making efficiency and effectiveness. However, it also demonstrates that such behaviors can be effectively curtailed when appropriate cognitive aids, whose design is based upon a knowledge of the cognitive requirements of a particular category of tasks, are provided to the decision maker. This conclusion is supported by the performance exhibited by individuals in the support conditions, with performance consistently improving as the level of computerized support increases. For instance, 95% or 68Ž out of 72 of the individuals provided with strategy. support and 89% or 64 out of 72 of those provided Ž . with memory support successfully reaching the best solution. This result can be contrasted with the performance of individuals in the control conditions, with only 72% or 26 out of 36 of them successfullyŽ . resolving the decision-making task. Moreover, the aÕerage difference between individuals’ net earnings and the best solution is US\$1772 or 0.2% andŽ . US\$3239 or 0.4% for those in the strategy support Ž . and memory support groups, respectively; as compared to US\$21278 or 2.5% for those individuals inŽ . the no support groups. Not only do such data provide evidence for the potential of cognitive aids, they also indicate that such aids make good business sense, as the costs involved in designing and developing such aids can be recouped through the improvements in decision-making effectiveness that they generate.

On another front, the results of this research are quite promising because they indicate that it is possible to design suggestive decisional guidance into computer-based decision-making support applications, therefore providing some empirical evidence in support of Silver’s conceptual suggestions 40–42 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> The results also indicate that the provision of such guidance—here operationalized as solution process monitoring tools—is legitimate, in that it improves both the efficiency and the effectiveness of individuals’ decision-making activities. The in-depth process study further identifies the promotion of increased learning and the facilitation of individuals’ reintegration into the strategy once an error has been committed as the main factors that facilitated this improvement—thereby increasing our understanding of the mechanisms by which solution process monitoring impacts an individual’s decision-making process. Such increased understanding is currently being used to refine and improve a second generation of solution process monitoring tools, which are currently under development.
