# Operational risks, bidding strategies and information policies in restructured power markets <sup>1</sup>

Dennis Ray <sup>a,)</sup>, Eileen Cashman <sup>b,2</sup>

MSB Energy Associates, Suite 200, 7507 Hubbard AÕenue, Middleton, WI 53562-3135, USA b College of Integrated Science and Technology, James Madison UniÕersity, Harrisonburg, VA 22807, USA

## Abstract

Advanced analytical tools are enabling the estimation of new measures of operational reliability in power systems. In bid-based and bilateral contract markets, these estimates provide valuable information to market participants. Such information improves market efficiency through more informed decision-making. However, it may also result in opportunistic bidding strategies that adversely affects economic efficiency. The relationship between risk and bidding strategies is explored in the paper. The importance of the availability of a good information set on reliability is demonstrated analytically. Examples of information availability in restructured markets is given and questions about the dimensions of market information policies are discussed. An illustrative example shows how private gains from withholding information can be obtained. q 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Risk; Generation bidding; Generation competition; Electric reliability; Operational limits; Information policies; Bidding policies; Power markets

## 1. Overview

Increasingly, market-based structures for power systems are serving as the basis for buying and selling electricity. For example, bid-based power market exchange systems for generation and demand are being used to establish competitive markets <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 9,13–15 . In addition, hybrid market systems are being formed using a mixture of bilateral or multi-Ž lateral contracts and a market exchange bidding. system. The hybrid system is an alternative to the bid-only market systems 2,5,15,18 . Additionally,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> theoretical and experimental work has been done on power markets that use transmission capacity rights trading 5,9 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

An important underpinning of any efficient market-based power system is the information set that is provided to participants. The public policy objectives in establishing the information set could include:

<sup>Ø</sup> achieving economically efficient bidding schedules,

<sup>Ø</sup> maintaining operational reliability,

<sup>Ø</sup> enabling market-enforcement of bidding rules

Žwhere rule violations such as collusive bidding could be identified by market participants , and.

<sup>Ø</sup> creating fair market rules to establish a ‘level playing field’ for market participants.

Information provided to a centralized bidding market is typically specified in a system code that covers power exchange and independent system operator ISO functions.Ž .

Operational risk assessment is important in bid schedule development. A concern to generation bidders is whether their bidding strategy results in generation choices by a power exchange or an ISO that increase the risk that the bidder will not be physically able or selected to supply energy to the market. On the other hand, bidding strategies can be chosen to take advantage of the risks of congestion, even if the result is inconsistent with efficient use of resources.

There is a parallel concern from a power system perspective on whether bidding schedules degrade operational reliability. Reliable operations are necessary for allowing the market to work and for avoiding uncompensated losses in value to generators and consumers. Economic incentives and physical controls can be used to address congestion problems threatening operational reliability 6 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Advanced computational techniques now exist for estimating new measures of operational reliability, such as the effects of incremental changes in a generation level on operational reliability 1,7,17 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> From a system perspective, that knowledge is useful for determining directions for reallocating generation Ž . or market transaction schedules to avoid infeasible system operating conditions. The information can also be used for determining ex ante nodal prices that would signal the value of such a reallocation.

From a bidder’s perspective, information on reliability can result in bidding strategy choices to achieve greater profitability. Without this information, generators may undertake independent evaluations that could lead to bidding strategies based on misinformation. The use of reliability-based nodal pricing in a market may reduce the scope of the information requirements 6 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

The objective of this paper is to explore the relationship between risk and bidding strategies, and the implications of that relationship for information policies in markets with centralized bidding and bilateral contracts. The usefulness of reliability information will be shown even for the simple case of a marginal cost bid schedule.

## 2. Operational risks

Operational risk is defined as the potential for economic losses or gains arising from uncertainties in the short-run demand for electricity and in the condition or status of the power system. These risks arise at the system level, but affect individual participants in the market. System condition uncertainties can produce various forms of resource scarcity or congestion such as transmission overloads and ac-Ž tive or reactive power generation shortages and. dynamic instabilities. The uncertainty causes profit risk because to maintain the desired level of operational reliability, actions may have to be taken to avoid unacceptable loss of customer service or damage to facilities. These actions for ‘congestion management’ could use economic incentives for altering generation and demand levels. They could also use direct control actions toward generation levels, customer loads, and transmission line availability 6 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

## 2.2. Operational risks and strategy

Key to whether the uncertainties are meaningful for decision-making depends, in part, on whether a generator can affect the uncertainties and the risk that arises from them. Many uncertainties are driven by exogenous factors that are related to location and system condition. However, uncertainties and risk can be affected by the bidder’s generation commitment at minimum, maximum, and preferred levels; its self-commitment strategies in markets permitting self-commitment; and its maintenance schedule. Owning multiple units enhances the bidder’s flexibility. Risk management can also be addressed through financial risk management instruments.

Risk strategies could be categorized in the following ways.

Ž . 1 Minimize risk. Risk minimization strategies focus on minimizing the risk of change in system and market conditions. For example, with a bilateral contract, a bidder may prefer point 5 in Fig. 1, which based on the distance norm from boundary B, represents a low risk generation allocation. If the bidder owns both units and they are on either side of a constrained line, reaching that least risk allocation is more easily controlled by the bidder.

Ž .2 Ignore risk. Here the bidder acts to maximize profit, but ignores risk. Although seemingly a naive strategy, it may be chosen in the absence of sufficient information or under the assumption that operational reliability will remain high due to system operator actions. This risk strategy could be represented by point 4 in Fig. 1.

Ž . 3 Seek risk. This strategy could be the preferred for particular generation technologies that are profitable only under adverse system conditions, such as high cost peakers used when generation shortages occur.

Ž . 4 Seek best risk-return trade-off. Consistent with shareholder wealth maximization, this approach seeks to balance risk and return. Point 2 could illustrate this objective. In seeking the trade-off, generators benefit from owning diverse production technologies Ž . by function and fuel source .

Which of these strategies is most consistent with common operational reliability criteria? Maintenance of operational reliability, as implemented through operating criteria, is a central function of an ISO.

The criteria typically specify minimums for operational risk rather than optimums. As a result, in market-based decision-making, generation and demand allocations need only pass a feasibility test, such as in the California ISO 2 . As a consequence,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> market participants have latitude in selecting their preferred risk level.

In the long run, business strategies focus on how profitable services are and what advantages can be used to sustain those profits. The embedded technologies and rules of trade in a market are key drivers of the choice of short-run operational strategies or tactics. The physical characteristics of generation units will be important. With heavy investment in large, baseload plant, low risk strategies will likely be sought to assure profitability. Short-run strategies will also be driven by the market structure as measured by the number and size of competitors, and the ease of entry. For example, the transportability of gas turbines could affect bidding strategies in transmission constrained areas.

## 3. Risk and a marginal cost bid schedule

Knowledge of marginal costs is useful information for developing a bidding strategy. In some cases, marginal cost will serve as a minimum bid price. In effectively competitive markets, the marginal cost bid schedule may be the most profitable one for bidders. Actual bidding strategies will likely call for bids in excess of this bid schedule; however, the risk adjusted marginal cost bid schedule shown in this section provides an illustration of why individual bidders may need detailed system information about uncertainties in developing a bidding strategy.

The possibility that generation levels affect the operational risk for an individual bidder will drive the estimation of an expected marginal cost curve. Risk assessment by individual bidders may be particularly important in zonal markets where local highrisk conditions are not reflected in an ISO’s zonal congestion assessment. This risk arises when the output of a bidder’s generator influences the risk that it or another one of the bidder’s generators will beŽ . outaged.

The possibility that a bidder’s strategy will influence the uncertainty it faces in supplying services will affect a marginal cost bid schedule. ## 4. Public policies toward information availability in power markets

Information availability for buyers and sellers is essential for decision-making in competitive markets. Policy-makers will be establishing or approving information requirements in such markets, particularly regarding information from $\mathrm { I s O } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { s }$ . Government policies toward information exist in other markets, such as the case of labeling requirements and worker safety information. An important distinction is that in power markets, particular focus will be placed on information availability to make the interconnected market work competitively. This section identifies key questions that arise in choosing a public information policy.

## 4.1. What oÕerall objectiÕe should be used in selecting an information policy?

This objective is linked closely to the overall objective of creating a competitive market. The information policy should support informed decisionmaking by all bidders and by the ISO, and should facilitate entry and effective competition among all participants. Any information that is withheld or disseminated discriminatorily could cause market failures in reaching efficient and fair competition.

The value of such information can be high from a societal and private perspective. This information can:

<sup>Ø</sup> facilitate efficient forward and spot market pricing,

<sup>Ø</sup> allow transparency in how prices are determined,

<sup>Ø</sup> promote economic efficiency in production,

<sup>Ø</sup> encourage informed bidding strategy decisions and shortened learning curves in strategy development,

<sup>Ø</sup> ensure minimum levels of operational reliability, and

<sup>Ø</sup> minimize the costs of congestion management, and facilitate efficient congestion and capacity contract markets.

Although ISO’s must be approved by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission FERC , there are Ž . no generic FERC policies on ISO information gathering and dissemination. Rather, the FERC is acting on a case-by-case basis for given ISO proposals.

## 4.2. Should the existing market structure or market rules affect the information policy?

Policy-makers can be faced with a difficult decision on information policies when the market, at least in transition, is not effectively competitive. In this situation, information availability can potentially produce rent transfers that do not promote efficient operation, investment or entry. This can occur if the information is used to exercise market power or to game the market rules.

For example, Ref. 10 has demonstrated how<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> independent decision-making in an otherwise competitive market could be lost through tacit collusion in setting bid prices under congestion. These ‘Orentype’ generators could use information about a forthcoming maintenance need that opens a transmission line to begin coordinating their bids or reconfiguring their unit commitment schedules. Generators with units on either side of a congested line could also influence the magnitude of the constraint and take advantage of price differences across it by altering their generation allocations 2,8 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

In some cases, policy-makers have actually proposed withholding market information to encourage competitive results. For example, early in the evolution of the New South Wales market in Australia, the Australian antitrust regulator the Australian Compe-Ž tition and Consumer Commission proposed that bid. information be withheld from the market even after the trading day. The rationale was that withholding the information would reduce the opportunity for collusive behavior 13 . Today’s Australian National<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Electricity Market has no such restriction.

From an efficiency standpoint, it appears that full information is preferred. Short-term profit taking due to congestion can be efficient if it motivates appropriate investment decisions. However, the profit-taking may also be deemed a market failure, particularly when constraints such as voltage create local mar-Ž . ket power 8 that cannot be reasonably expected to <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> be resolved through entry or when it enables undue exercise of market power.

Therefore, it appears that the information policy may not be neutral to the degree of effective competition and to market conduct, particularly under various forms of congestion.

## 4.3. What information should be aÕailable?

In principle, information should be available whenever that information could be reasonably expected to affect market participant decisions such as those decisions based on operational risk and price considerations. Since power systems are interconnected, considerable detailed information would be needed about the system condition including generation, loads, and transmission to conduct a full operational risk analysis including contingency and stabil-Ž ity studies as appropriate . Complete information . would describe the current system state and forward-looking economic and physical information such as on transmission maintenance schedules, loads, prices, and generation commitments.

Such information is needed by the system operator, by market participants, and even by the financial community or their expert advisors . The systemŽ . operator needs the information to assure the feasibility of the proposed transactions and the operational reliability of the current system state. Participants need the information for strategic and tactical decision-making. The financial community needs it for pricing in forward markets.

Actual implementation of information dissemination policies varies in today’s restructured or re-Ž structuring markets. For example, in the Alberta . Power Pool, few congestion problems exist so participant information is limited to such items as past, current and forecasted data on prices, loads, and bids <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 12 . On the other hand, the California ISO is evolving toward providing a much richer information set. Public market information will include additional economic and physical information such as loop flow forecasts, forecasted congestion prices, and inter-zonal transmission path capacities 3 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

There are several considerations in deciding what information should be publicly available. One is whether participants can supply or obtain it effectively. New technology challenges face system data collection, processing, and delivery for use in a competitive marketplace 16 . Another consideration<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> is the participant’s need for the information. In Alberta, comprehensive system information would not affect decision-making due to the lack of congestion.

The information needs will vary across market participants. For example, the large marketers may well be able to benefit from more sophisticated information than small marketers. On the other hand, participants with an inside understanding of the market and the power system may well prefer that public information be limited. Successful bidders may want information limited to make it more difficult for other bidders to discover the source of their advantage.

There are other technological challenges in enabling users to be effective consumers of the information. In many cases, the data will have to be analyzed and interpreted by an expert. The expert will need detailed information. On the other hand, there is a need to empower the less sophisticated participant to use system information in decisionmaking. Tools on a local computer 11 or tools that<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> are publicly available over the Internet 19 could<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> meet this need. In fact, a web-based ‘black-box program for conducting market and system analyses is planned for the California ISO.

Initially in restructuring there may be an information analysis advantage for incumbents who are far along the learning curve about their particular regional power system; however, given sufficient market incentives for expert analyses of publicly available information, this advantage should decline over time.

4.4. Will information be aÕailable on a non-discriminatory and timely basis?

Given the objective of creating a competitive market, policy-makers will undoubtedly support non-discriminatory dissemination of information. Here the appearance and reality of fairness will be important. Meeting this requirement may be even more important when the information comes from institutions such as reliability councils or power pools that have traditionally involved a limited number of market participants. The creation of the PJM and NEPOOL ISO’s suggests that such a problem can be overcome.

There will also be a credibility issue when the information source is a market participant, such as would be the case when a transmission owner is also a participant in the generation market. An ISO may also not be viewed as a neutral party in these matters. For example, if the ISO holds capacity contracts or receives revenue from congestion charges, it will have the incentive to withhold information or delay the release of information that could reduce profit.

The timeliness of information can be critical. Fig. 2 provides an illustration of why information timeli ness is important in market-based decision-making. Suppose the market allows for bid updating. Generators A and B have 500 MW units with significant start-up times, and with operating costs of 2 and 4 cents per kWh, respectively. Generator C has 500 MW of high ramp rate units operating at 10 cents Ž per kWh that can be used as operating reserves. . There is assumed to be a 500 MW load at bus 2.

Initially, if line 1–2 is in-service, the market price will be slightly less than 4 cents as Generator A under bids Generator B. If line 1–2 is taken out of service, the amount of advance notice will be critical to Generators B and C. If Generator B has not started its unit, Generator C will pick up the load and set a high price, perhaps up to the marginal value of service to customers. Once Generator B is on-line, the price will fall to slightly less than 10 cents as Generator C is under bid by B.

This example illustrates how profitability can be affected by the timeliness of information. The sooner Generator B knows about the need to take line 1–2 out of service, the sooner it will be able to capitalize on that knowledge. Further complications could arise if a conflict of interest occurs should Generator C own and maintain the line, and therefore, be the source of information about when the line needs to come out of service. In this case, Generator C would want to withhold the information about when the line would be outaged for as long as permitted by a system code. Doing so would allow it to achieve high profits while Generator B starts. Avoiding selfinterest information disclosure will be an important element of an information policy.

## 4.5. Who should pay for the information?

Using a market approach for information dissemination argues that market participants who value information the most should pay the most for it. Application of this approach has drawbacks. With the exception of participant-specific information Ž . such as bids , much of the information is inherently public in nature, particularly if the transmission system is considered a public infrastructure resource needed to make the market work. The collection and dissemination of the information comes at a very low marginal cost although the initial investment costŽ could be significant as long as the ISO is gathering. the information anyway for compliance with its service mandates. Transaction costs could be lower if the information is provided centrally. Finally, fairness may require information to be publicly available, particularly in verifying ISO decisions.

The value-added in the information process is the analysis and interpretation of the information. This will be a marketable service because the service would need to be tailored to the needs of the buyer, entry barriers for service providers should be low, and information availability would not be an entry barrier once it is publicly available.

## 5. Conclusion

Operational risks influences decision-making in several ways. Risk assessment is needed from a system perspective and a market participant perspective. Based on the underlying risk preferences, bidders may be inclined to assume different bidding and operating strategies. Toward baseload and generation under contract, risk minimization could be a driving strategy. Toward generation located in potential areas of congestion, more aggressive bidding strategies may be employed to capture resource scarcity rents. Strategy choices are shaped by the embedded generation technologies with their different physical and economic qualities.

Information policies are a fundamental component of restructuring policies in general. Information availability is essential for efficiency and fairness. Policies to achieve such a result will be more challenging to develop when market power and gaming opportunities exist. In this case, opportunistic behavior could be enhanced by the availability of good information. Technological assistance will be needed to enable timely delivery and analysis of information. Finally, market participants will be aided by the development of market and system analysis tools that they can use in understanding the implications of the available information for their market strategies.
