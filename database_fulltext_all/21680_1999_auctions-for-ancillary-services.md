# Auctions for ancillary services

Harry Singh

PG&E Energy SerÕices, 345 California St., San Francisco, CA 94104 USA

## Abstract

This paper discusses the procurement of ancillary services in a competitive electricity market. The paper assumes the existence of an underlying market for energy and explains why this energy market must be accompanied by a market for ancillary services. ancillary services include services such as the provision of operating reserves and Automatic Generation Control AGC both of which can require infra-marginal generators to choose between supplying energy, or operating Ž . reserves and AGC. Ancillary services are crucial for the reliable operation of an electric power system under a regime of competitive generation. Structures for organizing competitive auctions for ancillary services are examined. Initial experience and potential pitfalls from a market using such auctions is also reported. q 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Independent System Operators; Market power; Competitive electricity markets; Operating reserves

## 1. Introduction

The term ‘ancillary services’ generally refers to power system services other than the provision of energy or real power 1,5–7 . These services may <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> include services such as operating reserves, frequency control, loss compensation, energy imbalances, reactive power, black-start capability, etc. Our interest in this paper is limited to those services that can be supplied by generators and which bear a complementarity to the energy market. A competitive energy market typically involves a central auction for electricity to be delivered over short time periods. To implement such auctions, two distinct forms of market structure have been implemented around the world; a real-time market, such as the one that has been instituted in the Australian state of Victoria, and a day-ahead forward market such asŽ . the one that has been instituted in England and Wales. In Norway and California, both day-ahead and real-time markets have been instituted 2–4 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Since electricity is delivered instantaneously, even a day-ahead market requires a balancing service. In California, this is performed in a real-time market. Thus, the operation of the real time market is a fundamental responsibility of the Independent System Operator ISO . Since the ISO does not own anyŽ . generation, it must ensure that there is sufficient unloaded capacity among on-line generators to participate in the real-time market and secure the required amount of reserves that is needed for maintaining the reliability of the system. The ISO must also ensure that there is a sufficient quantity of Automatic Generation Control AGC capability andŽ .

quick-start generation capacity, and conform to mandated reliability criteria 9 . One way the ISO can<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> meet these requirements is to create a competitive market for ancillary services.

The market structure in California assumes that competitive markets for energy must be accompanied by competitive markets for ancillary services. This is particularly true for the services that bear a complementarity to energy markets. Two particular examples are spinning reserves and AGC. These services require generating units to maintain unloaded on-line capacity that may be used in real-time, to provide energy either for balancing purposes. The main characteristic of such ancillary services is that capacity is reserved in advance and then incremental energy is dispatched in real-time. This means that there are two relevant prices to be paid to generators. One is the price paid for reserved capacity and the other is the price paid for energy that is dispatched under certain defined conditions. Since the generator cannot anticipate or control the amount of energy it will be called on to produce, it is entitled to a variable energy payment.

Efficient dispatch requires that at least some generating units that are infra-marginal in the energy market, contribute towards the reserve requirements in a control area. These infra-marginal units may incur an opportunity cost for setting aside a part of their capacity to provide reserves instead of selling energy in the competitive market. One way to price the reserve services is to simultaneously procure reserves and energy in a single combined auction and compensate the units providing reserves at their opportunity costs derived from the energy auction. This approach can work in a market structure where the entity responsible for procuring ancillary services is also responsible for operating the energy market.

Another approach is to set up a separate auction for procuring reserves. A separate reserve auction is better suited for market structures where the ISO is separate from the Power Exchange PX , the entityŽ . that operates the forward energy market, as is the case in California.

This paper discusses the competitive procurement of ancillary services by an ISO. Section 2 of the paper describes the operation of the day-ahead forward energy market. Section 3 discusses the California model for procuring ancillary services while

Section 4 discusses the allocation of the cost of ancillary services. Section 5 describes how opportunity costs are incurred in providing reserves while Section 6 presents the structure of the reserves auction. Section 7 describes how ancillary services are dispatched in real-time and Section 8 addresses practical limitations of the proposed ancillary services auction model arising from market power concerns.

## 2. Day ahead energy markets

Given the close relationship between the energy and the ancillary services markets it is desirable to start by briefly describing the structure of the dayahead energy markets. The market structure assumed in this paper consists of a separate grid operator or ISO and market operator or Power Exchange PX . Ž . This model has been adopted in California. It is useful to begin with a conceptual description of the PX energy auction. The PX auction may be envisioned as a series of 24 auctions, one for each hour, conducted simultaneously. Generators submit price curves that describe their energy offers as a function of price; the quantity of energy offered must be a monotonically increasing function of price. Loads submit similar curves that are decreasing. The bid curves may be different for different hours.

The PX aggregates the curves into overall supply and demand curves for each hour. It determines the point at which each hour’s supply and demand curve intersects. This point is the PX Market Clearing Price MCP for that hour. Furthermore, the PXŽ . auction model adopted in California and discussed here recognizes no operational or inter-temporal constraints There may be iterations in the energy auction to help bidders satisfy their operational requirements, but they are not significant in describing the ancillary services procurement model 3,4 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

This means that the PX prices and associated dispatch patterns are not likely to lead to the conventional cost-minimizing dispatch for each hour. Units need not bid pure variable costs and are encouraged to internalize all physical constraints. This helps reduce the opportunities for arbitrage between the PX and the ISO’s real-time market and makes the pricing process more transparent. The goal of the PX auction is not necessarily to minimize production costs but to facilitate trade. The PX auction is intended to discover the prices at which parties are willing to transact.

## 3. Procurement of ancillary services

The ancillary services that the ISO is responsible for include spinning reserves, non-spinning reserves, AGC, replacement reserves, voltage support and black start. The first two are also referred to as operating reserves and the first three are sometimes classified as contingency reserves 9 . The reserves<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> and AGC services usually require generators to set aside unloaded capacity in the energy auction for possible use in real time by the ISO. The first four services can be procured by the ISO by means of daily competitive auctions. There are 24 independent hourly auctions for each service, paralleling the 24 independent hourly energy auctions in the PX. However, the ancillary services auctions occur sequentially after the close of the PX energy auction as shown in Fig. 1.

The last two ancillary services, i.e., black start capability and VAR support, are more suitable for procurement through long-term contracts from among those units physically capable of providing them. Spinning and Non-Spinning reserve requirements are typically defined by traditional reliability criteria <sup>1</sup> 10 . In addition, the ISO needs to make sure it has enough capacity online for a well-functioning balancing market. For this, the ISO may arrange for additional replacement reserves.

One can interpret reserve capacity as an option. The exercise value of the option is either the price that would otherwise be paid for balancing energy, or the value of lost load if demand were to be curtailed. An important characteristic of reserves, as distinct from financial options, is that they are procured not based on any dynamic hedging strategy but based on preset standards defined by the North American Electric Reliability Council NERC andŽ . the Western Systems Coordinating Council WSCC Ž . that relate the reserve requirements to overall generation.

## 4. Paying for ancillary services

The costs incurred by the ISO for purchasing reserves can be classified into two categories, aŽ . those that are mandated by reliability protocols, and Ž .b those that can be assigned to the ISO’s customers as a function of their observed performance and behavior. For our purposes it is sufficient to assume that the ISO’s customers are represented by Scheduling Coordinators SCs consistent with the CaliforniaŽ . energy market model. <sup>2</sup> Since the ISO’s reliability requirements are proportional to scheduled load, the reserve responsibility can be allocated pro rata to Scheduling Coordinators in proportion to their submitted demand schedules. This is by no means a prescriptive solution as the need for ancillary services may be correlated to the degree to which a Scheduling Coordinator causes a need for procuring a given service. However, the reliability requirements are assumed as given and the pro-rata allocation is viewed as a reasonable approach to start out with.

The one exception to the pro-rata allocation is the case of replacement reserves which are procured atŽ least in part on the basis of what the ISO expects to. be the difference between its own load forecast and the sum of the SC submitted schedules. In case the replacement reserves are actually dispatched, the associated costs are allocated to the Scheduling Coordinators in proportion to positive imbalances. <sup>3</sup> This mechanism attempts to relate the allocation of at least a part of the cost of reserves to those market participants that impose the greatest need for them. It also introduces an incentive for SCs to minimize their imbalances in the real-time market.

Initial experience from the California ISO’s market revealed that some SCs deliberately under-scheduled their generation and load to avoid paying for ancillary services. Their strategy seemed to work as long as they were able to ensure a balance between their generation and load in real-time.

SCs can also elect to meet their obligations by buying ancillary services bilaterally from generators in their portfolio or from other SCs. This feature is referred to as self-provision of ancillary services. Under self-provision, an SC is not charged for ancillary services by the ISO but pays for these costs through a bilateral arrangement with the supplier. However, self-provision is limited to the contractual arrangements for the purchase of ancillary services. The use of all ancillary services resources including those used for self-provision, is the exclusive responsibility of the ISO.

## 5. Opportunity costs

Before proceeding with the design of an auction for reserves, it is important to understand the nature of the costs incurred in providing reserves. There are two types of costs that must be represented. These include the opportunity costs of providing reserves and the costs incurred in case the generator is actually dispatched. To understand how opportunity costs arise, consider the case of a single reserve category; spinning reserves. We use spinning reserves as a prototype to motivate the analysis but the same logic applies to other ancillary services sharing the characteristic that capacity is reserved in advance and then used as dispatched generation depending on contingencies occurring later on.

Suppose that for a given hour the system load requirement is 6000 MW and that the spinning reserve requirement is 200 MW. Assume that 5600 MW of demand is met by a base load nuclear plant operating at full output. To supply the remaining 400 MW of demand and the 200 MW of spinning reserves, there are two units A and B with incremental energy bids of US\$4<sup>r</sup>MW h and US\$5<sup>r</sup>MW h, respectively and a capacity of 300 MW each. In the absence of any ramp rate limitations, the economic solution would be to dispatch unit A at its upper limit of 300 MW and unit B at 100 MW. Unit B would provide the spinning reserves from its unloaded capacity. The market price for energy is set by the US\$5<sup>r</sup>MW h generator. Since unit B is the marginal unit in the energy dispatch, it does not incur any opportunity cost in providing spinning reserves.

Next consider a scenario where both units are constrained by ramp rate limitations; each is able to provide no more than 100 MW in 10 min corresponding to a 10 MW<sup>r</sup>min ramp-rate. In this case, the 200 MW of spinning reserves must be provided from both units, each of which has to be dispatched at 200 MW. The market price of energy is still set by Unit B as it is the marginal resource. Unit A is an infra-marginal unit in the energy market, that incurs an opportunity cost by providing spinning reserves. Under the traditional operating paradigm of a vertically integrated electric utility, it is common to see infra-marginal units incurring opportunity costs in providing reserves during peak load hours. This suggests that bidders should be allowed to bid in a competitive auction for reserves, their assessment of opportunity costs in terms of a capacity reservation bid. Since these opportunity costs vary by hour and resource type, it is difficult to prescribe a single number to represent them in an ancillary service tariff as has been done in the past.

## 6. The reserves auction

This section presents the basic structure of an auction for competitively procuring ancillary services. There are separate auctions for AGC and each category of reserves spinning, non-spinning and re- Ž placement . Bidders submit separate capacity and. energy bid prices. They are paid a capacity reservation payment that is intended to reflect their opportunity costs. Bidders are also paid for energy if they are called on to provide it in real time. Depending on the specific case as determined later in this sectionŽ by the value of x. this energy price can be the market price or a ‘strike price’ as if an option were being exercised in the real-time balancing market.

Because the generator cannot anticipate the amount of energy it will be called on to produce in real-time, it must demand a variable energy payment. However, the variable payment must be determined when the unit is placed on reserve. Thus, in addition to the capacity reservation bid $R _ { i }$ Ž . US\$<sup>r</sup>MW , bidders must submit an energy bid $E _ { i }$ Ž . US\$<sup>r</sup>MW h .

Reserves auctions differ from the PX auction in one very important aspect. The goal of the PX auction is to facilitate trade because there will be many participants on both sides, i.e., buyers and sellers. <sup>4</sup> Ancillary services are solely the ISO’s responsibility, so the ISO is the single purchaser party in procuring reserve auctions to meet its reliability obligations. If the purchaser of energy from reserves could be identified in advance it could be forced to schedule capacity. Because the ‘energy purchase’ is really a random event, the ISO as the agent for all market participants buys the reserves according to a preset formula. As a single buyer it can seek to minimize its costs; the formula that governs the purchase should prevent strategic withholding of reserve demand.

An important property of opportunity costs is that the more infra-marginal a unit the deeper in the Ž loading order , the greater its opportunity costs in . providing spinning reserves. Thermal units’ capacity reservation bids should reflect their opportunity costs, which in turn reflect lost profit market energy price Ž minus marginal cost . The capacity reservation bids. $R _ { i }$ should be inversely related to the energy bids $E _ { i } ,$ if energy bids are cost-reflected as they ought to be in a competitive market. <sup>5</sup> Thus, to minimize the expected cost of reserves, the ISO cannot consider only the capacity or only the energy bids, but must estimate the probability that reserves will be utilized. It must include a probability factor x in the bid evaluation, i.e., bids are ranked on the basis of $R _ { i } + x E _ { i }$ . ## 6.3. The x( ) <sup>s</sup>0 case

The $( 0 \leq x \leq 1 )$ case may be criticized as being too complex with some susceptibility to being gamed. It is only an approximation to the true cost minimization, as the probability of calling upon a successful bidder to generate in real-time is likely to vary according to the individual energy bids. Presumably, intelligent bidders could game this market structure to increase their revenues. One possibility involves bidders lowering their energy bids in order to increase their capacity payments. However, this also increases the probability of the bidder’s getting called upon to generate in real-time. Ideally, a bidder would like to lower its energy bid as much as possible without altering its position in the merit order stack. Finally, the option-based case does not result in a uniform price for reserve capacity payments that are typical in the transmission tariffs of utilities used under the current market structure.

An alternative design, is to rank bids solely on the capacity reservation, i.e., the ISO takes bids in order of reservation bid, and pays each the highest reservation bid among those accepted. Since the energy bids are ignored in the bid evaluation, there is less justification to consider them as fixed strike prices. It might appear reasonable to assume that the reserve units are price-takers in the real-time market, i.e., they cannot set the ex-post price which they must accept. ## 7. Dispatch of ancillary services

All schedules received by the ISO from the PX and other SCs are balanced. In real-time, deviations from scheduled generation and load levels are inevitable, either as a result of normal load and generation variation or as a result of larger unplanned outages, or as a result of strategic gaming. Such uninstructed deÕiations from schedules result in an energy imbalance that is manifested in the Area Control Error ACE . The generators on AGC are theŽ . first to respond to the imbalances. This action takes place within a time-frame of seconds. ## 8. Addressing market power

In order to secure the regulators’ permission to sell energy at market based prices, generators must present a detailed analysis showing the absence of market power. Similarly, they must show that they lack market power in the ancillary services markets in order to be able to sell ancillary services at market based prices. In April 1998, there had been few, if any attempts to analyze market power in the case of ancillary services. This meant that generators had to accept traditional cost-based rates for ancillary. However, determining the cost based rates can be equally arbitrary. For example, the cost-based rates that applied as the initial set of price-caps on the resources of the three investor-owned utilities in California are summarized in Table 4.

The wide range of the price-caps reveals the lack of correlation with the actual value of the particular service in a competitive market. Moreover, choosing a single price to serve as a cap for all the hours is far from perfect. For example, in peak hours the opportunity cost incurred by a generator in supplying spinning reserves can be significantly higher than the price-cap. This would eliminate incentives for inframarginal generators to make unloaded capacity available for reserves instead of offering the same capacity in the PX energy market. The net consequence would be a shortfall in ancillary services. On the other hand, in off-peak hours, because of the acknowledged possibility of market power, it is quite possible that the prices for reserves would approach the cost-based rates, which can be higher than the competitive prices. This observation was almost the norm in the initial experience in the California market. The prices and quantities in the spinning reserves auction for one of the initial operating days are illustrated in Fig. 3. The prices during the off-peak hours were at or near US\$7.40<sup>r</sup>MW while those in the peak hours were at or near US\$9.22<sup>r</sup>MW. These figures happen to coincide with the price caps shown in Table 4. It may appear counterintuitive that the quantity of spinning reserves procured in the peak hours is less than that in the off-peak hours even though the prices are higher in peak hours. However, this is not surprising when one considers it is precisely the peak hours when opportunity costs of providing spinning reserves can exceed the price caps. The problem can become more severe with seasonal increases in the energy market prices.

At the same time, an elimination of price caps in the ancillary services market can also lead to unintended consequences. This is particularly true if price caps are relaxed for only some resources. Table 5 shows the prices for replacement reserves on July 9, 1998 for a few selected hours. These high prices of US\$5000<sup>r</sup>MW followed a June 30, 1998 ruling by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission FERC Ž . that relaxed the price caps for replacement reserves, in response to a filing by a new owner of divested utility generation. <sup>7</sup> On July 14, 1998 the prices for replacement reserves in hours 14–18 were as high as US\$9999<sup>r</sup>MW. <sup>8</sup> Following these prices, the ISO instituted price caps of US\$500<sup>r</sup>MW for all ancillary services, which were subsequently reduced to US\$250<sup>r</sup>MW. Prices at these caps have been observed many times since then. Clearly, such prices are not a reflection of opportunity costs but rather a sign of market power. Among the factors that may have influenced these prices is the fact that the ISO was unable to accept bids from outside its control area until August 5, 1998. Also worth mentioning is that fact that the demand of ancillary services is treated as inelastic except perhaps for replacement reserves where the ISO’s variations on the quantity procured can be interpreted an ad-hoc measure of demand elasticity.

## 9. Conclusion

Competitive markets for electricity require competitive markets for reliability or ancillary services.

This paper has presented a market structure for the competitive procurement of such services and discusses the role of the opportunity costs incurred in providing these services. The design of an auction for reliability services such as reserves presents an interesting dilemma between strict cost-minimization and efficient incentives for bidders. The proposed design seeks to choose efficient incentives over strict cost-minimization under the assumptions of a well functioning and competitive market.
