## Abstract

This paper contributes a methodology for integrating intelligent tutoring into a specific, total enterprise, role-playing gaming-simulation environment. The result of the application of the methodology is a prototype intelligent gaming-simulation environment. q 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

Although gaming-simulation is an important learning tool to accomplish learning, it, nevertheless, follows the concept of discovery learning or learning by performance which does not support any direct educational aim nor it constitutes a prolific educational experience 2–5,20 . Gaming-simulations pro- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> vide the player with the opportunity to develop the skills of hypothesis-testing, logic, and inductive and deductive reasoning indirectly 16–18,31 through a <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> sequential decision-making exercise whose basic function is to provide an artificial but realistic environment that enables players to experience the consequences of their decisions through immediate response 15,23,30,33 . The player is put in a position <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> where learning takes place through experimentation with a model that allows him to explore the rules of cause and effect neither with a any adaptability to Ž . his individual needs and preferences nor with b anyŽ . helpful and individualized guidance, or c any feed-Ž . back or remediation should any errors occur.

For example, the original version of the business simulation game that we use in this paper aims to teach managerial skills to students through experience during game play. However, the game does not adapt to the student-players’ knowledge, skills, needs and preferences. The student-players are merely provided with a business environment in which they have to direct and organize the game play themselves. As a consequence, students are left to manage their own roles, and any unfamiliar management tasks, such as pursuing sensible market research, are often avoided rather than attempted due to lack of any helpful and individualized guidance. Consequently, student-players either steer away from unfamiliar management tasks or if they embark on such tasks and make mistakes they usually straggle because of the lack of any feedback or remediation and that interrupts the game flow. Similarly, any intervention by the human tutor results in interruption of the game flow. Furthermore, student-players do not always call for the human tutor because they want their mistakes to be private and, therefore, wait for a reaction of the market as a way of feedback.

Although endowing a gaming-simulation environment with adaptability, individualisation, feedback<sup>r</sup> remediation does not necessarily improve the actual simulation game, it, nevertheless, makes it effective as a teaching or learning tool, one in which the student is not left completely on his own to understand and correct his mistakes and learn from them, make the right decisions at the right time, and generally optimise his experiences from the gaming-simulation environment in a way that suits his individual style of learning.

Intelligent Tutoring Systems can enrich a gamingsimulation environment that is used for learning by endowing the environment with a individualizedŽ . student guidance and support within the learning environment, b adaptation to student needs and Ž . preferences and c feedback and remediation where Ž . necessary 6,8,21,24–27,29,35 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

The purpose of this paper is to investigate how to integrate intelligent tutoring and gaming-simulation and, in particular, it proposes a methodology for integrating intelligent tutoring into a gaming-simulation environment. The discussion begins with a classification of gaming-simulations that helps define the exact typology of the gaming-simulation environment for learning in which intelligent tutoring will be incorporated. This is followed by a discussion of intelligent tutoring environments for learning and gaming-simulation environments for learning. The methodology for integration is then introduced and subsequently applied in the development of a business simulation game as an intelligent gaming-simulation environment. Finally, the environment is evaluated first internally, to determine the level of success of the development and thus the methodology itself, and then externally, to determine the educational effectiveness of the environment on the student-users.

## 2. Classification of gaming-simulation

Gaming-simulation can be classified in different ways 18 . One typology deals with<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> systemic games vs. role-playing games. Systemic games focus on interrelationships and interdependencies between elements of an economic, political or social system where players develop their own role behaviours. Role-playing games focus on given particular positions in the real world and relationships between these positions. Two other typologies 18 are partic-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ularly relevant to business games. The first one makes a distinction between general games and specific games to a particular industry. The second one makes a distinction between total enterprise games covering a wide range of managerial disciplines for example, production, marketing, etc. , and Ž . functional games with an emphasis on one specific discipline. Gaming-simulation is a sequential decision-making exercise, whose basic function is to provide an artificial environment where some characteristics of a real situation are replicated, enabling players to follow up the consequences of their decisions with rapid response. Its objective is to enhance a comprehensive understanding of complex systems and to develop learning skills.

The most popular gaming-simulation is that of the specific, total enterprise business game whose purpose is to give an insight into the work of the manager in a specific business, into the problems he faces, and the methods he uses to plan and control the business’ operations. The business manager has certain resources committed to his charge. His first responsibility is to identify a product or service which the community needs. Secondly, he must plan a strategy for the methods, volume and cost of production, while generating the necessary profit. Thirdly, he must control the planned operations and adjust them to suit any changes in conditions. This kind of gaming-simulation assumes that the user will take on the role of the business manager.

The business simulation game that we use in this paper is characteristic of a specific, total enterprise, role playing business simulation game. Such a gaming-simulation environment consists of twelve basic elements 11,12 which are described below. This <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> typology of gaming-simulation is, hereafter, the basis for the discussion in this paper.

Scenario is an outline of the plot of the game. It outlines starting conditions and describes circumstances leading into play. It deals with all aspects, i.e., economic, social, and political, which are either presented by text or supplemented with diagrams and illustrations. Role descriptions might be considered a part of the scenario, but are offered separately.

Pulse is some event or problem introduced during the course of the play to focus the player’s attention on a single aspect of the problem. The pulse may be either designer or player induced. It may be predetermined, random or triggered by a certain action in the game. A pulse is an organisational device, used to encourage multilogue i.e., multiple, simultaneousŽ dialogue by forcing players to focus on some shared . phenomena.

Cycle sequence is divided between the macro cycle sequence that takes into account preconditions to the game, the introductory cycle, the final cycle and the evaluation process associated with the total exercise and the micro cycle that takes into account the sequence of things that occur within each cycle, including initiation, policy, action, and cycle evaluation.

Steps of play are the explicit progression of activity in the game. There is a macro cycle in each cycle which includes four steps: initiation, policy, action, and evaluation. During the initiation, the players read the scenario, take into a cycle any pulses<sup>r</sup>events<sup>r</sup>issues that have occurred, and consider any new data available to them as a result of the previous cycle. During the action cycle, players make specific decisions according to a given order. During the evaluation phase of the cycle, all play stops and an intellectual discussion ensues, under the direction of the game operator which addresses two questions: What are the results of the cycle just completed? and How does this experience relate to the real-world problem? The next step is always recycling.

Rules govern any circumstances that may develop in a game, which go beyond the scope of the expertise of the game.

Roles are characters assigned to players with prescribed patterns of behaviour. They are predicated on known real-world counterparts. There are three kinds of roles that can be included within the game: pseudo, gamed, or simulated. Pseudo roles are invented on the spot to serve some immediate function, e.g., judges and technical experts. Gamed roles are built into the gaming situation framework and played by real players whose decisions are processed by the game’s accounting system. Simulated roles exist in the accounting system but not physically in the game-room itself. Often they represent broad classes or categories of people.

Models are devices derived from the accounting system to keep track of logical processes. They may be expressed in mathematical terms or illustrated graphically. There are three types of models: Analogue models which parallel the real-world phenomena and correspond to the real counterparts they represent at least at some level of abstraction, Iconic models which give the physical appearance of reality but need not act like reality and Heuristic or homologue models.

Decision sequence and linkage represent the typical sequence of decisions that players can make during a normal cycle of play. Often these are developed through the use of a matrix. Across the top of the matrix are all of the gamed roles and down the left side are the steps of play. This schematic is intended to answer the question: Who is doing what, when and how? It also provides data on information flows and feedbacks and role-to-role transitions. The matrix depicts the activity and intellectual process of each role during consecutive steps of play. The purpose of this matrix is to assist in visualising the sequence of play when the game is finished. The matrix helps to identify role linkages within the game framework, to chart the foreseen reactions of the participants to events during play, and to provide an initial analysis of all gamed, pseudo and simulated role results before play begins. In evaluating the contents of the matrix, the need will arise to adapt or change roles for one of several reasons. Players must be more or less equally loaded so that they are evenly occupied during the presentation. It is also necessary during an analysis of this chart to ensure that decisions are sequenced properly, one behind the other, so that feedback takes place.

Accounting system is a set of fixed procedures incorporated directly into the game to deal consistently with player decisions. These decisions are processed, acted upon, and forwarded to some other game component, feeding back either into an indicator, model, role, or some combination of the above.

The accounting system manoeuvres players’ responses through models, simulations, or very simple algorithms. It will always report to the players through various indicators.

Indicators are those aspects of the accounting system that the operator chooses to emphasise for the participants. They report on the game’s progress, that is the interaction of players’ decisions as filtered through the accounting system and linked to the models.

Symbology is the physical representation of indicators which are game-specific in that they lose meaning outside of the playing arena. They are integrated into the game to portray some reality such as the land-use or building pattern.

Paraphernalia is everything else required to successfully run the simulation exercise.

## 3. Intelligent tutoring environments for learning

An Intelligent Tutoring System must pass three tests of intelligence 1,10,14,32 . Firstly, the system<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> must know the subject matter well enough to be able to draw inferences or solve problems in the domain of application. Secondly, it must be able to deduce the student’s current understanding of the subject matter and use this individualized knowledge to adapt instruction to the student’s needs. Thirdly, the tutor must be able to apply suitable tutoring strategies that reduce the difference between the expert and the student performance. This includes formulating a representation of the subject material and selecting and sequencing concepts from that representation.

For this purpose Intelligent Tutoring Systems make use of an explicit representation of the domainspecific knowledge and the problem solving knowledge of the topic, which they intend to teach the Student. At the same time Intelligent Tutoring Systems are equipped with teaching expertise 13 . They <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> have the ability to determine the user’s current knowledge state, his missing conceptions and his misconceptions within the teaching domain. They achieve this by collecting feedback from the user during the course of interaction and by being able to analyze this feedback against a wide range of predefined player behaviours 35 . This enables the Intelli-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> gent Tutoring System to tailor its instruction according to the needs of the student. Most Intelligent Tutoring Systems are also equipped with the ability to help their users clear away any misconceptions and acquire any ‘missing conceptions’. A model of the standard architecture of an Intelligent Tutoring System is given in Fig. 1.

The model assumes that the two process models in the lower half of Fig. 1 do not directly take part in major pedagogical decisions. Instead, they support the tutoring process by making the representation of domain knowledge available. The domain process model can directly answer student questions or provide information to other models about the domain if and when they need it. To extract useful information from the domain knowledge representation, the domain process model applies reasoning. The interface process model translates the flow of information to and from the student-user.

The activities of the process models in the upper half of Fig. 1 result in decisions that shape the course of instruction. Didactics refers to pedagogical activities intended to have a direct effect on the student, as opposed to diagnostic activities 34 . The task of<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> these activities is to create a pedagogical bridge between the tutoring model, the domain model and the student model. Strategies for dealing with this take the form of pedagogical plans that incorporate fixed sets of diagnostic expectations along with mechanisms for dealing with common student problems, like misconceptions. There are three classes of circumstances each with different implications for the respective roles of student diagnosis and the didactic operation. In opportunistic strategies, the monitored activities provide a focus for both diagnostic and didactic activities but diagnosis is the driving force because it reveals opportunities for tutorial interventions. Pedagogical goals are associated with diagnostic units and their attainment is monitored by differential modelling. In plan-based strategies, the main task of diagnosis is to monitor the implementation of teaching plans that embody pedagogical goals. These plans provide a focus for diagnostic activities with the consequence that differential modelling is performed in terms of plan failures so that revisions can be made.

The pedagogical module is responsible for optimising the interplay of diagnosis and didactic operations thus providing for a coherent pedagogical strategy. It is central to an Intelligent Tutoring System because it includes decision making about the degree of control exercised by the system, the choice of teaching strategy to apply, the selection of strategic contexts i.e., opportunistic vs. plan-based contexts Ž . the interleaving of pedagogical episodes, the allocation of computational resources required by competing functions and the target level of operations i.e.,Ž behavioural, epistemic or individual . All these deci-. sion making aspects conjecture the didactic operation which is the mechanism by which the pedagogical process model drives the interaction in order to attain its pedagogical goals.

Consequently, learning with Intelligent Tutoring Systems involves adaptation, individualisation, and feedback<sup>r</sup>remediation. Tutoring is very structured, there are goals to be met, material to be covered and there is no room for free-experimentation. The student-user of an intelligent tutoring system never learns by observing the reaction of the system following his mistakes. All actions and mistakes are carefully prognosed, as far as possible, from knowledge and previous experiences. The student-user is simply guided through the interaction by a knowledgeable companion. As a result, the student-user does not enjoy the benefits of learning by discovery and experimentation offered by a more realistic gaming-simulation environment free of the formal tuition that an intelligent tutoring system usually imposes on the student-user. The user of an intelligent tutoring system is not free to discover or experiment as in a gaming-simulation environment because all learning has to take place through a formal guided learning process. Therefore, the flow of interaction will be regularly interrupted in the name of formal tuition, help, advice, feedback, remediation.

## 4. Specific, total enterprise, role-playing gamingsimulation environments for learning

The dividing line in Fig. 1 distinguishes between Intelligent Tutoring Systems which use all the process models and knowledge in the diagram, and gaming-simulation which requires only those in the lower half. With reference to Fig. 1, gaming-simulation entails only some of the process models of Intelligent Tutoring Systems, therefore, gamingsimulation implements a different and less sophisticated pedagogical approach. By simply making knowledge available for use rather than actively communicating it as Intelligent Tutoring Systems do, it leaves most of the responsibility of managing the learning process to the student-user. With gamingsimulation the student does not directly explore the knowledge the game entails but just the game itself. The student-user is expected to have enough understanding of the knowledge behind the game and be able to formulate his learning path through the game consciously, to decide what to explore or to experiment on next, as well as to interpret what is presented.

Gaming-simulation provides an environment that facilitates learning or the acquisition of skills by mimicking reality and by providing the student with entertaining challenges. The purpose of gamingsimulation is to present a variety of information like facts and principles, processes, the structure and dynamics of systems, skills such as problem solving, decision making or strategy formulating, social skills such as communication and attitudes and a variety of identical skills such as the nature of competition, how people cooperate, the dynamics of social systems, the role of chance, and the fact that penalties often have to be paid for just or unjust reasons.

Specific, total enterprise, role-playing gamingsimulation functions wholly on the basis of players decisions and the gaming-simulation environment and activities of participants have the characteristics of games: players have goals, sets of activities to perform, constraints on what can be done, and payoffs good and bad as consequences of the actions.Ž .

The elements in such a gaming-simulation environment are paradigmed from real life: roles, goals, activities, constraints, and consequences, and the linkages among them simulate those elements of the real-world system. Therefore, gaming-simulation is a hybrid form, involving the performance of game activities in simulated contexts. These may range from fairly simple decision-making exercises lasting no more than a few minutes to extremely elaborate simulations which may take a considerable amount of time for the completion of a single round of decision-making. The aim of such a gaming-simulation is to communicate principles and skills in such diverse areas as marketing, production, stock control, labour relations. Gaming-simulation can be equated with the phenomenon of learning the game ‘Monopoly’ by joining in someone else’s game rather than trying to begin by learning the detailed rules.

With gaming-simulations the responsibility of managing the learning process is with the student-user because not only he has to understand the simulation game, he must also formulate his own learning path consciously but largely ad hoc, through the gamingsimulation environment. The absence of a student model does not allow for any long-term adaptation of knowledge and problem solving to the student needs, or indeed any long-term individualisation other than a short-term reaction to a student input or mistake. In addition, the absence of didactics precludes the provision of formal feedback or remediation other than a short-term reaction, again as a result of a student input. The simulation game tends to motivate interested students who, nevertheless, focus their attention on the goal of the game rather than enhancing their learning. With gaming-simulation the student is totally free to explore and experiment within the rules of the game and concentrate on achieving the required performance to win the game. Of course, this defeats the primary purpose of gaming-simulation which is to facilitate learning rather than to declare a game winner 9,11,12 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Therefore, from an educational standpoint, an Intelligent Tutoring System for learning is more attractive than gaming-simulation because it offers individualised pedagogical control whose goal is to master knowledge through gaming and not to achieve a certain level of performance with the simulation game. The interventions of a tutoring component can be obtrusive especially in those situations where the flow of interaction is important. However, they can save the student from problems typical of unguided learning such as stagnating, floundering excessively, or overlooking learning opportunities.

Therefore, the first step towards a significant contribution in the field of both intelligent tutoring and gaming-simulation is to integrate intelligent tutoring and gaming-simulation 35 . Gaming-simulation<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> would benefit from the use of student modelling techniques, didactics and pedagogical control because the resulting environment would offer, either when necessary or when requested, formal tuition, adaptability, individualisation, feedback and remediation to the student-players. In addition, the use of domain knowledge modelling techniques would ensure that the gaming-simulation knowledge is not just operational for the purpose of executing the game but it is also communicable to the studentplayers. The environment resulting from such integration would actively help the student to convert the game situations in which they were in, into managerial knowledge and skills.

## 5.1. Phase 1: Game definition

Phase 1 that involves defining the basic elements that constitute a game is largely a game-dependent activity because the commonalities between simulation games in terms of content and environment are relatively very few 17 . What one expects to find <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> that simulation games have in common are that they should include the twelve basic elements that constitute a simulation game. We, therefore, proceed with our discussion of the methodology phases with Phase 2 of the methodology.

## 5.2. Phase 2: Intelligent Tutoring System models deÕelopment

The domain model contains all the knowledge about the game. This includes an initial scenario that introduces the game by stating its aims and principles, rules, gamed roles, and symbology. This is not intended as a replacement of the Player’s Manual but as a complementary resource which the user may recall at any time. The domain model should also include worked examples of any practical exercises that the players will do during game play. The domain knowledge will be used both for game play and as a source of information for tutoring purposes.

The domain model also includes the accounting system and any resulting logical models for performing various calculations and any indicators that the system may choose to emphasise. In addition to using its knowledge about roles and rules for allowing the game to run, the domain model may use its knowledge about the game, for example, to explain the rules of the game should it detect a deviation from them, to offer some help in applying them correctly, to explain the purpose of a particular role, etc. For this purpose, the domain model should have access to a library of all common misconceptions about the game.

The tutor model includes knowledge about the game’s teaching goals which it will try and help the student to attain through game play, the steps of play and the cycle sequence. Thus, it knows when, where and how to start, progress, and finish a game. The tutor model supervises the flow of the game by controlling the steps of play, from introduction to the game through to postgame discussion or critique and by introducing pulses during various steps in the cycle sequence. In addition, by being in control of the time mechanism, it is in control of all the time parameters. All tutoring activities are under the control of computational teaching strategies which the tutor model uses to let the game flow. If a misconception is diagnosed for a player, then the tutor model goes into remedial mode for that player. Which teaching strategy will be applied is decided either by reference to the student model or by the user, during the course of interaction. A teaching strategy is an implementation of a particular teaching strategy used by human teachers, for example, coaching, questions<sup>r</sup>answering, evaluation of student responses, etc. This contains tutoring knowledge about material presentation, for formulating tasks<sup>r</sup>responses to the student, for student evaluation and remediation.

The student model includes the current knowledge of the player about the game, especially how well he mastered the rules of the game, the role he played during game play, his performance during the different steps of play and how well he managed the resources he was allocated if any by the system.Ž . The student model also includes a record of all diagnosed missing conceptions and misconceptions, and whether these have been remedied at any stage,

## 5.3. Phase 3: Intelligent Tutoring System models linkage

The three models need to be interlinked and interconnected. For example, the contents of the student model should be linked to the ‘best’ for the student teaching strategy in the tutoring knowledge model and to those goals that have been attained by the student and those that are yet to be attained. The student model should also be linked to those domain knowledge parts that have been mastered by the student, perhaps including a measure of the level of mastery, for example through an overlay model. The student model should also be linked to those domain knowledge parts that are the source of misconceptions for the student. In the contents of the model of the tutoring knowledge, goals which the system will try to attain should be linked to that part of the domain knowledge that contains domain knowledge relevant to the goal along with a set of appropriate teaching strategies that would enable this.

## 6. Using the methodology for developing intelligent business gaming-simulation environments

In this section we apply the methodology to develop an intelligent gaming-simulation environment out of an existing business game.

## 6.1. Phase 1: Game definition

The Business Simulation Game that we employ was developed to give players an insight into the work of business managers and thereby acquire an understanding of business management. In this game the Company is a manufacturer and supplier of central heating boilers and radiators which are sold at prices between £1600 and £2500.

The player or group of players starts business as Ž . one of the managers of the Company having to solve financial, production or marketing problems. The business must be run efficiently to be able to pay for wages, materials and services and to cover the costs of the development of new production resources, such as an expansion of the size of the factory. The Company has up to four players who take over one of four roles. The Production Director has to make decisions on the amount of boilers to produce and has to determine the selling price. The Sales Director makes decisions on any market research or research and development to be undertaken, the number of sales persons to be recruited and which customers the sales staff should call on. The Financial Director has to master all calculations and is responsible for completing the Company’s accounts. The Managing Director is in overall charge; he is the final arbiter on all operating decisions.

There are two other companies which are proposing to make and sell similar equipment and therefore are competing in the market. To enable the players to plan, make decisions and control operations, each company uses two documents, the Company Decision Sheet and the Company Operating Statements.

## 6.2. Phase 2: Intelligent Tutoring System models deÕelopment

The game has been implemented using the object-based hypertext package HyperCard 7,19,22 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> All the data is incorporated into semi-structured hypercards. The semi-structured kind of card allows the use of labelled fields which can either be filled with default values or may be instantiated with non-default values as these occur. Related hypercards are grouped together in a stack. All stacks and hypercards are linked to other stacks and hypercards to construct the required network that represents the game.

The domain model contains the knowledge that is required to maintain and operate game play. The domain model contains the initial scenario with which to introduce the game by stating its aims, principles and steps of play. Furthermore, it contains all the information about the four manager roles i.e., theŽ Production, Sales, Financial and Managing Director .. It includes knowledge about the CDS and the three Control Statements that the players will fill in during game play. It also includes knowledge about production, production costs, the market, time sequence of production sales and cash flow, market research, selling the products, product advertising, R&D and finance. The domain model contains the rules of the game which it uses should it detect a deviation from them, to offer some help in applying them correctly and to correct any errors which may occur. Similarly, the player is granted direct access to the Rules if he feels the need to refer to them during the game.

The domain model also contains the factual data that is required to run the game, such as the market demand for the products, the distribution of the market demand on the customers in the current game and a table with all market demand distributions possible. It also records all the necessary information on the selling prices of all three competitors. This information is then provided to those players who demand market research information regarding their competitors’ selling prices. The model also includes the competitors’ cumulative expenditures on research and development. The model also contains the information about decisions made by the simulated roles of the two competing companies and, similar to the factual game knowledge, is used to run the game. Since this information is determined by the current market situation it is being generated and stored during game play.

Therefore, the domain knowledge also supports any calculations or estimates the player may want to do before making a decision on a particular aspect. The player, therefore, has access to it at any time and may interrogate it. The interrogation is then analyzed by the system if–then analysis to get an estimate ofŽ . the result a particular decision might have, e.g., the player may want to analyze all his expenditures in the current quarter to get an estimate of total costs per product to support the decision on a selling price. The environment’s accounting system uses the model for the evaluation of any decisions made during game play. The domain knowledge includes the knowledge and procedures used by the system to lead the player through a process in which they can consolidate newly acquired knowledge.

The model also includes the data that results from the system simulating all actions to be made by the Company. This data is based on the current market situation and is consequently generated during game play. In this way an ‘ideal’ student model of all four directors arises. The data is used to evaluate the decisions of any gamed roles in order to diagnose any errors. Alternatively, if the director role is a gamed role, the data is used as system-generated input into the game.

Additional Intelligent Tutoring System domain knowledge such as pre-worked examples have been integrated into the gaming-simulation knowledge. This additional knowledge provide for teaching support such as practice and assistance with problems and presentations of alternative problem solution approaches.

The tutoring model includes the teaching strategies which the environment uses to deal with players’ decisions and to guide the game. It includes knowledge about the game’s teaching goals which it will try and help the player to attain during game play. Thus, it knows when, where and how to start, progress, and finish a game. The model controls the steps of play and cycle sequence to supervise the flow of the game. The process of selling products to the customers is controlled by the tutoring model, i.e., it determines which company has sold how many products to which customer. The tutoring model is supported by information which it obtains from the domain model and the student knowledge model. The tutoring model randomly introduces pulses during the various phases of the cycle sequence.

The environment incorporates a student model for each player. This is an extension to the basic Decision Sequence and Linkage record that traditional gaming-simulation includes. The student model includes a library of all common errors in order to support error diagnosis. An overlay model represents the player’s current knowledge about the game, the role he plays in the current game and the roles he played in previous games, his performance during the different steps of the current and previous games and how well he managed the resources he was allocated by the system. This knowledge is represented as an overlay of the domain knowledge and is derived from the decisions recorded by the diagnosis process.

A student model contains information on all the errors a player has been found to have made, the context in which an error has been detected and the number of times it had occurred and how these errors have been corrected. Finally, a student model records all the decisions made by the players. The student model is a useful source of information during game play, because it provides the basis on which the accounting system can make decisions, such as further distribution of resources and role re-assignment. This information may be accessed by a human supervisor to assess the player’s performance.

## 6.3. Phase 3: Intelligent Tutoring System models linkage

The three models are interlinked and interconnected. The student model is linked to the ‘best’ teaching strategy for an individual student in the tutoring model and to those goals in the tutoring model that have been accomplished by the student and to those that are yet to be accomplished. The student model is also linked to those parts in the domain model that have been mastered by the student, with the inclusion of a measure of the level of their mastery. The student model is also linked to those parts in the domain model that have been inferred to be the source of the student misconceptions. Teaching goals in the tutoring model which the system has yet to accomplish are linked to those parts of the domain model that contain domain knowledge relevant to the goal as well as to a set of appropriate teaching strategies that would enable this. The linkage is easily achieved with hypertext links.

## 6.4. Phase 4: Intelligent Tutoring System process models function assignment

Remediation and feedback and, thereby, individualisation and adaptability is the heart of the intelligent gaming-simulation environment. The functionality of the process models evolves around these concepts. Therefore, every step of play which involves decision-making by either a gamed or simulated role is preceded by the following operation: the expertise process model determines all the correct or possi-Ž ble solutions for a step of play based on all previous. events and decisions in the game. These calculated solutions serve one of two purposes. In the case where the step of play involves decision-making by a simulated role, it uses the solutions as the input into the game. If, on the other hand, the steps of play involve decision-making by a gamed role, the solutions are used for error detection and diagnosis.

Error diagnosis takes place once the player has completed his tasks for a step of play. The player’s decisions and solutions are compared against the calculated correct solutions to detect any errors. The diagnosis process model then attempts to diagnose the error and determine a remedial goal, i.e., the misconception to be repaired or the missing concept to be filled. Once all possible errors within a gamed step of play have been diagnosed and the associated remedial goals have been determined remedial tutoring can take place.

A misconception or missing concept may be treated at different target levels. The target level determines whether the remedial intervention needs to correct an error through the correction of the student’s displayed behaviour, i.e., at the behavioural level, or through the correction of the error that caused the incorrect behaviour of the student, i.e., at the epistemic level. Both target levels may be addressed for novice or more advanced students. The remedial processes within the environment address both the behavioural and the epistemic target levels. Different teaching strategies may be used to address different target levels. The environment uses this approach within its remedial tutoring. For this purpose, the didactics process model incorporates the following remedial teaching strategies: successive refinement, cognitive apprenticeship, demonstration, Socratic hinting and reteaching.

The didactics process model uses these different remedial teaching strategies at different target levels. Successive refinement, for example, is a strategy used in remedial situations to address the epistemic level, whilst Socratic hinting the behavioural level.

The didactics process model’s remedial process follows a repair plan which consists of steps of action and uses remedial teaching strategies to lead the players through these steps of action. Any remedial process may include the steps of initial interruption, introduction, the actual repair and consolidation in which the student may apply his new understanding of the concept involved. Every remedial goal, i.e., every misconception and missing concept stored in the domain model, is associated with one or more of the remedial teaching strategies of the environment’s tutoring model. If a misconception or missing concept can be repaired using one specific remedial teaching strategy only, this strategy will be pre-programmed.

However, the tutoring model offers a choice of remedial teaching strategies for the remediation of most misconceptions or missing concepts. The selection of an appropriate remedial teaching strategy may then be carried out pre-programmed, student-led, companion-led or supervisor-led. The didactics process model applies the following reasoning mechanism in determining its planning approaches.

If according to the student model the player is an advanced student who has not committed the error before, the remedial process is pre-programmed. In this case the didactics process model uses the assumption that for advanced students, remedial tutoring at the behavioural level may be sufficient, i.e., that the student does not need a detailed explanation of the underlying misconception or missing concept. An appropriate strategy is Socratic hinting where the student is provided with a quick reminder to correct his mistake. Thus, it uses Socratic hinting in its initial attempt to correct the error if, according to the tutoring model, Socratic hinting is a remedial teaching strategy associated with the misconception or missing concept under consideration. If the advanced student fails to correct his error the advancement stage in his student model is down-graded to novice.

If the misconception to be repaired or the missing concept to be filled allows the application of more than one remedial teaching strategy the didactics process model incorporates all four planning approaches in the following order. It initially uses the student-led planning approach. For this purpose it retrieves all remedial strategies which are associated with the remedial goal being pursued. It then presents the student with these remedial teaching strategies from which the student my select his preference.

Once a remedial process has been completed, the player has to go back into the game to correct his mistake. However, the student’s strategy selection may not necessarily result in an optimal choice. If, as a consequence, the approach chosen by the student has not led to the correction of his misconception or acquisition of the missing concept, it uses the preprogrammed approach in a new attempt to correct the misconception or fill the missing concept. In this case the system itself decides on the remedial teaching strategy to be used next. For this purpose it uses strategy selection criteria for determining the most appropriate remedial teaching strategy for a particular remedial process. The strategy selection is not the only suitable approach but it enhances adaptability to the student. The selection mechanism takes into account the previous experience the student has had with remedial teaching strategies in earlier remedial interventions. It collects relevant data from the student’s history of remediation and infers the success rate of the various remedial teaching strategies which have been applied to a misconception or missing concept of a player during game play. The results of this investigation are then used to select a remedial teaching strategy for the misconception or missing concept under treatment.

If pre-programmed and student-led strategy selection have been unsuccessful the system explores the companion-led approach. For this purpose, the diagnosis process model accesses the student models of all the other players and checks for any players who may have experience with the concept which the student has been found to have a problem with. If such a knowledgeable player is found, he is provided with the source of the error and is asked to explain the erroneous concept to his colleague.

## 7. Internal and external evaluation of the intelligent gaming-simulation environment

The explicit purpose of internal evaluation is to assess whether development has produced a system architecture that yields the desired behaviour 28 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> The implicit purpose of internal evaluation is to assess the efficiency and effectiveness of the underlying development methodology. Internal evaluation compares the actual behaviour against the desired behaviour of the environment by examining the interaction within and between the components of the environment. This is achieved by seeking answers to three questions 28 : 1 what does the environment<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Ž .

The assessment is carried out by the environment developer and it can either be formative, i.e., during the process of development, or summative, after the completion of the development.

The purpose of external evaluation is to assess the educational impact of the environment on studentusers 28 . The external evaluation of an intelligent<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> gaming-simulation environment for learning consists of the assessment of learning achievement and learning affect. The assessment is carried out within an experiment with a supervisor usually the environ- Ž ment developer and a group of student-players of. different advancement stages who have been introduced to the game and have been prepared for the use of the environment.

## 7.1. Internal eÕaluation

The internal evaluation was summative and it was carried out by the environment developer after all development was completed. The results were collated from the questionnaire 28 that was used to <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> record the findings.

The domain knowledge model includes knowledge about management approaches and strategies in the form of rules, management techniques and examples of tasks that have to be carried out within the game. As a result students may be presented with alternative explanations of the same concept or referred to relevant rules or examples. The domain knowledge model also contains knowledge about common missing concepts and misconceptions. Each detected error is diagnosed and related to a misconception or missing concept which is then either remediated or filled. The explanations, exercises and examples the environment was able to offer provide a good source for understanding the concepts to be learned through.

The environment’s tutoring model uses several teaching strategies which use the domain or remedial knowledge in the domain knowledge model: cognitive apprenticeship, successive refinement, Socratic hinting, demonstration, practice, reteaching. The choice and subsequent use of strategy is adjusted according to the individual student and his student model profile.

The environment’s student knowledge model records knowledge about the needs and preferences of the student. Apart from a history of interaction and an overlay model, it records errors and all corresponding remedial interventions for each player. For a new player the advancement stage is automatically set to novice. Older players are usually asked for their choice of advancement stage at which they wish to interact and the student model is usually a good reference for the environment to agree or disagree with the student preferences. A teaching strategy is selected according to the advancement stage of the player and his needs and preferences. At the advanced level the environment avoids detailed explanations and the level of dependence on the contents of the student model is generally low. At the other end, novice students are given the additional opportunity to test and consolidate their knowledge in practice sessions through the firing of an appropriate strategy. The environment will switch between teaching strategies when the same mistake is repeated.

Although the environment maintains control at all times, the nature of interaction and the level of choice given to the student creates the feeling that the student is in absolute control.

Any remedial intervention is carried out when the system recognises a student’s need for it. The environment does not interrupt the flow of the game and it does intervene unless a step of play or at least a round of decisions has been completed. Of course, this does not allow for immediate remediation or help even though it may not affect at all the current round or any further decisions. On the one hand, delayed remediation may result in the student committing further errors as a result, but, on the other hand, it gives the student the opportunity to diagnose and correct a mistake himself. Which result will largely depend on the advancement stage of the student. At an early stage a novice is probably still unable to comprehend the interrelatedness of decisions within an entire step of play. Offering remediation to the student before an error has been committed is an issue of error avoidance rather than error correction. The environment does not provide remediation on request although help is available at all times. A major drawback of the environment is the lack of ability to link directly a misconception to a certain rule.

7.2. External eÕaluation: learning achieÕement and learning affect

The design of the external experiment is carried out under the guidance and observation of the environment developer. A reaction assessment tool, e.g., a questionnaire 28 , and setting for the experiment is<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> designed to cater for both the assessment of the learning achievement and the learning affect. In order to achieve this, the evaluator determines those rules or tasks of the game that underpin the gamingsimulation environment which should have been acquired by the student-player. The evaluator then constructs a game scenario and selects and allocates suitable roles to each student-player which will require the player to use the rules or tasks that should have been learned within the game scenario.

Such a situation can easily be created, since a game is divided into several playing cycles which generally involve the same tasks and application of rules under varying conditions. Therefore, the learning achievement can be assessed when the player is exposed to the same situation in succeeding playing rounds. If the player manages to apply the learned rule or do the task correctly within the scenario, learning achievement is considered to be successful.

The assessment of the learning affect of the gaming-simulation environment can be either carried out in combination with the assessment of the learning achievement within the experiment or by individual interviewing after the completion of a game. The questionnaire designed and used by Siemer and Angelides 28 caters for a combined assessment of the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> learning achievement and learning affect.

## 7.3. The external eÕaluation experiment

The evaluation of the prototype intelligent gaming-simulation environment was carried out with a group of 16 postgraduate students, reading for an MSc in Information Systems, under the supervision of the environment developer. All students had a management education background. The students were provided with a detailed description and the rules of the business simulation-game that underpin the environment in advance of the experiment. Prior to the experiment, the developer met with all the selected students to ensure that they understood the game and its rules and also to introduce them to the environment. The purpose of these preliminary meetings is to ensure that when the experiment begins the students spent their time playing the game and not learning the game or its rules or how to use the environment.

From the commencement of the experiment students interact with the environment in groups of either two or three players per game. Every group of student-players begin their interaction with the environment by starting business as the directors of a company which manufactures and supplies central heating boilers and radiators. There are two other simulated companies in the environment which manufacture and sell similar equipment and are competing in the market. The purpose of a game is to drive the competitors out of business.

The students’ company assumes four directors and each of the student-players takes over one of the roles. The Production Director has to make decisions on the amount of boilers to produce and has to determine the selling price. The Sales Director makes decisions on any market research or research and development to be undertaken, the number of sales persons to be recruited and which customers the sales staff should call on. The Financial Director has to master all calculations and is responsible for completing the Company’s accounts. The Managing Director is in overall charge; he is the final arbiter on all operating decisions. A student group does not comprise of more than three members in order that at least one of the roles is simulated. As directors the students have to solve financial, production or marketing problems or make arbitrations. The Company must run efficiently in order to pay wages, materials and services and to cover the costs of development of new production resources, e.g., a new factory. The players rotate between the roles at least every two games and after a minimum of eight games each student will have assumed all four roles. On average eight games take 12 hours to complete.

The students have the option to upgrade their advancement stage after they have completed their first game and thereby gain some understanding about business management strategies and techniques. After the completion of a minimum of two games and possibly a role the students have the Ž . opportunity to give their first feedback on the environment’s tutorial features by filling in part of the questionnaire 28 . After they repeat some tasks in <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> consecutive games, learning achievement is assessed from their responses in the questionnaire.

The delayed approach to treating errors is largely justified because the error may be detected and pointed out to the student. At the same time delayed remediation forces the student to rethink the overall step of action instead of creating local sub-optimal solutions. However, the environment could improve its timing of remedial interventions by considering the needs of the novice student who might be in demand of more immediate help, especially at an early stage when he is still unable to understand the interrelatedness of decisions within an entire step of play. Offering remediation to the student before an error has been committed is an issue of error avoidance rather than error correction. The use of preventive methods might be an issue for further research.

Also, the environment does not provide remediation on student requests. Although students were unable to trigger a remedial intervention themselves, the majority of students recognised that help could be acquired by making use of the facility to view the rules of the game at any stage within the game, thus allowing players to refer to regulations and operating examples before they made a decision. However, it was pointed out that a more context sensitive help facility would have been beneficial. The environment lacks the ability to refer the student directly to the rule that is relevant to the student’s problem in hand before the student has finalised a decision. This leaves a student to browse through the rules without further guidance. However, most of the time students were not aware of a problem before they committed a mistake and consequently had to rely on the environment to point out their mistakes to them.

Pro-active instruction is the obvious approach for a gaming-simulation environment where the players are expected to learn from the reaction of the environment and decisions have to be viewed within the context in which they are made. After remedial intervention the environment takes the student back into the game where is given the chance to correct his decision. Student-invoked remediation is neither required nor appropriate in order to avoid undesirable results due to insensible decisions. The student may reconfirm or supplement his knowledge from the rules to which permanent access is provided.

In order to assess whether the environment has led the student to understand the aspects of the game he should have learned, a game situation has to be designed that requires the student to use those aspects in a different context. The environment provides this game situation through its inherent quarterly structure, i.e., its recurring playing cycles. Every new quarter within a game provides the student with a game scenario in which the player has the opportunity to apply his acquired knowledge. The players’ student models record whether the player is able to apply his knowledge successfully. If the student can solve the problem, i.e., if he can apply the knowledge and skills in the scenario presented to him, the environment can be considered as having contributed towards the learning achievement of the student.

The results from the observation of the student’s performance during the experiment and the investigation of the student models afterwards correspond with the feedback from the players. Players were asked whether the environment provided useful tutoring to which the majority of players claimed that it provided useful tutoring most of the time 56% .Ž . Thirty-eight percent of the answers were sometimes. The remaining players claimed that tutoring was always helpful. More significantly, the answer to the question of whether players were able to apply their newly acquired knowledge successfully in a subsequent quarter were restricted to always 50% , most Ž . of the time 31% and sometimes 19% . Conse- Ž . Ž . quently, overall tutoring can largely be considered as successful.

However, feedback from some of the players indicated that tutoring could possibly benefit from the incorporation of more motivational issues. Al though entertaining and informative was a term to describe the general view of tutoring, some of the players displayed signs of decreasing interest and motivation at some stage in the game. The environment does not support the maintenance of motivation other than through student involvement. It does not take account of individual personal differences between players which may indicate how a particular player may be kept interested and motivated. An environment could adapt to such individual differences through actions, such as avoiding a certain task or concept temporarily or congratulating a student on a small success.

A further issue which arose from the overall external evaluation and which provides a suggestion for the possible refinement of the approach with which remediation is carried out is the aspect of remediation before an error occurs. The environment offers delayed remediation. However, students’ comments suggest the introduction of error prevention. Remediation may be carried out before the error can be committed in order to warn students about common errors. If students are made aware of errors in advance they might not make the mistake.

It takes three man-months to develop such an environment from first principles using the methodology and less than one man-month to set up the original simulation game. However, once the environment is complete it only takes one man-month to re-engineer the environment to play another game because the original environment then acts like a shell: the only component that is game-dependent is the domain knowledge model. The methodology is quite effective because with the exception of some minor drawbacks the resulting environment behaves as desired: an adaptive, individualised simulated environment where formal tuition, feedback and remediation is available where there is lack of conceptual ability. The methodology is efficient because both the processes with, and the learning goals at, which a student arrives within the resulting environment are now based on a formal definition of learning. As far as possible nothing is done ad hoc and students benefit from the presence of an invincible tutor whose goal is first to educate and not to assist in winning a game.

## 8. Concluding discussion

The results of the evaluation highlight why and when such an environment is desirable and what limitations to expect should one embark on it. From the gaming-simulation point of view, intelligent tutoring is desirable when it is perceived that there is lack of conceptual ability on behalf of the students, varying levels in knowledge and skills and the need for individual development. Intelligent tutoring system will provide adaptability, individualisation, remediation and feedback to the player through the use of different teaching strategies, adaptive tutoring, student knowledge and error modelling and advancement and informative explanations, exercises and examples. However, this results in a controlled environment that does not provide discovery learning by experience which is gaming-simulation’s underlying aim but guided learning by instruction. This is perhaps desirable among the novice but can be quite disruptive for the competent user who would like the game to unfold and perhaps learn from making a few mistakes.

From the intelligent tutoring point of view, gaming-simulation is desirable when there is a need for structured group learning through experimentation. Gaming-simulation will provide an entertaining and information-rich environment that supports group learning through experimentation. The structured environment and approach of gaming-simulation offers the opportunity of ultimately reaching an entertaining visual goal such as ‘winning a game’ as opposed to unentertaining educational goals hidden somewhere inside the tutoring model of an intelligent tutoring system.
