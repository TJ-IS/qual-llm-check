# A design of a DSS intermediary for electronic markets

Karl R. Lang <sup>a,)</sup>, Andrew B. Whinston <sup>b,1</sup>

## Abstract

This paper examines the decision support systems DSS area and concludes that the emergence of electronic markets, Ž . together with other advances in information and software technology, will have a strong impact on the DSS industry. Drawing on economic theory, we develop a new, more cost-effective and more flexible organizational design for the future DSS firm. q 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: DSS; Organizational design; Complementarity; Digital services; Electronic markets; Software components; Object technology

## 1. Introduction

As many voices in academia, industry, and other relevant circles of society have proclaimed a new era, the so-called information age, it may be a good point in time to review the decision support systems Ž . DSS field as one of the fundamental areas within information systems. In this paper, we will try to reinvent DSS and propose an organizational design for a DSS intermediary, a new kind of DSS company operating in the electronic commerce sector. Information, or knowledge, has now been widely recognized as the new, fourth production factor, after the classic ones—labor, land, and capital, and many companies across all industries are shifting their focus towards the procurement, management, production, and selling of information, either to accompany their traditional line of business or to offer new services in existing or in new markets. In its more than 20 years of existence, DSS has, of course, always been concerned with information and how to make effective use of it in decision-making scenarios 7 . An impressive stream of literature has been produced detailing elaborate methods of how to represent knowledge in models and how to gather new information and derive insights by solving models in order to help decision-makers in various business problem solving situations. More recently, since the advance of global communication infrastructures, which allow users fast and cheap access to an unprecedented amount of information provided by a countless number of web sites, digital libraries, and other sources, timeliness and relevance of information are becoming increasingly more important and, when possible, many companies opt for searching for wanted information rather than using sophisticated, time-consuming, and costly DSS methods by generating it themselves. Therefore, DSS research needs to develop new organizational designs that support the deployment of DSS products and services more rapidly, reliably, and cost-effectively in response to specific customer needs.

While no one can deny that DSS technologies can be and have been successfully implemented in practice in order to help solve business problems, it is also clear that the DSS industry must become more customer-oriented if it wants to play a significant role in the newly emerging electronic markets of the information age. Successful DSS applications have traditionally come out of long-term, large projects that typically require substantial funding, expertise, commitment, and strong support by the responsible management. Because of this high demand of resources, DSS applications have been largely relegated to big businesses which can afford their own DSS<sup>r</sup>OR divisions or can hire expensive external consulting firms. Without doubt, there will always be a market niche for large-scale, monolithic DSS solutions to complex problems of strategical importance, but if the DSS industry wants to grow, it must reach out to new customers and penetrate new markets.

In this paper, we present a new vision on DSS and develop a business model of a new generation of DSS service providers as virtual organizations. As we approach total connectivity via the Internet and the World-Wide Web and as electronic commerce becomes more and more an accepted mode of conducting business, it appears to be natural for DSS service companies to go on-line as well and take advantage of the new technological opportunities. Of course, all major consulting firms have already designed their own web sites offering their products and services. For example, the renowned consulting firm, Ernest & Young International, has developed a well-crafted on-line system called Ernie URLŽ http:<sup>rr</sup>www.eyi.com that has a web interface and . allows interested parties to browse through a collection of illustrative case demonstrations, including some DSS applications. However, such systems are merely an electronic marketing supplement promoting the existing line of products and services but they do not necessarily attract a new clientele. Considering all their teachings on business re-engineering, innovation, and adoption of technological changes, it is actually surprising how little the traditional consulting business itself has changed. One could argue that if the established consulting firms do not re-engineer themselves, they may soon face stiff competition from smaller, more flexible and more cost-effective on-line consultancies. In the future, business consulting will rely less on highly paid specialists—e.g., Ernest and Young, as the second largest management consulting practice worldwide, has nearly 10,500 consultants on its payroll—who spend a lot of time and resources on organizing repeated visits to client sites in order to carry out their analyses, and instead make efficient use of information network systems to gain faster and cheaper access to human expertise and automated intelligence in order to conduct their consulting business.

We propose a new and radically different approach to DSS, the virtual DSS company positioned as a low-cost alternative to traditional consulting firms targeting foremost small- and medium-sized businesses which have mostly been reluctant in seriously investing in DSS technologies. The organizational design of this new DSS business model is centered around the novel concept of DSS intermediaries who act in electronic markets, intermediating between customers, their needs, available DSS tools, and the actual producers of DSS technology. The intermediary provides core competence in DSS methods and applications, and an understanding of the business decision problems occurring at customer sites. However, the development and the production of DSS software and tools are left to third-party vendors who are subcontracted under specific terms, which will be discussed later. Interaction with customers is mainly done via an electronic storefront consisting of customer service and help desks, electronic shelves offering digital DSS products, sample cases, specially packaged samples of business solutions, education materials, literature and so on.

In order to achieve our main goals, namely high variety of products, or at least a certain range of a product line, and services, high-quality at everyday low prices, rapid responsiveness, reliable service, and close relationships with customers and suppliers, we are facing several challenges which require us to look at the virtual DSS organization from multiple perspectives. Thus, we will not only discuss the technical aspects of the design and the implementation of a system architecture for a virtual DSS company but also talk about relevant business and management issues as they arise, e.g., in marketing and manufacturing such as pricing issues and production issues. We will argue that the success of an organizational design or re-design depends stronglyŽ . on the choice of the organizational design and goal variables, and emphasize the importance of selecting a set of variables which is complementary to each other, i.e., variables that positively influence each other.

Middlemen have already appeared in electronic markets but the cases that have been reported in the literature deal mainly with physical products or traditional services. For example, Lee 17 describes Auc-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> net, an electronic auctioning system for used cars that has been successfully launched in Japan. Kalakota and Whinston 14 overview newly emerg- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ing electronic intermediaries in the financial service sector. In this paper, on the other hand, we propose DSS intermediaries who will not only offer a variety of existing products and services but who will also offer innovative, virtual DSS products which will be electronically assembled in a just-in-time JIT fash-Ž . ion according to specifications derived from particular customer requirements, and thus, create a new type of business in the DSS industry and in electronic commerce. Moreover, and in addition to the known literature which is mostly descriptive in a sense that it merely describes new, successful businesses as they have appeared in the electronic markets and changed the way business is done, we will also provide a theoretical foundation for the emergence of intermediaries in electronic commerce by tying results from recent research in economics into our new DSS framework.

Now, let us outline the structure of the remainder of the paper. Section 2 proposes a novel conceptual model for DSS businesses as virtual organizations. We will introduce the quintessential concept of the DSS intermediary, discuss an electronic storefront design as the DSS organization’s front-end to its customers, discuss the relationships with DSS suppliers at the back-end, explain the role of CORBA as the enabling technology, and discuss relevant marketing and manufacturing issues. Next, in Section 3, we will take our conceptual DSS model and look at it from an economics perspective. We will examine the economics literature and relate recent economics research results concerning the emergence of intermediaries, procurement systems, and contractual issues to our DSS framework in order to furnish it with an economics foundation. We will then review the main concepts of CORBA, a distributed, client– server computing technology whereon our implementation of the DSS intermediary model will be based. A first prototype system of a virtual DSS company will be described, along with an illustrative example. Finally, in Section 4, we will give a conclusion and a brief discussion of possible future research directions.

## 2. Conceptual model of the DSS intermediary

This section describes the economic and technological basis for a DSS intermediary in the context of electronic commerce. End-users or customers of the intermediary have specific decision problems to solve, while decision tool developers are oriented towards selling their tools. Typically, the decision problems will require a combination of tools often provided by different vendors. Since each vendor has developed and maintained its implementation independently on its own servers, we have both a technological challenge in managing the execution in this distributed and heterogeneous environment, and a marketing challenge of packaging the various solution methods, so that they address typical decision problems of the end-users.

Thus, the end-user view will be of virtual objects that are oriented towards providing a specific business solution. Software developers are experts in implementing specific tools such as in optimization, scheduling, statistical analysis and simulation. The DSS intermediary provides a bridge between these two groups and attempts to make a profit based on operations, since the intermediary will select ‘best-ofbreed’ software technology where it can be found and likewise look at the geographically distributed, potentially world-wide, base of customers. In order to establish an intermediary with geographically dispersed suppliers and customers, we propose the use of CORBA 20 as the enabling technology founda-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tion.

The rapid development and growth of the electronic commerce sector over the recent years has been accompanied by at least as many failures as successes. Many companies moved into this new line of business without having a clear understanding of how to use the new technology to advance their existing business or how to invent profitable, new kinds of businesses. Therefore, before implementing DSS technologies for electronic markets, we believe that it is imperative to develop a sound business model for an organization that offers such services first. Fig. 1 depicts an organizational design model for an electronic DSS company which emphasizes business reasoning over technicalities. Our model draws on complementarity theory, a classic theory developed in economics 21 , which has recently<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> been applied to business process re-engineering 4 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

The main idea behind complementarity theory is to recognize the basic relationships among organizational goal variables. We distinguish three types of such relationships, namely a independent, b con-Ž . Ž . flicting, and c complementary relationships. AŽ . complementary relationship among a set of variables means that moving one of the variables in the desired direction, and thus, increasing its derived business value, will inherently increase the values of the other complementary variables as a by-product. Many business re-engineering projects have failed mainly because they re-designed conflicting organizational processes and variables. For example, cutting costs and improving service quality are, in most contexts, desirable organizational goals and are directly linked to organizational outcome and profitability, yet trying to simultaneously cut costs and improve service quality usually fails due to their conflicting relationship. It has been shown that a successful organizational design must recognize these relationships among organizational variables, and that focusing the organizational re design on variables exhibiting aŽ . complementary relationship will result in the best business value 4,8 . Of course, identifying the sub-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> set of complementary variables among the set of the organizational variables of interest can very well be a rather difficult task, and one has to be extremely careful not to mistakenly include a variable that may later turn out to be in conflict with one or more of the others.

It appears to be useful to organize the organizational variables of interest into a multi-level, hierarchical structure. Hence, the organizational model for our proposed DSS company in Fig. 1 shows three levels of variables. At the top, we have profitability as a financial measure of organizational performance, the ultimate goal variable whose value we would like to see increasing but whose value is beyond direct and immediate control. However, at the second level of the hierarchy, we have some intermediate variables, namely product and service quality and size and value of the customer base. In a more detailed model, customer base could be further refined and linked to other organizational variables like number of customers, customer satisfaction, market share, and market growth. Intermediate variables are not financial measures themselves but variables which can be seen as driving variables determining or influencing profitability. Those directŽ . influences like better quality or larger customer base will result in higher profitability and are indicated by solid arrows in the diagram. At the same time, we have also some indirect influences among variables expressing complementary relationships, indicated by the dashed arrows. Specifically, we stipulate a complementary relationship between quality and customer base, meaning that better quality will increase our customer base and, assuming we are operating in a competitive market, a larger customer base will Ž . eventually improve the reputation of the quality of the products and services. The latter may occur through word of mouth, higher product or name recognition, or because buyers may perceive the quality as better simply because ‘everyone else’ seems to do business with that company. At the bottom level, we have a set of design variables which we can control directly and thereby influence the higher-level variables which will steer the organization eventually in the anticipated direction, achieving our organizational goals, at least to a certain degree.

## 2.1. The DSS intermediary

The idea of establishing electronic brokerages on the internet that are dealing with decision support technologies was first proposed by Kalakota et al. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 15 . However, in that paper, the authors limited their discussion to domain-specific problem environments, and presented a system design of an open DSS that offers customized OR tools for cash management and foreign currency exchange applications. In other related work, Bhargava et al. 5,6 report on their<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> DecisionNet project in which they have implemented a distributed software system with a web browser interface that allows users to access and to run a small selection of standard OR models coded in modeling languages like GAMS or AMPL. Rather than merely implementing traditional OR software in a distributed, internet<sup>r</sup>web-based computing environment, we would like to go a step further in this paper, and propose a comprehensive and novel DSS framework that includes a fairly complete business model based on intermediation, a discussion of the enabling technologies, and an economic foundation, positioning DSS as a new type of electronic commerce business.

Fig. 2 shows the function of the middleman placed at the center of the conceptual model of the DSS intermediary. Intermediation between consumers and producers has been a hallmark of trade since the emergence of barter economies, and has recently appeared in cyberspace business as well, but has so far mostly been limited to applications such as, e.g., the brokerage of traditional financial services 9 ,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> education and learning 12 , and to some extent, to<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> electronic publication 11 . However, using the Inter- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> net as the medium for both the production and the delivery of DSS technologies provides a new opportunity for the DSS industry to attract a new clientele in a world-wide market, and to keep up with the ever increasing pace of the technological changes and the volatile business environments. The DSS industry has to adjust to the globalization and competitiveness if it wants to play a significant role in the dynamic markets which will be more and more ruled by electronic commerce activities in the future.

The main reasoning behind the introduction of intermediaries in the DSS market is the currently highly confusing state in which numerous vendors offer arrays of products promising huge benefits to companies in various business situations. In practice, however, the successful implementation of DSS technologies is frequently hampered by the lack of expertise and manpower at the customer site with respect to the understanding of DSS methods, the selection of an appropriate product, and the maintaining and upgrading of an installed system as the business environment or the computing platforms change over time. Especially small- and mediumsized companies with limited financial resources are usually very reluctant in investing in DSS systems. Thus, we propose a DSS model in which DSS and computing expertise are concentrated on the virtual DSS company, embodied by a relatively small staff of human experts who are assisted by software agents performing the more routine and tedious tasks. As an intermediary, the virtual DSS company would outsource the actual production of the DSS tools, i.e., the costly and time-consuming programming work would be done by external suppliers, and focus rather on the selection, the integration, and the application of available DSS products. Value-adding functions of the intermediary include the provision of a better market transparency to the customers

## 2.2. The electronic storefront design

Now we discuss some issues related to the concept and the design of an electronic storefront for our virtual DSS company. Fig. 3a presents the functionality of the storefront at a conceptual level. At least for standard products, we would advertise high-quality products at a low fee. A virtual DSS company can be profitable despite charging low prices and lower margins because of the efficient access to a huge, world-wide market which should result in a much higher sales volume, and because of the low variable cost of producing digital DSS products through electronic replications. Thus, the usual method of requesting necessary credit card information alone is not sufficient for a virtual DSS company. Additionally, we will have to set up and maintain elaborate customer accounts allowing different kinds of electronic payments, including Ž . micropayments, lump sum payments, regular deductions, installment plans and possibly the creation of new forms of digital cash. At the same time, and especially for high value sales, security and confidentiality must be absolutely guaranteed. ## 2.3. Suppliers of DSS components

In order to succeed in today’s global and competitive markets, it is crucial that a virtual DSS company can offer high-quality products and variety in a certain range of products. As an intermediary, it is therefore essential to establish a carefully and tightly knit network of relationships with vendors who can supply DSS components that fulfill certain standards. To enable the creation of virtual DSS products through the integration of independent, reusable DSS components, possibly supplied from different vendors, it is important to commit the suppliers to supplying product in accordance to strict specifications, especially to ensure interoperability. It is again the CORBA technology that enables the development of compatible software products by wrapping the software with a domain and application-independent layer that defines an interface that is compatible with other CORBA-compliant objects, allowing objects to communicate and interoperate via date exchange and service requests. Moreover, since CORBA has been designed for the development of distributed, network applications, it also provides rapid access to the required DSS objects via the internet, and thus, offers the possibility of implementing an extremely efficient supply chain management system. Since timeliness is a decisive factor in the organizational design of the virtual DSS company, there is little or no time to explicitly evaluate or check the quality of the delivered software components, especially because testing software is generally a costly and time-consuming task.

Because of these tight requirements, it is important to take particular care in designing the business relationships between the DSS company and its suppliers, and hence, the contractual issues pertaining to product procurement will have considerable significance. We suggest the adoption of contracts that, on the one hand, clearly spell out what the DSS company expects from a particular supplier, namely high quality, CORBA compliance, and fast delivery, but, on the other hand, leave out certain details which would only obscure the operationality of the business agreement, e.g., exact definitions of product quality, terms of accepting or rejecting a delivered product. As a compensation for those tough requirements, the DSS company could be willing to pay a premium price and to guarantee a long-term trading relationship as long as the supplier’s performance is satisfactory. Again, the precise definition of ‘long-term’ and ‘satisfactory performance’ would remain unspecified or incompletely specified in the contract. Assuming that the two partners reach a common understanding of the implications of this kind of contract, they can both benefit. The supplier’s incentive to deliver quality would be guaranteed long-term sales at premium prices and the threat of getting cut-off in case the quality drops. The incentive of the DSS company would be assurance of receiving products according to the needed specifications, such that they can be used as building block and integrated with other components into higher-level, composite DSS products, and the option of terminating the relationship with a supplier at any time in case the quality stops meeting the required standards. A complementary feature to this approach is a careful selection of a rather small set of suppliers from the pool of competing vendors in the DSS market. In Section 3, we will return to the issue of procurement and contracting and extend this discussion with a motivation and a foundation based on recent results from the economics literature.

## 2.4. Marketing issues

From the discussion of our proposed business model of DSS intermediaries, as shown in Fig. 1, it should be clear that running a DSS company involves more than just developing good DSS software. The whole set of the classic business issues is relevant to the design of successful DSS organizations, although the DSS literature tends to ignore or neglect issues that are rooted in other fields. Without claiming completeness, we would like to use this section to raise some marketing issues which we see as particularly relevant in the context of our organizational design model for DSS intermediaries, and at least present a brief discussion thereof which could be the basis for subsequent research efforts. In general, marketing is concerned with the positioning of businesses in their markets, in our case, DSS companies in electronic markets, and takes into consideration organizational goals e.g., market share andŽ profits and customer needs e.g., solving business . Ž decision problems ..

In a supply-oriented market economy, it is typically the industry that competes for customers in order to sell them their products. When developing a marketing strategy for a DSS company, we can manipulate variables like pricing, quality, and variety in order to increase competitiveness. But in a market like DSS, which trades in highly sophisticated, technical products whose proper usage requires substantial investments and a high level of domain-specific expertise, sellers who can raise awareness and knowledge about their products and who can best understand the problems of specific customers can gain a significant competitive advantage. Thus, we suggest a marketing strategy that is both product-oriented and customer-oriented.

On the product side, we consider the variables pricing, Õariety, and quality which we see again as complementary variables, emphasizing low prices, some variety and high quality. Recent research in marketing and in economics has shown that a strategy of combining one-stop shopping offering ‘high product variety’ at ‘everyday low prices’ coupled with an appropriate advertising concept can compete successfully against specialized stores, despite lower profit margins, less service, and more difficult logistical problems 3,19,22 . On the customer side, we<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> think that it will be necessary for DSS businesses to care more about their customer base, conduct some market research studies with regard to DSS markets, learn more about customer needs, develop customer profiles, keep track about the performance of sold products, record information about their usage at the customer sites, compile and analyze customer feedback, study how the products are being used and when, why, and where products caused some dissatisfaction. All these activities will help gain trust, reputation and hence, build long-term customer relationships. A deeper and more rigorous discussion of these marketing-related issues in the context of the DSS industry is beyond the scope of this paper, but we hope that it is sufficient to raise awareness and interest in these important questions and stimulate research in this direction.

## 2.5. Manufacturing issues

We conclude Section 2 with a brief discussion of issues related to the manufacturing process in the context of our framework of DSS intermediaries. The main question here is how can we design and organize production processes that assure product quality, timeliness, and low cost? Once more, we see CORBA as the technological answer to these challenges because it can be used as a manufacturing technology that 1 enables low operating cost allow- Ž . ing mass production of certain DSS products, 2Ž . enables low distribution cost allowing fast product deliveries, and 3 perhaps, most importantly, intro-Ž . duces a new level of flexibility that enables rapid production of custom-tailored DSS products in response to specific customer requests. Flexibility, i.e., the ability to adjust the production processes to changing operating environments, is increasingly seen as a key success factor in the manufacturing industry 1 , and we conjecture that it will be simi-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> larly important in the information industry and in electronic commerce.

CORBA provides an ideal platform for creating flexible, cost-effective, and efficient logistic channels. For example, it gives the customer the option of either running a DSS model at a supplier site, thus taking advantage of possibly better computational environments, and sending the results back to the user, or to download a DSS software and run the model locally at the customer site. Naturally, the two options may imply different service charges and different accuracies of the model output. However, CORBA’s capabilities of the actual integration of several DSS components into a composite DSS product are limited to its provision of an application-independent interface of CORBA objects. The selection of the relevant DSS objects that can be combined in order to create a higher-level DSS product that is suitable to solve a given customer problem, and the actual software integration is an entirely different matter. Software integration and model integration have been studied separately and are the focus of Ref. 2 , which presents an overview of the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> topic and proposes a framework for model integration in an enterprise-wide environment.

## 3.1. The role of intermediation

As the electronic commerce is maturing, many kinds of intermediaries will evolve 9 . For example,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Cable News Network as an information intermediary collects, combines, and sells information products, leveraging the needs of information producers and consumers. More importantly, CNN also acts as a quality guarantor assuring a certain quality standard.

What do we actually mean by an Intermediary? An intermediary is a middleman who facilitates transactions between potential traders. An intermediary can facilitate transactions in four ways:

1. match buyers with sellers;

2. buy goods from sellers and sell them to buyers;

3. buy goods and sell them after modifications; and

4. sell transaction-related information only.

The first case is a simple brokerage connecting a buyer with a seller. A commodity is more marketable if there are more buyers and sellers. However, when the size of a market increases as in the case of the Internet, it becomes more difficult to match all bid and ask prices. The need for better communication between sellers and buyers is the rationale behind organizing an exchange market at a central location such as commodity trading markets or stock exchanges.

In the second case, the intermediary becomes an owner–seller instead of a simple broker. For example, a consignment store is a broker of the first type while most retail stores are sellers themselves, being the second type of intermediary. As compared to the first type of brokerage where sellers need to interact with buyers, albeit via an intermediary, to negotiate a sale or contract, original sellers in a market withŽ . the second type of intermediary need not be concerned with ultimate buyers of their goods. The intermediaries here are market makers who earn profits out of the spread between bid and ask prices in the market.

The third case is where an intermediary becomes a producer who adds value by engaging in market activities other than simply brokeraging or distributing goods. A simple example is a retailer who offers a packaged good combining products from different producers. This function of an intermediary involves some type of transformation in the characteristics of the product.

The fourth function of an intermediary is to sell only market information such as company performance data, macroeconomic indicators, stock quotes, and so on, which facilitates transactions. Financial newspaper and newsletter, financial cable networks, and on-line business information services are specialized information sellers. These intermediaries are in fact producers of information and don’t need to directly deal with financial assets.

The DSS intermediaries will primarily resemble the first and third case. Related work in economics <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 18 has shown, using a game-theoretic model, that middlemen or intermediaries will emerge endogenously in economic markets exhibiting asymmetric information among buyers and sellers. They will operate more efficiently in markets with price ranges and tend to trade-in high quality products, all these being characteristics that can be found in the DSS market. Therefore, we argue that DSS intermediaries are a concept with a sound economic foundation and fruitful direction for further research in DSS.

## 3.2. Procurement systems

Let us consider an electronic market consisting of two types of agents: DSS intermediaries who act as a buyers of DSS products and components DSS partsŽ . and DSS suppliers. We are interested in the question of how to design the trading relationships between an intermediary and its suppliers. We look at two basic procurement systems that have been studied extensively in the economics literature: the competitive-bidding system and the JIT purchasing system, and examine them upon their suitability for a virtual DSS company in terms of effectiveness and quality assurance. Both approaches have been developed and first used in the manufacturing industry but have meanwhile spread across many industries. Traditionally, the buyer is a manufacturer of physical devices or pieces of equipment and machinery which are assembled from parts and components whose production is outsourced and subcontracted from thirdparty suppliers. In our context, the buyer is a DSS intermediary who produces virtual DSS products that are made up of digital DSS components which are obtained from subcontracted DSS vendors.

The first subcontracting system relevant in our context, which is also known as American-style procurement because of its origins in the American automobile industry, is characterised by the following features. Each order, typically a reasonably large one in volume or in value, requires a new contract between buyer and supplier. The buyer releases a call for bids which describes the specifications of the needed product and lists terms for delivery, product inspection and payment methods. The supplier is selected after a round of competitive bidding among interested parties. Typically, the lowest bidder is awarded the contract, assuming that the subcontractor will deliver quality according to the agreed-on specifications. This decision is usually made independently for each order, i.e., previous experience with the bidders has little influence on the current deal. The main incentive for the supplier to indeed produce the promised quality is the buyer’s right to inspect the delivery, and to withhold or to reject payment in case of unsatisfactory quality or product defects. Once a shipment is accepted but later on turns out to be bad nevertheless, there is little the buyer can legally do about it. Therefore, product inspection is the essential means in competitive-bidding procurement systems to gain quality assurance. Inspection in traditional manufacturing normally involves physical examination of lot samples following some statistical procedure. In our electronic DSS environment, however, we are concerned with digital rather than physical products, where product inspection means testing and benchmarking DSS software components before deciding on acceptance or rejection. In either case, inspection of digital products can be pretty difficult, expensive and time-consuming and could thus add substantial secondary cost to the whole transaction.

The JIT purchasing system is the other procurement system of interest. It is also known as Japanese-style procurement since it was originally developed in the Japanese manufacturing industry. The Japanese system is fundamentally different from its American counterpart. It emphasizes more frequent orders, usually of smaller size, that are repeatedly ordered from the same supplier over long periods of time without competitive bidding or formal product inspections. As one of the consequences, the chosen subcontractor may not be the cheapest supplier available. If a supplier performs poorly and delivers low quality, the buyer will, of course, eventually recognize this, even without doing its own inspections. In such a case, the buyer may decide to terminate the business relationship and switch suppliers for future business deals. It is this threat of being cut-off as a supplier that provides the main incentive for supplying high quality in the Japanese system.

The two procurement systems outlined above were obviously developed within the manufacturing industry but they, nevertheless, bear characteristics that are well-suited for businesses operating in markets trading in digital rather than physical products, such as, e.g., DSS intermediaries and virtual DSS companies. Actually, one can argue that the software industry as a whole is undergoing changes in a process of maturing that are remarkably similar to those previously observed in the manufacturing industry. Just as the manufacturing industry moved from a supplieroriented market to a customer-oriented market, we can see the same tendencies in the software industry. In the early days, a company could rise with only a single product, or a small set of products, and build a reputation upon that without having to worry much about specific customer requirements or how the customer can put the product to effective use. Advances in production technology at the technical as well as at the managerial level and, more importantly, competitive processes have both enabled and required companies to heed individual customer requirements, to offer a greater diversity of products at a low cost, and to offer pre- and post-sales services assisting in the use of their products. And just as it is not enough to offer, e.g., a well-made, perhaps even superior, computer in order to succeed in an ever increasingly competitive computer hardware market, it is no longer possible to build a software company on just one or a few pieces of software products alone. More and more, clients demand customized and comprehensively packaged solutions to business problems rather than products that may help just somehow in solving some problems.

Changes in the manufacturing processes, introducing a higher degree of flexibility, have enabled companies to produce custom-tailored products in response to specific customer requirements, quickly, reliably, and in high quality. A key element in achieving more flexibility in the production technology is the possibility of decomposing a complex device into a set of independent modules that interact with each other through clearly defined interfaces. This makes it possible to outsource the production of many of the needed components, and to focus more on the production design of the assembling of customized endproducts from building blocks that are procured from external suppliers. Applying the same line of reasoning to the production of digital DSS products, we conclude that in the near future, a successful DSS company, too, will have to pay more attention to the customers’ woes and concerns. Just selling a particular DSS tool, be it a mathematical optimization software, a simulation package, or a statistical analysis system, will become less and less significant and will be outdone by sales of comprehensive, case-specific, custom-tailored business problem solutions. DSS software will be just a part of a whole service package put together in a direct response to a particular customer need.

In order to achieve short response times to customers and to be able to offer variety and quality products at a low fees, DSS intermediaries will need to adopt more flexible production strategies. Since the DSS intermediary is designed as a virtual organization mainly responsible for the design of customized solution packages built from externally supplied DSS components, it is crucial that procurement of the digital parts from the back-end suppliers is knit tightly and efficiently into the assembling and manufacturing processes. A recent study by Taylor and Wiggins 23 compared the American-style and<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Japanese-style procurement systems, using an analytical economic model, and come to two interesting conclusions. Firstly, both systems were found to be effective in general. But, secondly, under certain circumstances, which can be described by a set of complementary characteristics, one actually outperforms the other. Taylor and Wiggins identified two distinct sets of complementarities that determine the effectiveness of the two procurement systems in a particular organizational setting. On the one hand, a large pool of potential suppliers, frequent switching between suppliers, large orders, and elaborate inspections of product shipments, constitutes a set of complementary activities that suit the American-style, competitive-bidding system. On the other hand, and to us more interestingly long-term trading relationships with a relatively small number of suppliers, small orders, and the absence of formal inspections are the major contingent factors that make the Japanese-style procurement system work well. The latter scenario applies remarkably well to our conceptual model of DSS intermediaries. Therefore, we argue that modeling the trading relationships between the DSS intermediary and its suppliers according to the Japanese model will add a strong economic motivation to the organizational design of virtual DSS companies.

## 3.3. Contractual issues

Once two business partners have reached a basic agreement on how to design their trading relationship, it is necessary to specify the terms of the agreement in the form of a legally binding contract. Naively thinking, one would like to write complete contracts, i.e., contracts that explicitly designate all the rights and obligations for the involved parties under all present and future constellations imaginable. While such complete contracts would eliminate much of unwanted uncertainty, it would also impose overly restrictive bindings to both parties which could prove troublesome in the event of unanticipated changes in the business environment, a situation that is actually the norm rather than the exception in today’s dynamic markets. A good example of an unforeseen change is the emergence of electronic commerce as a totally new way of doing business which has raised numerous legal questions, many of which still unresolved. Hence, in volatile business environments like the newly emerging electronic markets, it can actually be preferable to write incomplete contracts, using ambiguous formulations or leaving certain aspects open. Adopting the Japanese-style procurement system, e.g., the contracts with the supplier are going to be essential in order to assure quality service while maintaining flexibility at both ends. The key to successful contracting is a mutual understanding of how the involved parties are doing business, and when this is achieved, they often abandon contractual completeness in favor of incompletely written contracts <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 10,13 .

Traditionally, the economics literature used to give two main explanations for this behavior of contracting parties concerning their preference of contractual incompleteness over contractual completeness which, albeit its counter-intuitiveness, has been observed repeatedly in many situations. One relates to transaction cost and says that neglecting unlikely scenarios and writing-simplified or incom-Ž plete contracts is plainly more practicable. The other. view refers to bounded rationality and simply assumes that contractual gaps are mostly unintentional. More recently, however, a theoretical economic model based on game-theory has been proposed 24 , which is used in a comparative study of contractual completeness vs. contractual incompleteness. The results support the earlier empirical findings, i.e., it does provide a theoretical foundation why economic agents prefer to write incomplete contracts.

In our context, the DSS intermediary would like to sustain a long-term relationship with its business partners and order repetitiously from the same suppliers, but would hesitate to prescribe a time horizon in a contract which would bind it to a particular supplier possibly for years. Preferably, we would want to write a contract that leaves the time aspect unspecified, with the common understanding that orders will be renewed as long as the supplied quality is satisfactory but that the contract can be terminated in case the supplier’s performs declines. Such a form of incompleteness may provide a better balance between opportunities repeated purchasing ,Ž . threats and severance of a supplier and ultimatelyŽ . serve both parties better than a completely specified contract. Assuming that the DSS intermediary as the buyer has the bargaining power, one would also include additional formulations into the contract which will force the vendors to supply their components according to some incompletely specified Ž . standard. In our case, this may mean that the suppliers have to accept a dictum to deliver their products exclusively as CORBA-compliant DSS objects.

## 4. Conclusion

In this paper, we have examined the state of DSS and conclude that the emergence of electronic markets, together with other advances in information and software technology, will have a strong impact on the DSS industry. Drawing on economic theory, we have developed a new, more cost-effective and more flexible organizational design for the future DSS firm. We discussed how to design distributed DSS in client<sup>r</sup>server environments and presented the economic implication of distributed DSS objects. As electronic commerce grows and various kinds of networked intermediaries evolve, DSS intermediaries will become a common type of on-line business consulting. DSS intermediaries will provide domain-specific decision support services to endusers and they will charge their services on a per-use basis.
