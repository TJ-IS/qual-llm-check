# The SEC-system reuse: support for scheduling system development

## Abstract

The development process of computer systems for task support of planning and scheduling is not a unique activity that must be performed from scratch each time. Reuse of previously created conceptual and technical products is possible and recommendable. We claim that adequate computer support starts with a cognitive task analysis which reveals general asŽ . well as unique aspects. The general aspects can be reused. However, a conceptual framework is necessary that separates general from unique aspects in the task domain and task performance. We propose the Scheduling Expertise Concept SECŽ . as such a framework for planning and scheduling. The framework is domain independent and takes into account organisational and task performance characteristics. This generic framework is implemented in a computer system, the SEC-system. The SEC-models and SEC-system enable faster development processes in the planning and scheduling domain. q 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

Reuse of conceptual and technical information that is obtained during the development process of software is gaining increased attention 55 . Gener-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ally speaking, reuse can be applied, to some extent, to everything that is structured. When this principle is used in practice, however, all kinds of restrictions arise. Programming languages must be the same or at least comparable, functional and technical specifications must utilise a common framework and users must execute comparable tasks. Despite these constraints, reuse in the development process is attempted to an increasing degree. This article discusses how we propose applying reuse in the development of decision support systems for planning and scheduling.

In Sections 2 and 3, we outline the requirements with which we think scheduling support and reuse support should comply. Section 2 discusses the application domain: planning and scheduling. Because of the diversity in planning aspects, we advocate that, in practice, planning and scheduling support demands an interdisciplinary approach. Section 3 deals with starting points that we think are essential in effective decision support in the case of planning, such as the bottom–up principle, the user-control principle and the ‘world-making’ principle. In Section 4, we describe several approaches that are engaged in modelling, scheduling system development and reuse. This will provide some requirements and guidelines with which to start. We conclude, however, that none of the discussed approaches complies completely with the requirements that are discussed in Sections 2 and 3.

In Sections 5 and 6, we extensively discuss the overall framework of the Scheduling Expertise Concept SEC . Section 5 describes the models that we Ž . use and provides an example. In Section 6 we go into the details of the partial implementation of the concept in the SEC-system as a reuse support system. In Section 7, we draw some conclusions.

The general idea behind SEC and the SEC-system is the following. A scheduling support system is based on an extensive analysis of the task context and task performance of human planners. Such an analysis reveals general and unique aspects. The general aspects are eligible for reuse. For this, however, we need a generic framework, or taxonomy Ž . SEC , that structures or categorises the general aspects in the analysis of the domain, the task execution and the development of the resulting system. In this way analyses and designs can be stored and retrieved. This speeds up the analyses of new situations and the development of new scheduling support systems.

## 2. The domain of reuse: planning and scheduling

Planning and scheduling are subjects of interest in various scientific areas and hence it is difficult to find or formulate unequivocal demarcations of the problem domain 26 . First, it is important to note <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> that we will use ‘planning’ and ‘scheduling’ to denote problems that human planners in an organisational context face. Several dimensions have been suggested to typify the differences between planning and scheduling. Examples are that the distinction is related to the level in the organisation planning:Ž strategic; scheduling: operational , to the time per- . spective planning: long term; scheduling: short Ž term , or to the entities involved planning: aggregate . Ž entities such as machine groups; scheduling: individ ual entities such as machines . We would like to. argue that the determinative distinction between planning and scheduling is related to the quantitative aspects or properties of the objects involved. The basic idea behind this distinction is that the hierarchy in the levels of measurement, including the permissible mathematical operations, is decisive in whether we are talking about planning or scheduling. Planning is used if we are talking about objects at a nominal or ordinal level of measurement. However, if we are talking about the properties of plannedŽ . objects, such as lorries, production machines or staff members, at an interval or ratio level of measurement the term scheduling is used. This reduces the difference between planning and scheduling to a clearly decidable issue.

In this section we review several scientific areas that engage in planning and scheduling. A complicating aspect of planning is that it is a research issue in many diverse fields. This review will make it clear that a method for scheduling or planning support development needs an integrated approach that incorporates several aspects of planning. The SECmodel that will be discussed in Section 5 combines the requirements for planning and scheduling sup-Ž . port that are imposed by the various fields of research such as cognitive psychology, management science, production management, operations research and artificial intelligence.

CognitiÕe psychology. From a psychological point of view, planning is a mental activity besides problem solving and perceptual and locomotive skills <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 11,14 . People establish an order or arrangement of how to solve problems beforehand. The degree of planning depends on the complexity of the problem and the kind of task. Mental arithmetic hardly requires planning, whereas a game of chess requires much planning. In this view, planning is part of a larger task. Planning, however, can also be a task in itself. In this perspective, planning is a synthetic task, since the outcome is a design 13 . Important<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> sub-tasks of planning are, for example, clerical work, counting, negotiating and problem solving 31 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Clearly, planning as a task like other tasks such as Ž chess or mental arithmetic incorporates planning as . a mental activity. The most apparent distinctive determinant between planning as a task and planning as a mental activity is the question of who will execute the plan. Somebody who mentally creates a plan Ž . e.g., in a game of chess will use this plan himself to perform the larger task. On the other hand, the outcome of a planning task is often used by others in their own task context, e.g., an employee works according to the schedule that is made by the planner.

Organisational theory. Planning in organisational theory deals with the allocation of resources, such as money to investment projects, products to machines or staff members to shifts. Often, the planning in an organisation is performed by more than one planner. In these cases, the organisation of the planners is important, i.e., the division of tasks and responsibilities between planners. Management theories often divide planning into several layers in order to cope with the complexity of planning 32 . Within those<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> layers, distinctions can be made in time horizons, time preciseness, scarcity of objects, geographical preciseness, etc. Anthony 1 , for example, distin- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> guishes strategic planning, tactical control, and operational control. Strategic planning has a long time horizon with abstract decisions, tactical control deals with medium term decisions, and operational control is about short term concrete decisions.

Production planning and control. Production planning and control frameworks not only have separate planning outcomes for different time horizons but also for different entities such as machines, products, machine operators and stocks 19 . In pro-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> duction planning and control, planning is conceived as resource allocation at several levels, which is done with the help of techniques such as MRP1 or MRP2. In an overview of scheduling packages for the production domain 5 , two very important conclusions<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> are drawn. First, software for production control is more than just supply management and materials requirement calculation. It is also more than a system that controls the complete logistic chain. Organisations require systems that are able to monitor, analyse, diagnose and control the complete flow of materials and services, including human resources information, after-sales services, marketing and production and procedures management. Second, many software companies are faced with the problem of how to offer the overall required functionality: should it be offered as one integrated software package or should it be presented as a box of loosely or tightlyŽ coupled building blocks? This is in contrast with the. former’s advantage of data consistency and no overlap in applications with the more clear-cut and specified applications of the latter. On the other hand, the disadvantage of the former is its more complex systems compared with the latter’s redundant applications. Berenschot and Logiplan 5 see a subdivi-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> sion along two lines. The first concerns larger systems that are more general and have a broad functionality, whereas the second concerns smaller systems that offer ‘deep’ functionality. It is not yet ascertainable which approach will be the most competitive for the near future. It is, however, of importance for the day-to-day planning or scheduling task that is part of the production control function and can be supported by a stand-alone system or within a production control system.

Operations research OR( ). OR uses a mathematical approach to solve well-defined scheduling problems such as job shop scheduling or the Travelling Salesman Problem 65 . Often, scheduling problems <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> are too complex to solve with optimising methods such as linear programming or dynamic programming. Heuristics can be applied to find sub-optimal solutions for such complex problems. OR uses abstraction of objects to make an algorithm more generally applicable. When a satisfactory algorithm is developed for a well-defined class of problems, other problem types can be translated to this class.

Artificial intelligence AI( ). Within AI, which is closely connected with cognitive science, two fields can clearly be distinguished: AI-planning and AIscheduling. AI-planning deals with intelligent agents that must create a plan for their future actions in an artificial world. Planning agents are often based on general problem-solving mechanisms, adding specific features for planning with respect to representations of goals, states, actions and solutions 43 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> AI-planning is roughly comparable with simulation of planning as a mental activity. The emphasis in AI-scheduling is mainly on the task of planners. Several AI techniques are used to solve scheduling problems. Results are reported with rule bases, neural networks, genetic algorithms, simulated annealing and constraint-based programming techniques such as constraint logic programming and constraint satisfaction programming 9 . AI-scheduling methodolo- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> gies divide into two areas: constructive methods and repair methods. Constructive methods start with an empty schedule and add assignments until the schedule is complete. Repair-based methods try to adjust an existing schedule in order to remove conflicts or to further optimise the solution 68 . AI-scheduling<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> techniques gain interest because they use generally applicable techniques that find satisfactory solutions.

We argue that decision support systems for planning and scheduling must focus on planning problems in practice. Most current practice-oriented scheduling support approaches acknowledge the fact that scheduling and planning are dynamic in nature and that solution methods should not only focus on scheduling but also on rescheduling. This, however, is not enough. The perspectives and results that originate from the various scientific approaches discussed can be used in cooperation with generic decision support approaches in order to design scheduling support systems in a structured way. Because of the diversity of planning approaches, an integrated approach is necessary to cover all aspects of planning.

By considering planning as a mental activity—the cognitive psychological perspective—the system developer gains insight into the problem space of human planners. The planner will only accept the solutions of a computer program if he understands the results, i.e., the user and the system should have coincident problem spaces 40 . Efforts to combine<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> scheduling support systems with the user’s perception of the problem have resulted in various mixedinitiative scheduling approaches 40,48,49,61 .

The planning task in practice is more than just problem solving. Other task components such as clerical work and counting must also be supported. In addition, planners must often collaborate to make a schedule, and they often have to negotiate about Ž . soft constraints and goal functions. This relates to the organisational perspective. Furthermore, the planning department itself must be organised by assigning tasks and authority relations. Effective planning support must take into account all these aspects.

Planning support systems can use techniques from OR and AI to generate schedules. The degree of structure of the problem under consideration determines what kind of techniques can be applied. Knowledge-based AI techniques fit with the problem space of planners, but often have to sacrifice performance with respect to optimisation. Constrainedbased techniques and OR algorithms are efficient and powerful, but show a disparity with the way human planners think. Nowadays, OR and AI approaches are often combined to take advantage of both worlds see, e.g., Ref. 37 .Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>.

All approaches to planning and scheduling deal in some way with arranging scarce resources. Examples of resources mentioned are products, money, vehicles, machines, staff, etc. More precisely, scheduling always concerns multiple tokens of different object types. Production schedules, for example, arrange products, machines and time intervals, whereas staff scheduling deals with staff members and shifts. This common feature of scheduling problems is used in the SEC-model as the main modelling principle.

We use the following definition to denote planning and scheduling problems: Ž . planning or scheduling is the attuning of instances of different object types, thereby upholding constraints and goals. This definition imposes restrictions on the models in the development process. The object-oriented approach, however, also creates possibilities for reuse. This will be explained in Sections 5 and 6. First, we must be more explicit about our perspective on decision support systems for planning: in other words, our view on the role of computers in task support.

## 3. Starting-points for decision support in planning and scheduling

As can be seen in Section 2, scheduling and planning problems can be approached from many perspectives. Traditionally, most research in the field of scheduling deals with automatically generating a schedule for a well-defined problem. Research on scheduling support tries to fill the gap between theoretical assumptions of mathematical approaches and applications in practice. Theory on organisational aspects of planning or cognitive aspects of schedule generation by humans is, in our opinion, underexposed. We think that scheduling support in practice needs to start with the tasks that the user performs in order to solve his or her problem. This means that, at least in the beginning, the mathematical complexity of the underlying scheduling problem is of no importance. After all, how complex can it be, if it can be solved manually? Clearly, satisfising and slack provide ways to solve even the most complex scheduling problems manually. According to our experiences, planners are more interested in manipulation and adequate information processing than in black box-like schedule generation. Therefore, we do not pay much attention to automatic generation of the planning. We think that showing the consequences of a decision is more important than letting the system make the decision itself. This view is justified by the fact that manually the planning can be made relatively easy. Often, little time is spent on actual plan generation. Verbraeck 62 even reports that in his<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Ž case the actual planning task problem solving only . Ž . accounted for 3% of the planner’s time. Our own surveys indicate that administrative tasks often account for 40% to 80% of planners’ time 31 . In our<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> view, support should start here. However, we do not want to diminish the importance of calculations; on the contrary, we want to emphasise that even the most elegant computations and powerful algorithms have to be interpreted by human planners. In our view, support in the form of schedule generation should only be introduced after human schedulers trust a system that supports their task 35 . We rarely<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> saw that the generative part of a scheduling support system was fully used in practice. In the remainder of this section, we will elaborate on the user orientation view that we take.

Bottom-up approach in decision support. In the first place we state that effective decision support starts with a detailed cognitive task analysis of planners. We do not want to start with the structures and processes in organisations, but with the individual planners. Users are complex information processing systems; not only from an expert point of view, but also from an interactive and communicative point of view it is necessary to know in detail what sub taskŽ . procedures planners use. Besides the procedures, the cognitive task analysis should reveal the intermediate results and the end results planners prefer. In addition, the cognitive limitations of the users and the inclinations to make and correct errors have to be assessed.

User-control principle. In the second place we contend that, despite the importance of computations Ž . especially in the field of planning and scheduling , users are more supported by devices and interfaces that give them the opportunity to manipulate all kinds of information, than by interfaces that only present the outcomes of powerful optimisation techniques. The organisational reality is such that changes and dynamics are standard. Support has to be adjusted to this reality. As a consequence of this, we argue that the planner and not the system is decisive in the acceptance and implementation of outcomes.

World-making principle. In the third place we argue that the development of a decision support system creates a new reality for a user. System development does not imitate an existing practice, that is to say a way of working that is depicted in a computer system. On the contrary, the development of a decision support system consists of inventing a new reality. This reality, however, gradually comes into being for two reasons. First, a decision support system, despite the fact that it is based on the task execution of planners, must become familiar to the user. This means that in the initial phase computer support partially exists next to the old way of working manually. A planner has to get used to a system and has to learn to trust it 35 , while in the mean-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> time he has to unlearn his old practices. Second, because of the implementation of a decision support system, even though it is based on cognitive task analyses, the so-called new way of working will gradually change. Planners can do more things with the system than they did manually before. They can play around with possibilities, meaning that they will change the task execution over time 31 . This means<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> that there is no stable situation that can be copied. Change, adaptation and un learning are basic ingre-Ž . dients of the development of decision support systems. We call this principle the ‘ world-making’ principle or the semiotic principle 24,25 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

To summarise, we advocate an approach to scheduling support in which the human planner or scheduler is the starting point. Thus, we look primarily at his or her task and how it can be supported. This stems from our observation that, in practice, scheduling systems are often not used if they are not made in cooperation with the planner, but rather solely from the perspective of the problem structure while ignoring human problem-solving strategies. We have made several scheduling support systems with this user-oriented approach. In this, we applied a pragmatic mix of computer science and cognitive science 31,60,34,54 . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

In this article we do not present a scheduling system. We describe a system that can help the process of analysing scheduling situations and building scheduling support systems. For this, we present an ontology the SEC that coheres to the starting Ž . points just described. With this ontology, we describe scheduling situations, task performance of human schedulers and scheduling systems. Such descriptions can be compared, and we expect that we can reuse parts of existing models if we analyse a new situation.

## 4. Approaches for modelling and support: a reuse perspective

## 4.1. Reuse approaches

Reuse is an important topic in software development. In reusing existing products in the development process, costs are decreased and accuracy is increased. Reuse can be applied to domain knowledge, design efforts, architectures, requirements, code and documentation 6 . Reuse also enables prototyp- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ing, since developers can get faster and more frequent feedback from users. Numerous approaches that formalise and implement reuse principles are described in the literature.

Reuse can be realised in two different ways. It can be based on a generation and b compositionŽ . Ž . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 7 . Both of these forms are applied in scheduling systems. The demarcation lines, however, are not very rigid. Generation-based reuse means that an exhaustive assessment of a domain has taken place resulting in a kind of mechanism, structured in such a way that if a problem turns up a solution can be generated. No new adaptations or adjustments have to be made to the mechanism. An example is a system in which the specific characteristics of a situation can be set up with parameters. The analogy with Transformational Generative Grammar TGGŽ . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 12 is more than just a coincidence. In TGG the internal language mechanisms are already there and they generate natural language expressions given all kinds of sensory input. Contrary to generation-based reuse, composition-based reuse relies on application over time to collect content. In the utopian end,Ž . however, all problem types will be addressed and no new knowledge has to be added. Then it will behave in the same way as a generation-based reuse approach.

In this article we describe a composition-based reuse approach for the planning and scheduling domain. Composition techniques merge existing and new components together to form a new application. New components can be added to the collection so that they can be reused in subsequent development efforts. Most composition-based reuse support approaches implicitly or explicitly use the concept of case-based reasoning. Case-based reasoning solves problems by adapting existing solutions that were used to solve comparable problems 42 . In general, composition-based reuse support approaches specify how to store and retrieve cases. These approaches differ in the primitives that are used to describe a case and in the techniques that are used to find existing cases that are comparable to a new case. Section 4.2 discusses several scheduling support approaches from this perspective.

## 5. The SEC

Based on the requirements described in the previous sections, especially Sections 2 and 3, this section describes the various models of SEC. The aim of the

SEC-approach is to facilitate the development of scheduling support systems. It contains a method for scheduling support system development that includes Ž . Ž . a models for domain, tasks and systems, b mechanisms to transfer results from one modelling phase to the next, and c mechanisms to reuse models. TheŽ . discussion in Section 4 showed that, to our knowledge, none of the available approaches offers a complete range of models for the domain, the task or application, the mechanisms for transition between these models and the abstraction of these models. Our claim is that the SEC-approach focuses on the various aspects of the generic task of planning and provides models for reuse.

Scheduling practice can be supported in three stages. First, organisational guidelines with respect to planning can be used to assess and possibly adapt the current practice. Second, the assessment can be used to design scheduling task support. Third,Ž . within computer support of the task, algorithms can be of help in schedule generation. We want to emphasise again that our first interest is in reuse with respect to the first two stages since efficient schedule generation algorithms are often very case specific. Taking planning as a generic task, the SEC-approach focuses on various models of task domains, task executions and applications in which flexibility, manipulation and planner orientation are central. This SEC-model has been incorporated in a software system, the SEC-system, which will be described in Section 6.

The aim of the SEC is to facilitate the development of scheduling support systems. In general, we can distinguish three areas for modelling in the process of decision support system development. First, we must be able to describe the problem itself. For scheduling and planning problems, according to the definition we gave in Section 2, this amounts to the entities that must be attuned and the constraints and goals that apply. Second, we must describe the task that is to be supported, in order to cohere to the people that are going to use the system. Third, the system itself must be modelled. The aim of the SEC is to contain a method for scheduling support system development that includes a models for these areas, Ž . ## 5.1. Domain models

The domain models represent the entities that must be attuned. In Section 4, we described various modelling approaches. An important distinction is domain dependence vs. domain independence. A domain-dependent technique usually has more expressive power with respect to detail, but the trade-off is a limitation in the number of problems to which it can be applied. From the reuse perspective, we search for a modelling technique that, with respect to its language, does not rely on specific characteristics of the underlying problem domain. For example, we do not want a concept ‘machine’ to be embedded in the structure of the modelling language, since such a concept would make no sense for staff scheduling.

For domain modelling, we turn to object-oriented techniques. This modelling language itself is empty with respect to domain knowledge, but allows for description of a domain at multiple layers of abstraction, thereby facilitating both comparison of problems at a high level and description of problems at a detailed level.

The scheduling domain is represented with three model types. First, the arrangement or attunement problem is modelled with object aggregation, e.g., the aggregate object assignment consists of the object shift and the object nurse. Decomposition by the planner of the problem into sub-problems is modelled with aggregate objects. Thus, the layers represent the aggregation levels as perceived by the schedulers. An object in our model is a construct from the schedulers’ perspective, i.e., it is a semiotic construct since it refers to signs as opposed to real world objects in an ontological sense 24 . This <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> releases us from the obligation to model the so-called ‘real’ world. From the point of view of the schedulers, it is their reality that we have to account for.

This means that one has to work towards some kind of a to-be-agreed-upon artefact—a model, domain or world—that is represented by signs and sign structures. The time one spends in realising such a consensus may be regained in minimising the after-effects of misrepresentation.

Second, illegal combinations of object instances are depicted by constraints, and third, goal functions depict preferred combinations. An example of a nurse schedule is depicted in Fig. 1. Objects are either singular Ž . nurse, starting time and ending time or composed of other objects; i.e., they are aggregate objects shift, scheduled shift and schedule . TheŽ . nurse schedule example consists of a number of scheduled shifts. A scheduled shift links a particular shift to a nurse. Shifts themselves are composed of a starting time and an ending time. Assignments in the schedule are depicted by instances of objects.

Fig. 1 also contains an example of a constraint. It states that student nurses may not work in the night shift. Therefore, if Sally in Fig. 1 is a student nurse, the schedule is illegal. The goal in Fig. 1 represents a preferred minimisation of the deviation of worked hours and the number of hours in the contract of the nurse. Of course, a domain model can contain several constraints and goals.

Generalisation of object models is performed in two ways. First, singular objects are abstracted by inheritance. Properties that are common to two object types are described at a higher ancestor object type. For example, the object type employee is an ancestor of both the object type nurse and the object type mechanic. These three object types show the differences and similarities between nurses and mechanics. The inheritance mechanism should not be restricted to singular inheritance. The object tree must be dynamically created when searching for similar objects. If, for example, we need for some reason the distinction between men and women in the tree and not between nurses and mechanics, single inheritance would, in this example, result in four object types male nurses, female nurses, maleŽ mechanics, female mechanics instead of the desired. two men and women .Ž .

Pillutla and Nag 36 use a roughly similar<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> object-oriented domain description, although it is limited to the production-scheduling domain. The ontology of Smith and Becker 50 also uses speciali-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> sation of models. Their basic sub-domains are activities, products, resources and demands. A scheduling problem is to be decomposed in specialisations of these sub-domains. Our abstraction does not have such an underlying ontology in which basic entities have predefined relations. Instead, we have tried to categorise the objects with terms that are used by human planners. Currently, at the highest level of abstraction we distinguish seven primitive singular object types: person, time, task, location, product, machine, and vehicle. All objects occurring in the scheduling domain are presumed to have one of these object types as the ultimate ancestor. This classification is derived from a survey of scheduling situations in more than 50 companies including different forms of scheduling, e.g., staff scheduling, transportation scheduling and production scheduling <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 2 . We emphasise that this classification is not definite. Other classifications are possible, since the classification scheme should remain in accordance with new scheduling and planning ‘realities’. Note that the object orientation paradigm allows for extension of this classification without altering the structure of the modelling language. Hence, we avoid domain dependency.

The second way of generalisation of object models is clustering of singular objects. We expect that in practice only a limited set of models will occur <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 58 . Staff scheduling, for example, often deals with persons, tasks and time or persons, locations and time, whereas production scheduling deals with machines, products and time, alternatively supplemented with, for example, persons or tasks. ## 5.2. Task models

In Section 3 we state that we adhere to the bottom–up principle for decision support. This means that we must somehow model the task that is performed by the human planner. To depict the results of a task analysis, we use task models. The reuse and transition requirements imply that these task models must a refer to the domain models, b allow Ž . Ž . reference by application models, and c allow ab- Ž . straction and comparison. The approaches that we described in Section 4 provide little reference to task performance.

A task can be defined as the realisation of a set of objectives given a set of constraints. It is almost impossible to demarcate a task objectively in terms of a single task unit. The level of aggregation is important, which means that, for example, doing the administration can be a task, negotiating with customers about due dates can be a task, or making an overall plan for the operation rooms and patient admission planning can be a task. It is also possible to call the first two tasks sub-tasks of the third task. In order to deal with this situation, we need a task model. A task model is a representation with assumptions and principles about a the objects, prop-Ž . erties of the objects and the relations involved, bŽ . the cognitive strategies and procedures for execution of the task by the actor, and c the organisationalŽ . context of the task 8,63 . Because the ultimate ob-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> jective is to realise user-oriented task support, we prefer a descriptive empirical task analysis to a normative, so-called rational task analysis. The task analysis resulting in the task model should make clear what part of the task can be supported by software, and what kind of software. This includes not only information about new cognitive strategies that should be learned in working with the supporting system, but also about the strategies and procedures that should be unlearned.

In the SEC, the scheduling task execution is represented by two model types. First, task decomposition denotes the sub-tasks that comprise the scheduling task. To meet the transformation requirements, sub-tasks are described as operations on objects in the domain model. The highest level of task decomposition shows sub-tasks such as administration collecting data, etc. , negotiation, communica-Ž . tion, attuning or plan generation and adaptation or replanning. The attuning and adaptation tasks deal with the actual assignment of object instances. These sub-tasks can be decomposed further into sub-tasks such as selecting, ranking, assigning and manipulation of constraints and goals. Second, the task strategy depicts the order in which the sub-tasks are performed. The order of execution of the scheduling sub-tasks, for example, may be administrate, attune, negotiate, adapt. The order within the attune sub-task could be rank, select, assign, manipulate. Of course, the strategy does not have to be solely linear. Iterative sequences of sub-tasks are likely to occur 31 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

By generalisation of task models, specific tasks are abstracted to task models that can be applied to multiple domain models. We follow three perspectives. First, due to their reference to objects, sub-tasks can be abstracted by referring to abstract object types. If, for example, a task model of nurse scheduling uses a subset of the available attributes that corresponds to the abstract object type ‘employee’, the task model can be used for, e.g., mechanics scheduling. A second form of generalisation of task models uses roles of objects. For example, a task can be described as matching supply and demand. This, however, requires other taxonomies for the domain than the object structure we propose, e.g., the activities, products, resources and demands 50 . The third<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> generalisation mechanism typifies scheduling tasks with respect to the task strategy. The strategy can be described with features such as periodically or continuously, batch or one by one, top–down or bottom–up, etc. 26,59 . The combination of such fea-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tures and predefined abstract task models will aid the analysis of scheduling tasks.

## 5.3. Application models

After composing domain and task execution models, the application components can be modelled. This means a shift in the development process from analysis to design. We need models that can be based on the domain models and task models, and that can be abstracted to induce reuse. The SEC uses a generic application taxonomy with components that can be used in any scheduling system. First, the user-interface presents the information to the user, e.g., an electronic planning board. A user-interface is composed of a number of components widgets Ž . such as menus and push buttons 17 . The compo- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> nents are either linked to objects or sub-tasks. Push buttons, for example, can be used to trigger the execution of sub-tasks. Commonly used user-interfaces to depict attunement are Gantt-charts and time tables. If sub-tasks or objects are reused in the development process, perhaps the accompanying user-interface components may also be reused. Second, the inspector examines what constraints are violated in a schedule. Third, the schedule is assessed by the eÕaluator with respect to the goals. Current research concentrates on the possibility of a generic inspector and evaluator by specifying a generic constraint language that is based on the SEC domain modelling technique. The fourth main application component is the generator. This component can, alone or in cooperation with the scheduler, create schedules. We use the term schedule interactor for a mixed-initiative system that includes each of those four components.

To accommodate the planner as much as possible, the generator should use the decomposition of the scheduling task execution of the human planner. In this way, planners are more inclined to accept the outcome of the system. It is at a low sub-task level that we expect black box generation techniques to be useful. An example is the task analysis of nurse schedulers that must make a 6-week schedule 31 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> First, they schedule the night shift for the first week, then the evening shift for the first week and then the day shift, again for the first week. These sub-tasks are repeated sequentially for the other 5 weeks. Instead of using a generation technique that creates the whole 6-week schedule at once, it was proposed to let the system search for alternatives at the described sub-task level 31 . The system proposes <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> alternatives for a shift type for 1 week. In that way, planners can adjust schedule proposals at a level that fits with their own task order, and they are more inclined to accept the ultimate outcome and defend it to the nurses.

Current approaches for efficient schedule generation such as linear programming and constraint-based techniques rely heavily on domain-specific constraints to search through the usually vast amount of possible schedules. This domain dependency stands in the way of decomposition into components that have a clear relation to components of domain and task models. This, of course, impairs reuse. If, however, components are used that correspond to subtasks at a low level, exhaustive search can be applied in the part of the schedule that applies to the sub-task under consideration. Thus, the search through the total problem space is directed by the task order of the human scheduler instead of calculations with constraints. This implies that we do not need complex solution techniques. This bottom–up approach is, of course, inclined to deliver sub-optimal solutions from a mathematical perspective. In our opinion, however, the practical advantage that human schedulers will be more inclined to accept the solution outweighs the advantage of mathematical opti misation.

## 6. The SEC-system

In Section 5 we discussed a set of models that depict the results from development processes in the planning and scheduling domain. The model types are specified in such a way that the actual compilation of these models does not have to be based solely on case-specific information. Model developers can use previously built models to create models for a new case. The search for reusable model components, however, requires considerable information processing. To aid the search, we developed the SEC-system, based on the SEC. With the SEC-system, it should be possible to find existing models on the basis of a description of a scheduling situation. Although the overall functional requirements of the SEC-system are described 45 , until now only limited functionality has been implemented in a prototype. The remainder of this section will discuss the functional requirements and a partial implementation of the SEC-system.

The SEC-system incorporates several reuse mechanisms. Reasoning by analogy and case-based learning can be used to identify similarities between cases <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 27,28 . Consider the analogy A is to B as C is to D. Reasoning by analogy involves three operations. First, the inference operation derives the relationship between A and B. Second, A is mapped to C. Third, the information that is revealed by the inference and the mapping is applied to D. There are three key issues for this 27 . First, cases which are a combina-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tion of a problem A and C and a model B and D ,Ž . Ž . must be represented and stored. Second, it must be clear how to identify similarities between models and problems, and third, it must be evident how new models can be constructed from existing ones. The modelling languages must incorporate problem features that allow fast recognition of similarities and differences 28 . In addition, the inference and appli-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> cation operations require fluent transformation from problem to model. Graphs can be used to make these issues operational 27 . This approach does not ex- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> plicitly relate models to software components. It is mainly oriented towards quantitative models. The general principles, however, do not exclude the application of these ideas to software reuse.

To apply reasoning by analogy, we need a set of models that can be decomposed into components. These components are models themselves that can be decomposed further. Decomposition can proceed until non-decomposable primitives are encountered. The SEC provides such models. The object models allow decomposition by means of aggregation and abstraction mechanisms. An example of decomposition by aggregation is the aggregate object shift. It is a component of the aggregate object scheduled shift, and it can be decomposed further itself into a starting time and an ending time. Abstraction is the second form of decomposition of object models. An example is the object employee that describes a subset of the properties of the object nurse. Decomposition can proceed until either singular objects or primitive objects are encountered.

Decomposition of other models takes place in the same way. The task structure can be decomposed into sub-tasks or abstract tasks by referring to abstract objects. The task strategy decomposes into strategy-parts and strategies that refer to abstract sub-tasks. The user-interface can be decomposed into groups of widgets and the schedule generator decomposes in the same manner as task structures and task strategies. The inspector and evaluator refer to singular or aggregate objects and hence decompose accordingly.

## 7. Conclusions and further research

There are two movements in computer system development that, although at first sight opposing, have led to the need for reuse of conceptual and technical products. On one hand, there is a tendency to create bigger and more encompassing computer systems. On the other hand, there is a demand for small intelligent systems that are really adapted to individual users. Both developments require that much time and expertise be invested in thorough analyses of task domains and the individual task performance of human users. With the need for more comprehensive and intelligent systems, the complexity, desultoriness and dynamics of systems increase. Nowadays, computer systems do not exist unaltered for 5 years anymore; they are continually adapted. For these reasons, it is apparent that reuse of conceptual and technical components is considered and will soon be inevitable.

Instruments to realise reuse in a simple way, however, are lacking. There are no generally accepted task structures or task models, and there is no firmly established ontology or semantic<sup>r</sup>semiotic framework. We encountered these deficiencies when we tried to formulate components for reuse after building several prototype scheduling support sys- Ž . tems. It turns out that computer science with regard to reuse issues is still lacking a firm foundation. It is not enough to set up a reuse approach consisting only of database theory, programming languages and decision support systems theory. Much more is needed, and for the time being the missing parts must come from other scientific fields.
