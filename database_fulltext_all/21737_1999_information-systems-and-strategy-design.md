## Abstract

Strategic thinking forms a wide-ranking, complex area of thought. It also seems to be an extremely interesting and important field in both management practice and literature. Recently, the areas that the different functions like accounting,Ž marketing, production and information systems of firm and strategic thinking have in common have been lively discussed.. In this paper, the applicability of information systems thinking to strategic thinking is outlined. For that purpose, three modes of strategy-making are explicated: strategy-making as management process, as game-playing and as leadership. In each of these three modes, the strategy design phase and especially the essentials of its knowledge creation function are presented. Finally, on the basis of this analysis, the information systems approach and strategic thinking are compared and the areas of mutual cooperation as well as those devoid of cooperation are revealed. q 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. ## 1. Introduction

The idea of strategy must be very old; for as long as human beings have had organizations, they have had strategies. As an explicit concept, strategy goes back as far as ancient Greece 12 . It was however, <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> only very recently, in the 1960s in fact, that strategy entered the language of management.

Strategic thinking then spread quickly and today it is one of the most important subdisciplines in the field of general management. In actual managerial practice, too, the concept of strategy is part and parcel of everyday language. Practically, every writer in the area of management wanted to make a contribution to the development of strategic thinking, and the result has been a glut of literature. So much was written and published that several schools of thought were quickly born. By the late 1980s, the vast number of schools of thought in strategic thinking were being subject to a process of labelling and classification. For instance, Karlof 31 was able to find 10, ¨ <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Nasi 43 picked out seven, Gilbert et al. 21 listed¨ <sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> six and finally Mintzberg 38 defined 10 schools of<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> thought.

This proves two things: first of all, strategic thinking seems to be an extremely interesting and seductive area. Secondly, it is obviously a wide-ranging and complex phenomenon, which can be approached from several directions and in many different ways.

Strategy did not remain for long a recipe for success in just general management; it quickly appeared in the different functional areas of the firm. Soon it was possible to speak about the likes of strategy and production 22,36 , strategy and marketing 1,14,15,18,54 , strategy and accounting 6,17, <sup>w x</sup> <sup>w</sup> 61 , and strategy and information systems 20,56,62 . <sup>x</sup> <sup>w x</sup>

In this paper, we are mainly interested in the connections between strategic thinking and information systems thinking. The article is written by a strategy thinker, and it is written for information system thinkers. By nature, the paper is introductory and comprehensive and it seeks to define the areas of current and potential cooperation between strategymaking and information systems development work.

Strategy in our paper means the plot of the action of the firm 44 or the logic of the action of the firm<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 21 . So defined, our concept of strategy encompasses the definitions of most, if not all, of the extant schools of thought. Strategy-making, then, refers to thinking 39 and doing, that is to the planning and <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> implementation of a strategy. Strategy design, which is critically important when deliberating the connections with information systems, includes a vitally important knowledge creation phase in ‘‘thinking’ and ‘‘planning’’.

An information system to us is a means of recording and communicating information to satisfy the requirements of all its users, the business activities they are engaged in and the objectives established for them 46 . Thus, it has a much broader connota-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tion than the traditional management information system MIS in that information is used for more Ž . than routine decisions supported by regular periodic reports 35 . Therefore, our information systems <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> thinking refers to development work which covers all managerial activity levels from top to transaction processing, all business functions and all types of decisions from structured to unstructured decisions.

Firstly, we will construct a comprehensive view of strategy-making by defining three of its modes: strategy-making as management process, as gameplaying and as leadership. This framework furnishes a simple synthesis of most mainstream approaches to strategic thinking. At the same time, it presents three patterns which are in heavy use in actual business practice. Secondly, we will concentrate on the strategy design phase and on its knowledge creation dimensions. These dimensions differ in different modes of strategy-making, which is important to recognize when outlining information system applications. The paper concludes by matching the strategy-making modes with information systems thinking in a schematic synthesis.

## 2. Strategy-making as management process

To consider strategy-making as management process is by a large margin the predominant perspective when looking at strategic affairs. The origins of the approach can be traced back to the original sources of the terms ‘‘management principles’’ and ‘‘management process’’, that is to Fayol in 1916 19<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> with his elements of management, namely, planning, organizing, command, coordination and control. By 1950, a large measure of unanimity existed among the classic texts as to how to divide managerial work into phases and as to how recommendations for decision-making should be delivered.

The same ideology appeared in strategic thinking as soon as the strategy concept emerged in management literature in the 1960s. Slight variations can be observed in what the classics of the 1960s focused on. Ansoff 3 concentrated on strategic planning,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Anthony 4 on planning and control, and Andrews <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 2 on planning and implementation. Nevertheless, the underlying message was the same: a systematic process approach facilitates mastery of the complex world of strategy. It was not long before the concept of strategic management was generally understood to refer strategy-making as a management process.

The essence of the approach is a preplanned, predefined process through which the making of strategy is carried out and the implemented. The best possible processes that have been presented vary from writer to writer, but not by much. Thus we can discern, for example, five main phases: determine the mission and objectives, analyze the environment, analyze and then select the strategic alternatives, implement the strategies, and finally evaluate and control the actual performance Fig. 1 55 . Or weŽ . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> may come across a three-step model embracing several sub-strategies: strategy formulation with nineŽ sub-steps , strategy implementation with three sub-. Ž steps and strategy evaluation with two sub-steps . Ž . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 13 . Nevertheless, in these different process patterns the underlying idea is the same: in order to be able to better understand and fully master the strategic entirety you need to divide it up into distinct, detailed phases. ‘‘The process’’ is often a formal one and so its phases are predetermined. There is a starting-point at a specific time, a finishing point and the phases that come between them, in other words a timetable. The various company levels involved and the individuals are defined as well as their tasks and responsibilities in the process. Other lists are drawn up, for example, of the matters to be analyzed and forecasted, of the estimates to be prepared, of the issues to be settled, of the forms in which measures are to be presented and of how what will happen is to be studied later.

Documents are a crucial part of this management process. Such a process turns out many familiar phenomena as creeds, missions, policies, long-range and short-range plans but above all it produces strategies and control documents. It prints out corporate, business and functional strategies, or, in some companies, company, division and product<sup>r</sup>market strategies. By their nature strategies and plans are nothing but recommendations and instruction documents dealing with key features of motivating, organizing, allocating resources and the operating of major functions.

I have earlier 44 used the expression ‘‘the plan-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ning machine’’ to describe the heart of the kind of strategy-making. Information, instructions, timetables and managers are fed into the machine. The wheels grind and then the machine spews out plans for the different levels of the corporations, for different time spans, executives and units.

In spite of the problematic bureaucratic consequences famously listed by many authorities all the way from Weber 63 to Mintzberg 40 , this sort of <sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> approach is widely used in practice. In one manifestation or another, it is more or less obligatory in all large corporations and in many medium-sized firms, too. Obviously, this is due to the ideal and instruments it upholds of simultaneously maintaining order and integration.

Very seldom it is pointed out that the arduous task of simultaneously achieving these two things are related to the view that the dominant managerial function in this approach is not planning but control. When making strategy through a management process, the past is controlled in detail. The aim of the process is to control also the future and over a long timespan. Finally, the strategy-making process itself is controlled in minute detail, if possible.

## 3. Strategy-making as game-playing

As a conscious, explicit approach, game-playing is of much younger origin. As an unconscious, implicit one, it is perhaps as old as competition itself. Whereas the management process approach underscores the firm’s internal processes, the game-playing perspective looks a bit more outwards. It seeks to envisage the situations in which the competition will be met and bested.

The conceptual background to this mode of strategy-making lies in portfolio management. The overriding aim of portfolio management is for management to be able to position the company’s strategic units products, product lines, productŽ <sup>r</sup>market combinations, etc. in some wider context, be it product. life-cycle or industry, for instance, and then to assist in making the right decisions called for by the positioning. Portfolio management was created mainly for the benefit of large companies with several more or less closely related strategic units. In this context the key questions that had to be addressed were how should such a broad repertoire be balanced and administered, which units would it be wisest to retain, which should be expanded and which ended for example.

To answer such questions numerous frameworks have been created, which with window matrices try to simplify the positioning and the decision-making that arise from it. The most famous are those of the Boston Consulting Group, McKinsey and GE, and perhaps also the numerous variants of Hofer’s models 23,25,27–29,51 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

During the course of the 1980s, the game-playing perspective has become increasingly visible and now can be found in the classic works of distinguished authors. For example, Porter’s 51–53 major works can be taken as a manifestation of game-playing ideology. Porter’s ‘‘firms’’ refer to players, ‘‘the field of competition’’ and ‘‘industries’’ to game arenas, ‘‘generic strategies’’ to move selection and ‘‘competitive advantage’’ and ‘‘value chain’’ to instruments for choosing the right moves. Finally, ‘‘home base’’ and ‘‘diamond’’ belong to explicit game-playing terminology. Even Mintzberg’s 37<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> classification of generic strategies could be interpreted as a game-playing framework. His basic categories are locating, distinguishing, elaborating and extending the core business and reconceiving the business. These are further divided into 16 sub-categories submoves , which are then chopped up intoŽ . 44 sub-sub-categories sub-sub-moves .Ž .

However, there is still a pressing need for conceptual frameworks to assist the understanding and developing of company game-playing practice. Figs. 2 and 3 present two such frameworks by way of examples.

The first figure is a development and extension of Ansoff’s 3 famous product <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>r</sup>mission framework. Now, however, the figure contains the additional assumption that the game-playing window of a company is wider than before. A company can, of course, still develop by expanding, be it market expansion, product expansion or diversification. But a company can also grow stronger through moves in which things are given up: it can shut down a certain product line, it can withdraw from certain markets and it can terminate an individual product<sup>r</sup>market combination. The alternatives displayed in this first framework are in fact part of everyday strategy-making in actual company life. A typical large, diversified and dynamic company may make dozens of expansion and reduction moves at this level every year.

The second framework classifies possible opening and closing moves. The simple, traditional way to start is to ‘‘do it all yourself’’: develop the product line, acquire the technology, have the buildings erected, recruit the personnel and arrange the finance. A corresponding closing move is to let the product or product line die, more or less slowly. Company deals and acquisitions generate much activity. A company can enter a new field or extend its existing domain by buying parts of other companies or whole companies. Similarly it can sell off large or small parts of its own assets.

The third basic form is cooperation, its commencement and its termination. Today, it is becoming increasingly common for companies to seek a competitive advantage through a strategic alliance and should a company feel that there is no longer any advantage to be gained, then it is also prepared to dismantle the cooperation that has been built up. As with the previous framework, Fig. 3 offers new lenses for everyday things since the six-part move window can be seen operating all around us in company practice.

When talking about strategy-making as gameplaying, certain elements in the process are highlighted and we can interpret them as follows; there are players companies, units and managers whoŽ . play on game fields or arenas industries, sub-in-Ž dustries and fields of competition and when playing, . they make moves. These moves can be, for instance, offensive, defensive or neutral. They can be real or mere diversions. They might be part of a carefully considered plan or they might be an ad hoc response. They can be voluntary or they can be compulsory, made out of necessity under the pressure of events. All this takes place within a framework of rules Ž . laws, accepted practices and traditions . The rules may vary from one industry to the next from one country to the next; they can also change with time.

When strategy-making is seen as game-playing, the role of key players cannot be neglected. ‘‘Players’’ refers not only to firms but also first and foremost to individuals with their visions and instincts and their capacity to proact and react.

## 4. Strategy-making as strategic leadership

The third important mode of strategy-making is part of the ‘‘new humanistic wave’’ 45 in manage-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ment and it involves strategic leadership. This encompasses not only the leader but also the led and their interaction with the leader.

Two closely related fields of debate provided fertile soil for the growth of the idea of leadership as a subject in strategic thought. The believers in success factors, whose bible is Peters’ and Waterman’s ‘‘In Search of Excellence’’ 50 claimed to have<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> found the ingredients that made a company highly successful. The common denominator among all such writers was faith in the human factor. At the same time the company culture fashion was born, the midwives being Deal and Kennedy with their book Corporate Cultures 16 . The adherents of this fash- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ion spoke of heroes, rites and rituals and they also brought us a new model of have outstanding, humane leadership, which they christened symbolic leadership.

Leadership proceeded to develop into an explicit area in strategic thinking as a consequence of books such as those written by Kotter 32–34 , Bennis and<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Nanus 5 and Peters and Austin 49 . They sought to<sup>w x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> answer questions like ‘‘what does a successful leader really do?’’, ‘‘what are the traits of a super-leader?’’ and ‘‘is there anything to learn from such people?’’. After perusing John P. Kotter’s works, we can say that the leader is lively, optimistic individual who keeps him- or herself well informed. He or she likes to be in the midst of people and is not afraid of uncertainty. But first and foremost the leader has two main areas of activity: agenda setting and network building. He or she is able to create a vision and develop a sensible strategy. Secondly, he or she is capable of building an implementation network that includes key power centers: bosses, peers, subordinates and outsiders, for instance.

Bennis’ and Nanus’ leaders are also able to turn intentions into reality, their ideas into action. Their ideal leaders have four models of action or strategies. The first involves the winning of people’s attention through vision and the second the giving of meaning through communication — a kind of social architecture. The third involves creating trust through good positioning. Fourthly and finally, leaders make themselves receptive and they ‘‘give their everything’’.

The striking novelty in Peters’ and Austin’s account was what they called ‘‘management by wandering around’’: good leaders wander around with and among customers and are frequently looking over the shoulders of the people producing an innovation. Above all, they wander around among their own people, are visible to them and they are always alert, inquisitive, mobile and supportive.

The final boost to the acceptance of leadership as an essential element in the strategy debate came when an extremely respected practitioner, Iacocca <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 30 , published his autobiography. This was the first of a series of such works, all written from inside the real world of the strategist.

Soon various new schools of leadership thought stepped out onto the stage. Good examples are visionary leadership see, e.g., Ref. 64 , charismatic Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. leadership see, e.g., Ref. 9 and transformational Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. leadership see, e.g., Ref. 58 , all of them picking Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. out and describing key ideas within leadership. Thus, when we talk about the personality traits a strategist possesses or about what a strategist really does, we are talking about strategic leadership. ## 6. Strategy design and its knowledge creation dimensions

In spite of the fundamental differences in the characteristics of the three modes of strategy-making, one unifying factor can be clearly identified that is important in all of them and that is the strategy, of course. Over and over again, in management process, in game-playing and in leadership, strategy is created, developed, formulated or designed. In this paper, we are interested in the mysteries of strategy design and especially in its knowledge creation in all three of the strategy-making modes that forms the basis for further speculation about the possible collaboration between these modes and information systems thinking. There are always several dichotomies present when a strategy is designed. These dichotomies reveal just how awkward it is to acquire sufficient and appropriate knowledge for successful strategy-making.

## 6.1. The past and the future

Every time outlining a strategy, its maker is involved with two time dimensions. The maker is aiming at the future but is forced to lean on the past and its latest moment, that is the present. The future of humankind, a company and a single strategist is always dependent on the past in one way or another. It is inconceivable that sensible strategies can be created without understanding some history.

The past is the time that existed before the present time together with the things that happened during that time 66 . It is something bygone and ended<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 65 . The future, on the other hand, is the period of time that will take place after the present 66 . It is<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> the time to come and something about to be 65 . In<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> this context, the key point to be grasped is that the past is something a strategist can know about because it has existed. It follows that the past can be treated as a distinct entity. It can be remembered and often documented. Hence, it is accessible and can be returned to for speculation and deliberation. The problem with the future is that it has never existed empirically. It can exist only conceptually. There is no ‘‘true future’’, nor will there be. The consequences are intractable, for all we can do about the future perceptually is to imagine, assume or forecast; we can never know.

The strategist is a human being and as such is able to develop and improve his or her knowledge and understanding of the past. He or she can become better and better at answering the questions ‘‘what’’ and ‘‘why’’ as they relate to history. But as to the future the strategist is always faced with a problem. No management art, technique or tool can provide a certain, proven aid for seeing into the future. There is no doctrine guaranteed to improve people’s capabilities in delineating the time to come. Futurology, the science of the future is still in its infancy.

## 6.2. Facts and Õalues

The second pair of complex phenomena consists of facts and values. We cannot think about a strategy with facts or values, only with facts and values. They can both be found in the records of the past as well as in the imagined accounts of the future. But neither of them can just be listed and served up for further treatment and processing. Facts and values are hidden, they overlap and in the first place they must be explicated by the strategist. He or she has to find, define, modify and refine them for strategy design.

The facts are items of knowledge that are true and can be proved; they are reality 65,66 . The values <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> are different: they refer to worth, something which renders anything useful or estimable, to efficacy, importance and excellence 65 . The values of a <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> person or a group are the moral principles and beliefs that they think are important in life and that they tend to live their lives by 66 . What follows<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> from these definitions is that facts for a strategist are something which can be true and that this, at least in principle, is universal: the truth is the truth. Values, however, express something highly problematic for a strategy designer. His or her things or ideas may have value: they might be appropriate or not, or meaningful or not. Thus, a value is something which always returns to the concept of goodness and what is good varies from country to another, one industry to another, one firm to another and one strategist to another.

Facts and values, mixed together, are what the strategist has to cope with in his or her strategy design. The factual area is not the problem. After all, almost all of our education and training ideology is centered on helping us to handle facts. The facts, then, constitute a mental environment in which the strategist can learn to operate more and more effectively with an ever increasing degree of sophistication. It is the values that constitute the problem. Since there is no universal agreement as to just what the values really are as to what they mean and as to how they can be measured, there are no solid or all-embracing methodologies for improving a strategist’s ability to take them into account.

## 6.3. Analysis and synthesis

In a typical case, there are two different thought procedures which every strategist has to rely on when evolving a strategy. When trying to acquire a thoroughgoing knowledge of something, the strategist has to use a lot of analysis, but some synthesis is called for, too. In order to create something new, the strategist has a much greater need of synthesis, but he or she cannot afford to neglect analysis entirely. By analysis we refer to a resolving or separating of a whole into its elements or component parts 65 and <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> scientifically it means an examination of something in order to discover what it consists of 66 . By<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> synthesis, we mean the process of making a whole by putting together its separate component parts; it is the combination of separate elements of thought into a whole 65,66 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Dismantling a whole into parts, then into sub-parts, and perhaps into sub-sub-parts is typical for and essential to analysis. It is also fundamental to all teaching and learning as well as to science in general. In the management world, there is a huge number of methods available for improving a strategist’s analytic skills. Synthesis, though, is a skill or method very different from analysis. Combining separate things into a whole might involve many alternative possibilities, like playing with Lego. Indeed, the situation might be even worse: it might seem that the blocks do not fit together or they might not actually be visible. As to learning, thus, with synthesis a strategist can learn some techniques, just as an author can learn certain techniques that will help him or her to write better or an artist can learn some techniques to paint better, but the ultimate expression, the creation of the whole, is something that lies beyond mere technique.

## 6.4. Logic and rationality Õs. intuition and discoÕery

The problem of strategy design also involves other types of mental activity. It is only natural that the designer would like to be wise, to master logic, and to be as conscious as possible. But something more is needed in addition to this cold power of reason. In strategy design, there are always many ideas and insights, that come into being in a way that we simply cannot adequately account for. Thus, there is clearly logic, correct reasoning 65 and logic <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> is connected with rational terms. The designer is being rational when he or she is able to think clearly and to make decisions and judgements that are based on reason rather than emotion 66 . But intuition and<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> discovery are needed, too. Intuitions are unexplained feelings within us that something is true or exists or is valid even when we have no evidence or proof for it 66 . To discover is to see, find or learn for the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> first time 65 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Being rational and logical is mental activity which strategists are able to handle and develop consciously. But how can they be sensibly creative? How can they use their intuition to discover sense for their strategies? These are matters which are not systematically taught in the regular curriculum of any business school.

## 7.1. Strategy-making as management process

Things, items, and concepts should be in order; they ought to be measurable and the activity engaged in should be subject to clear and explicit norms. With this done a real control function is in place. Of course, the other side of the dichotomy should also be important in the management process, but it is the list on the right which makes controlling possible: the past, the facts, analysis and logical reasoning are better suited to serve the control idea than their counterparts, which explains why we locate the management process doctrine slightly to the right on our map in Fig. 6a.

## 7.2. Strategy-making as game-playing

The ideals of strategic game-playing are ‘‘both– and’’. The focus of attention is placed on the gameplaying situation. The perspective is not too long ahead nor too far behind. The strategist needs sensitive antennae to feel what is really going on out here on the fields and among the players. He or she must be able to sense what moves others have already made, what their motives are and what rules they are playing by. What is also required is a supple sharpwitted mind which is making moves in simultaneously both logical and intuitive. Thus, this mode we locate in the middle; a game-player needs a lot from both sides of the dichotomy to be successful.

## 7.3. Strategy-making as leadership

This third mode contrasts strongly with management process when enumerating the areas it wants to discuss. The leadership doctrine accentuates human features such as heroes, rites, rituals, interaction, personal traits, individual acts, motivation, negotiation, visionizing and coaching. All of which does not mean that a leader can dispense help from the right side, just that the focus is clearly on the left side of our diagram.

## 8. Information systems for strategy design

Before matching our knowledge creation synthesis with informations system it is needed to recall that our information systems concept covers all the systems from top level to transaction processing, from traditional MIS to artificial intelligence and from office automation systems to expert systems Žsee, e.g., Refs. 10,11,35,41 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>.

## 8.1. Strategy-making as management process

Strategy-making as management process provides the best arena for information systems thinking since their world views are almost identical and emphasizing ‘‘the right side abilities’’. Firstly, the subjects of both the information systems approach and the management process approach can be considered as systems, entireties made of elements, with inputs and outputs and with steering activities between those inputs and outputs. Secondly, the control idea dominates both worlds and involves controlling afterwards, controlling beforehand and controlling during the steering process. Thirdly, in order to exercise appropriate and comprehensive control over the subject, a great deal of emphasis is placed on planning, the basic methods of which are hierarchization and sequencing 42 . When planning different kinds of<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> states and descriptions of states e.g., goals, resultsŽ or organizations , hierarchization into sub-elements. and different levels is used. As planning concerns acts and action and their description, sequencing this action into phases and into forms of processes is used 42 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

It is very interesting to note how far-reaching the closeness of these two approaches frequently is. Namely, it is not difficult to find presentations where strategic management process descriptions drawn up by strategy experts like, for instance, Smith et al. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 55 on the one hand, and information systems planning descriptions set out by information systems experts such as Henderson and Sifonis 26 on the <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> other, look almost identical.

## 8.2. Strategy-making as game-playing

In order to connect information system thinking with strategic game-playing, it is first of all necessary to draw a distinction between well-planned and ad hoc game-playing patterns. The former refers to situations where the strategist has the time and will to analyze and synthesize important matters before making the strategic decisions. The ad hoc pattern refers to situations where the strategist’s opportunities for deliberation are restricted to, say, a few minutes or, at the most, a couple of days. It is the well planned pattern which provides an important working arena for information systems thinking.

The future will show how deep into ad hoc game-playing the new tools of information systems thinking can penetrate. There are some additional promising points emerging from information systems literature that will enhance the fusion. For instance, it is a fact that many writers on information systems have for some time now been explicitly and actively interested in the game-playing world and especially in Porter’s classics 8,47 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

## 9. Conclusions

Strategy-making as management process and information systems thinking both have almost identical world views, governing ideals and they rely on similar mental procedures to clarify and solve their problems. Yet there has been very little dialogue between them and so a discourse problem exists: the two parties speak about almost the same phenomena in slightly different discourses. The result is that they talk past each other. My view is that it would be to the information systems developer’s advantage to approach the strategy community and demonstrate how appropriate the tools they have to offer are and convince the strategists of their worth.

Strategy-making as game-playing is still in the formative stage. It probably manifests itself more often in actual practice than it does on the pages of the books written by the strategy theorists. In this area, information systems thinking will have excellent opportunities for broader cooperation if it develops fast, simple and realistic tool-kits for sophisticated managers to use in their games. As far as ad hoc game-playing situations are concerned, realism is even more important. Many good suggestions will be rejected by busy managers if they feel them to be in any way artificial.
