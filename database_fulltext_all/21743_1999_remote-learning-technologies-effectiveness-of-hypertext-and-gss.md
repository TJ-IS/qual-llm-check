# Remote learning technologies: effectiveness of hypertext and GSS

## Abstract

We examine the effectiveness of two important technologies for remote learning. More specifically, we investigate the effect of hypertext on individual learning and the effect of group support systems GSS on collaborative learning. WeŽ . propose and empirically test two models explaining these effects. The first model stipulates that hypertext can support knowledge construction by facilitating the transition from declarative knowledge to structural knowledge. The second model stipulates that GSS enhance knowledge construction by supporting the active participation and cooperation attributes of effective learning. Based on concept mapping techniques, the empirical results confirm predictions of both models. q 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Hypertext; Group support systems; Knowledge construction; Collaborative learning; Knowledge structure; Concept mapping

## 1. Introduction

Information technology has become an enabler for reengineering the pedagogic strategies of the teacher and for widening the contexts within which learning can occur. Teaching is no longer confined to a time and a place. With computer and communication technologies, the time and physical boundaries of the traditional classroom are being stretched to a learning space. A growing number of universities worldwide are now offering virtual education programs. Several companies are also providing on-line training for their employees. A simple search on the Web will result in hundreds of sites offering virtual courses or resources for developing and delivering such courses. Distance education, virtual classes, and online training are just a few of the multitude of terms that are used to describe different implementations of this technology-enabled learning space. Although the interface, the format and structure of these products may differ, some common characteristics seem to be emerging. Most products seem to fit a web-based, asynchronous, collaborative, hypermedia framework. Hypertext and collaborative technologies e.g., group Ž support systems GSS constitute an important en- Ž .. abler of most of the learning space implementations. Previous studies have identified some potential positive effects of hypertext and GSS on learning, but without producing models for explaining these effects. Modeling the effects of hypertext and GSS on learning will enable us to understand how these two technologies can complement each other, develop better design guidelines and implement more effective learning spaces.

To study the effect of these technologies on learning, we have to first define ‘‘learning’’. Summarizing several studies on learning in the cognitive and educational psychology literature, Entwistle and Entwistle 8 identified two components: a personal<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> component and a social component. Learning is personal in that it depends on the ability of the learner to assimilate and interpret new information. The prior knowledge of the learner determines how the new information will be interpreted 13 . It is, how-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ever, the way in which prior knowledge is organized rather than merely the amount that is important 2 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Learning has also a social component. Pask 27 , in his conversation theory of learning, suggests that understanding can be built up through conversation with other individuals about the subject. In social conversations, meaning is negotiated and shared. Tan <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 35 suggests that effective communication in conversation is critical to the development of individual understanding and that effective communication also enhances shared understanding between individuals. Information technology can enhance both components of learning. As illustrated in Fig. 1, this study focuses on the contribution of hypertext to the personal component and the contribution of GSS to the social component. Based on well-established theories of learning, we developed two models that describe and hence predict the effects of hypertext and GSS on learning. We then conducted two empirical studies to verify the models’ predictions. The first empirical study examined the contribution of hypertext to knowledge construction. The second empirical study, on the other hand, examined the effect of GSS on the knowledge structure acquired by the participants.

The paper is organized as follows. First, we propose and justify a model explaining the potential effects of hypertext on knowledge construction. This is followed by a description of an empirical study conducted to verify the predictions of this model. We then present another model explaining the potential effects of GSS on the learner’s knowledge structure and describe an empirical study designed to test the second model. This is followed by a discussion of the results of the two empirical studies. Finally, we conclude the paper by highlighting the implications of our findings and discussing future research directions.

## 2. Contribution of hypertext to individual learning

Traditional computer-based learning CBL sys-Ž . tems are linear in their format and hence do not support the associative nature of the human mind. They offer very little referential branching that is slow and inconvenient to follow. The inadequacy of linear text for representing referential and associative links is one of the main arguments for the development of hypertext-based learning systems. Several studies demonstrated empirically the superiority of hypertext knowledge representations over linear representation in supporting learning e.g., Refs. 16– Ž 19,22,23,33,34 . However, it is not sufficient to<sup>x</sup>. establish empirically that a given hypertext contributed to a higher level of learning than a given linear text. Such results do not contribute much to the development of guidelines for designing hypertext systems and for using them effectively. To be useful, the empirical results should not only identify the effects of hypertext systems but also explain them. In this research, we investigate theories that allow us to explain the main effects of hypertext on learning. Based on these theories, we propose a model that describes and hence predicts under what situations hypertext can enhance individual learning.

Our research is based on the cognitive learning theory which assumes that learners interact with new information, interpret it and build new personal knowledge representations by relating the new information to their prior knowledge. Information itself is not knowledge, but rather the stimuli. Knowledge is constructed through the cognitive processing of information. Our research is also based on the constructivist epistemology, which views knowledge not as information transmitted from the teacher ob-Ž jectivism , but rather as the result of the active. engagement of the learner in the comprehension and conception of the information. Hence, we consider learning as generative. The learner actively constructs new knowledge. Generative processing of information takes place when the learner assigns meaning to new information by relating it to prior knowledge 39 . The underlying assumption of our<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> study is that hypertext is a cognitive tool that actively engages the learner in knowledge construction.

Different levels of knowledge can be identified: declaratiÕe, structural and procedural. The lowest level, declarative knowledge, refers to the awareness of some object, event or idea. The highest level, procedural knowledge, describes the use or application of the declarative knowledge 31 . Structural<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> knowledge, on the other hand, is an intermediate knowledge mediating the translation of declarative knowledge into procedural. It refers to the learner’s awareness of his<sup>r</sup>her knowledge structure or mental model. It can be viewed as the knowledge of how concepts within a domain are interrelated 7 . It<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> involves the construction of relationships that integrate the declarative knowledge into useful mental models. Preece 29 defines it as the cognitive struc-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ture or the pattern of relationships among concepts in memory. Structural knowledge has also been referred to as internal connectedness, integrative understanding and as conceptual knowledge. The acquisition of structural knowledge facilitates recall and mediates the acquisition of procedural knowledge 16 . How-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ever, it is important to notice that although the categorization of knowledge into declarative, structural and procedural can explain most performances, it is not always adequate. Certain performances may be based on tacit knowledge 28 or may have be-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> come so automatic that the performer no longer activates the knowledge originally required for the performance. In this research, however, we are interested in the construction of explicit knowledge.

In our hypotheses, we stipulate that hypertext facilitates the acquisition of structural knowledge. Although hypertext can support the entire process of knowledge construction, we focus on what is seemingly the most important effect of hypertext: facilitating the transition from declarative to structural knowledge.

Hypothesis: Learners who use hypertext will acquire more structural knowledge than will those who use linear text.

The positive relationship between the hypertext information representation and the acquisition of structural knowledge by the user can be attributed to the explicit illustration of relationships among concepts with physical links in the hypertext navigational structure. This premise is consistent with the framework proposed by Mayer 21 for studying how<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> to organize information in order to improve learning. According to Mayer’s framework, the way information is organized and presented affects the retention and recall of different types of knowledge: descriptive knowledge, explanative knowledge and creative problem solving. Mayer’s definition of explanative knowledge is similar to that of structural knowledge. Like structural knowledge, explanative knowledge refers to knowledge in which relationships among different concepts are expressed. Summarizing several previous studies, Mayer 21 concluded that the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> use of clues to help one integrate information in a macrostructure improves the acquisition of explanative knowledge. The hyperlinks, when they carry a meaning, represent additional clues that help the learner integrate specific concepts into more general concepts or macrostructure. In other words, the hypertext navigational structure, when designed according to a coherent mental model the links representŽ meaningful relationships between concepts , pro-. vides a framework for the construction of structural knowledge.

## 2.2. Measurement of structural knowledge

One way of representing the structural knowledge acquired by a given learner is through the usage of concept mapping techniques 24 . A concept map is a<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> graphical representation of meaningful relationships between concepts. It is a semantic network describing a cognitive structure: ideas and their interrelationships. Concept maps can be used to describe a person’s structural knowledge 15 . With concept<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> maps, learners can develop a schematic representation of their mental model regarding a particular knowledge domain through the externalization and visualization of concepts and meaningful relationships between them. With concept mapping, each key concept can be represented as a hierarchy of concepts going from a higher level of abstraction Ž . Ž general concept to lower and lower levels specific concepts, examples, objects and events . As illus-. trated in Fig. 2, a concept map consists of a graph where the nodes represent the concepts at different levels of abstraction and the links and cross-links represent meaningful relationships between these concepts. Links relate concepts that belong to the same hierarchy. They are useful for defining general concepts in terms of more specific concepts. Crosslinks, on the other hand, relate concepts from different hierarchies. They are useful for representing meaningful relationships between different concepts. Cross-links are usually more difficult to learn than direct links.

Concept mapping is an accepted method for monitoring student comprehension 12 . Novak and Staff<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 25 proposed a number of strategies for helping students to construct their own concept maps. A simple method is to supply the students with a list of related concepts and ask them to construct a map, placing the most inclusive, most general concept at the top and then showing successively less inclusive concepts at lower positions on a hierarchy. The students must decide how to construct the hierarchy and what words or expressions to use to link theŽ . concepts together. We adopted this simple concept mapping method to measure the structural knowledge acquired by the subjects of our empirical study. The subject’s knowledge structure, as represented by his<sup>r</sup>her concept map, can be characterized by its complexity and its level of integration intercon-Ž nectedness . Complexity and integration are the main . characteristics that differentiate the knowledge structure of the expert from that of the novice. More able individuals have richer, more interconnected knowledge structures than do less able individuals 5 . As<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> expertise is attained through learning, the elements of knowledge become increasingly interconnected <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 30 . As a measure of the complexity of a knowledge structure, we used the total number of valid direct links in the associated concept map. To measure the integration of the knowledge structure, on the other hand, we used the total number of valid cross-links. ## 2.3. Systems design

The hard copy, the hypertext and the computer linear text versions of the lecture notes all had identical content. The main difference between the linear and the hypertext versions was in making the relationships between various concepts explicit in the hypertext through links between the screens<sup>r</sup> windows describing related concepts. The navigational structure of the hypertext system was designed according to the mental model of the instructor of the course, as represented by her concept map. Hence, the mental model of the instructor, as represented in the navigational structure, provided a framework to the subjects of group 3 for constructing structural knowledge. Key and general concepts were described in separate screens one screen per concept . Ž . Pop-up windows were used to describe additional specific concepts or<sup>r</sup>and examples used to define the general concepts. These windows could be triggered by highlighted words<sup>r</sup>expressions in the screens representing the parent concepts. The words<sup>r</sup>expressions were carefully selected to reflect as accurately as possible the meaning of the links. The full path of triggered windows was always displayed on the computer screen. This has been achieved by cascading superimposing downwardsŽ . the visited windows. In addition to alleviating the lost-in-space problem, this feature also made visible the hierarchy of specific concepts defining a general concept. Relationships between concepts that did not belong to the same hierarchy cross-links were rep-Ž . resented by allowing different screens<sup>r</sup>windows to call the same windows.

## 2.4. Experimental procedure

The computer linear text and the hypertext were installed on a PC LAN that was dedicated to the experiment. The students could access the systems at anytime. Students of groups 2 and 3 were given passwords providing them access to their assigned system linear text for group 2 and hypertext for Ž group 3 . The passwords were also used by the . systems to create protocol files keeping track of the screens explored by each student and the time spent on every screen.

The exams consisted of concept mapping questions. The students were given a list of concepts and were asked to construct a concept map illustrating as many meaningful relationships as possible between the given concepts. All relationships had to be labeled with an expression indicating their meaning. Prior to the experiment, students were familiarized with concept mapping through a number of exercises. The complexity number of valid links andŽ . integration number of valid cross-links of the con-Ž . cept maps produced by the subjects were evaluated by two different experts professors who taught theŽ course before . Since the hypertext navigational. structure was designed according to the concept map of the instructor of the course, the instructor did not participate in the evaluation process so as not to bias the results. The scores given by the two markers were averaged. Hence, every subject ended up with two average scores for each exam: map complexity score and map integration score.

A total of five exams covering five different lectures were administered. The students were given 1 week after every lecture to prepare for an exam on that lecture. The first exam was used as a pre-treatment test of possible group differences. All three groups had only hard copies of the lecture notes to prepare for the first exam.

## 4. Discussion

The results of the first experiment indicate that the advantage of hypertext over linear text both softŽ and hard reported in earlier studies can be attributed. at least in part to the effect of hypertext information organization on the structure of the knowledge acquired by the user. Hypertext users were able to acquire more sophisticated mental models as measured by the complexity and integration of the knowledge structures represented in the users’ concept maps. We argue that such results can be explained by the ability of hypertext to convey the mental model of an expert to the learner in a more explicit way through its non-linear navigational structure. While the main concepts of the covered knowledge domain can be described in the screens<sup>r</sup>windows nodes of the hypertext system , Ž . the propositions relationships between concepts can Ž . be represented explicitly in the hyperlinks. There is a lack of guidelines for designing the navigational structure of hypertext in general and hypercourseware in particular. The relative effectiveness of a navigational structure designed according to the instructor’s mental model, demonstrated in this study, indicates that one possible design guideline is the explicit representation of a ‘‘referent’’ mental model using concept mapping techniques. Based on our preliminary results, future research can develop more specific navigational structure design guidelines. The importance of such research stems from the proliferation of hypertext information organization through Internet and Intranet systems.

The results of the second experiment indicate that GSS can enhance the mental models of the participants in a collaborative learning environment. Actually, any meeting can be considered as a collaborative learning experience where the participants exchange information that can be useful for validating or enhancing their mental models. We argue that the positive effects of GSS can be attributed to enhanced group process gains, i.e., more active participation, more feedback, more objective evaluation and alleviated process losses, i.e., less domination, less air-time fragmentation and less evaluation apprehension. These enhanced process gains and alleviated process losses in turn contribute to two important attributes of effective learning: active engagement and cooperation. One limitation of this study, however, is that its results cannot be generalized to any GSS-supported meeting. The subjects of the experiments were homogeneous similar background and hadŽ . sufficient prior knowledge in the subject area to lead a meaningful discussion. Active engagement and cooperation contribute to more effective learning only as far as the participants have relevant knowledge to share. To be effective, collaborative learning must be preceded by individual learning. As illustrated in Fig. 6, hypertext and GSS can actually complement each other in a two-stage learning cycle. The contribution of hypertext is mainly in the initial stage of learning where the learner acquires a preliminary knowledge structure of the subject area, preferably based on a referent mental model derived from an expert. The contribution of GSS, on the other, is mainly in the second stage of the learning cycle, where the learners exchange information to validate and enhance their initial mental models.

## 5. Conclusion

In this research, we have examined some of the potential contributions of information technology to learning. We have explained and demonstrated the effect of hypertext on the personal component of learning and the effect of GSS on the social component of learning.

To explain the contribution of hypertext to personal knowledge construction, we developed a model grounded in cognitive learning theories. The main premise of our research model is that, compared to linear text, hypertext improves the transition from declarative knowledge to structural knowledge. The justification of this premise is that the hypertext navigational structure provides the users with a framework for knowledge construction. The links represent structural clues that facilitate the acquisition, organization and subsequent recall of knowledge. The results of our first empirical study support the premise of our model. Compared to both hard and soft linear text, hypertext improves the students acquisition of structural knowledge, as measured by concept maps.

The results of this study have important implications for the design and usage of hypertext systems in training and education. The importance of the navigational structure of hypertext in knowledge construction implies that more care should be exercised in the design of these structures. Better design guidelines and methodologies should be developed. The role that concept-mapping techniques can play in designing the hypertext navigational structure should be explored further. In this study, the mental model of the instructor, as represented by her concept map, was used to design the navigational structure of the hypertext. The explicit representation of the instructor’s mental model provides the learner with a framework for constructing his<sup>r</sup>her own knowledge. Some researchers, e.g., Jonassen 15 , argue that such an approach prevents the learner from constructing new knowledge based on faulty models. It can, however, limit the learner’s creativity and the possibility of constructing novel knowledge structures.

To explain the effect of GSS on knowledge acquisition, we developed a second model that integrates GSS research with cognitive learning research. Our model stipulates that the GSS enhancement of certain group process gains and its alleviation of certain group process losses contribute to the main attributes of an effective learning environment, i.e., active engagement and cooperation. Particularly, the enhancement of the cooperation attribute creates an environment where the feedback received from the other participants helps the learner to validate and reorganize his<sup>r</sup>her knowledge leading to the development of a more sophisticated knowledge structure. The results of our experiment empirical study 2Ž . confirm the expected positive effects of GSS on learning. The GSS users were able to construct a more complex and better-integrated knowledge structure, as measured by concept maps. But, should we always expect such effects, regardless of whether the participants have sufficient knowledge to share in the first place? How does the learner’s prior knowledge affect the effect of GSS? What happens to the group dynamics when the participants come from different backgrounds? These are important questions that future research should address.

The potential limitations of hypertext limitingŽ creativity and GSS insufficient knowledge to share. Ž . can be alleviated by integrating these two technologies: collaborative hypertext. In a collaborative hypertext environment, the learners start with a referent mental model derived from experts in the knowledge domain and then build more complex structures by introducing changes to the navigational structure under the moderation of an ‘‘expert’’. With collaborative hypertext, both components of learning per- Ž sonal and social can be supported simultaneously. . The potential of such an environment remains to be investigated.
