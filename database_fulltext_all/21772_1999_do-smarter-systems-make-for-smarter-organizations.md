# Do smarter systems make for smarter organizations?

Paul H.J. Hendriks )

Nijmegen Business School, UniÕersity of Nijmegen, P.O. Box 9108, NL-6500 HK, Nijmegen, Netherlands

## Abstract

This article explores the role of knowledge-based systems KBS in knowledge management KM . KM is considered anŽ . Ž . umbrella term, incorporating various issues related to the value of knowledge as a production factor. This study assesses three research themes which together address the main object of KM. First, how do the introduction and utilization of KBS affect the organizational core competencies? Secondly, is the knowledge content of products and services affected by KBS? Thirdly, how do these systems affect job quality? The results of an empirical investigation in 24 knowledge-intensive, commercial organizations show that KBS usually have a positive impact on organizational competencies. They reveal that KBS may both threaten and enhance the knowledge content of certain tasks. They also show that, in general, the introduction of KBS leads to a slight decrease in job quality. q 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Organizational impact of knowledge-based systems; Knowledge engineering; Knowledge management; Core competencies; Job quality

## 1. Introduction

Knowledge-based systems KBS are nowadaysŽ . part and parcel of any discussion on the potential value of information and communication technology Ž . Ž . ICT for knowledge management KM . When these systems are related to KM, a certain tendency exists to regard them as failed attempts to replicate human expertise e.g., Ref. 33 . As such, they are associ-Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. ated with their historical background, research on artificial intelligence, and with the fact that ‘‘ . . . neither expert systems nor any other branch of artificial intelligence has lived up to its predictions’’ ŽRef. 10 , p. 126 . Consequently, these systems are <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> . treated as not inherently different from popular technologies used in KM, such as groupware for knowledge sharing or document management systems for disclosing knowledge sources see, for example, Ref.Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 12 ..

This approach is open to criticism. It takes one possible definition of KBS, i.e., the human-centred definition, as its starting point, ignoring questions concerning the validity of this definition. Treating KBS as one more form of ICT introduces the risk of undervaluing the specific potential of KBS for KM, as well as the distinctive threats their introduction may involve. Here, an alternative definition is used which focuses on knowledge modelling see also Ž Ref. 18 . KBS are defined as systems that capture <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. basic conceptual schemata through some kind of knowledge representation formalism. In other words, KBS are ‘smart systems’, with the degree of their smartness depending on the degree in which knowledge is successfully modelled in their knowledge base. Given this definition, first generation expert systems 20 based on the representation of ‘surface<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> knowledge’ do not qualify as KBS. Neural networks, case-based reasoning, as well as second-generation expert systems, representing ‘deep knowledge’, do qualify as such. Introducing a KBS in an organization has therefore certain implications for how the organization explicates, structures and codifies its knowledge. The potential of KBS lies in the concepts, methods and tools offered for defining knowledge models and in the improved access to knowledge once it has been stored in the knowledge base. The main risks involved are related to the fact that explicating and structuring knowledge is necessarily a limited, perhaps even biased, operation, as it ignores the possibly essential tacit or unstructured aspects of knowledge. The risks associated with explicating and structuring are perhaps even more severe in KBS. Research has shown that knowledge, once it is stored in a KBS, is taken for granted, and that its validity is much less actively questioned 11 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

KM refers to the explicit attention for the knowledge intensity of an organization a more encompass-Ž ing definition is explored in Section 2 . It is gener-. ally aimed at making the organization smarter, or at least keeping it as smart as it is. Since KBS affect how the organization deals with its knowledge, a relevant issue for the KM is how the use of KBS fits in with KM goals. The question to be addressed then is whether the smartness of KBS matches the smartness of the organization that KM aims at. The objective of this article is to explore these relations between KBS and KM, both theoretically and empirically. For this purpose an empirical investigation will be presented of 24 organizations that have recently Ž . less than 3 years ago introduced a KBS.

The article is organized as follows. In Section 2, the broadness of the KM concept will be explored, resulting in the identification of three main discussion areas contributing to the general discussion on KM. Next, we will address the question of how to relate KBS to KM. Section 4 presents the research plan of the empirical investigation of this relation. Sections 5–7 describe the outcomes of the investigation following the three discussion areas identified in Section 2: the value of KBS for the organizational competencies, their impact on the knowledge content of tasks and their impact on job quality. At the end of this article, we will comment on the combination of the three areas and briefly outline the strong and weak points of the study.

## 2. Knowledge management: an umbrella concept

The term ‘knowledge management’ KM is aŽ . source of confusion. It is used in various senses and various contexts. At one end of the scale, discussions concerning KM include very practical, down-to-earth concerns such as managing an intranet for knowledge sharing or drawing the knowledge map of an organization. At the other end, there are the more theoretical and abstract concerns, including discussions about how and when learning in organizations occurs and discourses on organizational knowledge creation. An important source of the confusion surrounding KM is the fact that KM also involves several contributory discussions which lack clear and unanimously accepted definitions e.g., discussionsŽ concerning organizational learning, intelligent organizations, organizational competencies, human talent management, or professionalism . Also, the meaning. of ‘management’ in KM is interpreted in myriad ways, ranging from broad perspectives addressing theoretical and conceptual foundations of coordination tasks, to more narrow interpretations in quality management, auditing, or in approaches addressing the value of knowledge assets on the balance sheet. Although several authors embrace the view that KM is a broad concept, a satisfactory overview of alternative conceptions of the concept is still lacking. Attempts to present such overviews are almost invariably limited in scope, highlighting some approaches while ignoring others, and fail to assess how the contributions relate to each other e.g., Ref. 44 . AsŽ  . a result definitions of KM are either vague and lack directive qualities for further understanding, or in their specificity address only some individual elements of KM, thus failing to encompass the broad KM picture. In short, KM is still on its way to maturity, with most developments contained within specific, individual approaches rather than between or across alternative perspectives.

KM thus appears as a ‘confusion of confusions’, covering in some unclear way several competing issues and approaches which are sometimes ambiguous themselves. In this article, I therefore try to avoid both vagueness and narrowness in defining KM. In my view, a satisfactory conception of KM is only possible if KM is recognized as an umbrella term integrating a great many theoretical and practical notions. The common denominator of these notions is that they concern the organizational value of knowledge, with the ‘management’ component indicating that the focus on organizational knowledge should, eventually, lead to a greater insight into possible management measures. Three issues may be presented to elaborate this basic definition aimed at removing some of the equivocality while doing justice to the umbrella character of the KM concept.

First of all, the organizational value of knowledge has been identified on the basis of the fact that organizations, because of their knowledge, have the capacity to distinguish themselves within their com petitive environment. Distinction then refers to an ongoing process, and not to the outcome of a one-time exercise. Knowledge is seen as a resource, not as an asset in the accounting sense 4,14 . It is particu-Ž . <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> larly seen as a resource stressing ‘doing’ rather than ‘having’ 13 . This line of thought leads to a compe- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tence perspective on organizations as distinguishedŽ from, or complementary to, the product-market, strategic business unit, or brand share perspectives and the like see Refs. 15,29 . More particularly, it. Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. leads to discussions at the strategic level, because the organizational value of knowledge specifically surfaces at this level. Not all resources are strategically important 4 , but knowledge clearly is or can be .  Ž . Knowledge is the basis for defining and redefining what the organization itself stands for and how it environment is conceived. Through collective learning processes, knowledge may help ensure a sustainable competitive advantage as opposed to a coinci-Ž dental, short-term advantage 15,16,26 . In the dis-. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> cussions referred to, knowledge is explicitly linked to the core competencies of a firm. As organizational competencies, these are defined by the fact that they are pooled competencies and therefore distinct from <sup>w x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>14 , but linked to 22,29 , the competencies of indi viduals. As core competencies, they are also characterized by the fact that they create customer value, allow competitor differentiation, and offer a gateway to new markets 14 . Thinking in terms of core<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> competencies makes it possible to conceive the organization as largely independent of current products and services, by focusing on the rationale underlying the current business. Sony, for instance, conceives its core competence as ‘miniaturization’, a much more future-oriented and thought-provoking characterization than, for instance, ‘producer of user electronics’.

The strategic level defines a major focal point in KM. At this level KM can be understood as competence management. Measures and instruments for managing knowledge at this level refer to such issues as how to uncover the competencies of a firm and its competition, address practical issues of competence analysis and strategy formulation, and build competency frameworks, etc. 6,21,24 . Competency man-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> agement can be considered as a distinct task. Ericsson, the Swedish telecommunications company, for instance, has introduced the position of competency manager. The task of competency managers is to assess the competencies asked for by the market and to link those to individual competencies available in the firm.

The second point to be assessed concerns the fact that strategic benefits of knowledge can only be reaped if the full potential of the knowledge within the organization is realized at the operational level. The concept of the organizational value of knowledge at the operational level has, for instance, been elaborated in the concept of the knowledge value chain, comprising the following six classes of activities: assessment of current knowledge, acquisition of required knowledge, knowledge development, knowledge distribution, knowledge application, and knowledge evaluation 40,41 . The idea of a knowl-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> edge value chain helps define the concept of ‘knowledge potential’. An organization may ask itself whether its knowledge achieves its full potential with respect to each individual link of the knowledge value chain. It may, for instance, wonder if the available knowledge is used effectively for creating new knowledge stage 3: knowledge development ,Ž . or if creative thinking is sufficiently shared within and between groups stage 4: knowledge distribu- Ž tion . The organization may also ask itself whether. the individual links fulfil the potential of making a true knowledge value chain. It may, for instance, wonder if the right knowledge is distributed in the most appropriate form stage 4: knowledge distribu-Ž tion to stimulate both learning stage 3: knowledge. Ž development and unlearning stage 6: knowledge . Ž evaluation . Many KM efforts of organizations are. aimed at ensuring that knowledge achieves its potential. Some of these concern efforts within the individual stages. For instance, the pharmaceutical company Glaxo Wellcome has a separate Business Intelligence department whose primary task is to scan the environment for relevant data stage 2: acquisition ofŽ required knowledge . Other efforts are made across. stages. Hoffman-LaRoche, another pharmaceutical company, has, for instance, introduced electronic yellow pages to provide a catalogue to people via their areas of expertise. These yellow pages are a means to retain knowledge stage 1: assessment ofŽ current knowledge , promote its dispersal stage 4: . Ž distributing knowledge and encourage contacts dur-. ing knowledge creation stage 3: knowledge devel-Ž opment ..

The role of KM at the operational level is to ensure that the conditions for an optimal operation of these knowledge functions are met with reference toŽ the strategic goals of KM . The specific challenge. for the knowledge manager is to guarantee that the concern for the organizational value of knowledge is based on a sufficiently rich conception of knowledge. This implies that, in studying knowledge functions and designing KM measures, adequate attention should be given to the combined aspects of skill and cognition. A frequently mentioned distinction relevant within this context is the distinction between tacit and articulated knowledge 27 . The particular<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> relevance of this distinction is based on the fact that — as opposed to other resources and production factors — knowledge does not primarily derive its strategic organizational value from its presence but from its potential to generate new knowledge. Particularly for knowledge creation, the relevance of the tacit dimension of knowledge has been stressed 26 .<sup>w x</sup>

The third issue concerning the nature of KM is that, whereas knowledge at the organizational level — or shared knowledge — defines the focus of KM, knowledge in its true sense does only exist at the individual level. If individuals do not possess or acquire the appropriate knowledge, neither will the organization as a whole. At the group level the concept of knowledge is used in a derived, metaphorical sense. An organization may only hope to gain actual competitive advantage from its core competencies if these are matched by the individual competencies of knowledge workers 29 . In other words,<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> the knowledge ambition of an organization i.e., theŽ competencies it wishes to possess in the future is. only viable if it is accepted on a sufficiently wide scale in the organization and if the necessary skills and fields of expertise are present in the current workforce or can be acquired by hiring new personnel or through specific learning activities. Also, operational objectives such as retaining, sharing or developing knowledge should not only be studied at the organizational level, but also in relation to their relevance for the individuals using, sharing, developing knowledge. In KM it is therefore essential to address the tension between individual ambitions of knowledge workers and the shared ambition of the organization. This implies that the attention given to measures aimed at explicating and directing personal ambitions and improving their effect on the shared ambitions is a key issue on the agenda of the knowledge manager most of such measures will belong to Ž the realm of human resources management and include, for instance, the use of competency frameworks; see Ref. 24 . Furthermore, the crucial role<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. of the individual knowledge worker in knowledge-intensive processes and organizations, in relation to the specific nature of knowledge work, has also implications for possible structural measures. It has distinct implications for structuring knowledge processes Ži.e., processes whose input, throughput and output is knowledge; these processes defy engineering; see Ref. 9 , and knowledge organizations i.e., organi- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. Ž zations whose primary processes are predominantly knowledge processes; variety instead of routines should be institutionalized in such firms; see Ref. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 25 ..

In summary, KM is defined here as the concern for maintaining and enhancing the effectiveness of knowledge as a production factor, eventually resulting in greater insight into possible measures and instruments aimed at:

– developing and maintaining the core competencies of the organization,

– enhancing the value and effectiveness of knowledge in the production of products and services of the organization, and

– ensuring the conditions for individual knowledge workers to fulfil their personal ambitions, while aiming to match these ambitions with the overall knowledge ambition of the organization.

The introduction of KBS within an organization represents one possible measure or instrument in terms of KM. The given definition of KM suggests that this introduction will have to be studied to assess its consequences on all three dimensions.

## 3. The role and value of knowledge-based systems in knowledge management

When linking KBS to KM, the fundamental question is how to define a perspective that adequately reflects both the broadness of the KM concept and the specific features of KBS as defined in SectionŽ 1 . Several classes of studies may be relevant as . potential sources of inspiration when addressing this question. Firstly, some studies present a framework for identifying ICT applications that may prove useful in KM such applications are summarized underŽ the heading ‘techknowledgy’, e.g., in Ref. 10 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. KBS are usually mentioned as an example of these ICT applications. One class of these frameworks simply arranges types of techknowledgy by distinguishing certain features such as active vs. passiveŽ support without specifically relating these to KM. Žsee Ref. 33 . Another class links ICT applications<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. to specific KM functions see Ref. 10 . Frame-Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. works in the latter class define KM functions at the operational level, thereby focusing only on a subset of KM concerns. To my knowledge, no studies are available that do justice to the broadness of the KM concept.

Secondly, the KBS literature also addresses some issues relevant to KM. Two subclasses may be discerned here. The first subclass consists of various authors who have studied the organizational impact of KBS e.g., Refs. 2,19,36,37 ; for an overview,Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> see Refs. 17 . However, as a source of inspiration <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. for assessing how KBS may prove valuable or obstructive in KM, these studies have only limited value. They do not provide an integral picture of the effects of the introduction of KBS as far as organizational knowledge is concerned. Some individual elements in these studies are nonetheless clearly relevant within the present context, such as the impacts on job quality or decision quality. These will be assessed in later sections. The second subclass consists of several studies that explicitly examine the value of KBS for KM. These include studies propagating the use of knowledge engineering KE meth-Ž . ods and principles as inspiration for KM 43 . They also include discussions on how KBS may facilitate knowledge explication, which, in turn, is defined as an important focus area for KM 30,34 . There are<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> also studies that present KBS as more intelligent forms of ‘techknowledgy’, relating them loosely to such KM functions as knowledge retention or knowledge sharing 35 . However, none of these contribu-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tions recognize the broadness of the KM concept identified in Section 2. Thus, they do not identify the risk that KBS may be propagated as valuable for KM in one area e.g., the operational arena , while prov-Ž . ing counter-productive in other respects e.g., in the Ž HRM arena . What seems lacking, therefore, is an . approach to assess what impact KBS have within the integrated picture on knowledge-intensive work the KM perspective intends to promote.

The question then is how such a broader perspective can be constructed. This paper does not aim at answering this question in full, but, more modestly, to explore some elements that may constitute an answer. It is more important to get the questions right than to provide any definite answers in this respect. To explore a possible elaboration of these questions, an empirical investigation will be presented below. Its primary aim is to clarify the concepts presented above and show how these may provide pegs for linking KBS and the smartness of organizations, and thus to provide inspiration for conceiving a more encompassing perspective on the relation between the smartness of KBS and organizations. The discussion above suggests that the introduction of KBS should at least be studied with regard to the impact of these systems in the following three instances:

– at the strategic level; how KBS affects the position of the organization in relation to its environment this may be assessed by linking their Ž introduction to the core competencies of the organization ;.

– at the operational level; how the richness of knowledge in processes and products of the organization is affected;

– at the level of the knowledge workers; how the introduction of KBS may increase or decrease the tension between individual and organizational ambition.

As an important step towards a more integral approach, these three definition elements may provide a stepping stone for designating indicators that may help to assess the value of KBS for KM. Once an acceptable picture for each of these dimensions is provided, an overall evaluation may be made by combining the outcomes.

## 4. A survey

The relation between KBS and KM was explored in an empirical investigation. This enquiry was of an exploratory nature, and not aimed at painting a representative picture of KBS use in organizations. A sample was constructed based on several guiding principles. First, cases were selected based on the recency of KBS introduction more than 1 year ago,Ž less than 3 years . This was done to allow a compari-. son between the situations before and after KBS introduction. Secondly, only commercial organizations were included in the sample. The reason for this is the fact that the concept of environment, which is very important for the discussion of core competencies, takes on a different meaning for governmental bodies. Thirdly, the aim was to ensure sufficient variation in the sample, not only including examples of the well-known types of KBS users Ž . banks, insurance companies , but also instances of KBS use in process industry, wholesale business, etc. Several mechanisms were used to detect suitable cases, including snowball sampling, enquiries in umbrella organizations, etc. Firms that had participated in previous surveys were also approached. The final selection included 28 organizations that had implemented one or more KBS in the last 3 years. Of these, 24 agreed to take part in the research. Tables 1 and 2 give a characterization of the investigated organizations. The managers responsible for the knowledge domain in which the KBS was operative were interviewed.

During the introductory section of the interview, we gathered general data on the nature of the KBS, its development and implementation history, its current use, and the relevant plans for the future. For the main part of the interview, we focused on how the systems affected the core competencies of the organization, the way exceptions were handled, and job quality. To avoid asking the interviewees directly for the effects on these three concepts, which is unsatisfactory for obvious reasons, we used the following procedure. For each of the three concepts, a set of indicators was selected the indicators are presentedŽ in Tables 3, 5 and 7; the rationale for their selection will be discussed in Sections 5–7 . These predefined. indicators provided focal points for an open discussion with the interviewees, resulting in a verbal, qualitative account of the variables related to the three concepts. To conclude these accounts, the interviewees were asked to rate the individual indicators on a five-point scale, ranging from strongly improved to strongly decreased by KBS. All interviews were conducted by two interviewers. In a separate analysis based on the written account of the interviews, the organization was evaluated with regard to the three concepts e.g., did job qualityŽ improve, deteriorate, or remain the same? . These. evaluations were carried out independently by the two interviewers and the author who acted as coordinator of the research project. In almost all cases these evaluations resulted in the same classification. Any discrepancies were resolved in a subsequent discussion based on a thorough reexamination of the research material. The alternative procedure of using weighted scores on individual items and some kind of combination rule was rejected because such a procedure does not do justice to the intricate ways in which individual items may be related.

The empirical research described here has some limitations. Firstly, each of the three dimensions was studied with a limited set of indicators which willŽ be presented in Sections 5–7 . Other indicators, as. well as the relations between these indicators, require further study. Secondly, the investigation focused on the impacts within each of the three dimensions of KM, thus ignoring their interrelations. Studying these interrelations is an important area for further research. Some first steps in studying these interrelations will be discussed in Section 8. Thirdly, the data collection method of asking system managers has some drawbacks. Recording their opinions is but a first step in drawing an integral picture. It may also prove worthwhile to approach other parties involved, particularly system users and strategic management. Fourthly, the value of interviews in assessing KBS impacts is limited. They should be amplified with other methods of measurement, including longitudinal research, participatory observation, and more objective measurement methods. Fifthly, the validity of the data relies on the adequacy of the respondents’ memory. Even if the recollections asked for go back no further than 3 years, caution is called for in interpreting them. They may well be coloured by more recent developments or distorted by memory gaps. One should therefore not draw any definite conclusions based on the outcomes of the investigation. The results should primarily be read as an illustration and an elaboration of the conceptual discussion presented above.

## 6. Making exceptions

Because KBS are based on modelling knowledge, they stress a particular aspect of knowledge while ignoring others e.g., tacit, unstructured knowledge .Ž . The usefulness of a KBS does, therefore, not only refer to how successfully knowledge was modelled, but also to the degree in which the knowledge entered into the system is complete and needs support from other knowledge types. KBS users should always be aware that KBS ‘‘do not know the limits of their own expertise’’ Ref. 42 , p. 53 . In the Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> . research, insight into the completeness of the knowledge involved in the task processes was sought by studying how exceptions were dealt with before and after the introduction of KBS. Detecting exceptions and knowing how to handle them require more knowledge than is needed for operating the system. An exception is not just a situation that is not covered by the rules, predicates or structured case descriptions stored in the KBS. An exception may also point to a case in which unwanted side-effects of correctly applied formal knowledge occur, perhaps referring to higher order goals not represented in the system. The way an organization deals with exceptions may change significantly after the introduction of KBS 36 . Studies show that the introduc-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> tion of KBS may lead to a significant decrease in the freedom of individuals to handle exceptions 48 . In<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> other words, because of the KBS, supervision may considerably increase when handling exceptions 19 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> There is also some evidence that exceptions are more difficult to identify because of the KBS 1 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

## 7. Job quality

The third aspect of the relation between KBS and KM concerns the human factor in knowledge work. This aspect was addressed in the investigation by means of the impact of KBS on job quality. Some evidence suggests that the introduction of KBS may affect aspects that relate to job quality. However, this evidence is equivocal as to whether and when the effects are positive or negative. For instance, one study shows that the composition of the workgroup has changed, and that the degree of lower qualified personnel has increased, as a result of the introduction of a KBS. In consequence, it was established that the KBS has led to an increase in boring work <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 36 . KBS are also accused of reinforcing Tayloristic principles concerning the division of labour, thus reducing the completeness and autonomy of tasks <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 32,46 . Other studies, on the other hand, established an increase in job quality 19 . Also, by reducing the <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> number of routine tasks and increasing the freedom for creative work, KBS are credited for ensuring better work conditions 7,8 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

The job quality concept has been approached by different authors from many different angles and from different interest positions, all using different concepts and tools. It has been studied at the microlevel of job characteristics, and also at the macro level of organizations and their environment; objective as well as subjective approaches can be found, varying as to how they value the perception of the worker, etc. for an overview, see Ref. 38 . InŽ <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. consequence, the concept lacks clarity. Here, the concept is used in an indicative sense and studied by examining a set of relatively ‘objective’ characteristics at the level of the individual job. Following the approach in a previous study 5 , five indicators of job quality were used in the investigation: degree of difficulty of the job, completeness of the job, autonomy of the job, knowledge and skills required for the job, and openings for making contact. Table 7 presents the assessments of these five factors.

The first factor, i.e., the impact of KBS on the degree of difficulty of the job, was studied using three alternative types of technology impact: regradation, degradation and polarization see Ref. 38 . Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. Regradation occurs when technology in general, or KBS in particular, reduces unpleasant aspects of a job, and, consequently, makes the job content more complex. Ten instances of regradation were found. These included situations in which the expert was given more time to deal with complex cases because the standard cases were dealt with more efficiently in the KBS. They also included situations in which the KBS brought broader expertise into the decision situation, thus presuming more knowledgeable decision makers. If, instead, the introduction of the KBS implies standardization and routinization of the task, the situation is characterized as degradation. In this case, complex tasks are reduced to simple, monotonous repetitions. Five situations of degradation were encountered in the investigation. These concerned situations in which the KBS dictated the sequence of actions for handling problems, changing the role of the KBS user into a reactive instead of an active or proactive one. Polarization, the third possible impact of KBS on job difficulty, refers to a situation in which the tasks involved are split up into simple tasks performed with the assistance of the Ž KBS and complex tasks, and subsequently assigned. to different groups of people. Nine situations of polarization were identified, mostly in firms where new, low-qualified jobs were created for handling the data entry and operating the KBS.

The completeness of the job, the second factor studied, has to do with how much of the complexity of a task is present within one job. A typical example of how a KBS may enhance the completeness of a job is that it allows for fields of expertise to be introduced into a decision situation that would otherwise have to be introduced by conferring with a special advisor. The job of the KBS user thus becomes more complete.

The third indicator of job quality, i.e., the autonomy of the function, refers to the decision power associated with the job. A high job quality is present if the regulatory powers in a job match the regulatory needs of that job. KBS may affect the autonomy of a function, particularly if it is introduced to replace, rather than support, the expert. Most of the situations in which degradation of work through KBS was encountered, were also characterized by a reduced autonomy of the function. If a KBS dictates which tasks are handled in which sequence, it reduces the autonomy of the job.

Knowledge and skills required for the job, the fourth factor, relate to the degree in which KBS use may affect the inclination to learn. For instance, the training needed for using the KBS, and also the advice the KBS renders, were found to lead to enhancements in knowledge and learning activities of the KBS users involved. More often, however, situations were identified in which the explanation facilities were either absent or hardly used. In such situations KBS users almost blindly followed the advice of the system, using their own creative thinking only in situations in which the system could not be applied see also Ref. 11 .Ž <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>.

The fifth factor concerns the degree to which the job allows for making contact with colleagues. For example, consider a front office worker who learns from his colleagues how to solve certain problems through a case-based reasoning system. Situations like these, which in some form or other were encountered in three firms in the investigation, reduce the need to confer with these colleagues, thus, in most cases, lowering job quality.

## 8. Combining the dimensions

The primary aim of the research was to provide eye-openers for the breadth of KBS impacts, to advance the awareness that local advantages may conceal global disadvantages. Its primary aim was not to discuss interrelationships between these dimensions explicitly. Establishing such interrelationships presents a formidable research problem in itself. Clearly, no simple calculation can be performed; compensating negative scores on one set of indicators by positive scores on another is usually not possible. For instance, perceived gains related to core competencies may prove soap-bubbles if job quality is affected in such a way that experts leave and, thereby, a flexible adaptation to future settings is threatened.

Even if exploring the links between the dimensions is not the primary objective of this article, some comments about the combination of the three dimensions do seem in order. As shown by Table 9, most firms revealed a combination of positive, negative and neutral impacts. None of them revealed entirely negative or entirely positive impacts. Three firms were classified as revealing no clear KBS impacts on either dimension. Only for a relatively small subset of six firms where no negative effects were found. Four of these firms were assessed as having positive impacts both on core competencies and job quality and as neutral on knowledge con-Ž tent . For all 15 others, we assessed, on at least one. dimension, threats or reductions. Particularly those organizations would benefit from a further exploration of the nature of the interrelationships between the dimensions.

## 9. Conclusion

Do smarter systems make for smarter organizations? Or: does the knowledge content of KBS serve to enhance the knowledge content of organizational products and processes? The main argument presented here is that finding more elaborate ways of formulating these questions is perhaps even more important than finding definite yes or no answers. Some elements of such yes or no answers were nevertheless brought to light. The empirical outcomes presented in this article suggest that a balanced judgement will involve a mix of positive and negative elements. The outcomes show that the main knowledge-related risk associated with the introduction of KBS is their potentially negative impact on job quality. Knowledge-intensive processes require motivated knowledge workers. If systems, however smart they may be, lead to the degradation of the work of professionals and, consequently, to a reduction in job satisfaction and even to the departure of the knowledge workers, their introduction will not have been a smart choice. The results presented above do describe some pieces of the puzzle, but one should nevertheless be aware of the limitations of both the empirical part of the study and the explorative nature of its theoretical part. A further elaboration of the argument developed here calls, first and foremost, for a more thorough examination of the concept of a ‘smart organization’ and an elaboration of this concept geared towards appropriate measurement procedures for establishing the impact of smart systems. Such an elaboration also calls for more comprehensive and less tentative empirical investigations using additional research strategies such asŽ longitudinal research, other data sources than the opinions of system managers, more ‘objective’ or intersubjective measures for impact assessment, a larger sample, etc. ..

Two points summarize the main argument developed in the article. One is that a broad perspective is needed when linking smart systems to smart organizations. Concentrating on local advantages for ex-Ž ample their ability to improve decision making may. block the awareness of their global disadvantages Žfor example threats to personal knowledge ambitions . The three concepts of core competencies,. operational value of knowledge in the value chain and the match between personal and organizational knowledge ambitions were presented as a first step towards an elaboration of such a broad perspective. The second point is that one should be aware of the fact that linking the smartness of KBS to the smartness of organizations does bring two very different worlds together. These are two different types of smartness; the processes leading to their smartness are also different. KM and KE, or the art of conceiving and building KBS, are almost completely distinct activities. While KE thrives by explicating and engineering knowledge, KM based on undue attention to explication or engineering may lead to a deterioration of the organizational knowledge. Also, the specificity required for KE is likely to be detrimental to KM: knowledge work depends on sufficient room to manoeuvre, and much less on intricate distinctions between knowledge atoms, elements, regions and domains 45 . Knowledge in KM is studied with<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> regard to its organizational value, and not, at least not primarily, with regard to its content, as it is in KE. One may even say that, because of their differences in perspective, KM and KE are concerned with different types of knowledge.

Essential for any attempt to link KE and KM adequately is an awareness of the fundamental tension between the two this tension is almost com-Ž pletely ignored in Ref. 43 . Only through an aware- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. ness of the tensions involved in relating KBS to KM one may hope to gain insight into the potential contribution KE may make to KM. At the same time, an exaggerated emphasis on the tension between the two concepts should be avoided, as this might prevent us from recognizing the useful contributions that KBS may make to KM.
