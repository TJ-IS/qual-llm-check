## Abstract

Argumentation support systems have both advantages and disadvantages. For the individual, there is an increased power to express herself and to gain recognition and reward for the extra effort and frankness required, but at the cost of slowing down work, making the user constantly explain herself, and putting her statements at risk of being taken out of context. For the organisation, there is a gain in increased information sharing — the bane of current computer-mediated communication Ž .CMC systems — as the user is motivated to present relevant and convincing evidence to back up her statements, but at the cost of undermining ‘traditional’ organisational structures and hierarchies. q 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. ## 1. Introduction

Current computer-mediated communication Ž . CMC systems do not fit satisfactorily into emerging working practices or organisational environments. Firstly, managers prefer to gather soft information anecdotes, rumours, opinions in unsched-Ž . uled meetings for their greater timeliness and relevance over computerised information or formal reports. This suggests that one important dimension of a GDSS is satisfying those social, emotional, and symbolic requirements provided by the face-to-face nature of managerial work 101 . Secondly, current systems over-emphasise the quantitative aspects of decisions voting, scoring attributes, decision theoryŽ notions rather than the qualititative nature of deci-. sions. Qualitative aspects involve discontinuous conflict resolution via position-taking and persuasion via argumentation rather than a continuous, summative consensus process. Thirdly, organisations are increasingly devolving responsibility onto lower levels, closer to where decision impacts are felt, by means of creating temporary task forces, delayering, and by means of resource access on a need-to-use basis, and information access on a need-to-know basis. This suggests that decision making is becoming more pluralistic and less hierarchical, determined not so much by position in the corporate hierarchy and more by the argumentative and evidential value of what one has to say. Such ephemeral statuses can only be captured by systems which mediate and transcribe argumentation and the shifting contracts which are continually being created as work tasks progress and vary through time. Fourthly, as such qualitative processes come to the fore, there is an increased need to evaluate and increase their quality. This means that such systems must have the ability to structure arguments are broken down into evi-Ž dence, warrants, and claims , externalise via screen . Ž diagrams and hypertext links and remember avoid- . Ž ing old arguments and using existing knowledge .. Structuring, externalising, and remembering are potential aids to increased rationality of decision making. Fifthly, organisations are continually remaking themselves, and this requires that transcripts generated routinely by CMC systems should be used to rewrite the organisation’s evolving business rules.

Argumentation support systems record, organise, regulate and coordinate argumentation processes in organisations and enable managers to argue about decisions, issues, and goals. Argumentation support systems are able to ‘understand’ arguments by sim-Ž plifying them and identifying the main elements ,. they enable the human user to present arguments electronically to others, and they have rules of thumb Ž . e.g., simplicity, relevance for estimating the strength of arguments. Such systems can ‘see’ their human users or opponents or collaborators, by means of a simplifying model in the form of a list ofŽ previous claims and premises used by that person .. They represent debate in the form of a diagram shown on the computer screen, in which arguments are arrows from premises to claims.

The argument diagram is updated as new evidence is added, and the user is prompted when vital evidence is missing. Given a claim to substantiate, an argumentation support system searches for necessary premises from a database. Given premises in a database, it searches for all supportable claims. And given premises which are inadequate to support or attack a claim, it says what extra premises are needed. To do debate planning it suggests the best order in which to present claims and premises, where particularly strong arguments should be emphasised, and where particularly weak arguments should be bolstered or disguised. It gives a ‘current score’ of the strength of the user’s case. The system spots gaps in an opponent’s case, finds the weakest part of the opponent’s case, and identifies where the opponent has to give more evidence.

The context in which such systems currently operate is as electronic meeting support systems, although their potential, it will be suggested, goes far beyond this. Because of this greater potential, however, they offer a large and real threat to the ‘traditional’ organisation. This paper assesses this new technology’s strengths and weaknesses, with particular regard to the organisational tasks, roles, and contexts of users.

## 3. Computational models of argumentation

The main model used in computational modelling of argumentation is by Toulmin 119 . This includes<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> the following elements: the premises the initial stateŽ of factual material which leads to the claim ; the . claim the goal state or conclusion one wants to Ž reach ; and the warrant a transition rule to infer the. Ž claim from the premise ..

Mitroff et al. 79 and Hamalainen et al. 43 have<sup>w x</sup> <sup>w x</sup> suggested Toulmin’s premise–warrant–claim model as a basis for policy making using formal logic. Ramesh and Whinston 91 instead used claims–<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> arguments–proposals and proposed three formalisms — coordination, games, and a dialogue grammar, together with formal logic.

However, cognitive scientists have studied the everyday use of logic such as syllogisms in theŽ . persuasion process and have concluded that persuasive reasoning does not follow any logical pattern <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 3 . As an alternative to formal logic, ‘informal logic’ has been extensively catalogued by Perelman and Olbrechts-Tyteca 87 , who developed a classifi- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> cation and underlying theory of persuasive effect. Their contention is that informal logic comprises many hundreds of rules such as fairness, commitment, reciprocity, deterrence, sacrifice or consequences.

## 5.2. Slowness

Moreover, although most<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> discourse has high requirements of coherence trust,Ž honesty, obviousness, explicitness , argumentation is. a type of discourse in which deviousness, bluff and obfuscation are acceptable norms of behaviour. Such norms require software which enables qualifying, hedging, implicitness, politeness, topic avoidance, distraction, and emotional appeal 101 . Ar-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> gumentation support systems are vulnerable to diversionary tactics: requiring rules to control topic shifting, turn-taking and maximum floor time 102 . Contributors to argumentation could be provided with feedback about the effect of their contributions by means of a moving scoreboard next to the argument diagram. This feedback would need to be integrated with an organisational reward system for encouraging widespread and conscientious information sharing. The way the argumentation leads to a particular organisational action would also need to be shown at relevant moments, because this is an important way of showing the contributor the benefits of her information to the organisation as a whole.

In order to scan databases for information which is relevant to an ongoing debate, such databases need to be properly indexed, requiring formalisation. This is time-consuming. However, an argumentation system is able to archive interactions as series of claims connected by attacking and supporting links. Thus, the links between current debates and former debates would be automatically established, without any need for expensive indexing activities.
