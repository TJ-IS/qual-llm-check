# SlotManager: a microcomputer-based decision support system for university timetabling

## Abstract

A user-friendly, menu-driven decision support system that can be used to construct university timetables is described. The system is written in Microsoft Access, within the Microsoft Windows environment, for an MS-DOS personal computer. A case study is reported, involving the successful use of the system in the environment that motivated its development, namely course timetabling at a New Zealand university. The system provides a useful addition to the university timetabler’s toolkit and an interesting application of the DSS approach in an important area. q 2000 Elsevier Science B.V. ## 1. Introduction

Over the last 25 years, many approaches and models have been proposed for dealing with a variety of timetabling problems. However, it is in the area of educational timetabling that most attention has been focused, with problems ranging from the construction of weekly timetables in schools, colleges, and universities 2,13 , to the timetabling of <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> conferences 7 and examinations 5,10 . This ongo-<sup>w x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> ing interest has been fuelled by two major developments.

Firstly, the nature of the education curriculum has changed substantially. In recent years, new subjects, programmes of study and degrees have been introduced, often with different working patterns and facility requirements and, even more significantly, students have been given much greater freedom of choice in their programmes of study. Many programmes are now arranged on a modular basis, where students select subjects either from pre-defined combinations or freely from a set of allowable subjects. As a consequence, the timetable has become much more complicated. It is not many years since the school or university timetable was almost totally static, with only minor changes from year-toyear. The timetable evolved slowly over a number of years and was simply rolled forward from one year to the next with occasional modifications to allow for changes in staff or facilities. Now, the timetable must allow for this increased choice by allocating subjects to pre-determined time periods or ‘slots’ in such a way that popular or realistic combinations are permitted. Indeed, there are now approaches that aim to satisfy all student choices on a semester by semester basis 21 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>

Secondly, computing facilities and expertise are now available in all western education institutions so that the timetabler no longer has to retain an intimate knowledge of teaching facilities and requirements, or risk being swamped by paper-based methods. The classic timetable, showing where every student and teacher is for every hour of every day, in the form of a huge colour-coded chart, is now a thing of the past. The information can be stored much more efficiently on a microcomputer and appropriate information extracted as required.

These developments are, in many ways, complementary in that the increasing complexity of the timetable is or can be made bearable by appropriate Ž . use of computers and information technology. However, the key word here is ‘appropriate’. Just what is an appropriate use of computer processing in the management of a modern school or university timetable? This issue is developed in the following section. We then introduce the traditional timetabling scenario in Section 3, and motivate the contribution that the DSS approach can make within this environment through a case study report in Section 4. In Section 5 we report on a specific DSS that was developed to aid timetabling for the Waikato Management School, within the University of Waikato, New Zealand, before offering our conclusions in Section 6.

## 2. The role of the computer in educational timetabling

From the outset, the computer has been seen as an indispensable element in virtually all automated timetabling systems. Indeed, many of the developments in school and university timetable construction have been reported in the computing rather than the operational research or education literature 4,12,18 .<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> In essence, the role of the computer in timetabling can be seen as satisfying two distinct requirements. Firstly, there is a need for computational power in the execution of increasingly complex timetabling algorithms and heuristics, and secondly, there is the ‘book-keeping’ or database function that is required to maintain the lists and tables that are a necessary part of almost any timetabling system.

As regards the first requirement, the computation involved can be very extensive and time consuming, and so tends to be done on larger, more powerful computers. Thus, the more successful applications tend to be in the university sector, or in school situations that are more centrally planned and coordinated, for example, Alvarez-Valdes et al. 1 in<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Spanish schools, and Birbas et al. 3 for Greek high<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> schools. In many countries, however, high schools are too autonomous, and their individual requirements and constraints too diverse, for there to be many cases where timetables are created by computer, either by the school itself or by some central coordinating body. Even if software is available to meet their individual requirements which usually it Ž is not , there may be reluctance on the part of. experienced timetablers to accept the solution of a process that they do not understand 15,19 . In<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> essence, most western high schools have timetabling requirements that are too complex for the automatic construction of timetables by computer to be regarded as a feasible proposition, although Wright <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> 22 describes an apparently successful model developed for a British school.

In higher education, however, there is greater in-house expertise and more acceptance of computer-generated timetables, but the size and complexity of most institutions is such that automated timetabling on an institution-wide basis is seldom attempted. There are reported examples of successful implementation at the departmental, faculty, or school level 14,16 . Generally, however, most institutions<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> find it more acceptable to use the judgement of an experienced timetabler who constructs timetables manually, based to some extent on the pattern of previous timetables, and relying on computer support to highlight potential clashes or the violation of resource constraints. Chahal and de Werra 6 de- <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> scribe an interactive system for creating feasible timetables, and although the application is not large in comparison with many school timetables, it does represent a fruitful role for computerized timetabling in providing interactive decision support for an experienced timetabler.

In contrast, the ‘book-keeping’ requirement, a necessary part of any timetabling process, can be very easily computerized by means of either tailormade programs, or with general-purpose software such as a database system 11 . Such systems enable<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> the timetables for both student groups and for teachers to be printed automatically. In a large institution, the typesetting or writing of timetables for all teachers is a considerable clerical task that is often avoided because of the effort involved. Likewise, student timetables can be prepared automatically, if necessary on an individual or group basis, rather than students having to consult a complicated master timetable to extract their own individual timetables. As well as staff and student timetables, various lists and forms can be prepared for such things as:

<sup>Ø</sup> activities on a single time period basis,

<sup>Ø</sup> the utilization of individual facilities,

<sup>Ø</sup> department and programme teaching requirements, and

<sup>Ø</sup> resource utilization.

Provided that the relevant data is captured and stored in an appropriate form, these all provide management information that is a direct consequence of the timetable itself.

## 3. The traditional timetabling problem

In a review article, de Werra 20 noted that there<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> are two distinct stages to the educational timetabling process, namely

1. ‘‘First, the curricula are defined for each class or for each group of students, and the necessary teaching resources staff, equipment, and spaceŽ . are assigned.’’

2. ‘‘Second, when an agreement has been reached concerning these assignments of resources, then one tries to see whether a workable detailed timetable can be worked out which is compatible with all the previously defined requirements.’’

Although some work has been done on the assignment problems implicit in the first stage 8,12 , the<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> traditional timetabling problem is usually identified with stage 2 above, namely ordering classes orŽ . groups over some time period e.g., a week subjectŽ . to various resource constraints. Indeed, most of the approaches described in the OR and computing literature relate to some aspect of the second stage, often in the context of a specific problem situation. At its most basic, this second stage consists of a multi-dimensional assignment problem involving various elements, namely sets of students groups who followŽ . defined programmes of meetings classes with spec-Ž . ified sets of teachers in a selection of facilities Ž . Ž . rooms at a specified set of times periods within the week. The problem can be modeled in terms of a set of binary variables of the form $x _ { i j k r } ,$ where $x _ { i j k r } = 1$ if group i meets teacher j in period k in room r, and 0 otherwise. There is then a series of requirements surrounding these variables which either must be satisfied hard constraints , should ifŽ . possible be satisfied medium constraints or it wouldŽ . be ‘nice’ to satisfy soft constraints . What classes asŽ . hard, medium, and soft constraints varies from situation to situation. For example, a hard constraint would prohibit a teacher from being with two classes at the same time, a medium constraint could prevent all the classes in a certain subject from falling on the same day, and a soft constraint might concentrate a teacher’s classes into specified days of the week.

In some timetabling problems, there are sufficiently many constraints that even finding a feasible solution is an achievement. The situation may therefore be seen as a ‘constraint satisfaction’ problem rather than an optimality problem. Where one orŽ more objective functions is specified, these can. focus on many different aspects of the problem, such as:

<sup>Ø</sup> achieving feasibility, e.g., minimize the number of unscheduled classes, or

<sup>Ø</sup> timetable quality, e.g., maximize some measure of student and<sup>r</sup>or teacher ‘convenience,’ or

<sup>Ø</sup> resource utilization, e.g., minimize the number of rooms used, or

<sup>Ø</sup> a weighted composite of some or all of the above.

Various techniques have been used to solve such optimization problems, the more popular ones being mathematical programming 2,3,12 , the gener-<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> Ž alized assignment algorithm 16 or a meta-heuristic. <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> such as tabu search 1 . In practice, those situations<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> where automated timetabling has been successfully reported tend to be ones which are limited in scope Ž . a high school or a single university department , or highly centralized systems which change little from year-to-year, or where the element of student choice regarding their subjects of study is restricted or non-existent.

## 3.1. Approaches to the timetabling of optional subjects

Probably, the most difficult constraints to deal with are those which occur when students have an elective element in their course selections, where they choose a number of optional subjects from a specified list. In junior schools, the amount of student choice is usually limited, but in high school and higher education, the choice increases significantly. In particular, many university programmes are now completely modular, with students having almost total freedom in choosing a specified number of modules to make up an overall programme of study. In terms of the basic model, this can be dealt with by defining each different combination of subjects as a distinct group, with different groups combining to form a class for each subject. In reality, this severely complicates the formulation of the problem, and reduces the likelihood of a feasible solution being found.

In general, there are two broad approaches to the timetabling of optional subjects. Traditionally, the approach has been to decide a priori those combinations of subjects that are desirable or allowable and to devise a timetable that allows a restricted element of choice from within the allowable combinations. A prime example of this is where senior high school students are channeled into either ‘arts’ or ‘science. A rather more flexible version of this approach is to create combinations or ‘stacks’ of subjects, with the more popular subjects falling in more than one stack. If students are not allowed to choose more than one subject from each stack, then all subjects in each stack can be timetabled to occur at the same time. The problem for the timetabler now lies in the effective determination of the number and composition of the various stacks.

The alternative approach to option timetabling is to attempt to be as permissive as possible by finding out which options students want to study and then to construct a timetable which, as nearly as possible, satisfies those desires 21,22 . This is clearly a much <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> more difficult and time-consuming process, and it may result in failure to satisfy some student choices. The timetabler is then faced with the problem of how much effort to put into trying to satisfy as many requests as possible, and how to decide which students shall not be given their first choice of options if they cannot all be accommodated. There is also the complication of having to elicit student choices well in advance, in order to allow sufficient time for the timetable to be devised.

This permissive approach can lead to a timetable that changes dramatically from semester-to-semester or from year-to-year. There may be fixed or compulsory elements that can be carried forward from one timetable to the next, but optional subjects must be re-planned each time to accommodate the requests of each new group of students. In contrast, the traditional or legislative approach usually results in a more or less static timetable that can be carried forward from one year to the next, subject to minor modifications and refinements.

In reality, the approach used in constructing most educational timetables is a compromise between these two extremes. It is usually both undesirable and unnecessary to construct a timetable that is completely divorced from previous timetables because there are usually continuing features that can be carried forward. It is then a matter of fitting the new elements of the timetable around this common core. The feasibility of this approach depends on the size of the common core. However, if the dominant ethos is a legislative one of ‘This is what the timetable allows you to do’ rather than a permissive one of ‘What do you want the timetable to allow you to do?’ then there should be significant commonality from one year to the next. It would therefore seem intuitively obvious, and practically more appealing, to maintain the basic structure of an on-going timetable which is modified each semester or year as circumstances require. This clearly results in a more restrictive timetable than might ideally be the case if every single subject is re-timetabled each time the timetable is constructed. In most institutions, however, this marginal loss of freedom is seen as a small price to pay if it prevents the timetabling process from becoming a major planning challenge.

## 4. Case study: the Waikato Management School

The University of Waikato UW is a federally Ž . funded, general tertiary institution of 12,000 students, located in Hamilton, New Zealand. It comprises seven schools of study: arts and social sciences, computing and mathematical sciences, education, law, Maori and pacific development, management, and science and technology. As is usually the case in British Commonwealth universities, the undergraduate timetable at UW has much less structure than that of a typical university in the US. In the latter country, it is common for all lecture-based courses to be of 3 hours duration per week. Each such course is taught in a single slot, which is selected from a set of slots, into which the entire teaching week is partitioned. Each slot comprises either three 50-min sessions on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday; or two 75-min sessions on Tuesday and Thursday. Often, all of the sessions of a slot start at the same time of the day. Slot systems of this type aid the timetabling process as they allow for the ‘packing’ of courses that can be taught simultaneously into identical hours of the week, i.e., the assignment of the same slot. As will be seen later in this paper, a modified version of this system has been introduced within one school at UW.

## 4.1. Timetabling at UW

Despite the advantages of the American slot system, it is impossible to introduce it in UW, and most other British Commonwealth universities, for the following reasons. Firstly, there is significant variation in the number of weekly hours, and the structure of those hours, in which courses are taught. At UW, courses commonly require two, three, or four 50-min sessions per week allowing students a 10-min breakŽ between classes which may be scheduled as single,. double, or triple sessions. This is described in more detail in Section 4.2.

Secondly, it is traditional for a genuine attempt to be made to meet the timetabling needs of all staff and most students involved in cross-disciplinary and other programmes. This often leads to lengthy consultative processes at both departmental and school level, involving teaching faculty and student representatives. The outcome of this is usually a coordinated request from each school for its timetabling needs for a full teaching year. These requests are sent to a central university timetabler who finalizes them by checking for conflicts between schools, and resolving such conflicts by negotiation with school or departmental timetabling representatives, who are usually teaching faculty. Thus, it has traditionally been the case that most of the timetabling for a forthcoming year has been carried out by departmental or school teaching faculty, with central administrators having the relatively minor role of checking allocations and resolving room conflicts. This contrasts sharply with the North American situation, where timetabling is usually carried out entirely by central administrators.

However the situation at UW changed rather abruptly when the central administrators responsible for timetabling recently announced that they had purchased a computer-based timetabling package that would replace the previous system, which was largely pencil-and-paper-based with support from a simple PC database. It was also announced that, henceforth, the central timetabling office would be responsible for constructing next year’s timetable. Departmental representatives would simply advise the central office of basic course details, which would be inputted to the system, and the timetable would be published in due course.

While, in principle, the teaching staff involved in timetabling welcomed the removal of a burdensome task, they had some misgivings about the ability of the new software to provide a good timetable. The system was designed for English universities, where timetabling needs are markedly different from those at UW, and the system was to be operated by central university timetabling staff who had little idea of its capabilities. This latter fact was underlined by the central administrators’ insistence that from the input of basic course data, the system would produce a satisfactory timetable. What remained unanswered was how the system would resolve conflicts over room use from different departments who, unknowingly, might request the same room for some time period. There was no provision in the proposed new procedure for preliminary coordination between departments to reduce such conflicts, or for their fair and effective resolution if they occurred.

At this point a number of senior members of the Waikato Management School WMS met with theirŽ . dean to discuss how best to respond to this new situation. It was decided that the WMS departmental timetable representatives would be invited to form a WMS timetable committee, chaired by a senior member of the school with experience in both timetabling and DSS development. The purpose of this committee was to coordinate the development of a WMS timetable for the coming year, that was reasonably equitable for all WMS students and teaching staff, and that involved effective and efficient use of WMS teaching facilities. These facilities comprise 18 rooms ranging in size from a 394-seat lecture theatre, to 50-seat lecture<sup>r</sup>case study rooms and 12-seat tutorial rooms. Most WMS teaching takes place in these rooms, which are seldom used by the rest of UW, and WMS has priority over the other schools in the timetabling of them. The next sections outline the timetabling issues within WMS, and how the school responded to them.

## 4.3. The DSS enÕironment at WMS

Prior to the changes to the university’s timetabling procedures mentioned earlier, the WMS timetable for the forthcoming year was based on a simple slot system comprising 14 slots of 2 or 3 h scattered through the week, covering the more desirable teaching hours in the middle of each day. The priority of courses was: the compulsory first year courses, all other compulsory courses, remaining second year courses, remaining third year courses, remaining fourth year courses, and all graduate courses. Until 1990, when WMS comprised only two departments and less than 1000 students, the system worked reasonably well, with conflicting requests for rooms being resolved by negotiation, facilitated by the one timetabler. The process was essentially a manual one, aided by a simple PC database and record-keeping system. In the 1990’s, as the number of departments, courses and students increased, the system became more and more difficult to operate. The process took longer and longer, negotiations became strained, there were increasing complaints from students over course clashes, and from staff over inappropriate room allocations and awkward times. Overall, there was a feeling within WMS that the timetables were more inconvenient than they needed to be and, in general, poor use was being made of the WMS teaching facilities.

As a consequence, the new system introduced by the central timetablers triggered some significant changes in the way that the WMS timetable was to be produced. At the outset, there was an agreement between WMS and the central timetablers that, in the future: 1 WMS would continue to have first prior- Ž . ity over the use of its teaching rooms, and 2Ž . Central timetablers would not be involved in the construction of the first draft of the WMS timetable. Rather, the WMS timetable committee would devise a complete timetable for submission to the central timetablers as a coordinated request, with the expectation that it would be incorporated into the university timetable with little alteration. Any alterations that were necessary would first be negotiated with WMS.

The committee felt that the procedure that had been used in the past was basically sound and should be continued. In addition, what was needed was a comprehensive slot system that covered the entire teaching week, and a DSS to keep records, check for inconsistencies, and provide suggestions and alternatives when required. To minimize the committee members’ time, it was agreed that meetings would be held only as needed, and that the chairperson would devise the timetable by consulting with the departmental representatives individually. This process would be facilitated by the DSS and by a clerical assistant who operated it.

It was decided that the same order of dealing with courses would be followed, beginning with the compulsory first year courses and ending with graduate courses. The representatives provide the chairperson with their departments’ timetable needs at each level, along with preferred slots and rooms. Clashes are resolved by negotiation, facilitated by the chairperson, before the next level of courses is considered, in this way building up the timetable level by level. The DSS would be designed to facilitate both the modification of a previous semester’s timetable, and the creation of a completely new timetable from scratch.

During the two main semesters, different slot systems are used for undergraduate courses and for graduate courses. However, the DSS has to deal with all undergraduate and graduate courses simultaneously to devise a timetable for either semester, and to check for conflicts. Therefore, the DSS must accommodate slots with different numbers of hours, as well as interdisciplinary programmes, and piggybacking. As the timetable is being built-up, a read-only version of it is included on the WMS website. This enables teaching staff to view the tentative assignments made at each level and to make observations to their departmental representatives. The DSS allowed for displays of sub-timetables for individual teachers, rooms, interdisciplinary programmes, departments, course levels, and hours of the day. We shall now discuss the individual components of the DSS.

## 5. SlotManager

SlotManager is a user-friendly, menu-driven DSS that has been developed at the WMS for use by WMS timetablers in the construction of the school’s annual teaching timetable. Although it is clearly a Specific Decision Support System Ž . SDSS , its use is potentially much more general, as the only constraint on its use is that the timetable in question operates on a slot basis. Even then, it is possible to define each period within the week as a unique slot, so that classes can be timetabled on a period by period basis, which is what happens at WMS for graduate level and summer school courses. Furthermore, there is no a priori, fixed definition of the slot structure, which can be freely and easily changed to suit different requirements. In the context of UW, it is feasible to use SlotManager to construct a timetable within each school individually, and then to use it at the university level to consolidate all the separate school timetables into an institution-wide timetable.

SlotManager is divided into three components: the user-interface, the modelling base and the database. The broad structure that integrates these three components is shown in Fig. 1. This follows the generic framework, originally proposed by Sprague and Carlson 15 as a flexible, solver-oriented DSS, and<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> subsequently cited by many other authors e.g., Hol-Ž sapple and Whinston 9 , Thierauf 17 , Turban 19 .<sup>w x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup>. ## 5.2. The model base

SlotManager was originally conceived as a computer-based system to aid the process of allocating courses to slots and rooms by keeping records of various aspects of the allocation, and facilitating the printing of a variety of reports. The only ‘intelligence’ initially built into the system was the ability to detect conflicting allocations where:

<sup>Ø</sup> A room was being double-booked.

<sup>Ø</sup> A room was being allocated to a course whose enrollment exceeded the room capacity.

<sup>Ø</sup> A course leader was being timetabled to teach two classes at the same time.

<sup>Ø</sup> Two courses within the same year of a programme were being allocated to the same slot.

In particular, there were no suggestions as to how a conflict might be resolved, which was left to the experience and insight of the timetabler. However, during the evaluation of the initial prototype, a number of suggestions were made by the timetablers as to how the model base could be extended to examine additional features of a proposed allocation and to give a variety of advice on how conflicts might be resolved.

The incorporation of such advice is significantly more complex than simply detecting that a conflict or violation exists. For example, when a room clash is discovered, there are usually alternative rooms available at the relevant time, but these rooms may not be equally suitable. In particular, they will vary both in their capacity and their location. The alternatives must therefore be filtered in some way to sift out the inappropriate ones, and those remaining should ideally be ranked in decreasing order of suitability. After detailed discussion with the timetablers, an extensive rule base was incorporated that replicated the factors that the timetablers would consider when selecting an alternative room from those available. SlotManager was, in essence, assuming some of the features of an expert system. In those instances where the rule base would have filtered out all available rooms, the system would preserve some of these so that at least one or two alternatives can be offered, but their inappropriateness, for whatever reason, is flagged.

In a situation where the competition for teaching rooms can become acute, particularly for large rooms in the more popular slots, it is important that such rooms be used efficiently. It was felt to be appropriate, therefore, not only to signal violations of room capacity, but also to indicate where a room is being allocated that is significantly larger than is required to accommodate the anticipated number of students. This is not a simple issue in that under-utilization can be measured in various ways, and different measures are appropriate for different rooms. For very small rooms, there is generally no need to signal under-utilization at all, whereas for the larger rooms, it is usually a percentage utilization factor that is appropriate. For medium-sized rooms, the utilization measure should also take into account the actual number of seats unused. Again, a rule base was developed, which signals when the allocated room might be too large, and proposes, where possible, a range of alternatives.

The programme and course leader information described in the previous section is used to identify when two courses involving either the same teacher or the same group s of students have been allocatedŽ . to the same slot. In either of these cases, one of the conflicting courses should, if at all possible, be moved to an alternative slot. In passing, it is worth noting that the concept of a programme, consisting of a number of courses being taken by the same group of students, can be used quite flexibly. It may comprise a group of courses within one year of an actual programme, or across a number of years when the courses may be taken at different stages. In some cases, however, a ‘programme’ is simply an artificial construct to group together a set of courses that, for some other reason, cannot be timetabled together.

Finding an alternative slot for a conflicting course is, in a sense, similar to the task of finding an alternative room in the case of room clashes. The size and location of a room is analogous to the structure of a slot and its position within the week. Finding an alternative slot requires one that is ‘similar’ in structure and timing to the one originally allocated. The structure of any slot is defined in terms of its pattern of single, double and triple periods, as described in Section 4.2, and it is assumed that any alternative proposed should, if possible, follow the same pattern as that originally requested. If this is not possible, an alternative pattern has to be used, but one that is close to the original. For example, if a 1,1,2 slot is required and none isŽ . available, then the closest pattern is deemed to be Ž . Ž . Ž . 1,2,1 followed by 2,1,1 and finally 2,2 . The proximity within the week of all slots of a particular pattern is measured by a distance matrix, and the possible alternatives having the same, or nearest available, pattern are listed in decreasing order of proximity.

In trying to resolve any of the conflicts described above, the search considers alternatives for the course currently being timetabled. If none of these alternatives is attractive, the timetabler can ask SlotManager to display alternative rooms and slots for the previously allocated course with which the current one is in conflict. This introduces a limited amount of backtracking into the model base, but this is only invoked if the current course cannot be satisfactorily accommodated. It would be possible, in principle, to build in more levels of backtracking, but experience in using the system indicates that it is almost always possible to resolve conflicts with just one level of backtracking, along with the timetabler’s judgement.

## 5.3. The interface component

As previously stated, SlotManager is composed of a number of modules that facilitate the acquisition and organization of the relevant information relating to: GROUPS, SLOTS, PROGRAMMES, COURSES, ROOMS, and RESERVATIONS in any year. The modules contain various procedures for inputting and outputting information, although the GROUPS, ROOMS, and PROGRAMMES information tends to be reasonably static from year-to-year. The SLOTS and COURSES information usually require a small amount of updating each year, whereas the RESER-VATIONS data can be expected to change substantially. The first five modules are reasonably independent of each other, and so can be used in any order, but the RESERVATIONS information for any year is usually inputted only after the other modules have been used to incorporate any changes that are necessary.

The GROUPS module defines the various student groups, such as undergraduate, summer, and graduate, being timetabled. The master screen allows any one of these groups to be selected, along with the year to be timetabled. As the groups use different slot patterns, the SLOTS module is then used to define the various periods day and time that makeŽ . up each slot for the selected group. The only limit on the number of slots that may be specified is the number of periods in a week. The slot information can be displayed either chronologically in slot order or in the form of a two-way table by day and hour. A report of this latter display can be printed if required. The ROOMS module allows the list of teaching rooms and capacities to be created, amended, or printed as required.

## 5.4. Implementation of the DSS

SlotManager is implemented within a local area network that provides access to certain WMS database files held on a central server. All data, other than that relating to WMS rooms, courses and staff, is held locally within the system. The DSS was developed using an iterative prototyping approach Ž . which ‘‘is the most common in DSS development since the information requirements are not known precisely’’ 19 . Systematic testing assured that each <sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> module was error-free before additional modules were incorporated, and that the modules currently in place interfaced correctly with each other.

## 5.5. General discussion

Our approach does not automatically generate timetables that attempt and probably fail to satisfyŽ . all the various constraints and complicating factors that will inevitably exist in any practical timetabling situation. Rather, the purpose of the DSS is to assist an experienced timetabler to allocate courses to both rooms and slots when producing a timetable from scratch or, more likely, modifying an existing timetable. As discussed in Section 4.1, the option of using the university’s automated timetabling system was rejected by the WMS timetable committee because they had no faith in the system’s ability to deal successfully with the many complications of the WMS timetable. Furthermore, there was no allowance for the considerable timetabling expertise that exists within WMS to be incorporated within the proposed system, and there was a strong belief that the timetablers would be left to ‘pick up the pieces’ if an unacceptable timetable was generated automatically. This was seen as much less desirable than being in full control of the timetable generation process from the outset. This supports the belief of many writers on DSS that experienced users are often reluctant to accept the solution of a model or process that they do not understand 15,19 and that an effective DSS should support, and not replace the decision-maker.

This should not be taken to imply that there is no place for a highly sophisticated model base in any timetabling DSS, indeed, there have been a number of successful applications reported 1–3,15,21,22 . In<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> this situation, however, there was a unanimous belief within WMS that the decision-maker’s insight and judgment were needed to control the process. This belief has been largely vindicated by the experience of other Schools within UW, much smaller and less complex than WMS, where the use of the automated system has been less than successful, with many of the problems anticipated by the WMS timetabling committee arising.

SlotManager includes most of the characteristics of an effective DSS noted by Turban 19 in that it<sup>w</sup> <sup>x</sup> supports a semi-structured decision where parts of the analysis can be systematized for the computer; it supports all phases of decision-making; it combines modeling techniques with database and presentation techniques; it emphasizes ease of use, user friendliness, user control, flexibility and adaptability; and it interacts with other computer-based systems to download and upload information.

SlotManager has been in use within WMS for 3 years and is now accepted as an integral part of the timetabling process. The time spent by the timetablers in developing the timetable has been reduced significantly, and the efficiency of the record-keeping and report preparation has been greatly improved. The creation of the timetable is still largely a judgmental process, but one which is now undertaken more professionally and, as a consequence, gives rise to far less dissatisfaction from both staff and students and leads to more efficient utilization of an important scarce resource, namely teaching rooms. This view is supported by the School’s Associate Dean and Director of Undergraduate Programmes, who has recently commented that ‘‘before SlotManager, the WMS timetabling process took weeks of intensive effort, resulting in a relatively poor outcome. Now, this DSS enables good use of our facilities to be identified in about five working hours.’’

## 6. Summary and conclusions

We have described a DSS that has recently been developed and implemented at the Waikato Management School to improve the timetabling process. Although it has been developed in the context of a particular university and school, the only significant constraint on its general application is that the teaching situation being timetabled operates on a slot basis. It is therefore a specific DSS only in the sense that it applies specifically to timetabling decision situations, rather than being a generic decision support tool. SlotManager was designed to assist timetablers at every stage of the timetabling process. It does not automate the timetabling process but, rather, helps timetablers by providing powerful and wide-ranging tools to designate the required slots, allocate courses to feasible slots and rooms, and create a wide variety of reports on the outcome of the timetabling process.

In general, DSS benefits are often uncertain and difficult to assess. With the prototyping approach, where development is evolutionary, this is especially so. The ongoing changes to the education system, and the continuing search for greater efficiencies in the use of both staff time and physical resources, make it even more so. The true value of a DSS is whether it improves both a decision-maker’s effectiveness and the quality of the decisions made, which is not easily measured. Therefore, the traditional cost–benefit approach may not be able to capture all the DSS benefits. In our case, however, some of the benefits can be measured, such as the time taken by the timetablers to produce an acceptable timetable. The system has clear benefits, even if they are not easily quantified. Usage of the increasingly scarce resource of teaching rooms has demonstrably improved, and there is clearly a greater level of acceptance, on the part of both students and teachers, of the perceived quality of the teaching timetable. Furthermore, when the inevitable disputes arise over the slots or rooms allocated to courses, these are more efficiently handled by a system which can show the alternatives or lack of them that exist. In particular, Ž .

the ‘what-if’ questions concerned with a perceived unsatisfactory allocation can be much more effectively explored and, if possible, resolved.

In summary, this paper examines why timetabling is a complex and difficult task, involving both subjective and objective information and expert judgement. SlotManager is designed to augment the expertise of an experienced timetabler, usually in a situation where timetables evolve from one year to the next, rather than being created, ab initio, on each occasion. The reactions of experienced timetablers who have used SlotManager have been entirely positive and it has been accepted as an indispensable aid to timetabling at WMS.
