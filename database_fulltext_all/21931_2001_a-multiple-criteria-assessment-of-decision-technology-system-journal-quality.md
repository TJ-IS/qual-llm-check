# A multiple criteria assessment of decision technology system journal quality<sup>\$</sup>

Guisseppi A. Forgionne<sup>a,\*</sup>, Rajiv Kohli<sup>b</sup>

Information Systems Department, University of Maryland, Baltimore Country, Catonsville, MD 20228, USA <sup>b</sup>College of Business and Economics, Lehigh University, 621 Taylor Street, Bethlehem, PA 18015, USA

Received 15 May 2000; accepted 25 October 2000

## Abstract

Over the years, a number of researchers have assessed the quality of information systems (IS) journals. Most of these studies have assessed general IS journals, but few have specifically examined journals that focus on decision making support systems. Furthermore, even though there are many factors that measure journal quality, very few gauges have been used in the previous evaluations.

This paper offers a multiple criteria quality assessment of decision making support journals. Initially, the article presents the criteria and a methodology for consolidating the multiple criteria into an integrated measure of journal quality. Next, there is a discussion of the data collection process and the resulting multiple criteria evaluation. The paper concludes with a summary of the evaluation and the implications for information systems theory and practice. <sup>#</sup> 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information systems meta study; Decision support systems; AHP applications; Multiple criteria decision making; Journal ratings; Journal rankings; Journal quality

## 1. Introduction

The timely communication of peer-reviewed research in quality publications is crucial to the advancement of the theory and practice for any discipline. It also has a significant impact on the career progressions of the contributing theoreticians and practitioners. Identifying quality publications, then, is an important endeavor.

Over the years, there have been several studies that rated the quality of information systems (IS) journals. While most have rated general IS journals, a few have focused on decision support system (DSS) publications [15,23]. Some of the assessments are based on expert perceptions [11,21,24,29–31], perceptions combined with objective counts [5,12,22], or an objective citation analysis [14,16,28]. For the most part, the previous studies have focused on a very limited set of (often one or two) quality measures.

In reality, there are many measures of journal quality. Moreover, effective support for decision making could involve several concepts besides DSS [9,17,27]. Among other ways, such support can be delivered through: (a) DSS; (b) intelligent decision support systems (IDSS); (c) executive information systems (EIS); (d) idea processing systems (IPS); (e) artificial intelligence (AI); including knowledge-based systems (KBS); expert systems (ES); and artificial neural networks (ANN) and (f) management support systems (MSS). Collectively, these information systems that directly assist humans in making decisions can be called decision technology systems (DTS).

An empirical study that seeks to assess decisionmaking-support journal quality, then, should utilize multiple criteria and examine DTS-oriented outlets. This paper reports such a study. First, the article presents literature-inspired criteria and develops a methodology for consolidating the multiple yardsticks into an integrated measure of journal quality. Next, there is a discussion of the data collection process and a display of the resulting multiple criteria evaluation. The paper concludes with a summary of the evaluation and an examination of the implications for DTS research and practice.

## 2.1. Efficiency

Progress relies on individual and group creation of new, significant knowledge and on the implementation of the innovation in the pertinent field of endeavor. Identifying and disseminating this knowledge in a timely manner will be critical to this progress. The department by which such communication is achieved will determine a journal’s efficiency.

Among the variables in Table 1, efficiency can be measured through the journal’s: (a) circulation (CIR); (b)feedbackformat(FDF);(c)frequencyofissue(FRE); (d) Internet coverage (INT); (e) processing time (PRT) and (f) turnaround time (TAT). Fast processing and turnaround times with extensive feedback, Internet coverage, frequent issues, and a large circulation will promote the rapid communication of knowledge. Journals with these characteristics, then, will be efficient.

It can be argued that popular journals will attract many submissions, and the volume will increase pro cessing and turnaround times. Other things being equal, however, an efficient journal will add staff to handle the additional volume. When budgets limit staff, the journal may sacrifice efficiency to achieve other quality objectives (such as selectivity).

## 2.2. Focus

There are many sources of information and knowledge. Not all will be of interest or relevance to DTS researchers and practitioners. Time limits and professional considerations will encourage these people to consult the sources that present the most pertinent and meaningful DTS knowledge. Focus will be determined by the extent to which a journal publishes such knowledge.

Among the variables in Table 1, focus can be calibrated through the journal’s: (a) DTS papers accepted (DPA); (c) DTS papers submitted (DPS); (d) DTS special issues (DSI); (e) papers accepted (PAC) and (f) papers submitted (PSU). Meaningful DTS knowledge will embody a large percentage of papers submitted (PDS ¼ DPS/PSU), represent a large portion of papers accepted (PDA ¼ DPA/PAC), and command special issues. Journals with such properties, then, will have a DTS focus.

It can be debated that a DTS-oriented journal should have no need for special issues on the topic. Even within DTS, however, there are new or emerging topics that deserve distinct attention and coverage.

## 2.3. Impact

Significant contributions to knowledge will influence other research and practice. Researchers will build on seminal work, and practitioners will apply the original and augmented research. A journal’s impact will be determined by the extent to which its content reflects and inspires the new, relevant knowledge.

Among the variables in Table 1, impact can be gauged through the journal’s: (a) citations (CIT); (b) index presence (IJC) and (c) manuscript length (MSL). Significant knowledge will be substantially cited, reach many consequential indexes, and exact lengthy manuscript coverage. Journals with such properties, then, will have a high impact.

It can be argued that the volume of submissions to popular journals will force editors to restrict manuscript length. It can be further argued that a restrictive length requirement will force authors to be more concise and more to the point. Other things being equal, however, shorter articles will convey less detailed knowledge than longer articles. When budgets limit the number of issues, the journal may sacrifice impact to achieve other quality objectives (such as scope).

## 2.4. Scope

There will be a diverse group of potential contributors to knowledge. Some will be applied and theoretical researchers, while others will be managers and staff within public and private enterprises. The effort will take place regionally, nationally, and internationally. The mode in which this eclectic audience is reached will determine a journal’s scope.

Among the variables in Table 1, range can be metered through the journal’s: (a) affiliation (AFL); (b) outlet type (JOT); (c) manuscript type (MST) and (d) scope (SCO). Broad-minded sponsorship, wideaudience appeal, diverse articles, and international distribution will foster the comprehensive dispersal of knowledge. A journal with these attributes, then, will have a broad scope.

It can be asserted that a targeted journal will reach the most appropriate audience. However, it may difficult and costly to define, identify, and acquire such an audience in practice. A broad-scoping strategy may offer a better possibility for effective audience targeting than a self-limiting scheme.

## 2.5. Selectivity

As with other things, some new knowledge is better than other learning. Identifying and disseminating the better knowledge will reduce fruitless research and advance practical progress. The demeanor by which such discernment is achieved will determine a journal’s selectivity.

Among the variables in Table 1, selectivity can be assessed through the journal’s: (a) papers accepted (PAC); (b) papers submitted (PSU); (c) DTS papers accepted (DPA); (d) DTS papers submitted (DPS); (e) editorial board composition (EBR); (f) nature of the review (REN); (g) reviewers per submission (RPS); (h) revisions (REV) and (i) type of review (RET). Low acceptance rates (PAS ¼ PAC/PSU and DAS ¼ DPA/DPS), expert editorial boards, refereed submissions, myriad blind reviewers, and multiple revisions per submission will encourage sagacity. Journals with these desirable selectivity traits, then, will be selective.

It can be argued that many revisions suggest poor quality referees. More likely, however, the multiple revisions are a consequence of the: (a) blind reviewing process; (b) the difficult communication inherent in editing or (c) conscientiousness of the editors and reviewers.

## 2.6. Composite rating

Some rating variables (such as circulation and total papers accepted) can be expressed in absolute numerical terms. Others are categorical in nature, such as affiliation, DTS special issues, and Internet coverage. As noted in the earlier presentation, variable measures also can lead to quality conflicts. For example, a journal may have to decrease manuscript length to decrease processing time or decrease the portion of DTS papers accepted to increase circulation.

Under these circumstances, a multiple criteria decision making (MCDM) methodology will be useful in arriving at a composite rating of journal quality [25]. One of the most popular MCDM methodologies is the analytic hierarchy process (AHP). In AHP, the decision-maker first breaks down the problem into a hierarchy of interrelated decision elements. The overall decision criterion is placed at the top, variables in the middle, and alternatives at the bottom of the hierarchy. Next, elements (alternatives or variables) at one level are compared pairwise for their relative importance in attaining the elements (variables or decision criterion) at the next higher level. These data are used to estimate the relative weights of the variables. Then, the weights are aggregated into a set of ratings for the decision alternatives.

Fig. 1 gives the decision hierarchy for the rating problem of identifying high quality DTS journals. As this figure illustrates, the overall criterion, composite quality rating, is determined by the journals’ efficiency, focus, impact, scope, and selectivity. These factors, in turn, are set by defining variables in Table 1 (shown as their labels in Fig. 1). The alternatives will be the list of DTS journals.

Equal or different weights can be assigned to each rating variable. Similarly, each journal can receive an equal or disparate weight in the overall evaluation. In a completely impartial scheme, each variable would receive an equal weight, and each journal would have the same weight. By aggregating the estimated weights through the hierarchy, the evaluator will obtain an overall rating for each DTS journal. The overall criterion ratings then provide a basis for identifying high quality journals. High quality journals will receive the largest rating values.

## 3. Data collection and analysis

Data were collected through the pattern outlined in Fig. 2. First, we identified the list of DTS journals to rate. Next, values for the variables were obtained from the journal editors and through a citation analysis. Then, these collected values were used to develop composite journal ratings.

## 3.1. DTS journal list

Previous IS ratings studies provided a preliminary list of journals to evaluate. New journals have emerged since then, and others may have been excluded because of the methodology utilized in the earlier examinations.

To be thorough, we used two additional approaches to potentially expand the preliminary journal list. In one approach, we examined the stated editorial philosophies of new journals. This examination revealed further outlets, such as the Journal of Decision Systems, that were purely DTS in nature. In addition, we searched Melvyn, Uncover, and other bibliography networks online by keywords (such as decision support systems, executive information systems, and artificial intelligence) to catalog journals publishing DTS journals. Such a search disclosed previously excluded outlets (such as Computers and Operations Research) that published large quantities of DTS articles.

At the end of the three-phase investigation, we had compiled a list of 46 DTS journals that warranted an evaluation. The list is presented in Table 2. Many attempts were made to collect data on variables in Table 1 for each of these journals.

## 3.2. Questionnaire and citation analysis

Most of the required data was available only from the journal editorial office. A questionnaire, which is in Fig. 3, was the instrument used to collect such editorial dependent data. Initially, this instrument was mailed to the editor of each included journal. The mailing was followed up with multiple (at least four) e-mail reminders, faxes, and telephone calls until all involved outlets had responded or refused to respond. The process took more than 2 years. Eventually, 23 or one-half of the journals responded to the questionnaire. Three respondents provided incomplete information and did not respond to repeated requests for the missing data. Since the AHP methodology requires complete data, these three journals had to be excluded from the final ratings. Twenty journals, then, received final ratings. Table 3 lists all journals contacted, the corresponding editors, and the responses.

As Fig. 3 illustrates, the questionnaire defined DTS for the respondent. However, we let the respondent interpret the definition and all other items on the questionnaire. Responses were assumed to be honest and accurate.

At the same time, we inspected the reference/bibliography lists of all online-searched DTS articles from the 46 included journals for the period from 1991 to 1995. Eventually, we discovered that the Science Citation Index provided the best forum for citations. From the inspection, we counted the number of times each included journal was referenced in the bibliographies over the same period. Non-included journals were also noted for the record, but each of the many such outlets was cited very infrequently. For this reason, the results for the non-included outlets are not reported here.

To account for differences in journal longevity and frequency of issue, the citations were standardized [6,19]. In the standardization, each journal’s reference count was divided by the total number of articles published by the outlet. Citations also were used to help define the concept of an expert editorial board. Previous studies had identified influential DSS scholars and institutions in the US and Canada [7,8]. To update and expand the previous list, we added previously non-included scholars and institutions contributing 10 or more referenced DTS articles. Expert editorial boards then were defined as panels with large fractions of influential DTS scholars and institutions.

The evaluator uses available computer technology to organize the collected rating variable data, structure the decision hierarchy, and use the AHP to identify high quality DTS journals. As indicated by the top feedback loop in Fig. 4, these data, the hierarchy, and the journal ratings created during DSS processing can be captured and stored as inputs for future analyses and evaluations. These captured inputs are stored as additional or revised statistics and formulas, thereby updating the database and modelbase dynamically. The user executes the functions with mouse-controlled point-and-click operations on attractive visual displays that make the computer processing virtually invisible (transparent) to the user. Processing automatically displays the variables, allows evaluators to modify the displayed data, and stores the information. Outputs include: (a) journal rating statistics and (b) presentations that summarize the statistics into reports desired by the evaluator. The system also makes available explanatory details that justify the summary reports.

As indicated by the bottom feedback loop in Fig. 4, the user can utilize the outputs to guide further DSS processing before exiting the system. Typically, the feedback will involve sensitivity analyses in which the user experiments with variable and journal weights and observes the effects on the final journal ratings.

## 4. Conclusions

This paper has presented a methodology that objectively rates decision technology system journals across multiple, and relevant, dimensions. It also has presented an information system to consistently deliver the methodology in a transparent manner to researchers, academic administrators, and practitioners. We feel that the AHP-based rating model and the DSS-delivery mechanism are the major contributions of the research.

With the available data, interested parties can easily apply the methodology with their own weights and preferences. The AHP hierarchy will identify the sources of relative strength and weakness, by category and variable. Such focused analyses can help editors, and other interested parties, in formulating strategies for improving, or maintaining, quality.

In this paper, the methodology and information system delivery mechanism have been applied to DTS journal evaluation. However, the concepts are equally applicable to journal evaluations in other fields.

Using equal weights for all measures and categories, the multiple criteria assessment has resulted in a journal ranking that is in some ways similar, and many ways dissimilar, to the results reported in previous studies. There are some important implications from these findings. For one thing, these results suggest that non-multiple-dimensional studies may give an incomplete view of journal quality. Indeed, the models used in previous studies can be viewed as special, and extreme, cases of the multiple criteria approach used in this examination. Another implication is that DTS and MIS may have evolved into distinct fields within information systems, each with its own research philosophy and methodology, scholars, and literary outlets. If so, it would be wise to recognize the diversity in information systems curriculum, academic structure and administration, and practice.

Diversity may also exist within decision technology systems. For example, artificial intelligence may have a different research community and set of outlets than decision support, and related, systems. With the available data, it is possible to rank the journals within these two major DTS areas. This additional analysis can be a direction for future journal evaluation research. Also, it would be useful to update the AHP analysis by incorporating future data from participating, and new data from currently abstaining, journals.

This study focused on a fairly comprehensive list of objective measures of journal quality. Others may wish to expand the list, including additional measures, such as the journal’s perceived reputation, perceived value, and readership. The AHP methodology, and DSS-delivery mechanism, can accommodate such model modifications and evaluations easily as part of a sensitivity, or new, analysis.
