# The assimilation of computer-aided marketing activities $^{*}$

## Abstract

The use of computers has been assumed to provide substantial benefits in organizations (e.g., competitive advantage). Consequently, marketers are increasingly accepting computerization, although empirical evidence supporting the value of this technology is limited. To explore this phenomena, data were collected from 195 marketing executives in a national survey. These data were used to test a model involving the benefits from computerization. The results indicate that computerization aids in the assimilation of tactical and strategic activities. The benefits and implications of this integration are discussed. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information systems; Marketing and information systems; Strategy and information systems

## 1. Introduction

The application of technology in the firm has chiefly been directed towards improving the productivity and profitability of specific users of technology $[20]$ . Consequently, the organizational $[2]$ and marketing need for a competitive advantage $[10]$ is increasingly said to rest with technology. Correspondingly, the evolution of technology as a force in dictating success has resulted in increased computer usage by marketers. Yet, despite such attention, little is understood about the direct impacts of computerization, as only limited empirical evidence supports specific advantages for marketers utilizing this technology $[13]$ . For this reason, understanding the linkages between computerization and its specific impacts on the firm provides valuable managerial and academic information.

The basic reason for the use of IT (information technology) must be that it accomplishes specific tasks and does not become a task to manage $[18]$ . In this regard, an inspection of the literature indicates that the focus of computerization to date has been on its discrete application to either tactically or strategically oriented activities $[4]$ . For example, the enhancement of single tasks such as document processing has become a common thread underlying many computer-based applications and investigations. Yet, this usage is hardly consistent with the idea of coupling tactical and strategic activities. For instance, when sales managers consider weekly (tactical) account information as part of their long-term (strategic) personnel assignments, it improves overall productivity. The ability of computers to service an array of marketing situations and conditions $[3]$ is consistent with the need for marketers to address both tactical and strategic activities [27]. In turn, the potential for the integration of such responsibilities underscores a key value of computers to marketers, where only one aspect of technology (e.g., strategic) exists.

The common bond between strategic and tactical activities is that both are designed to increase productivity, suggesting success in one should logically foster output in the other. In this vein, the purpose of this research is to establish if the integration of tactical and strategic activities occurs through computerization, and if so, what antecedents engender this assimilation. To explore these research questions, strategic and tactical responsibilities of 195 marketing executives were studied in the context of using computers. Specifically, respondents to a mail survey were used to investigate the influence of computer training, organizational computer access and use, and the frequency of their use on computer-aided tactical and strategic marketing activities. Additionally, the impacts of tactical marketing activities on computer-based strategic activities were examined. From the empirical results, managerial implications and conclusions are presented.

## 2. Technological expectations within marketing

Faced with increasing turbulence, marketers must develop and maintain a competitive advantage $[8]$ . In this setting, the availability and accessibility $[16]$ of information has escalated, increasing reliance on marketplace intelligence $[19]$ . Accordingly, utilizing technology to alter the competitive paradigm suggests intertwining computerization with key marketing activities offer critical advantages.

IT is expected to accomplish specific tasks that enrich the user and the goals of the organization [28]. For the marketer therefore, IT is chiefly visible through computer technology. Consistent with the premise that each discipline (e.g., accounting, marketing) seeks unique outputs, the successful usage of computerization should vary depending upon the needs of the user. Thus, it is necessary to address computer usage within the context of marketing needs. For example, because a sales manager may regularly need reports on salespeople's performance, a successful marketing computer system should be configured providing such tactical information. However, this technology is not limited to a single type of task, providing instead a common enrichment of strategic and tactical activities.

## 3. Strategic and tactical activities

This examination of computer use is directed to strategic and tactical activities as separate, but related entities. For example, the information systems (IS) literature notes differences with respect to task complexity and emphasis [29], suggesting that tactical activities are performed differently when computerized than are strategic tasks. Also, the expectation of reaping unique outcomes differs across these types of task [4]. For example, sales managers utilizing computers to rank salespeople would expect different outcomes than when they are assessing the viability of entering new markets. Accordingly, the marketing literature also addresses strategic and tactical issues separately [25], frequently implying differences in the types of tasks performed and who performs them (e.g., salesperson, marketing manager). Consistent with the belief that the characteristics of knowledge guide its use, this study's tactical activities focus on routine, day-to-day, marketing tasks, while strategic activities address responsibilities critical to long-term objectives [27].

Because tactical activities tend to focus on routine responsibilities employed regularly (e.g., daily), it is expected that in a framework combining computerization and marketing, tactical activities should emphasize a processing-oriented perspective. For example, the analyst who reports on daily sales is engaging in a tactical activity. In contrast, because strategic activities demand more long-term oriented tasks, the decisions and information are more closely related to long-term needs (e.g., the market researcher who uses statistical analysis to segment potential markets).

While it is advantageous when one activity supports others, such conditions do not always exist (e.g., a product analyst may make decisions concerning who should receive new product brochures while lacking complete data about other recent new product failures/ successes). Accordingly, it is preferable when tactical and strategic activities are enriched through mutual reliance. For example, if a product manager is provided tactical product information (e.g., daily reports from salespeople identifying marketplace reluctance to a product), this may impact future strategic decisions concerning the development and introduction of other products. Thus, when fluid exchanges exist, the integration of tactical and strategic information is beneficial. In this context, it is proposed that IT allows information to be more fully integrated into an organization's operations. Because computerization can assimilate diverse data, an amplification of information occurs with users having better access to data. This has been termed “full integration” and is consistent with the need for marketers to merge tactical and strategic activities $[7]$ . The purpose of full integration is for computerization to create a state where boundaries that separate functions are removed and IT is implemented to allow synchronization and coordination of operations. It is a setting where the completion of one activity fosters development in others. Full integration provides the theoretical basis for this study. ## 4. Antecedents of computer use

Many qualities contribute to effective computer usage $[14]$ . However, they generally focus on technological acceptance and usage as they relate to an individual user, the organization, or both $[5]$ . The influence of the firm on technological innovation implies that while the organization has a key role in the implementation of IT $[1]$ , this influence affects the individual. The literature suggests there are two forms of organizational support that should provide critical links to strategic and tactical activities of marketers within the specific framework of their organizations. Specifically, computer training, company access and use, and marketing computer use directly and indirectly influence computer-aided strategic and tactical activities. These interrelationships result in eight hypotheses that are displayed with the theoretical model in Fig. 2.

## 4.2. Computer training

Marketing computer training focuses on the perceived instruction in computer use provided by the organization to its marketing executives and employees. The initial resistance to computer adoption reinforces training as a critical factor influencing productivity on both strategic and tactical levels $[11]$ . For example, managers at Ault Foods (a food service company) found that training was a key element in getting marketers to regularly use computers $[22]$ . Can a critical mass in the marketing organization develop the requisite computer skills as the technology and conditions of usage advance when computer training is not available? If not, this suggests training influences short and long-term conditions. Correspondingly, marketers who are trained to use computers are likely to apply them to a specific marketing task, which, in turn, should impact tactical and strategic activities. Accordingly, the association between computer training, usage, and strategic and tactical activities should be:

## Hypothesis 3. H3

The provision of computer training in the marketing organization is positively correlated with computer use in the marketing organization.

## Hypothesis 4. H4

The provision of computer training in the marketing organization has significant, positive impacts on computer-aided strategic marketing activities as perceived by marketers.

## Hypothesis 5. H5

The provision of computer training in the marketing organization has significant, positive impacts on computer-aided tactical marketing activities as perceived by marketers.

## 4.3. Marketing computer use

Marketing computer use measures the perceived degree of regular computer use by individuals employed in the marketing organization. The increased usage of computers by marketing professionals provides the basis for the proposal that general computer use within the marketing unit impacts computer use on specific marketing activities. The need for computers to provide individual usefulness is the key rationale that use differs among functional responsibilities $[9]$ . A recent industry report noted that successful insurance agents of the future must be able to use computers for both procedural and strategic tasks $[30]$ . When a specific environment is conducive to computer usage, the user should be able to apply the technology to tactical and strategic tasks.

## Hypothesis 6. H6

Computer use in the marketing organization has significant, positive impacts on computer-aided strategic marketing activities as perceived by marketers.

## Hypothesis 7. H7

Computer use in the marketing organization has significant, positive impacts on computer-aided tactical marketing activities as perceived by marketers.

## 4.4. Tactical and strategic assimilation

Computer-aided marketing activities examine the perceived computer-aided support for activities of marketing executives. Computerization enriches the flow between tactical and strategic activities. IT encourages the merging of discrete activities through computerization. As proficiencies in tactical activities are initially developed, the marketer is able to expand strategic coverage through computerization.

## Hypothesis 8. H8

Computer-aided tactical marketing activities as perceived by marketers have significant, positive impacts on computer-aided strategic marketing activities as perceived by marketers.

## 5. The research design

To examine the eight hypotheses, a questionnaire was developed. It included demographic questions regarding the respondents and their firms. One demographic item allowed respondents to self-classify their position in the marketing organization. There were also 19 items concerning computer availability, use, and training in the company and its marketing organization as well as the types of activities that computers aid. For all but the demographic items, the responses were recorded on a five point Likert-type scale of: (1) strongly disagree; (2) disagree; (3) neutral; (4) agree and (5) strongly agree. The demographics are reported in Table 1 and the questionnaire items are reported in Table 2.

To examine the face-validity of the items, the questionnaire was reviewed by five marketing executives who are familiar with, and use computers and IT. After modifying it based on their comments, the new questionnaire was pre-tested on a sample group of 15 marketing managers. Based on this, the final form of the questionnaire was developed and mailed to 1500 marketing executives. They were selected from a national mailing list in a systematic random fashion. This approach was used to avoid any bias, since the mailing list was ordered by zip code. Upon mailing the questionnaires, no further attempt was made to contact the individuals, due to mailing list restrictions. From the 1500 mailed surveys, 225 usable responses were received, producing a 15% response rate. Such a response rate compares favorably with similar research. The approach is also consistent with other research on marketers' perceptions of computers in that the survey was administered in multiple organizations rather than in a single firm [23]. All the respondents self-classified themselves as marketing executives.

## 6. Response bias

As in any survey research, response bias was a concern. In order to examine its possible presence, a holdout group was established. This involved setting a date for the return of the mailed questionnaires. All questionnaires received after this date were grouped to simulate nonrespondents $[24]$ . In this study, the group of simulated nonrespondents contained 30 individuals leaving 195 responses in the sample. The demographics of the simulated nonrespondents were compared to the demographics of the sample respondents using t-tests. The specific values of these t-tests were: -1.55 for sales level; -0.16 for the number of sales representatives; 2.51 for the number of employees; -1.05 for firm classification; and -0.18 for the percentage of international sales.

The only meaningful difference found was for the number of employees in the firm. The nonrespondents were, on average, from significantly smaller firms. Due to this difference, additional tests were performed. These summed the questionnaire items that formed each measure and compared them for mean differences across the respondents and nonrespondents using t-tests. The resulting t-values were: -0.45 for company computer access and use; 1.28 for marketing computer training; -0.31 for marketing computer use; 1.27 for computer-aided tactical marketing activities; and 1.28 for computer-aided strategic marketing activities. Since the study focuses on these measures of the model's constructs and no differences were found across the sample and the holdout group, response bias should not present a serious problem.

## 7. The sample

In order to provide a clear representation of the sample respondents and their firms, values for the demographic variables were computed. These values are shown in Table 1 and indicate a wide range of firms and respondents in the sample. These also indicate that the responding marketing executives had appropriate experience for the study. Most of the firms (82%) sold products or services as business-to-business firms. The mean sales level of the respondents' firms was over \$15 million with a mean percentage of international sales of just under 8%. The mean number of sales representatives was 22.5 and the mean number of employees was 104.7.

## 8. The measures and their psychometric properties

The five measures were developed from several questionnaire items. Each item was used to measure only one construct. The groupings of these questionnaire items into the five measures were refined or purified by a series of confirmatory factor analyses using a structural equations approach. The motivation for the analyses was to develop measures with desirable psychometric properties and to eliminate any items not measuring the constructs well $[12]$ . Each confirmatory factor analysis was performed using CALIS (covariance analysis of linear structural equations) in PC SAS version 6.12 using maximum likelihood estimation. In each analysis, the questionnaire items were the indicants of the constructs. The constructs were also pair-wise correlated. A total of three purifying confirmatory factor analyses were performed. Each factor analysis eliminated indicants with low standardized path coefficients (i.e., <0.50).

The fit of the final confirmatory factor analysis was summarized by several statistics. The goodness of fit index was 0.90 and this same index adjusted for degrees of freedom was 0.85. The root mean square residual was 0.05, and the significant chi-square statistic had a value of 158.0 with 80 degrees of freedom. The corresponding normed chi-square value was 1.98. Bentler's comparative fit index was 0.96 while Bentler and Bonett's normed and nonnormed and Bollen's normed and nonnormed indexes ranged from 0.89 to 0.96. From these values it can be concluded that the fit of the final confirmatory factor analysis to the data was good. The measures, questionnaire items that formed each measure, and the individual standardized path coefficients are displayed in Table 2.

Using the results of the final confirmatory factor analysis, the psychometric properties of the measures were examined. The first property examined was the average percentage of shared variance among the items within each measure. For the measure of company computer access and use this value was 64%. For marketing computer training the average shared variance was 83%, while for marketing computer use it was 81%. These values for the final two measures of computer-aided tactical and strategic marketing activities as perceived by marketers were 70 and 64%. All values indicate that the measures employed in the study exhibit satisfactory average shared variance [26].

The reliability coefficients for the measures are also displayed in Table 2. These coefficients ranged from a low of 0.78 for the measure of company computer access and use to 0.94 for the measure of marketing computer training. The remaining reliability coefficients were 0.87 for computer-aided tactical marketing activities, 0.87 for computer-aided strategic marketing activities, and 0.93 for marketing computer use. From these values, it is concluded that the measures demonstrate satisfactory composite reliability [21].

Discriminant validity was the final psychometric property examined. If two measures demonstrate discriminant validity, their squared correlation is less than the average percentage of shared variance for each. To examine discriminant validity, the correlations from the final confirmatory factor analysis between all pairs of measures were squared and compared to the appropriate average percentage of shared variances. These squared correlations ranged from 0.01 to 0.21 while the average percentage of shared variances ranged from 64 to 83%. Because all the squared correlations were less than the shared variances, the measures satisfy discriminant validity.

Several conclusions can be made from these results regarding the measures' psychometric properties. Because each standardized path coefficient is 0.76 or larger, item reliability is satisfied. Further, because the average percentage of shared variance for each measure is larger than $50\%$ , all the measures display adequate convergence as measured by these values. In addition, the measures have satisfactory reliability, since all the reliability coefficients are 0.78 or larger [15]. These results imply that convergent validity is satisfied [17]. Coupled with satisfactory discriminant validity, these results imply that the measures display construct validity [24].

## 9. The model and its estimation

In order to examine the interrelationships between the measures of the five constructs, structural equations with latent variables was employed. As in the confirmatory factor analyses, the questionnaire items were indicants of the latent variables for their respective constructs. The interrelationships between the measures as summarized by hypotheses (H1)–(H8) were also represented. The developed model was estimated using the previously discussed 195 responses and the procedure CALIS in SAS PC version 6.12. The estimation method used was maximum likelihood.

## 10. The results

Using the statistics summarizing the model's fit to the data, it is concluded that the fit between the proposed model and the data is good. The goodness of fit index equaled a generally accepted cut-off level of 0.90. The root mean square residual was a relatively low 0.06. Further, while the chi-square statistic (164.4 with 82 degrees of freedom) was statistically significant, the normed chi-square statistic (2.01) was in an acceptable range. In addition, Bentler's comparative fit index was 0.96, Bentler and Bonett's normed and nonnormed indexes were 0.92 and 0.94, while Bollen's normed and nonnormed indexes were 0.89 and 0.96.

The details of the estimated model are displayed in Fig. 3. All the indicants were significantly different from zero, using a 1% significance level. They had standardized path coefficients with the expect signs and were sufficiently large to be meaningful. The values of these coefficients ranged from 0.76 to 0.96. Six of the seven path coefficients for the hypotheses (i.e., all except (H6)) were significantly different from zero, based upon a 1 or 5% significance level. All had meaningfully large coefficients with the expected signs. The significant, estimated path coefficients in standardized form ranged from 0.18 to 0.40. The empirical results provide evidence to support all the hypotheses except that marketing computer use does not significantly impact strategic marketing activities as perceived by marketers. The total implication of this is unclear. Yet, while marketing computer use does not directly impact marketers' perceptions of computer-aided strategic marketing activities, this impact occurs indirectly through marketing computer use improving the perceptions of computer-aided tactical marketing activities.

## 11. Managerial implications

This study suggests that an important managerial concern should be to address the balance of technology with its use. In this light, associations between competitive advantage and planning suggest that marketers can utilize organizational blueprints to incorporate technology. Benefits are more fully developed through the insightful participation of management where linkages between firm controlled conditions (e.g., computer access and use and training), the use of computers, and the expected outcomes from computer use (e.g., tactical and strategic outcomes) can be managed.

The identification of antecedents influencing the computerization of strategic and tactical marketing activities indicates that the evolution of these tasks can be encouraged or impaired, based on how this technology is organizationally supported and used. Further, the results indicate, at least in this case, that computers have developed an assimilative role for these tasks. By supporting and encouraging computer usage, discrete activities can be integrated creating technology synergy. In this context, computerization allows managers to work with higher levels of information. Thus, the integration of strategic information to the local level allows decisions to extend control boundaries beyond what this manager might ordinarily have access to within his/her responsibilities. Accordingly, opportunities for more effective decision making are provided at all managerial levels.

The application of computers to tactical activities was found to positively impact computer-aided strategic marketing activities. Consistent with the contention that integration is a crucial IS offering $[7]$ , such assimilation was experienced in the survey. This should reduce friction between key managerial actions as internal consistency is technologically encouraged between tasks and actions. Hence, assimilation has substantial merit when consistency exists between the tasks. Further, computers used in assimilation may make difficult tasks easier to accomplish. As an illustration, the collection of complex data may occur less frequently (e.g., avoiding dual data collection for separate activities). Computers represent an “escalating tool,” as benefits accumulate based on increased usage.

The empirical results also indicate that encouraging computer use in the marketing department has direct, positive impacts on computer-aided tactical marketing activities. Consequently, management must encourage individual and functional (e.g., sales personnel) usage if the need exists to apply computers to the types of tasks addressed in this study. Such a perspective requires a futuristic vision on the part of upper management. If usage is not encouraged, the opportunity to integrate computerization at the more distant strategic level may be encumbered.

Similarly, the results indicate that providing computer training has direct, significant, and positive impacts on both computer-aided tactical and strategic marketing activities. This suggests that an organization that provides computer training for employees can subsequently improve both tactical and strategic marketing activities.

The significant, positive correlation between marketing computer use and training implies that marketers perceive computer training as encouraging computer use, and vice versa. The more computers are used, the greater the demands for computer training in the marketing organization. Similarly, the more/better the computer training, the more use will be made of computers in the marketing organization. Thus, computer training provides a background to assist the use of computers in tactical marketing activities as well as a basis from which strategic marketing activities can be enriched.

## 12. Conclusions

Like other functional users, marketers are applying information technologies in new ventures, as organizations strive to assimilate new technologies. Such exploration encourages the application of computerization beyond traditional parameters to include both tactical and strategic marketing activities or tasks, as marketers utilize computer technology to address a variety of conditions. However, to date, little was known about the degree to which computer technology assists and/or impedes the integration of separate, but key, interrelated tactical and strategic activities. To address this issue, 195 marketing executives were surveyed for the organizational usage of computer technology. Interestingly, the empirical results found strategic and tactical marketing activities are merged through computerization. In this regard, the results support the use of technology as a growing and encompassing concern with long-term advantages.
