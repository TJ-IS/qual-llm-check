# A perception-based model for EDI adoption in small businesses using a technology-organization-environment framework

Kevin K.Y. Kuan $^{a}$ , Patrick Y.K. Chau $^{b,*}$

$^{a}$ University of Michigan Business School, Ann Arbor, MI 48109, USA

$^{b}$ School of Business, The University of Hong Kong, Pokfulam, Hong Kong

Received 15 September 2000; accepted 28 December 2000

## Abstract

The wide adoption of electronic data interchange (EDI) has been argued to be important for the success of the technology. Past studies on EDI have focused mainly on large firms, as they were the major users at the time. With the advance of technology, however, EDI applications that used to require mainframe computers can be used on PCs at a much lower cost. At a result, small businesses are now able to enjoy the benefits of EDI. Using a technology-organization-environment framework, this study proposes a perception-based small business EDI adoption model that is tested against data collected from 575 small firms in Hong Kong. Six factors are tested using logistic regression and five are found to be significant in distinguishing adopter firms from non-adopter firms. The results suggest the perception-based model using a technology-organization-environment framework is a useful approach for examining factors affecting the adoption decision. For small businesses, while direct benefits are perceived to be higher by adopter firms than by non-adopter firms, indirect benefits are not perceived differently by either adopter firms or non-adopter firms, contrary to the findings in studies on large business. In addition, adopter firms perceive lower financial costs and higher technical competence than non-adopter firms do. Also, adopter firms perceive higher government pressure but lower industry pressure than non-adopter firms do. Implications of the findings and future research areas are discussed. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Electronic data interchange (EDI); Technology adoption; Small business; Perception-based model

## 1. Introduction

Electronic data interchange (EDI) is an inter-organizational system that involves “the movement of business documents electronically between or within firms in a structured, machine-retrievable, data format that permits data to be transferred, without re-keying, from a business application in one location to a business application in another location” [16]. While EDI has been discussed in the literature as a technology that can provide several advantages, both strategic and operational, to its adopters, the adoption rate has not been as high as predicted [3]. As the wide adoption of EDI could increase the economic welfare of the industry as a whole [7], efforts have been made to understand more about the technology and to identify factors affecting the adoption decision.

A review of past EDI adoption literature indicates that past studies have focused mainly on large businesses. This is understandable, as EDI has been the privilege of large businesses because of the huge investment involved in the past. EDI required prior arrangements and dedicated lines, and it was often found to be costly and complex, especially by small-and medium-sized business $[3]$ , which had not been able to realize the benefits of the technology. However, with the advance of technology, EDI applications that used to require mainframe computers can now be used on PCs at a much lower cost. As a result, small businesses are now able to enjoy the benefits of EDI. In addition, governments, trade associations and other organizations have started paying increased attention to promote the use of EDI in small businesses.

Small businesses are the backbone of the economy in many countries. For instance, in the United States, small businesses created two of every three new jobs, produced 39% of the gross national product, and invented more than half of the country's technological innovations in 1997 [38]. Despite the fact that small businesses represent such an important sector of the economy, few studies have been done on EDI adoption by small businesses. Since small businesses possess certain unique characteristics as compared with large businesses, the general applicability of past EDI adoption studies to small businesses is questionable. This paper extends the research in the area of EDI adoption to small businesses. An EDI adoption model for small businesses is developed to investigate the factors affecting the decision to adopt EDI. In particular, the model focuses exclusively on secondary (subjective) characteristics in innovation adoption, as compared with many prior studies that have mixed primary (objective) and secondary (subjective) characteristics together in their adoption model. Downs and Mohr [10] argued that the focus on primary characteristics and the failure to distinguish between primary and secondary characteristics creates inconsistency in findings in innovation studies.

The organization of the paper is as follows. The following section reviews the literature of EDI adoption and small business studies. Next, the research model and the hypotheses are described. The research method, data analysis and results are then presented. The research findings are discussed and the paper concludes with discussions of the study's limitations, the contributions of the study and future research directions.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. EDI adoption

Quite a few studies have focused on different aspects of EDI adoption. For example, Bouchard [4] studied the decisions to use EDI based on what the business partners were doing. Banerjee and Golhar [2] examined the positive and negative impacts of various factors on the EDI selection decision along with the impact of EDI on a firm's employees. Premkumar et al. [28] examined the relationship between various innovation characteristics and various attributes of diffusion of EDI implemented firms. In another study, Premkumar and Ramamurthy [27] examined the role of inter-organizational and organizational factors on the decision mode for adoption of EDI. Hart and Saunders [18] studied the role of power and trust in EDI adoption and use. The study also evaluated the differences between proactive and reactive firms in terms of the extent of adaptation, external connectivity with trading partners and the integration of EDI information. More recently, Lee et al. [22] examined the benefits of EDI to adopters and found that EDI adopters could achieve dramatic performance improvements if EDI networks were used for interfirm process reengineering. All these studies, however, focused mainly on large businesses and did not make a distinction between large and small businesses, although they have very different characteristics. Therefore, the applicability of their findings to small businesses is questionable.

## 2.2. Small businesses

Small firms have long been found to be different from large firms in the information systems (IS) context and they are not a simple scaled-down model of large firms $[30,39]$ . In general, small businesses face substantially greater risks in IS implementation than large businesses do because of inadequate resources and limited education about IS $[8,12]$ . Although, in theory, appropriate IS can help small businesses to develop their markets, to increase sales turnover and to raise profitability, severe constraints on financial and human resources often cause small businesses to lag behind large businesses in the use of information technology (IT) $[44]$ .

Besides resources, small businesses generally lack computer experience and do not have sufficient internal IS expertise $[9,15]$ . They have difficulties in recruiting and retaining internal IS experts due to the scarcity of qualified IS experts and the limited career advancement prospects in small businesses $[23]$ . As a result, factors such as external IS expertise are important in the small business context $[39]$ and factors such as internal support and internal training are found to have little support in the small business context $[20]$ .

In spite of so many differences between small and large businesses, there are but a few prior studies that focus on EDI and small businesses. These studies include Chen and Williams [6], Iacovou et al. [19], Raymond and Bergeron [29] and Tuunainen [43]. Among these, the study by Iacovou et al. presents the most comprehensive research that focuses on EDI adoption in small businesses.

## 2.3. Small business EDI adoption

To address the issue of the major factors that influence the adoption and impact of EDI in the small business context, Iacovou et al. formulated a small business EDI adoption model and tested it based on a multi-case study. The authors argued that small firms resisted becoming EDI-capable because of the (1) limited impact that IT had on small firms due to under-utilization and lack of integration; (2) low levels of IT sophistication and (3) weak market positions of small firms and the network nature of the technology. Based on a review of the past conceptual and empirical research on EDI adoption and small businesses, three major factors that influence the EDI adoption practices of small firms were identified. These factors included (1) perceived benefits; (2) organizational readiness and (3) external pressure.

Perceived benefits referred to the level of recognition of the relative advantage that EDI technology could provide to the organization. As such, they were divided into two categories. The first one was direct benefits, which were mostly operational savings related to the internal efficiency of the organization. The second one was indirect benefits, which were mostly tactical and competitive advantages that had an impact on business processes and relationships. It was argued that small firms that recognized the benefits of EDI would be more likely to adopt EDI than those firms that had a lower level of recognition of the technology's perceived benefits would.

Organizational readiness referred to the level of financial and technological resources of the firm. There were two dimensions in organizational readiness. Financial readiness referred to the financial resources available to pay for EDI installation costs, for implementation of any subsequent enhancements, and for ongoing expenses during usage. Technological readiness referred to the level of sophistication of IT usage and IT management in an organization. It was argued that small firms with higher levels of organizational readiness for EDI would be more likely adopt the technology than firms with lower levels of readiness would.

External pressure referred to the influences from the external business environment. There were two main sources of external pressure: competitive pressure and imposition by trading partners. The former referred to the level of EDI capability in the firm's industry and of its competitors while the latter referred to the potential power and the chosen influence strategy of the trading partners who sought EDI adoption. It was argued that small firms that encountered pressure from their partners or from their competitors would be more likely to adopt EDI than those firms that did not encounter such pressure.

Broadly speaking, Iacovou et al.'s study investigated EDI adoption in small businesses by focusing on factors at the technological (perceived benefits), organizational (organizational readiness) and environmental (external pressure) contexts, which is very similar to the general framework in innovation studies suggested by Tornatzky and Fleischer [41]. According to Tornatzky and Fleischer, there are three contexts that may influence the process by which technological innovations are adopted and implemented: the technological context, the organizational context and the environmental context. This framework has been empirically tested and has been found useful in understanding the adoption of technological innovations. A recent example is Chau and Tam's [5] study on the adoption of open systems. The study reported here integrates the work by Iacovou et al. and Tornatzky and Fleischer, and examines small business EDI adoption from the technological, organizational and environmental contexts.

## 2.4. Perceived characteristics and innovation adoption

At the start of their meta-analysis of 75 articles about innovation characteristics, adoption and implementation, Tornatzky and Klein [42] cited the critique by Downs and Mohr regarding the sources of extreme variances in the findings of innovation studies. According to Downs and Mohr, many innovation studies focus on both “primary” and “secondary” characteristics of innovations but fail to pay sufficient attention to the distinction between the two. Primary characteristics are inherent to the innovation or technology and invariant across settings and organizations, while secondary characteristics are perception-based characteristics. Downs and Mohr argued that the focus on primary characteristics and the failure to distinguish between the primary and secondary characteristics created the inconsistency in the findings of innovation studies.

Based on the critique by Downs and Mohr, Tornatzky and Klein argued that “while so-called primary attributes of innovations can be measured objectively, the meaning of the objective measure of the characteristics is subjective, that is, in the mind of the perceiver” (p. 28). They found that perceived innovation characteristics could predict adoption of various innovations with some degree of consistency across various settings. Similarly, Moore and Benbasat [24] argued that “because different adopters might perceive primary characteristics in different ways, their eventual behaviors might differ” .... Furthermore, studying the interaction among the perceived attributes of innovations helps in the establishment of a general theory (p. 194). As such, in the development of an instrument as a tool for the study of adoption and diffusion of IT, Moore and Benbasat focused on various “perceived” characteristics of using an innovation.

Based on the above argument, this study focuses on the secondary characteristics over the primary characteristics of EDI adoption in small businesses. Just as many characteristics of innovation turn out to be secondary, many organizational characteristics turn out to be secondary as well, as argued by Downs and Mohr. We further argue that characteristics of the external environment also turn out to be secondary.

## 3. A perception-based model for EDI adoption in small businesses based on a technology-organization-environment framework

Based on the works by Iacovou et al., Tornatzky and Fleischer, and Downs and Mohr, a perception-based model for EDI adoption using a technology–organization–environment framework is developed and depicted in Fig. 1. There are three contexts of small business EDI adoption. The technological context refers to perceived technological benefits; the organizational context refers to perceived organizational resources; and the environmental context refers to perceived environmental pressure. Each of these three contexts is discussed below.

## 3.1. Technological context: perceived technological benefits

Characteristics of innovation have been frequently studied in the research on innovation adoption. According to Rogers [32], the adoption of innovations is related to the attributes of the innovations as perceived by potential adopters. Five attributes, including relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, observability, and trialability, are suggested to influence adoption. Quite a number of innovation adoption studies have used Rogers' attributes as their theoretical basis (e.g. [4,17,24]).

Tornatzky and Klein conducted a review and meta-analysis of 75 innovation studies and identified ten characteristics that had been addressed most frequently, which included the five attributes identified by Rogers. Among them, compatibility, relative advantage and complexity were found to have consistent associations with innovation behaviors. However, relative advantage is the only variable that has been consistently identified as a critical adoption factor and as the most important factor for IT growth in small firms [8]. The relative advantage of EDI was found to be one of the major predictors of the initial use in the first application [28].

In this study, relative advantage is proposed to be the key factor in the technological context. In particular, the factor is operationalized as perceived technological benefits, which refer to the degree to which EDI is perceived as providing the benefits to the organization. This is similar to the definition employed by Iacovou et al., and the focus here is on perceived benefits rather than benefits that are actually provided. As argued by Rogers, it is “the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being better than the idea it supersedes that has a direct impact on the likelihood of adoption” (p. 213).

Among the benefits of EDI, some are strategic and some are operational [31]. The former relates to the development of corporate strategies through the building of external relationships with customers and competitors, and was grouped by Iacovou et al. as “indirect benefits”. Examples include improving the organization’s image, improving competitive advantage, benefiting other business practices, improving customer services and improving relationships with business partners. Operational benefits refer to improvements made to the internal functioning of the organization apparent in everyday activities $[2,31]$ . They include improving data accuracy, improving security of data, improving operation efficiency, speeding up application processes and reducing clerical errors. Iacovou et al. called these “direct benefits”. This study adopts the same distinction between the two types of benefits. The above arguments lead to the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1a. Adopter firms perceive higher levels of direct benefits than non-adopter firms do.

Hypothesis 1b. Adopter firms perceive higher levels of indirect benefits than non-adopter firms do.

## 3.2. Organizational context: perceived organizational resources

While it is important that the company perceives the benefits of the technology, it is also important that the benefits perceived by the company can be achieved within the allotted resources. If the perceived benefits cannot be achieved due to lack of resources, adoption is meaningless to the company regardless of how great the benefits are. As Kwon and Zmud [21] asserted, successful IS implementation occurs when sufficient organizational resources (sufficient developer and user time, sufficient funding, sufficient technical skills, etc.) are directed, first toward motivation, then toward sustaining the implementation effort.

A review of past studies shows that costs and technical compatibility are two major predictors of the initial use of EDI in the first application $[28]$ . Also, costs and technical knowledge have been identified as two of the most important factors that hinder IT growth in small organizations $[8]$ . Iacovou et al. called these two factors organizational readiness in their study. Defined as the levels of financial and technological resources of the firm, organizational readiness was suggested to be a factor affecting EDI adoption in small business. Indicators such as the quick ratio, the working capital/sales ratio and the general and administrative expenses/sales ratio were used to measure the level of financial readiness of an organization. The level of technological readiness was measured using the IT sophistication scale developed by Pare and Raymond $[26]$ . Both these types of readiness focus on the amount of requisite resources that the organization possesses to be able to carry out the course of action in question. In other words, they are operationalized as primary characteristics of the organization.

As argued in the previous section, the focus on primary characteristics might have lead to the inconsistency of findings in prior innovation studies $[10]$ . Moore and Benbasat $[24]$ argued that while actual cost was a primary characteristic, perceived cost was a secondary characteristic. What might appear to be costly to an adopter could be inexpensive to another. Therefore, given two firms with the same level of financial readiness as measured by the financial indicators as employed by Iacovou et al., one firm may perceive itself with high financial readiness, but another firm may perceive itself with low financial readiness. In fact, this may partly explain the weak relationship between organizational readiness and adoption in the study by Iacovou et al.

Based on the above arguments, we modified the financial readiness and the technological readiness factors used by Iacovou et al. and focused exclusively on the perceived characteristics of organizational resources. Factors such as financial cost and technical knowledge were identified as important factors for EDI use and small firm computing $[8,17]$ . These two aspects of organizational resources are included in our model as perceived financial cost and perceived technical competence. The above viewpoint leads to the following two hypotheses.

Hypothesis 2a. Adopter firms perceive lower levels of financial costs than non-adopter firms do.

Hypothesis 2b. Adopter firms perceive higher levels of technical competence than non-adopter firms do.

## 3.3. Environmental context: perceived environmental pressure

External pressure is also included in our research model to refer to influences from the external environment. In many cases, a company may adopt a technology due to influences exerted by its business partners and/or its competitors and the decision has nothing to do with the technology and the organization per se. Pressure from business partners and/or competitors has been found to be an important factor in

EDI adoption [4,18,27]. External industry pressure is used in this study to capture this kind of pressure from the industry (i.e. business partners and competitors). A firm may feel pressure to adopt the technology if its business partners request or recommend it to do so. A firm may also feel pressure when it sees more and more companies in the industry (either its business partners or competitors) adopting the technology and therefore feels the need to adopt in order to remain competitive.

Another pressing and practical reason for small businesses to adopt IT comes from government policies. For example, in the United States, an executive order was issued in response to the paper reduction act, which required federal agencies to convert to electronic commerce by January 1997 [37]. A small business that wants to do business with the federal government must therefore be EDI-capable. In our study, the EDI system in question, called ValuNet, is a system used between business organizations and the government for import and export declarations. The government has required that all import and export documents to be submitted through the EDI system by a specific deadline. In addition, government counters for paper-based applications for import and export declarations will be closed by the same deadline, and EDI will be the only means of submitting an import or export declaration. The pressure exerted by such government policy may be perceived differently by different firms. Based on the above arguments, the following hypotheses are posited.

Hypothesis 3a. Adopter firms perceive higher levels of industry pressure than non-adopter firms do.

Hypothesis 3b. Adopter firms perceive higher levels of government pressure than non-adopter firms do.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Research strategy

Both case-based and survey-based approaches have been used to explore issues of EDI adoption in both the large and small business contexts. While the case study method can provide richer descriptions of the issue/phenomenon being investigated, surveys can “provide a basis for generalizing, allow for replicability, and permit some degree of statistical power” [4] (p. 367). The survey method was chosen for this study to allow for easy replication and for findings to be reconfirmed or disconfirmed.

Since organizations but not individuals adopt EDI, the unit of analysis for the study was therefore at the organizational level. Subjects for this study were required to be senior informed respondents within the organization. With the assistance of the organization that sponsored this research, the names of either the owners or the top managers of the targeted organizations were obtained. As our target was small businesses, the owner and the top manager were often the same person. A letter stating the purpose of the study and the strict confidence of the data was sent along with the survey package.

The EDI system being investigated in the study is called ValuNet. It is an EDI software that runs on a personal computer to allow a trading company in Hong Kong to lodge trade declarations for import and export via electronic means. Import or export declaration is mandatory for every consignment shipped in or out of the region. With ValuNet, companies can lodge their declarations directly with the government through their own computers in their offices and pay the appropriate charges electronically. A quasi-government company called Tradelink, which has been granted a 7-year franchise, provides the service. $^{1}$

## 4.2. Instrument development and operationalization of factors

As discussed above, only a few studies have been conducted in the context of small business EDI adoption and most of them have used a case-study approach. Therefore, to operationalize the constructs in the model, direct use of instruments in previous studies is not always possible. Over half of the items used were therefore specifically developed for this study based on the literature in a variety of sources, including trade journals and pamphlets published by vendors of EDI products. Appendix A lists the items used in the study. Operationalization of the factors is discussed as follows.

Perceived direct and indirect benefits were operationalized by items adapted from Iacovou et al. [19] and

Arunachalam [1]. Respondents were asked to give their level of agreement or disagreement on potential direct or indirect benefits of adopting the EDI product on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Five items were used to measure direct benefits and five were used to measure perceived indirect benefits.

Perceived financial costs were measured by three items based on either prior studies $[1,11]$ or articles in various IT magazines. These items cover costs related to set-up, training, and running of the hardware and software involved. A seven-point Likert-type scale was used, with anchors ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”.

Perceived technical competence was operationalized with three items that measured the organization's IT performance in terms of expertise and experience in providing general IT and EDI supports. Respondents were asked to evaluate these aspects on a seven-point Likert-type scale with anchors from “very bad” to “very good”.

Perceived industry pressure was measured by six items that were related to the degree of influence exerted by business partners and competitors on the adoption decision as perceived by the respondents. Perceived government pressure was operationalized with two items that measured the degree of influence of the mandatory measures carried out by the government. Respondents were asked to describe the degree of influence on a seven-point Likert-type scale with anchors from “no influence at all” to “very strong influence”.

The dependent variable, EDI adoption, was determined by a binary measure: adopter firms or non-adopter firms. Organizations were classified as adopter firms if they met the following two criteria: (1) already a subscriber to the EDI system being studied and (2) had lodged at least one trade declaration using the EDI system in the past three months. Instead of asking the respondents to report their transactions, the list of the adopter firms was obtained from the EDI service provider.

## 4.3. Subjects

Consistent with Igbaria et al. [20] and many other studies on small firm computing, small businesses in this study were defined as firms with not more than one hundred employees. Subjects were drawn from a list of current or potential customers of the EDI service being studied that was provided by the EDI service provider. The list contained 3000 organizations. A letter of purpose about this study, together with a questionnaire, was sent to the senior executives of these organizations. A follow-up telephone call was made to each of these senior executives after 2 weeks.

Seven hundred and seventy-one questionnaires were received within a month after the questionnaires were sent, with a response rate of 25.7%. However, 46 responses were excluded because of incomplete responses or because the firms were found to have more than 100 employees and did not meet the small business qualification. Among the remaining 725 respondents, 263 were categorized as adopter firms based on our definition stated above. Three hundred and twelve small firms were non-adopter firms and had no plan at the time of the survey to adopt the system. There were another 150 firms that had not adopted the EDI system at the time of the survey but had a clear plan to adopt the system from within 1 to 3 months in advance of the government-set deadline. As the focus of our study was to find out the differences between those companies that had adopted the EDI system and conducted at least one transaction (i.e. adopter firms), and companies that had not adopted the EDI system and had no plan to adopt the system (i.e. non-adopter firms), this third group of small businesses was excluded from the data analysis. Therefore, the sample for our data analysis included 263 (45.7%) adopter firms and 312 (54.3%) non-adopters firms.

## 5. Data analysis and results

## 5.1. Evaluation of non-response bias

Non-response is a potential source of bias in survey studies that needs to be properly addressed [14]. The potential biases in this study were evaluated by comparing the responses between early and late respondents on the following two groups of measures: demographic data (number of employees and annual turnover) and responses to the question items for the six constructs in the research model. Early respondents were defined as those who had completed and returned the questionnaires within the initial 2-week response window while late respondents were those who returned the questionnaires after the specified response period. Around 60% of the responses (351 out of 575) were from early respondents. No significant difference was found in terms of number of employees ( $\chi^{2}=1.736, P=0.784$ ) and annual turnover ( $\chi^{2}=9.754, P=0.283$ ). As for responses to question items for the six constructs, as shown in Table 1, no significant difference was found. The above analysis suggests that non-response biases, if any, should not be serious.

## 5.2. Measurement model

The measurement model was evaluated for reliability, convergent validity and discriminant validity. Construct reliability or internal consistency was assessed by computing Cronbach's alpha. Table 2 shows the reliability results of the constructs. The alpha values range from 0.7132 to 0.9609. The relatively low Cronbach's alpha for “perceived government pressure” can be partly explained by the small number of items (only two items used) as the calculation of the alpha is affected by the length of the construct. However, it is still above the recommended value of 0.7 [25]. Construct reliabilities are therefore deemed to be sufficient for all factors.

Convergent validity and discriminant validity were assessed by performing a factor analysis. A principal components analysis was performed and all unrotated factors with eigen-values at least one were extracted. The solution was rotated using the Varimax criterion. Convergent validity is demonstrated if the items load strongly (loading > 0.50) on their associated factors. Discriminant validity is achieved if each item loads stronger on its associated factor than on any other factors. Table 3 shows the results. All items have loadings >0.50 on their associated factors and load stronger on their associated factors than on other factors. Convergent validity and discriminant validity are therefore demonstrated.

## 5.3. Research model

The logistic regression technique was applied to test the research model. This multivariate statistical technique was chosen over multiple regression analysis because the dependent variable (adopter firms versus non-adopter firms) was dichotomous. Using a dichotomous variable in multiple regression analysis would necessarily violate the assumptions for hypothesis testing. Also, logistic regression analysis requires fewer assumptions than discriminant analysis; even when the assumptions required in discriminant analysis are satisfied, logistic regression still performs well [35].

The significance of the regression coefficients of the hypothesized independent variables was examined to determine support for the hypothesis. Wald statistics were used in the significance test. Table 4 shows the results. The goodness-of-fit statistic ( $\chi^{2}=489.947$ , significance =0.9921) indicates that the logistic regression model is not significantly different from a perfect model that correctly classifies all respondents into their respective groups: adopter firms and non-adopter firms.

All factors except “perceived indirect benefits” had coefficients significantly different from 0 (P < 0.01). Therefore, Hypothesis 1b, which postulated that “adopter firms perceive higher levels of indirect benefits than non-adopter firms do” was not supported. In addition, Hypothesis 3a, which stated that “adopter firms perceived higher levels of industry pressure than non-adopter firms do”, was supported (P < 0.01) but in the opposite direction, which meant that adopter firms were found to perceive lower industry pressure than non-adopter firms did. Other than those exceptions, very strong support was found for all other hypotheses as postulated (P < 0.01).

The model was also assessed for its discriminating power. As there were 263 adopter firms and 312 non-adopter firms, guessing the adoption decision by random choice would result in a $50.36\%$ accuracy $((263 / 575)^{2} + (1 - (263 / 575))^{2} = 0.5036)$ . The logistic regression model achieved a classification accuracy of $77.04\%$ , which is much better than by random choice.

## 6. Discussion

The goal of this study was to extend understanding of EDI adoption in small businesses by identifying factors that distinguish adopter firms from non-adopter firms. In the following section, we discuss each of the factors that have been identified in this study.

## 6.1. Perceived technological benefits

Many studies in the innovation and IT adoption literature begin with the attributes of the innovations. As Rogers argued, adoption of innovations is related to the attributes of the innovations as perceived by potential adopters. In studies of EDI adoption, many considered the EDI benefits as one of the explanatory factors.

Respondents from small firms in our study were asked the extent to which they agreed that EDI could help them achieve certain direct and indirect benefits commonly identified in the EDI literature. Consistent with the findings of many prior EDI studies in both large and small firms (e.g. [40]), it was found that perceived direct benefits were significant in EDI adoption for small business as well. In other words, compared with non-adopter firms, EDI was perceived higher by adopter firms to help the business achieve certain benefits such as improving data accuracy, improving security of data, improving operation efficiency, speeding up application processes and reducing clerical errors.

Perceived indirect benefits were not found to be significant in distinguishing adopter firms from non-adopter firms in our study. One plausible explanation for the insignificance of indirect benefits in small businesses might be the lack of awareness of those benefits by both adopter firms and non-adopter firms. In general, small businesses are not as well informed and connected as large business. As Iacovou et al. found in their study, while most of the small firms (adopter firms and non-adopter firms) they studied recognized the direct benefits of EDI, only adopter firms recognized the indirect benefits of EDI. Even so, non-adopter firms did not perceive the lack of EDI capability as a competitive disadvantage to their businesses. In other words, even though small firms were aware of such indirect benefits, such purported advantages were not perceived as particularly important by respondents. More promotional effort would therefore be needed to increase the awareness of these benefits of EDI in order to attract a small business to adopt the technology.

Another explanation for the insignificance of indirect benefits is that while EDI provided positive indirect benefits to small firms, it fell short of the firms' expectations and therefore positive perceived indirect benefits did not result in positive adoption decisions. As Fearon and Philip [13] found in their study on evaluating the benefit success associated with EDI, there was in general a shortfall or negative gap (deficiency) experienced between expectations and perceptions of realized benefits of EDI throughout companies as a whole. If this is the case, not only was it important for EDI service providers to provide information on positive indirect benefits to users, but it is also important that such benefits meet, or even exceed, the expectations of the users.

## 6.2. Perceived organizational resources

Compared with large businesses, small businesses tend to lack the resources that are necessary for EDI and other IT adoption $[4,33]$ . Therefore, having the required financial and technical resources becomes an important concern for EDI adoption in small businesses.

Respondents of small firms in our study were asked the extent to which the required financial costs would pose an obstacle to the adoption of EDI. It was found that perceived financial costs were significantly distinguishing adopter firms from non-adopter firms in the case of small businesses. Adopter firms perceived financial costs less as an obstacle than non-adopter firms did. One plausible reason might be due to the fact that non-adopter firms were not financially ready to adopt EDI. Another reason might be because these firms perceived the costs of adopting EDI to be too high, even though they actually had the necessary financial resources. Therefore, in the former case, financial assistance such as software and hardware discounts would encourage the adoption. In the latter case, while financial assistance might still help, more promotional efforts are needed to help these small firms realize the true value and costs of adopting EDI that will eventually justify their investments.

Companies were also asked to evaluate the performance of their internal technical competence in terms of general IT support and EDI support. The results showed that adopter firms had a better perception of their internal technical competence than non-adopter firms did. This implies that compared with adopter firms, non-adopter firms perceived themselves not to have the necessary technical resources to support EDI. External support, therefore, might help in this area. Because of little internal IT expertise, small businesses are generally very reliant on the advice and support from vendors $[8]$ . In the context of small businesses, external support has several advantages over internal support. Simon $[34]$ suggested at least four advantages. First, small businesses do not need to maintain expensive internal IS staff when the implementation is completed and maintenance is infrequent. Second, they do not need to provide expensive ongoing professional training for the internal IS staff to keep up with advancing technology. Third, it is difficult to engage qualified internal IS staff due to their scarcity and the limited career advancement prospect in small businesses. Fourth, the increasingly complex technology will require hiring of various specialists which is not feasible in small business. EDI providers could cooperate with hardware and software vendors to develop specific hardware and software support packages to small businesses in order to facilitate the adoption.

## 6.3. Perceived environmental pressure

In a complex business environment, it is expected that a firm's adoption decision be affected by the external environment. This is especially true for inter-organizational systems such as EDI, which involves more than one organization or a network of organizations.

Surprisingly, those small firms that had adopted EDI in our study perceived significantly lower pressure from the industry than non-adopter firms did, as opposed to what most past literature suggested. A plausible explanation is that when those adopter firms introduced the technology to their companies, there were not many firms in the industry using the technology. As a result, the adoption decision might be based on other concerns such as benefits and costs. As for non-adopter firms, while they might not have been able to see the benefits of adopting the technology, and the cost of adoption was perceived to be high, they saw the use of EDI as an industry trend and they did feel pressure to adopt it. However, they decided to defer the adoption decision as long as possible. As Clemons and Row [7] suggested, even though it was widely believed that industry-wide acceptance of EDI would increase the economic welfare of the industry as a whole, many organizations declined adoption. This was because it was not clear that their firms would receive any of the surpluses. Furthermore, many companies might believe that the technology would result in an overall weaker bargaining position and therefore lower profits.

As postulated, adopter firms perceived higher pressure from the government than non-adopter firms did. The pressure primarily came from the government's plan to terminate all the paper submission of import and export declarations, which were essential for all legal imports and exports. Although the government pressure could eventually push all companies to adopt the technology, some chose not to adopt before the government deadline. This was especially true if the firms did not perceive a need to adopt the technology besides fulfilling legal requirements. As Stern and Kaufman [36] found in their study on EDI in selected consumer industries, the majority of EDI users believed that their EDI partners benefited more from the system than themselves. Some small firms might believe that the government benefited more from EDI than they did, and so they would not take the initiative to adopt the system by themselves. Therefore, while legal requirements and enforcement could push the adoption, other factors, such as EDI benefits and costs should be taken into consideration should the government or other EDI initiators want to see their targeted firms take a proactive approach in the adoption decision.

## 7. Limitations

Our study has a number of limitations. First of all, this study focuses only on the adoption decision but not on implementation. A longitudinal study on the implementation issue is suggested to facilitate understanding EDI implementation in small businesses. Second, the EDI system under study was based on one EDI system for import and export declarations between companies and the government. Findings of prior studies on the role of power and trust between firms on the adoption and use of EDI (e.g. [18]) may therefore be difficult to compare with our findings, and vice versa. In other words, interpretation of the findings should be done with care when extending it to those EDI systems of a business-to-business nature. Third, single responses from each small business were collected, thus leading to the possibility of response bias. The problem was deemed not to be a serious one given the unique characteristics of small businesses in terms of their top management structure, with decision making done mostly by a single person (i.e. the owner/manager) and not by a group of senior management.

## 8. Conclusion

Given the importance of wide spread adoption for the success of EDI and the “much-slower-than-expected” growth of EDI adoption in small business, there is certainly a need to understand more about what factors are important in the EDI adoption decision of a small business. This study represents an early attempt to examine a perception-based small business EDI adoption model that was theoretically grounded in a technology–organization–environment framework. The model was empirically evaluated using data collected from 575 small firms, with 46% of them having adopted the technology. Several implications can be drawn from the findings of the study.

First, while prior studies on EDI adoption focused on large businesses, this study advances the understanding of EDI adoption in small businesses. More importantly, such understanding was achieved through distinguishing between primary and secondary characteristics and focusing on the latter. In agreement with Downs and Mohr, Tornatzky and Klein, and Moore and Benbasat, we believe the focus on secondary characteristics would facilitate a more general understanding in IT adoption. In fact, of the six “perceived” factors, five of them turned out to have significant influences on EDI adoption by small businesses. Future research work on IT adoption should therefore seriously look into the distinction between primary and secondary characteristics of a technology adoption when developing research models.

The study also confirms the usefulness of the technology–organization–environment framework for studying adoption of technological innovations. The decision to adopt an IT, and inter-organizational systems, is not primarily based on the characteristics of the technology itself. In the case of EDI adoption, the decision also depends upon other factors related to the internal organization and the external environment. Future work is needed, however, to re-examine the proposed model in other EDI adoption settings, particularly in a business-to-business EDI context, and/or with other IT innovations.

The focus of this study was on adoption of EDI, which should be considered as part of the broader issue of management of technology. As adopting a technology should eventually lead to some form of positive impacts or advantages, which can be operational, managerial, and strategic in nature, for an organization, evaluation and assessment of the adoption and implementation of the technology are important too. Additional work should therefore be done to extend the model examined in this study to include integration and impact, as suggested by Iacovou et al.

From a managerial standpoint, the findings of the study reveal that it is important for small businesses to recognize and positively perceive the benefits of EDI. While EDI can provide both direct and indirect benefits, small businesses are more aware of the direct benefits. This suggests that given the importance of indirect benefits that might help a small business to maintain or even to gain long-term competitiveness, more effort is needed to increase the awareness of these types of EDI benefits. Moreover, it is important for a small business to perceive costs not as an obstacle to adoption and to perceive the prepossession of technical knowledge and expertise as necessary to support the use of EDI. This could be achieved by providing appropriate financial and technical support from EDI providers and other relevant parties. Finally, as a high penetration level is important for the success of EDI, EDI initiators and advocates, including the government, EDI vendors, trade associations and IT vendors, should consider devising appropriate non-coercive strategies and plans to promote the adoption of EDI among their targeted organizations.

He received his PhD in Business Administration from the Richard Ivey School of Business (formerly Western Business School), The University of Western Ontario, Canada. His research interests include issues related to IS/IT adoption and implementation, information presentation and model vi-

sualization, and electronic commerce. He has papers published in major information systems journals including Management Information Systems Quarterly, Communications of the ACM, Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Sciences, International Journal of Human Computer Studies, Decision Support Systems, Information and Management, Journal of Organizational Computing and Electronic Commerce, European Journal of Information Systems, etc.
