# Determinants of the intention to use Internet/WWW at work: a confirmatory study

Man Kit Chang $^{a,*}$ , Waiman Cheung $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Finance and Decision Sciences, School of Business, Hong Kong Baptist University, Kowloon Tong, Hong Kong, PR China $^{b}$ Department of Decision Sciences and Managerial Economic, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, PR China

Received 19 June 2000; received in revised form 5 December 2000; accepted 17 January 2001

## Abstract

Previous studies on adoption of the Internet and WWW have investigated factors affecting usage. We believe that the intention to use a technology is equally important, not only for promoting the technology but also for encouraging its voluntary continued use. Although the Triandis model has been applied to a number of technology adoption studies, its adequacy has not been assessed by using a confirmatory analysis method. Structural equation modeling may be used to assess the adequacy of the model for a technology adoption study and to compare it to alternative specifications that integrate previous research results. To prove our hypothesis, data were collected from 255 part-time MBA and diploma students. The results show that our proposed model provides a better fit than the original model. The structural path model shows the important factors that influence the intention to use the Internet/WWW as well as interrelationships between the factors. Comparing previous results on actual usage, the relative importance of factors affecting intention formation is different. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Triandis model of choice; Internet/WWW adoption; Confirmatory analysis

## 1. Introduction

According to an Internet demographics survey of June 1998, 50% of the Internet users, estimated to be 79 million in the US and Canada alone, use the Internet at work [26]. In a recent American Internet User Survey [15], 77% of the American adults agreed that online services make their work more productive. Therefore, it is important to understand factors affecting the adoption of the Internet and the WWW at work and, subsequently, to provide guidance to encourage productive use of the technology.

A number of studies have investigated factors affecting the current usage of the Internet or the WWW at work $[5,24,30]$ . We believe that it is equally important to understand the determinants of “intention” to use the Internet/WWW at work, since such an understanding is useful for promoting, as well as encouraging continuous voluntary use of a technology. As social psychological theories (such as the Theory of Reasoned Action) postulate, formation of intention precedes actual behavior and intention is the best predictor of the actual behavior. An individual may discontinue the use of a technology after using it for a period of time. In other words, they do not intend to continue using the technology. For instance, the American Internet User Survey reports that 15.9 million US adults tried the Internet in the past 12 months, and, for whatever reason, are no longer users. As Herold et al. [22] pointed out, an intention is somewhat less influenced by external or environmental pressure relative to the behavior, and that determinants of an intention are often different from those of the actual behavior [2,3,34]. Thus, investigating factors affecting intention formation will provide insights on how to stop a person discontinuing working with a useful technology.

Recent studies on Internet adoption all focus on current usage and are generally based on the technology acceptance model (TAM) $[16,17]$ or extensions to it by including enjoyment as one of the predictors. In contrast, enjoyment or affect has always been part of the Triandis model. It also includes constructs, such as social factors and facilitating conditions, which we believe are important in understanding the intention to use the Internet/WWW. Therefore, the Triandis model was chosen as a theoretical foundation for this study.

The Triandis model has been used in studying the adoption of the personal computer $[28,31,32]$ and executive information systems (EIS) $[10]$ . Path analysis or partial least square analysis was used to identify determinants in these studies. While the constructs included in the Triandis model were found to be useful in predicting the usage of the technologies, the overall model fitness was not assessed. In a confirmatory study, an alternative model specification was found to fit the empirical data better than the original

Triandis formulation for predicting the intention to use mammography $[6]$ . Our study uses structural equation modeling to assess the adequacy of applying the Triandis model of choice in an Internet adoption study. Alternative specifications are formulated by modifying and extending the Triandis model according to previous study results. By assessing the adequacy of these alternatives, we have gained a better understanding of the interplay between the different determinants of the intention to use the Internet/WWW.

## 2. Triandis model and its applications

## 2.1. Triandis model of choice

Fig. 1 depicts the Triandis model of choice, which stipulates an attitude–intention–behavior relationship. The model postulates that the probability of performing an act, such as using the Internet/WWW, is a function of (i) habits, (ii) intention to perform the act, and (iii) facilitating conditions. Facilitating conditions refer to the objective factors, ‘out there’ in the environment, that make an act easy. On the other hand, the intention of performing a certain act is a function of the (i) perceived consequences of the act, (ii) the person’s conception of what he or she should do or social factors, and (iii) what one would enjoy doing or pure affect.

![](/api/attachments/ADBFZJGF/fulltext/images/ae2ecdd1e93191aa1d810e00fd4efa98011fa202f3f919cd7a4808c24d6fe578.jpg)  
Fig. 1. Schematic of the Triandis model (adopted from [34]).

Perceived consequences represent the question: does the behavior have some consequences of value to the performer? Thus, the perceived consequences of a behavior are defined in [33] as

$$
C = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} P _ {c _ {i}} V _ {c _ {i}}
$$

where C is the value of the perceived consequences; $P_{c_{i}}$ the subjective probability that a particular consequence i will follow a behavior; $V_{c_{i}}$ the value of a consequence i; n the number of consequences that a subject perceives as likely to follow a particular behavior.

While perceived consequences are the cognitive part, affect is the affective component of an individual's attitude: the pure emotion-excitement, joy, pleasure versus depression, disgust, displeasure-associated with the thought of an actual act [35]. Some acts may have valuable consequences but are unpleasant to perform.

Social factor is an individual's internalization of the local culture or agreements made with others. Subjective culture consists of ways of categorizing experiences, beliefs, attitudes, ideals, roles, norms, and values that a human group views the human-made part of its environment. This concept is similar to Ajzen and Fishbein's subjective norm in their theory of reasoned action.

The Triandis model has been found useful in explaining and predicting a variety of intentions and behaviors such as the prediction of the intention to use mammography, exercise intention and behavior $[37]$ , and intention to engage in casual sex. The Triandis model has also been applied to the study of IS usage.

## 2.2. Systems adoption — studies using Trandis model

Thompson et al. used the Triandis model to perform two studies on personal computer (PC) usage. Intention and habit in the model were not included in this investigation. Three categories of perceived consequences were conceptualized and operationalized. The first was job fit or near-term consequences; these were defined as the capabilities of using a PC to enhance an individual's job performance. The second was long-term consequences; these referred to outcomes that would pay-off in the future, such as more meaningful work. The last was complexity; this was defined as the perception of relative difficulty of use of the PC. The results showed that social factors, job fit, and complexity influenced PC usage significantly. Long-term consequences were found to be significant in the first but not the second study. Facilitating conditions were found to have a significant impact in the first study but an unexpected negative relationship in the second. Finally, affect was not found to be significant in either study.

Pare and Elam also applied the Triandis model to study the use of the personal computer. Their results found that social norms, consequences (i.e. perceived usefulness, beliefs about computers), facilitating conditions, and habits all had significant positive influence on PC use. Anxiety was found to have a significant negative impact. This study confirmed all the postulated relationships in the Triandis model.

In a study of EIS usage perceived consequences, affect, and facilitating conditions were found to significantly influence the intensity of usage, and only affect was found to significantly influence the frequency of use. Both habit and social factors were found to have no relationship with either dependent variables.

In general, the utility of the Triandis model in explaining IT has gained support. However, results explaining how certain factors, such as affect and facilitating conditions, influence usage are still mixed. Since all these studies used path analysis or partial least square for data analysis, no confirmation of the correctness of model specification was made. Moreover, all these studies investigated factors affecting current usage of the technologies, bypassing intention. We believe that understanding the dynamic of intention formation is equally important, particularly in predicting the continuing of use of the technology.

Three models, one simple adoption from the original Triandis model and two modified/extended versions based on previous study results, are specified for adequacy assessment and comparison. The first model contains only direct paths from determinants (social factors, affects, and perceived consequences) to intention, as posited in the original Triandis. It should be noted that the nature of perceived complexity as a consequence is quite different from short-term and long-term consequences. Triandis stated that a person's perception that the act is easy is an 'internal' factor and should be included in perceived consequence terms. That is to say, the perception of the complexity of using the Internet and the WWW should be put under the construct perceived consequences because it is an "internal" factor. Thus, perceived complexity is treated as a perception of the characteristics of the technology being used instead of as a consequence of using the technology in this study. Complexity, in the context of the use of the Internet, may include such areas as difficulty for the user to understand its operations.

The second model contains all the paths in model 1 and a path from facilitating conditions to intention (the dotted line in Fig. 2). In Triandis' original formulation, facilitating conditions only affect the actual behavior, but not the intention. However, empirical investigations have shown that facilitating conditions could have a significant positive impact on intention. This is also consistent with the theory of planned behavior [1], which posits that intention will be affected by perceived behavioral control; this is similar to facilitating conditions in the Triandis model.

The third model contains all the paths from model 2 plus all the paths indicated using thick arrows in Fig. 2. Previous studies have found that ease of use, which is the opposite of complexity, does not affect usage directly but indirectly through its positive impact on perceived usefulness [18,20]. Also, it was found that perceived ease of use affects perceived enjoyment positively in the use of computer technology [23]. That is to say, users feel happier using a technology if it is easy to use. A path is also added between social factors and affect because studies based on the theory of reasoned action found that normative and affective structure are not independent and social norms have a positive impact on a person's feeling toward his/her behavior [13,29,36]. In other words, how one feels about using the Internet/WWW will be affected by their perception of whether their “significant others” approve or disapprove of using it. Finally, near-term consequences are hypothesized to have a positive impact on long-term consequences [14].

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Subjects

Data were gathered from part-time MBA and diploma students in two universities in Hong Kong. A total of 320 questionnaires were distributed and 261 were returned. Six questionnaires were not finished and were therefore discarded. This yielded a total of 255 usable questionnaires (a net response rate of 78%). Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics of the respondents.

## 4.2.1. Complexity

The measurement scale was developed based on Thompson et al. and adapted for use with the Internet/WWW. Four statements, such as “working with the Internet/WWW is complicated, it is difficult to understand what is going on,” were used. All questions in nearly all parts, as shown in the Appendix A, were rated on a five-point scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5).

## 4.2.2. Near-term and long-term consequences

The value of a perceived consequence is equal to the product of the subjective probability that a particular consequence follows a behavior and the value of that consequence. Four items on near-term consequences and four items on long-term consequences were adapted from Thompson. Respondents were asked whether they agreed or disagreed that certain consequence would happen. In addition, the respondents were asked how they would value such consequences. Here, a five-point scale ranging from very bad $(-2)$ to very good $(+2)$ was used. By multiplying the two scores for each corresponding item, a perceived value for a consequence was derived. Thus, the resulting score varied from -10 to +10.

## 4.2.3. Affect

To measure different aspects of affect, we followed Triandis' suggestion to operationalize the affect construct by using a four-item semantic differential scale. On a five-point fully anchored scale, respondents were asked whether they felt using the Internet/WWW was enjoyable—disgusting, dull—exciting, pleasant—unpleasant, and interesting—boring.

## 4.2.4. Social factors

We developed a new scale according to Triandis to measure social factors. The subjects were asked whether they perceived that their work group (top management, immediate supervisor, and coworker) thought they should use the Internet/WWW and whether they would follow what others thought they should do. A five-point scale was used. The measure of social factors was derived by multiplying the two scores in pairs. This operationalization is similar to the one used by Bergeron et al.

## 4.2.5. Facilitating conditions

In terms of Internet and WWW utilization, facilitating conditions were conceptualized as support provided by the company and ease of access to the Internet. Two items were adapted from Thompson et al.'s original scale. In addition, one item measuring ease of access to the Internet/WWW and another for general support were added.

## 4.2.6. Intention

Two items were used to measure intention to use the Internet/WWW.

## 4.3. Data analysis

Structural equation modeling (SEM) with latent variables was used for data analysis. EQS [9] was used to perform the structural modeling analysis. SEM is a confirmatory approach to data analysis [12], which is highly appropriate in the present context. Since the Triandis model has been applied and validated in a large number of studies, we have strong theoretical support to specify our models. Another advantage of using SEM is that it can test the measurement model and the path model simultaneously.

Model fit was evaluated using the comparative fit index (CFI). CFI has an advantage over other fit indices because it is able to avoid underestimation of fit in a small sample $[7]$ . Also, a number of researchers have also suggested CFI as the preferred model fit index $[21,25]$ . A CFI value of over 0.90 is desirable and it indicates a good fit of model to data $[8,19]$ .

The data were analyzed using the two-step approach suggested by Anderson and Gerbing [4]. In the first step, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to determine whether the measured variables reliably reflected the hypothesized latent variables. All latent variables were allowed to intercorrelate freely without attribution of a causal order. Any problem in the measurement and measurement model should be resolved at this stage. Convergent validity was assessed based on the criteria that the indicator's estimated pattern coefficient is significant on its posited underlying construct factor. Discriminant validity was assessed by constraining the correlation parameters between constructs to 1.0. A significant lower $\chi^2$ value of the unconstrained model suggests the achievement of discriminant validity. Cronbach Alpha was used to test the internal reliability of the final scales. The minimum acceptable level of reliability was set to 0.7 as suggested by Nunnally and Bernstein [27].

In the second step, a series of structural equation path models specified in the research model were tested. Nested-model comparison was used to find out which model best explains the sample covariance. Models are nested when a more restricted model is obtained by imposing constraints on a more general model. In our analysis, the original Triandis model (model 1) is nested within model 2 by setting the path from facilitating conditions to intention to zero. Model 2 is nested within model 3 by setting the following paths: from complexity to near-term consequences, long-term consequences, and affect; from near-term consequences to long-term consequence; and from social factor to affect to zero. Chi-square difference test or the likelihood ratio [11] was used to analyze whether the improvement in the model fits is significant.

## 5. Results

We first assess the validity and reliability of our instrument.

## 5.1. Confirmatory factor analysis

The factor variances were fixed at unity and all constructs were allowed to correlate freely. The confirmatory factor model adequately reflects a good fit to the data, $\chi^{2}(254,N=243)=419,P=0.001$ , CFI = 0.95. The CFI indicates that the seven-factor structure is valid. Table 2 shows the factor loadings of the observed variables on the latent constructs as estimated from the confirmatory factor analysis. All factor loadings are significant at an alpha level of 0.05, and the factor loadings are fairly high. This supports that the measurements show convergent validity. Cronbach's Alphas for all the scales are higher than the acceptable level of 0.7; it supports the convergent validity of our measurements.

Discriminant validity was assessed by constraining the correlation parameters to 1 between the constructs one by one and chi-square difference tests were performed. The results show that the $\chi^{2}s$ for all the constrained models are significantly lower than the corresponding unconstrained models. This supports the discriminant validity of the constructs.

## 6. Discussion and implications

This study applied the Triandis model of choice to investigate factors affecting intention to use the Internet/WWW. With a confirmatory design, we used structural equation modeling to assess the adequacy of the original Triandis model. We also compared the original model with two modified and extended models. The results show that the theoretical constructs in the Triandis model are useful in explaining the intention to use the Internet/WWW. However, the original specification does not fit the data very well. The structural equation modeling analyses show that our last model provides a better fit. The additional paths are all significant except that from complexity to long-term consequences. The modified model not only shows the direct impact of the factors on intention formation, but also the interrelationships among the determinants. The new model, thus, provides a better picture of the dynamic of intention formation. As such, it should provide a better theoretical basis for further study and understanding of IT adoption.

The new model shows that affect, social factors, facilitating conditions, and perceived near-term consequences all have positive impacts on the intention to use the Internet/WWW. The results are quite consistent with the previous studies with one exception — affect. This has been found not to be significant in influencing usage in three out of four studies using the Triandis model. Studies utilizing other theoretical frameworks also found that enjoyment, one kind of affect, not related to PC usage nor course-related WWW usage. Enjoyment was also found to be the least important determinant of Internet usage. However, here we found affect the most important determinant of the intention to use the Internet/WWW. It seems clear that the relative weight of the factors affecting the intention and usage is different. Affect plays an important role in intention formation but not in actual behavior. A number of social psychological theories have also postulated that formation of intention precedes the actual behavior for most voluntary acts, thus, it is important to understand the different dynamics of intention formation and actual behavior. This will provide guidelines for encouraging the adoption of the technology when it is first introduced, as well as its continuing utilization.

Our study also shows that facilitating conditions not only affect actual behavior as postulated by Triandis, they also affect intention. That is, if an individual perceives that there are enough support and resources, the intention to use or continue to use the Internet/WWW will be stronger. This finding is consistent with the Theory of Planned Behavior. Thus, management hoping to promote the use of the Internet/WWW at work should provide not only easy access to the Internet, but they should also provide sufficient technical support. Further, the availability of such support should be well known among the workforce.

Social factors are also a very important determinant of the intention to use the Internet/WWW. It not only affects intention directly but also indirectly through affect. Users will enjoy using the Internet/WWW more if they perceive that using the Internet/WWW is approved by their superiors and peers. Thus, management should create a supportive social environment to encourage the productive use of the Internet/WWW.

Although perceived complexity does not have a significant direct effect, it does have a significant negative indirect effect on intention through its influence on near-term consequences and affect. Therefore, designing an easy to use system is still an important goal for system development projects. An easy to use system will lead to a belief that the system is more useful and more enjoyable.

Near-term consequences are found to positively influence the intention to use the Internet/WWW, but are not as important as they were found in influencing the actual usage in existing studies.

There are a few limitations in our study that should be considered when interpreting the results and implications. Firstly, our sample size is adequate but on the low size for structural equation modeling. To avoid potential bias, CFI was also used to assess the model fits. Future studies may want to use a larger sample. Secondly, our respondents' education level is on the high side. Although it has been said that Internet users tend to be more highly educated, the user base is rapidly expanding. Due to the difficulties in getting a sampling frame for Internet users, the sampling procedure of this study is not random. Moreover, our respondents are all Chinese; it may be fruitful to replicate the study in other countries in order to test the generalizability of our model.

## 7. Conclusions

In this study, the Triandis model of Choice was adopted to study intention to use the Internet/WWW. We found that its constructs are all useful in predicting the intention. However, the original model, which includes only direct paths from the determinants to intention to use, does not fit the data as well as the modified and extended model developed for this study. The confirmatory analysis using the structural equation modeling technique shows that the modified model provides a better fit with the data collected from 255 part-time MBA and diploma students. All but one of the additional paths were supported in the analysis. The model also shows the interrelationships between the determinants, which contributes to a better understanding of the dynamic of intention formation and provides a useful framework for guiding future adoption studies.

We chose to study intention to use the Internet because we believe that an understanding of intention formation provides better insight on promoting technology as well as its voluntary continued usage. We also found that the relative importance of the determinants for intention to use and actual usage is different. Our results show that affect and the social factors are the most important determinants for forming the intention to use the Internet followed by near-term consequences and facilitating conditions. Complexity does not affect intention directly, but it does affect intention indirectly through its effect on perceived near-term consequences and affect. These findings provide further insight to management who would like to promote productive use of the Internet/WWW at work.
