## Abstract

Career (or job) satisfaction is recognized as an indicator of employee turnover. It is also well known that certain internal anchors play a role in predicting the career satisfaction of information system (IS) professionals. Researchers have speculated on the influence of external opportunities on career satisfaction, but none have empirically tested the premise that both internal and external anchors better predict the career satisfaction of IS professionals in the entry-level stage. A sample of IS professionals supports the use of both anchors in improving career satisfaction, and, hopefully, employee turnover. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Career satisfaction; Internal career anchors; External career opportunities

## 1. Introduction

Retention and recruiting of information systems (IS) professionals in the US has become a major crisis for employers. There is a critical shortage of IS professionals, including skilled programmers, system analysts, and technical support staff. For example, according to the Fortune 500 firms experience 25–35% turnover rates in their IS departments and are often forced to delay important projects [17]. The Information Technology Association of America reported 346,000 vacant jobs for programmers, system analysts, and technical support staffs [26].

An IS employee shortage and high turnover can contribute to slower business growth and diminished product and service quality. US businesses have lost an estimated \$59 billion as a result of failed software projects, more than half of which are at least 80% over budget. High IS staff turnover contributes to project failures, delays and overruns.

What do IS managers do about the shortage? They seem to be counting on pay as the main recruitment and retention method. A 1998 survey found that 42% of organizations have reviewed their basic pay policies, 13% have introduced bonuses, and 15% have made other pay structure changes [21]. For example, Fisery, a company in the Atlanta region of the USA, pays about 15% more salary than average market rates to its IS staff and has succeeded in maintaining low turnover in its 132-person department [8]. Lanier Worldwide gives staying bonuses of \$10,000–\$30,000 to induce IS professionals to stay for a minimum 2-year period.

IS managers have been following a strategy of bidding for IS professionals in the external labor market. Attracting and retaining IS staff by pay competition is realistic and has several advantages. Yet there are challenges to using labor market based pay as the only method for attracting and retaining IS professionals. Double-digit annual percentage increases in the IS budget can be the result of focusing solely on achieving or maintaining parity with the external labor market. A continuation of such large pay increases is unlikely, since IS staff expenses already consumed an average of 43% of the IS budget before the 1990s [24]. Several recent studies have criticized the exclusive focus on external labor market parity, and have urged IS managers to also use other human resource management (HRM) tools and viewpoints. One criticism is the focus on the financial rewards as opposed to other factors that influence career decisions, known as career anchors.

Are there any effective alternatives for reducing IS turnover, other than pay-related methods in the IS HRM literature? Unfortunately, the guidance generally available is quite limited. There are many studies in the general HRM literature that find that high job or career satisfaction is related to lower rates of turnover or other forms of job withdrawal. Thus, job or career satisfaction is likely to reduce turnover. It is likely that increasing job satisfaction would lead to lower turnover. Considerable research in general HRM has concentrated on finding correlates or causes of high job satisfaction. If such connections can be discovered then there may be opportunities for raising job satisfaction and reducing turnover by modifying the variables involving job satisfaction.

Better career path management is one popular candidate for increasing career or job satisfaction of professionals. Much opinion and limited prior research on IS HRM has focused on defining career paths for IS professionals; e.g., implementing a dual career ladder to allow both technical and managerial career path options. Yet several studies have found that many IS professionals do not prefer either the technical or managerial career paths as their primary career anchor or success criterion $[12,19,31]$ . Other perceived important career anchors for success include organizational stability, identity, geographic security, and service. Collectively the IS HRM literature suggests that employers should provide a variety of career opportunities to match the variety of individuals' career anchors. Accordingly, creating opportunities besides technical or managerial career fulfillment to attract and retain IS professionals has been encouraged.

## 2. Background and hypotheses

Career management for IS employees requires some understanding of the psychological attraction that the professions have for IS practitioners. Schein [27,28] proposed a model of career anchors, that is a pattern of self-perceived talents, expectations, motives, and values that serve to guide, stabilize, and integrate the person's career. It is a broad concept that becomes part of a person's total self-concept. An employee is unwilling to relinquish these career anchors, even when forced to make a difficult choice. The career anchor or career orientation is salient, because it influences career choices, affects decisions to move from one job to another, shapes what an individual is looking for in life, determines personal views of the future, and affects the selection of work settings. Schein identified five career anchors:

1. Managerial competence — individual purses greater responsibility for accomplishing results through others.

2. Technical competence — individual focuses primarily on the exercise of their technical expertise.

3. Organizational security — individual seeks company loyalty, tenure, and financial security.

4. Geographic security — individual links oneself to a particular area on a long-term basis.

5. Autonomy — individual seeks situations in which they will be free of organizational constraints and control.

DeLong [7] independently confirmed the existence of the original career anchors plus hypothesized anchors of identity, service, and variety.

6. Identity — individual has strong desire for status and prestige from belonging to certain organizations.

7. Service — individual dedicated to helping other people and contributing to causes.

8. Variety — individual desires a number of different tasks and challenges.

In the IS HRM literature, Ginzberg and Baroudi [13] first proposed the use of internal career anchors to examine career desires among IS professionals and found a variety of career orientations, but without any dominant orientations. They also determined that industry and geographic region do not have any important relation to career orientations. Igbaria et al. [19] found that IS personnel have a wide variety of career orientations, with managerial and technical orientations as dominant anchors. Crepeau et al. [5] also found a variety of IS professionals' career orientations, with organizational stability emerging as a dominant anchor. Other research also supports the conclusion that large individual differences in career anchors are typical of IS professionals [20].

However, Van Maanen and Schein [30] argued that it is necessary to consider two components, internal career anchors and organizational (external to the person) career anchors, to understand career satisfaction. The perceived organizational career situations concern the objective categories that describe job titles, promotions, working environments, and status. If an employee is to remain satisfied, there must be personal motivation (internal career anchors) as well as a favorably perceived external (organizational) career situation.

Attempts to measure external career opportunities have been divided into two broad categories: objective and perceptual measurement techniques. But the most immediate cause of job satisfaction is probably the perceived characteristics of the job instead of objective job attributes $[15]$ . There have been several efforts to identify dimensions of external (organizational)

career situations [9]. Although independently developed by different authors, the aspects of external job opportunities are comparable to internal career anchors.

External career situations had not been included in any prior IS career management study found by the authors. The literature has examined the relationship between internal career orientations and career satisfaction. For example, studies found significant positive relationships between career satisfaction and internal career anchors on technical competence, variety, autonomy, and geographic security $[1]$ . The conclusion, with no empirical evidence on external opportunities, is that IS employees have differing career needs and that organizations should attempt to provide incentives that are consistent with underlying career values.

The purpose of our study is to examine the importance of the ignored dimension, external opportunities, to career satisfaction. Following the suggestion of Van Maanan and Schein that the external opportunities proved by the organization will be positively related to career satisfaction, we also propose that the external opportunities and internal anchors will in combination be better predictors of satisfaction than either alone. This leads to three hypotheses that can be tested:

H1: Internal career anchors will be positively related to career satisfaction.

H2: The external career situations will be positively related to career satisfaction.

H3: Internal and external career anchors will have more predictive power than either variable alone.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. Subjects

Questionnaires were developed and distributed to three large software development organizations located in a major metropolitan area of the southern US. An IS manager from each of the three participating organizations asked 40 entry-level IS personnel in their own division to complete the survey. Entry-level subjects were selected to maintain a standard career level in the sample. A total of 101 questionnaires out of the possible 120 were returned and used in the data analysis. Respondents included programmers, system analysts, and technical support professionals. These were chosen because shortages and turnover are severe in these job categories. All respondents were assured that their individual responses would be kept confidential. Demographic features of the sample are shown in Table 1.

External validity refers to the extent to which the findings can be generalized across times, persons, and settings [4]. Because of the limited number of organizations, subjects in non-participating organizations could have differed systematically from the participating organizations on some important characteristics, such as career desires, which could be a critical determinant of one's career satisfaction [3]. We, therefore, compared our subjects' career satisfaction and anchor means with those found in another study having a large number of organizations represented [18]. No difference fell outside of a $95\%$ confidence interval. Non-participating bias appeared unlikely.

The external validity of the findings is also threatened if there is a systematic bias with respect to career satisfaction, for example, if responses were mainly obtained from relatively satisfied or unsatisfied employees. The mean of this dimension was 3.55, the median was 3.47, the skewness was -0.97, and the kurtosis was 0.83. The responses had good distribution on career satisfaction since the mean and median were similar, skewness was less than 2, and kurtosis was less than 5 [11].

Similar demographic content to other studies also helps to prove that there is external validity and that it is possible to compare results across studies. One IS personnel study was conducted in the same geographic region as ours, using subjects with advanced IS-majors $[25]$ . We compared our samples' demographics with this study, since we expected entry-level qualities to resemble student characteristics. The numbers proved to be quite similar, falling within a 95% confidence interval over gender ratio and age. Another study involving entry-level subjects also had similar numbers $[29]$ . Studies of more advanced career stages had more advanced age and larger percentages of males $[2,6]$ . In sum, although the number of participating organizations was small, the series of tests did not identify any significant threats to external validity.

## 3.2. Measurements

## 3.2.1. Internal career anchors

The scale used was adopted from $[5]$ ; it included 36 items measuring the degree of individual preference for managerial competence, technical competence, organizational security, autonomy, geographic security, identity, service, and variety. This scale is a slight modification of the original concepts and tools promoted by Schein. The response options on a 5-point Likert scale ranged from 1, “not important” to 5, “very important”. Scale reliability was examined by computing Cronbach’s $\alpha$ for the items intended to measure each construct, by principal components analysis of all the 36 items together and by intercorrelations of the scales.

The number of eigenvalues of the unrotated components greater than chance expectation helped to determine the number of latent dimensions or scales to be kept for further analysis $[23]$ . Nine eigenvalues were greater than chance expectation. Most items loaded together on the expected factors after varimax rotation. The ninth component was composed of two items from the managerial anchor and one from the technical anchor.

Separate principal components analyzes were performed for the items intended to measure each of the eight anchor scales. There was only one eigenvalue greater than one within each of the eight intended anchor scales. This is a high degree of conformance to expected dimensionality at both the instrument and scale levels. The coefficient $\alpha$ of these eight anchors range from 0.77 to 0.89 (see Table 2). Discriminant validity is highlighted in Table 3; this shows that the covariances between two anchors are always lower than the average variance extracted by the items measuring each construct [10].

It is also possible to test for and measure the overall discriminant validity of a set of scales. Consider people and scales as the two independent variables. Then items can be considered replicates within cells defined by people and scales. That is, each item within a scale is intended to measure just that scale, but people may give somewhat different answers to each item within a scale. If there is a main effect due to people, then the scales share some common variance. If there is a main effect due to scales, then the scale means differ. Most importantly, if there is an interaction effect, then people respond differently to the different scales. The presence of a person by scale interaction effect means that there is demonstrable discriminant validity. $\eta^{2}$ can be used as a first approximation to the size of each effect. An unweighted means analysis of variance was conducted to test the null hypothesis of no discriminant validity. The people, scales, and interaction effects were significant beyond the 0.05 level. The people, scales and interaction effects, respectively, accounted for 13, 14 and $34\%$ of the total sum of squares. The remaining $39\%$ of the total sum of squares were due to variation within people's responses to the raw score items within each scale.

## 3.2.2. External career job design

The external career concerns the objective categories which includes job titles, the changes in working conditions, promotions, salary, or status. In order to measure this concept, Ferratt et al. adapted Hackman and Oldham's [16] job diagnostic survey (JDS) scale and constructed an external job opportunities scale. We used their instrument to construct the eight items to match the internal anchor scales, including variety, autonomy, identity, service, geographic security, organizational stability, managerial competence, and technical competence. An example of an item is

How much “Identify” does your job provide? That is, to what extent does your job allow you to be identified with a powerful or prestigious employer or organization?

The response options ranged from 1, “not at all” to 5, “extremely”. The principal component analysis conforms to expectations from the original source. The eight items loaded into one factor with a Cronbach’s coefficient $\alpha$ , 0.84.

In reverse, the next question is whether the external anchors, by themselves, can model job satisfaction as well as the combination of internal and external anchors. Again, an F-test was tested to examine if the addition of the set of internal anchors to the set of external anchors improves the model of career satisfaction. The results $(F\text{-value}=2.28$ and p-value=0.03) suggest that the combination of external anchors with internal anchors does better than just the external anchors alone. We, therefore, have shown that the internal and external anchors together account for more of the variation in career satisfaction than either set does alone. We thus support H3.

Additionally, we conducted a total of eight independent regressions (each with the corresponding pair of internal anchor and external situation as the independent variables). The results are shown in Table 5. The values indicated that all the models (with a pair of external situation and internal anchor), except geographic security, were significant at a p = 0.05 level. This reconfirms that career satisfaction is a function of both external career situations and internal career anchors. In addition, seven out of eight external anchors (except geographic security) were significant at p = 0.05 level; however, only one (job variety) out of eight internal anchors was significant. More importantly, only one internal coefficient was negative (autonomy) and all other coefficients were positive. A sign test indicates that there is no significant number of “negative” coefficients in either of the independent variable sets. The result argues that one’s career satisfaction has a “positive” relationship with both internal career anchors and external career situations. In addition, the external career situations may play a more significant role than the internal anchors in career satisfaction.

## 5. Discussion

This study was intended to provide empirical evidence to support the argument that external career opportunities are as influential in career satisfaction as the well researched internal career anchors. For the 101 IS programmers, system analysts, and technical support staffs, the results supported this argument. They indicate that career satisfaction is positively related to both individuals' internal career orientation and the external career situations provided by their organizations. In other words, the stronger one's internal career desires and the better the external career situations provided by the organization, the higher one's career satisfaction.

This result is hardly surprising: it is consistent with the IS literature. One's internal career desires “motivate” individuals and literature has demonstrated that the level of “motivation” has a “positive” relationship with measures of “success” and “satisfaction”. In other words, we believe that the extent of one's “internal career anchors” should be seen as IS professionals' career “motivations”. Also of importance are the external career situations on IS professionals' career satisfaction. This important factor had been overlooked in the IS literature.

The results of this study have several implications to IS management. First, IS employees have a diversity of career needs. Providing a better external career situation for satisfying their multidimensional career needs could be the most critical thing to retain them. The importance of the “identity” and “technical competence” external situations could be further supported by the observation that Microsoft and American Management Systems hire only about 2% of those who apply, while many IS departments have experienced dramatic difficulty recruiting their IS employees. Many IS people generally consider working for prestigious companies because of their status (identity) and leading technology (technical competence). For an IS manager in a big company, this may be good news, however, retaining them could be a challenge and must be incorporated into strategic HRM planning. Other forms of rewards should be considered to keep critical IS people. On the other hand, for an IS manager in a small company, different strategies must be pursued. For example, such an organization may emphasize family friendly corporate cultures and concern for the IS individual, in contrast to the workaholic ethic at some big companies. Furthermore, the job variety and external autonomy career opportunities could be considered in job design and emphasized in recruiting campaigns.
