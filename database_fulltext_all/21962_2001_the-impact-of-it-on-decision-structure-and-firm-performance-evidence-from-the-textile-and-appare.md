# The impact of IT on decision structure and firm performance: evidence from the textile and apparel industry

Torben J. Andersen $^{a,*}$ , Albert H. Segars $^{b}$

$^{a}$ School of Management, Enterprise Hall, MSN 5F5, George Mason University, Fairfax, VA 22030, USA $^{b}$ Kenan-Flagler Business School, University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, NC 27599-3490, USA

Received 31 January 2000; received in revised form 2 November 2000; accepted 16 January 2001

## Abstract

Clarifying the relationships between information technology (IT), organizational performance, and decision structure remains an important area of inquiry in IS research. Through an empirical analysis and complementary case examples, our study examines these associations among firms operating in the US-based apparel and textile industry from 1992 to 1997. Based on data gathered from 50 public firms located across the USA, the study finds that IT used to enhance internal communication supports a decentralized decision structure, which in turn is associated with higher financial performance. Hence, IT exhibits an indirect performance effect. However, use of IT to enhance communication is also found to have a direct performance effect in large organizations. This paper proposes that use of communication enhancing IT can support organizational learning processes by facilitating flexible exchange of skills and knowledge across functional areas. Case examples are used to illustrate how these learning effects can materialize. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Communication enhancing IT; Decentralized decision structure; Authority; Participation; Financial performance; Organizational learning

## 1. Research issues

Investment in new information technology (IT) remains a central strategic issue as firms try to gain competitive advantage in the increasingly dynamic environment, even though the impact on performance is not fully understood. Some empirical analyses indicate that marginal return from computer investment is higher than return from investment in other industrial equipment $[1]$ . However, prior research has been less equivocal in the assessment of the performance effect of IT investment [2], while other studies have provided mixed evidence. Weill [3] found a positive performance effect from transaction processing technology, but no effect from informational IT supporting communication and administrative tasks. In contrast, Dos Santos et al. [4] reported that innovative IT investments, including investment in informational IS, led to higher market value.

While these studies found some evidence of positive performance impacts from IT, the underlying causes are not clear, which underscores a need to improve our understanding of the potential performance effects. It has been argued that IT supports organic organizational forms $[5]$ , and improves the performance effect of decentralized decision structures [6]. However, the literature generally contends that IT can support both centralized and decentralized decision structures $[7–10]$ , which leaves the relationships between IT, decision structure, and performance largely undetermined. This article reports on a project developed to improve the empirical and practical insights into these fundamental relationships.

## 2. Theory and model

## 2.1. Communication enhancement

IT is often viewed as a means to expand an organization's data processing capacity. It can increase the volume, speed, and accuracy of data exchange and extend access to information [11–14]. Cost effective handling of information intensive transactions, such as order entry, inventory control and invoicing improves productivity [15]. Effects associated with this type of operational efficiency underpin most investigations of the direct performance effect. For example, Weill found a positive performance effect from IT investment to automate the organizations' transaction processes, and Brynjolfsson and Hitt [16] found higher marginal returns from similar technology investment. However, it is not obvious that such productivity gains lead to sustainable excess return in individual firms. Transactional data are usually transmitted in standardized form, so transaction processing technologies can often be replicated. In contrast, use of IT to enhance an organization's ability to exchange information across functional, geographical, and time barriers can enforce existing idiosyncratic processes that are difficult to imitate, and thus lead to sustainable performance effects [17,18].

The transaction efficiency perspective implies a direct relationship between IT investment and organizational performance. However, a number of researchers suggest that social conditions influence the application of IT and obscure the performance relationship. Such influences have, for example, been considered within organizational contexts [19], work group characteristics [20], routinization of organizational procedures [21], and managerial support [22]. Thus, IT investment is a necessary but not sufficient condition for an organization's use of IT. In essence, what should matter to performance is the extent to which organizations use it in support of core capabilities, such as communication enhancement. In this vein, our study uses communication enhancement, i.e. IT facilitated exchange of information and data across different parts of the firm, to classify organizational use of IT.

Use of IT to enhance communication may exert a positive influence on performance. For example, Cohen and Levinthal $[23]$ suggested that communication capabilities are inductive to innovation. Furthermore, Huber argued that communication technologies support a decentralized decision structure by making information available to dispersed decision makers: proximity to prevailing market conditions and instant access to information lead to quicker and better decisions. These effects are particularly interesting to investigate as switching technologies improve the ability to integrate organizational information flows $[24]$ .

## 2.2. Decision structure

Organization theory argues that predictable environments impose few discrepancies on organizational processes and thus favor a hierarchical decision structure and standardization $[25]$ . Conversely, in unpredictable environments organizations are faced with non-routine situations, that are better coordinated through mutual adjustment processes, where individuals doing the actual work can act without delays from hierarchical approvals $[26]$ . Hence, in dynamic industries firms use informal communication to coordinate activities, whereas firms operating in stable environments take advantage of standardized procedures $[27,28]$ . As industrial environments are exposed to dynamic competition $[29]$ , a decentralized decision structure is likely to become more important.

Galbraith [30] argued that task uncertainty increases the amount of information decision makers must process, and noted that information systems can accommodate hierarchical decision processes. However, he [31] also suggested that decentralized decision making is more effective in dealing with task uncertainty, and that IT can facilitate the informal lateral communication required in a decentralized decision structure. Accordingly, researchers have noted a trend toward technology supported decentralization [32–35].

The IS literature reflects a similar duality in the relationship between IT and organizational structure. King [36] argued that IS should support prevailing organization structures. Zuboff showed how IT can support informed autonomy and encourage an organization's business development. However, she also illustrated how IT can be applied to centralize power. Gurbaxani and Whang adopted a comparable set of dichotomous arguments. They analyzed IT's influence on the allocation of decision making authority based on transaction cost economics and agency theory. Their framework was based on the relative effects of processing efficiencies in hierarchical decision-making and agency costs associated with dispersed decision power. IT can reduce the information processing costs of a centralized decision structure, but can also reduce the agency costs of decentralized decision-making. Hence, the eventual decision structure should be determined by the relative size of these two effects.

Other aspects affect the relationship between IT and decision structure, e.g. IT reduces information processing costs, and can make lateral coordination of decentralized decision processes more efficient. In contrast, IT enhanced information processing can lead to information overload, as centralized decision nodes are unable to handle the volume of decisions referred upwards in a hierarchical structure. Both aspects suggest that a decentralized decision structure is more effective in changing environmental settings.

The decision structures analyzed here relate to operational and task oriented decisions, although the analysis could be extended to strategic decision making. It is generally recognized that strategy arises from centralized planning as well as decentralized emergent decisions $[37–40]$ . So, strategic decision making processes transpose a comparable dichotomy between centralized and decentralized structures.

Determining whether IT induces centralized decision-making or decentralized decision structures that lead to superior organizational performance is eventually an empirical question. Fig. 1 presents the research model adopted to test these relationships.

## 2.3. Research model

Our model suggests that IT influences the degree of decentralization. Decentralization reflects dispersion of decision power to lower hierarchical levels and is captured by two indicators: authority and participation. ‘Authority’ is the extent to which decision-making is authorized at lower hierarchical levels. ‘Participation’ is the extent to which individuals at lower hierarchical levels influence decisions. IT enhances the organization’s communication capabilities and supports decentralization.

A decentralized decision structure allows individuals at the operational levels to decide quickly based on situational insights and general organizational information, which leads to faster and better decisions. The ability to take decentralized actions combined with an electronic communication network allows flexible exchange of knowledge across functional areas that can support organizational innovation and performance $[41–43]$ . IT may also have a direct performance effect as improved communication capabilities reduce the organization’s information processing costs, and make organizational procedures more cost effective. In the next section we develop specific hypotheses based on the proposed research model.

## 3. Hypotheses

Information technology used to enhance exchange of information and data provides individuals with better access to knowledge residing in different parts of the organization. For example, sales people can access financial information at headquarters to assess the profitability of new orders and determine whether they fit the return criteria. They can also contact manufacturing to discuss the technical feasibility of specific product adaptations. Sales representatives can quickly relay competitive market intelligence across the organization, which can improve responsiveness by coordinating the sales, product development and manufacturing activities. Similarly, customer related problems may be handled quickly by communicating on-line with experienced colleagues in various functional areas. In other words, IT enhanced communication capabilities improve informal lateral coordination and enable individuals to take fast and effective responsive actions. This reasoning suggests the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1. The higher the use of IT to enhance communication across an organization's functional areas, the more decentralized the decision structure.

In a decentralized decision structure individuals close to the actual market interface can identify problems and opportunities faster. Computerized information systems can accommodate more accurate intelligence and avoid information distortion associated with long hierarchical transmission chains. Higher information accuracy and faster reaction to emerging events should lead to better and more profitable business decisions. A decentralized decision structure improves an organization's ability to coordinate actions and perform lateral functional tasks simultaneously rather than sequentially [44]. This makes organizational activities more effective and improves responsiveness.

Communication enhancement and improved access to information combined with the authority to take actions can increase the innovative capacity $[45]$ . For example, individuals can easily obtain market intelligence, information about process improvements, etc. and exploit this information in their own initiatives. Individuals can share knowledge in reciprocal information exchanges, whereas such opportunities might be overlooked in hierarchical organizations. Opportunities can be better exploited in a decentralized organization, because dispersed decision makers possess the detailed knowledge required to make effective responses. This leads to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2. The more decentralized the organization's decision structure, the higher the financial performance.

Use of IT to enhance communication capabilities lowers information processing costs [46]. These efficiencies will accrue within a given organizational structure, because IT reduces the information processing cost associated with both hierarchical and decentralized decision processes. This leads to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3. The higher the use of IT to enhance communication across the organization's functional areas, the higher the direct effect on financial performance.

Based on the research model and associated hypotheses, we outline the methodology applied to assess the proposed relationships.

## 4. Methodology

We performed an empirical test on a sample of firms in the textile and apparel industry (SIC: 2200–2399). Instability measures projected an industrial environment with an intermediate level of uncertainty. The textile and apparel industry has been instrumental in the application of IT to improve responsiveness along the industry value chain by introducing technologies like bar coding, universal product codes, electronic data interchange, electronic packaging and delivery systems [47]. These techniques now permeate the textile and apparel industry, as well as other industries, and have become virtual industry standards. The quick response technologies helped standardize transactional practices and increase operational flexibility in the industry. As practices become standardized, however, they are no longer a strong source of excess returns to individual firms. Instead, use of IT to enhance an organization's idiosyncratic communication capabilities may possess such qualities.

## 4.1. Data collection

Public firms (137) in the textile and apparel industry were extracted from Compustat. The industry also comprises a number of private firms. However, there is no reason to expect that effects of IT vary with the type of firm ownership. Foreign owned firms and organizations with less than 100 employees were excluded to secure data consistency and ensure that communication effects were associated with IT enhancement rather than oral interaction. All the chief information officers (CIOs) in the remaining 121 firms were contacted by phone. They were chosen as prime contacts, because CIOs expectedly have comprehensive knowledge about IT applications in their organizations. Twenty firms had been exposed to major structural changes, mergers, or acquisitions, and displayed unstable organizations. In three cases, the CIO had resigned and not yet been replaced. Finally, seven firms declined to participate for policy reasons. These 30 firms were excluded from the sample to ensure responses from executives that were willing to share their insights. The project was designed as a longitudinal investigation of the relationship between IT and decision structure, and financial performance in subsequent time periods. A questionnaire was mailed to the remaining 91 CIOs during 1995 to assess IT use and decision structure in their firms. Financial performance was derived from formal accounting sources. Ideally, the study would have access to accounting data in 1995–1997, and beyond. However, several firms were privatized and restructured along the way, which significantly reduced access to reliable financial performance data beyond 1997.

## 4.2. Measures

The study measured IT, decentralization and financial performance. The first two constructs were based on self-assessed scaled items derived from questionnaires. The self-assessments were made by CIOs, i.e. members of senior management, as is common practice in organization studies. Financial performance was based on archival data.

IT denotes use of IT to enhance communication, and is reflected in the extent to which information systems are used to exchange information and data between different parts of the firm. A number of items measure IT including use of electronic mail service, computerized data transmission, own telecommunication networks, fiber distributed data interfaces, and client servers. The items were identified in the IS literature and validated by two experienced IT specialists. The list is not exhaustive, but refers to technologies generally applied by modern firms. The items are shown in the Appendix A.

Decentralization was assessed by adapting Aiken and Hage's measures of hierarchy of authority and actual participation [48]. The measures have been highly reliable in other organizational studies [49]. The items in the authority measure indicate the extent to which individuals have authority to make operational decisions. The items measuring participation refer to individuals' participation in decisions on resource commitments, internal rewards, and process developments. Each item was assessed on a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5, and aggregated into the construct measure. The items are shown in the Appendix A.

Financial performance was measured by net profit margin, operating margin, return on assets, and adjusted cash flow margin aggregated into a single indicator. The financial ratios were obtained from Compustat, Disclosure, and annual reports. The measures were calculated as 3-year averages. A pre-period performance measure (1992–1994) preceded the mailing of the questionnaire. A post-period performance measure (1994–1996) extended beyond the mailing. A third performance period (1996–1998) was not incorporated, because corporate restructurings reduced the number of reliable data points. Macroeconomic indicators were analyzed to assess effects associated with economic trends over the consecutive periods. Regressing the annual GDP in the industry during 1992–1996 resulted in an insignificant and slightly negative regression coefficient. Hence, there is no evidence of macroeconomic distortions in the performance measures.

The questionnaire aimed to avoid ambiguities and ensure reliability of responses in accordance with the total design method $[50]$ . The questionnaire was pre-tested by six IT officers in two firms within the sample industry. Effective reliability of respondents was 0.81 and 0.93 at the two test sites, which is considered satisfactory $[51]$ .

The measures were exposed to factor analysis, which supported four independent factors corresponding to financial performance, IT, authority, and participation (Table 1). The four factors explained $60.9\%$ of the total variance. Computer conferencing did not load strongly on IT, and was excluded from the measure. Participation in policy decisions loaded both on authority, participation and IT, but the item was ascribed to participation. However, analysis excluding the item from the measure did not alter the results. Cronbach's alpha on IT was calculated as 0.70, and 0.76 on authority and participation, which is considered satisfactory [52].

## 5. Analyses and results

Questionnaires were mailed and collected in the second quarter of 1995. A 50 useable questionnaires were returned, corresponding to a response rate of 54.9%. Possible non-response biases were investigated by applying analysis of variance to test for differences in mean values of total assets and financial ratios between respondents and non-respondents. The analyses revealed no discrepancies. To check further, the measures of IT and decision structure were evaluated through conversations with managers (other than the CIOs) in eight firms (16% of the sample). The evaluations were consistent with the quantitative measures. A self-assessed indicator of IT effectiveness included on the questionnaire had a significant positive correlation to the archival performance measures. The data derived from the questionnaire and the archival sources are visualized in a three-dimensional plot (Fig. 2).

## 5.1. Hypotheses testing

Hypothesis 1 was tested in regression analyses using authority and participation as the dependent variables and IT the independent variable. Hypothesis 2 was tested in regression analyses using the financial performance measures as the dependent variable and authority and participation the independent variables. Hypothesis 3 was tested in regression analyses with the financial performance measures as dependent variable and IT the independent variable.

The regressions were controlled for firm size. The natural logarithm of total assets was used as control variable, because the distribution of total assets is positively skewed. Similarly, the regression analyses controlled for interaction effects between size and IT, and between size, authority and participation.

## 6. Discussion

The correlation and regression analyses supported the proposed model. There was a positive and statistically significant relationship between IT and a decentralized decision structure. Authority seemed to be positively affected by the use of IT (Tables 3 and 4). However, as IT and decentralization were measured simultaneously, there was no clear evidence of causality. The analyses did not indicate whether IT led to a decentralized decision structure through an enabling capacity, or if decentralized organizations were more in favor of new IT. On the other hand, it is highly probable that structure and technology applications are mutually reinforcing, which may be reflected in the high correlation between use of communication enhancing IT and decentralized decision structure (Table 2).

The potential for reverse causality was assessed by regressing financial performance during 1989–1991 on IT. A positive relationship between earlier performance and IT would indicate that firms with strong earnings paths use financial reserves for IT investment. However, the analysis showed no significant effect. This is consistent with Weill's study, which found no relationship between prior performance and IT investment. Furthermore, applications of IT should not depend on performance if the development of IT enhanced communication capabilities follows a commonly acknowledged path dependent process. The study did not explicitly measure how long the organizations have used IT in combination with a decentralized decision structure. However, the imposition of a particular organizational structure and a related communication enhancing technology infrastructure does not happen overnight, and therefore is likely to reflect organizational processes that have been in place for some time. The study investigated the possible influence of organizational change by incorporating a categorical control variable indicating whether or not the firm had been exposed to major changes in organization and work processes. The variable did not affect the results.

Hence, the regression results seem to indicate that performance is caused by a decentralized decision structure (Tables 5 and 6), which means that IT has an indirect performance effect through its support of a decentralized decision structure (Tables 3 and 4). The inclusion of IT, authority, and various interaction terms in the same regression analyses confirmed that both decentralized decision structure and IT have performance effects (Tables 7 and 8), although the effect of IT only applies to large organizations. The direct performance effect of IT may derive from an ability to reduce geographic and time barriers particularly in large dispersed organizations [53].

A potentially useful rationale can help explain the indirect performance effects of IT as facilitation of organizational learning processes. Decentralization allows initiative and responsive behavior among individuals, while electronic communication systems can link expertise between the decentralized decision nodes. By enhancing the firm's communication capabilities, IT can facilitate learning and innovation among autonomous individuals located across the organization. Excess returns can arguably be ascribed to an organization's idiosyncratic learning processes as these are amplified by IT and a decentralized decision structure.

Organizational learning was not measured in this study, but learning processes were observed in a number of representative firms from the sample. To better understand the mechanics of the performance relationships, managers in five organizations, three high performers and two low performers, were interviewed. The interviews were taped, summarized within 48 h and conferred to ensure accuracy. The interviews revealed distinct differences between the high and low performing firms, and sketched characteristics of learning environments in the high performing firms that were non-existent among the low performing organizations.

\- Three examples of high performing firms

Example 1. This firm manufactures and sells specialized women's clothing and has sales representatives scattered across the country.

1. Information technology enables decentralization. Employees communicate extensively through electronic mail via a server located at headquarters. All types of information are shared electronically, e.g. customer behaviors, competitive moves, financial results, etc. The electronic communication system is actively used to exchange data files, etc. so for example, sales representatives can make customer presentations, calculate profitability of product enhancements, and place orders directly at the manufacturing department. The system allows the sales representatives to learn about customer needs and new market developments much faster and effectively through the exchange of experiences with their peers across the country. Hence, the system gives the sales representatives the ability to decide on new orders and product enhancements directly with their customers in the field and coordinate the execution with manufacturing, suppliers, etc.

## 2. Financial performance effect of decentralization.

When a sales representative learns about a customer's interest in a particular product enhancement, the information is passed on to the other sales people across the country, which allows the sales organization to be more proactive towards their customers, e.g. by proposing the new enhancement to other clients. The ability to make decisions in the field allows the sales representatives to respond effectively to their customers' immediate needs. Also, if employees are faced with particular problems, they can be solved through peer consultation communicated via the system.

## 3. Direct performance effect of IT.

The company has invested heavily in new note books to support internal communication in the organization. Whereas, this is thought to have a reward by enabling the employees, it is also expected to reduce the amount of paperwork in the firm.

## 4. Organizational environment.

The use of the communication system is supported right from top management and down to the supervising middle managers in the firm. There is a strong commitment to train people in the use of the electronic communication system. There is a very informal and entrepreneurial spirit in the firm with few formal restrictions imposed on functional entities. Management encourages new useful ideas from employees, which is also reflected in the firm's bonus program.

Example 2. This firm is engaged in the design, manufacturing, and sales distribution of branded women's apparel.

1. Information technology enables decentralization. Internal communication and data access is considered important so management actively supports investment in electronic communication. The communication system gives access to data files on EDI and inventory replenishment transactions that allow individual product managers to adapt product designs and distribution systems to on-going changes in the demand pattern. There is complete openness in the organization to ask around and acquire relevant information, which helps individual managers implement new activities.

2. Financial performance effect of decentralization. The ability of product managers to quickly adapt product designs and distribute products to fit current market demand enhances product sales and reduces idle inventory. Flexible access to specialized skills in other parts of the organization allows managers to effectively introduce new product adaptations.

## 3. Direct performance effect of IT.

The extensive use of EDI and automated inventory replenishment systems enhances the firm's transactional effectiveness.

## 4. Organizational environment.

Top management is highly supportive of creative business developments. The CEO clearly supports technology driven cooperation among employees and with key customers. The firm operates an employee involvement program to encourage new ideas and suggestions. This system is not considered ideal, but reflects senior management's encouragement and willingness to support doing things in new and better ways.

Example 3. This firm operates within a corporate structure of related entities each promoting their independent, but comparable apparel products.

1. Information technology enables decentralization. Corporate investment in IT supports electronic communication systems that improve internal management information and facilitate technology transfer between different parts of the corporation. The communication systems provide managers with on-line accounting information that helps them decide on product and process enhancements.

Operational information is widely accessible to employees in all parts of the corporation, and there are clear synergies associated with increased information access. The corporate wide communication capabilities allow process knowledge to be transferred easily between functional areas in different corporate entities. For example, when one corporate entity introduces a new process improvement, managers in other group entities can download the information directly from the corporate data network, and decide to implement the procedures they find are relevant.

2. Financial performance effect of decentralization. Managers can decide themselves, when it is opportune to apply new product or process enhancements developed in other corporate entities. There are clear incentives to share information across the corporation, because exchanges of effective practices improve the efficiency of all the corporate entities involved.

## 3. Direct performance effect of IT.

Both executives and people in the organization have access to group wide information, and top management encourages creative ways of exchanging knowledge to improve products and processes across the entire corporation. Management does not consider the corporation a technological trend setter, but encourages creative improvements and exchange new improved practices among corporate entities.

## - Two examples of low performing firms

Example 4. This firm is engaged in the design, manufacturing, and distribution of women's basic and fashion apparel. The firm is a wholesale operation. It maintains showrooms at headquarters, and sells its apparel products to retail outlets through a sales force scattered across the country.

The firm uses a mainframe computer to handle transactional processing, which also provides the firm's sales representatives access to centralized information on current inventory levels, ‘cost sheets’ to assess production costs of particular orders, etc. However, only a few sales people use a lab top computer. A lot of people in the organization are not computer literate, which creates resistance to the introduction of electronic communication systems. They plan to link the PC's in a local area network to the main frame computer through a client server. The primary user will be financial control, and to some extent the sales area. However, management will limit the access to “confidential financial information”, so the sales people are not expected to use the system very much. Very little information is exchanged electronically in the firm, and virtually all internal communication is done on the phone or by fax.

The business planning process is very hierarchical. Once a year top management sets goals, which is reflected in sales targets for the product lines. An annual meeting between top management and the product division managers determines how these sales goals should be met in terms of new products and designs. This information is passed on to the design department, which then develops the new designs for the production plants. The executives meet regularly to discuss business performance. Few decisions are made without the involvement of at least one of the executives, and the decision process is slow. There is little freedom and no incentives to generate new ideas.

Example 5. This firm is specialized in the design, production and distribution of textile prints. The firm has a centrally located sales department, and separate manufacturing, warehousing, and distribution facilities. Product sales are geared towards apparel firms in women's garments and retailers distributing fabric to individual customers. The firm is increasingly engaged in direct sales of their own fabrics and designs. The ‘stylists’ at the sales office design the new patterns, and products become standards when manufacturing has completed a trial run. New orders are registered by the sales office and faxed to the manufacturing plant.

The firm operates a main frame computer system with interacting work stations that can download centralized information. However, there are no LAN extensions that allow information and data exchange between decentralized work stations, because management has not yet identified a need to do this. The computer system primarily supports accounting, inventory management, and process control functions.

Employees in manufacturing are welcome to come up with new ideas that can save the firm money. However, it is considered management's job to develop good business ideas, while the employees are hired to handle the machinery.

The high performing firms exhibited environments, where management encouraged individuals in the organization to be creative and take initiatives. This characteristic was reflected in a decentralized decision structure. Management had installed information systems that gave individuals access to organizational data and provided communication links across the organization. As expressed by one CIO: “people do not just talk to headquarters, but talk to each other and pass on tips to the other guys”. Business developments were often initiated by individual actions and coordinated between functional areas over the information systems. These observations indicated that organizational learning was supported by management’s imposition of electronic communication in the high performing firms. This corresponds to Zuboff’s characterization of the informed organization, and DeSanctis and Poole’s notion that management intent influences the effectiveness of information systems.

In contrast, management in the low performing firms were fairly autocratic and displayed little interest in changing well served practices or applying new ideas from individuals. As a manager said: “all decisions pass through the executive team, so why bother with new ideas”. The low performing firms provided limited access to centralized information and allowed little interaction between decentralized data networks.

Managerial encouragement, training and compensation systems may influence the performance effect of IT supported decentralized decision structures. Interviews with the managers in high and low performing firms revealed that use of IT, decision structure and organizational environment are intertwined in the learning organization. The high performing organizations all had a very supportive management team encouraging individual initiative and extensive use of information systems. There was high emphasis on IT training right from top executives down to supervising middle managers in the field, and effective use of the systems was reflected in performance related compensation.

The results suggest that performance effects from IT are indirect. IT enhances communication capabilities and thereby support the effectiveness of a decentralized decision structure. There is also evidence that IT has a direct performance effect in large organizations. However, the results are derived from an analysis of firms in the US textile and apparel industry, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, more empirical research in other industrial environments is needed to retest the proposed relationships and assess their external validity. There is also a need for continued refinement and validation of the IT measure, particularly as new communication technologies are developed and implemented.

There is no consideration of such confounding variables as incentive schemes, career development, and training. As mentioned, these phenomena are likely to be engrained in organizational environments characterized by high levels of communication enhancing IT, decentralization and learning. Hence, such variables could constitute relevant refinements in further studies aimed to provide more detailed insights about the value creation dynamics of IT within decentralized decision structures.

## 7. Conclusion

We investigated the direct and indirect performance effects of IT in the textile and apparel industry. The results indicated that performance effects of IT can accrue from an ability to enhance communication capabilities that make a decentralized decision structure more effective. The indirect performance effect of IT emerges from inducement of high performing decentralization that enables individuals to take effective action. A direct performance effect is also found in large organizations. Apparently, an organizational learning perspective may explain the performance effects of IT in a decentralized organizational setting.

The study provides interesting empirical findings in support of IT's indirect performance effects. These seem to work by enabling decentralized organizational structures, where the communication enhancing capacity of IT is a driving element of the value creation process.
