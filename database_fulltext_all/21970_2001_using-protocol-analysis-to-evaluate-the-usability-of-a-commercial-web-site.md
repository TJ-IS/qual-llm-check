# Using protocol analysis to evaluate the usability of a commercial web site

Raquel Benbunan-Fich $^{*}$

Computing and Decision Sciences Department, Stillman School of Business, Seton Hall University, 400 South Orange Ave, South Orange, NJ 07079, USA

## Abstract

Despite the increasing popularity of electronic commerce, there appears to be little evidence of the methodical evaluation of the usability of commercial web sites. The usability of a web site defines how well and how easily a visitor, without formal training, can interact with the site. This paper reports the results of a research project, which applies a systematic qualitative technique known as protocol analysis or think aloud method, to examine the usability of a commercial web site. About 15 usability principles and 3 evaluation parameters (content, navigation and interactivity) were used as a framework to analyze the verbal protocols of a sample of users interacting with a greeting card web site. The protocols provided evidence of usability problems caused by crowded content, poor navigation and cumbersome interactivity. These results underscore the importance of two crucial usability goals for commercial web sites: clear path to products and transparency of the ordering process. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Usability; Protocol analysis; Commercial web sites; Electronic commerce

## 1. Introduction

Due to the popularity of the World Wide Web, many organizations are developing commercial web sites and adding web interfaces for their existing information systems. But very little research is concerned with the systematic evaluation of the usability of these web-based systems. Usability refers to how well and how easily a user, without formal training, can interact with an information system or a web site.

Most of the recent web site usability studies are focused on how users search for information on the web (e.g. [10,13]). But very few of those studies deal with how users order products through web sites (for an exception see [21]). Clearly, better usability will result in more efficient interaction between the user and the site and will increase the probability that the user will return and/or make a purchase [12].

The aim of this paper is to apply the protocol analysis methodology to assess the usability of a commercial web site. To this end, we present the results of a study that systematically observed and analyzed a sample of users ordering free electronic greetings through a greeting card web site. Protocol analysis also known as the “think aloud” method is a qualitative research technique, well suited to determine how users interact with web sites, how users feel about a site (very important when use is discretionary), and how and when usability problems occur.

This paper is organized as follows. The next section reviews the literature on usability and protocol analysis. Section 3 justifies the selection of the product and the web site. Section 4 describes the methodology and the data analysis. Section 5 presents and discusses the results. The last two sections address the implications and the conclusions of this research.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Usability

It is well known that an information system should provide the necessary functions, so the users can accomplish their tasks. But functionality alone is not enough. The determinants of system acceptance are functionality (the degree in which the system provides the functions the users need to do their task) and usability (ease of use, a user-related and a task-related concept). Complete functionality cannot be achieved if the system is not usable $[8]$ . Thus, “usability must be elevated to the same priority as functionality” $[1]$ , p. 12.

Basically, usability refers to the extent to which the user and the system can “communicate” clearly and without misunderstandings through the interface. Goodwin [8] defines usability as the degree of compatibility of the system with the users’ cognitive characteristics for communication, understanding, memory and problem solving. Nielsen [14] defines usability as a measure of the quality of the user experience when interacting with a web-based or a traditional software application, and specifies five characteristics: ease of learning, efficiency of use, memorability (ease of remembering), error frequency and subjective satisfaction. It is worthy of note that Nielsen includes subjective user satisfaction as one component of usability.

However, in the context of the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), Venkatesh and Davis [24] draw a distinction between objective usability of a system and perceived ease of use, and argue that ease of use is determined not only by the system's usability features but also by the users' proficiency with computers, which may be different for each user. Moreover, the authors theorize that ease of use is affected by objective usability only after direct hands-on experience with the system. Fig. 1 depicts the theoretical relationship between objective usability and perceived ease of use.

Usability can be tested through different methods, which consist of observing actual users interacting with the site and collecting information about the specific ways in which the site is easy or difficult for them to use $[5]$ , and the users' satisfaction with the system. Some of these usability-testing methods focus more on objective usability while others try to assess the users' perceptions about the interaction. There are four broad categories of methods, namely: objective performance, subjective preference, experimental evaluation and direct observation.

\- Objective performance measures how capable the visitors are at using the site, by measuring the amount of time or their efforts at completing specific tasks through the system.

\- Subjective user preferences assess how much the users like the system by eliciting their opinions, or by asking them to rate the site on a questionnaire.

\- Experimental evaluation is based on controlled experiments to test hypotheses about design and their impacts on user performance and preferences.

\- Direct observation consists of inspecting the users and monitoring their behavior while they are interacting with the system to detect usability problems.

In the context of web-based systems, some of these approaches are limited or misleading. For example, objective performance measures are insufficient to measure usability of web-based systems. Any productivity assessment measure is limited because it can be influenced by variables beyond the control of the user $[15]$ . In the case of web sites, factors such as connection speed and Internet traffic may affect the time that a system takes to completely display a web page or process a request, thus affecting the amount of time required by the users to successfully complete a task.

Subjective measures can be difficult to obtain if the user population is widespread and diverse as it generally is for many commercial sites. But more importantly, subjective preferences can be misleading because users tend to give high ratings to a site even if it is unusable $[13]$ . In fact, a recent study found that 58% of the time users were unable to find specific information on several commercial web sites. But when asked about “overall ease of use”, these users gave a rating of 4.9 to the site (on a 7 point scale, where 7 was best) $[20]$ . These results seem to indicate that subjective ratings alone are inadequate to assess web site usability.

Experimental evaluation is particularly useful when the interface is being developed $[16]$ or when the web site is being designed. But once the system is implemented, this approach may be difficult to set-up and expensive to conduct. In the case of commercial web sites, formal experiments to test specific design features involving a representative sample of the user population may not be feasible to carry out.

Direct observation is the most feasible and efficient approach to evaluate the usability of web-based systems, not only because of the limitations of the other methods but also for its own strengths. From all the methods based on direct observation, protocol analysis — which is based in the examination of verbal protocols or user verbalizations — is the most systematic and valid $[6]$ . Protocol analysis taps into information that is not available through other methods. It gathers qualitative data from concurrent verbalizations while a real user-system interaction is taking place. Thus, avoiding the problems that may arise with ex-post interviews and questionnaires. Any kind of retrospective probing of the user may be limited because of the tendency of people to forget much of their experiences or justify their actions once they have completed the task $[6]$ .

The advantages of protocol analysis versus other methods have been documented in the literature. For example, Henderson et al. [9] examined four usability evaluation methods: logged data of objective performance, questionnaires, interviews and verbal protocol analysis to evaluate different software packages (spreadsheet, word processor and database) and found that protocol analysis is the most efficient in terms of its ability to highlight specific usability problems.

## 2.2. Protocol analysis or “think aloud” method

Protocol analysis consists of asking the users to perform a specific task and “think aloud” as they work. The process of verbalization reveals the assumptions, inferences, misconceptions and problems that the users face while solving problems or performing tasks $[6]$ . The methodology is appropriate for research efforts based on process tracing $[22]$ , knowledge acquisition $[23]$ , model formulation $[18]$ , and decision-making behavior $[3]$ . This technique has been widely used in the Information Systems literature particularly for systems development tasks $[7,25]$ , model formulation for Decision Support Systems $[17]$ and for usability studies of computer-based systems or interfaces $[4,11]$ .

In particular, protocol analyses of interfaces are based on the direct observation of a real interaction between the user and the system. In each protocol analysis session, the user is asked to perform a predefined task using the system and verbalize his/her thoughts concurrently by “thinking aloud”. The subjects should give a running commentary on what they are attempting to do, what problems they are encountering and other task-related thoughts $[4]$ . Concurrent protocols capture how users approach a task and why problems occur when users interact with computer systems $[22]$ . This technique is used to understand how users form their cognitive model of the system and investigate causes of errors, mistakes and misinterpretations.

User verbalizations and keystrokes are recorded, using video or audio tapes and screen capture routines (if possible). At the end of the session, a full transcript of the session is made, matching verbalizations with keystrokes and screens. “The principal type of data generated by think aloud research consists of a catalogue of episodes, critical as well as typical, exemplifying theoretical points” [4], p. 292. Due to the richness of data obtained via protocol analyses, it is often argued that large sample sizes are not necessary for these studies [18,22,25]. Typically, a small group of users representative of the target population can yield important results. A sample of five users can uncover 80% of the site-level usability problems [13].

Bouwman [3] presents different methods to analyze verbal transcripts from protocol studies. When the researcher is interested in a specific, well-defined group of items, modeling is used to split the protocol into small parts and to code them according to the activity performed. Todd and Benbasat [22] recommend developing an “a priori” coding scheme by which the protocols can be broken down. A priori determination ensures that the findings of the study are not data driven [22], p. 499. Protocols are generally scanned for anecdotal information and scoring techniques are used to tabulate the frequencies of occurrence of key items. The selection of a particular analysis technique depends on the research objectives. In the study of human-computer interaction processes any combination of scanning and scoring can be useful to pinpoint usability problems [3].

Some critiques to this method are the lack of realism due to the presence of the experimenter and the need for concurrent verbalization. Since the talking can be executed almost automatically, it should not be considered as a disturbance to the thought process $[23]$ . Unobtrusive think-aloud protocols do not alter the underlying cognitive processes of users that are being examined through this technique $[22]$ . However, because of the need for concurrent verbalization users spend more time completing the task. There are also substantial differences in the ease with people verbalize their thoughts $[23]$ , and not all subjects are equally suited for the think aloud method.

Protocol analyses of users interacting with commercial web sites can answer the following research questions: how easy to learn and to use is a system, how flexible is it to accommodate different modes of interactions or different users, and why users form specific opinions or attitudes about a system. Studies of commercial web sites share some common features of general usability analysis of systems' interfaces, but present additional challenges stemming from their commercial purpose.

## 2.3. Evaluation of commercial web sites

Scharl and Bauer [19] advance three criteria to evaluate commercial web information systems, namely: content, navigation and interactivity. They deal with three different but complementary aspects of web-based systems, namely: presentation of the information, navigational cues and nature of the interaction between the user and the site.

The academic literature has not given much attention to evaluating the usability of commercial web sites. In one of the few studies of its kind, Tilson et al. [21] asked sixteen subjects to evaluate and compare four e-commerce sites (two that sold clothing and two that sold computer products). The users were instructed to express what they liked and disliked about each site as the interaction was taking place.

Based on the results of the study, Tilson et al. [21] propose 15 usability principles for commercial web sites. These guidelines include: simplicity of product menus, helpful categorization of merchandise, support for narrowing down product lists, availability of product pictures and description, obviousness of order buttons/links, support for understanding the ordering process, appropriate feedback, interference of security notices, reversibility of actions and support for users who want to continue shopping. Table 1 lists these attributes and maps them to Scharl and Bauer's [19] concepts of content, navigation and interactivity.

In doing this mapping, any attributes related to the presentation of the products were associated with content, characteristics dealing with frames or buttons were related to navigation and principles directly related to the ordering process were linked to interactivity.

## 3. Product and web site selection

Tilson et al. [21] identify security concerns as the primary reason why more people refrain from purchasing items online. Therefore, a very convenient way to examine user interaction with commercial web sites is to select free Internet-based products, where users are not concerned about giving out their credit card information. Electronic greetings are one of such products. Another advantage of working with electronic products is that they are ordered and delivered online, overcoming many of the distribution challenges of physical products requested via the Internet.

Electronic greetings can be described as paperless greetings or virtual cards offered through a web site and delivered via email to the recipients. Like their paper counterparts, virtual cards are composed of a design (a combination of a picture and a pre-defined message for a specific occasion). They can be described as a combination of an email message and a greeting card [2].

The nature of the greetings and the process of sending them vary across companies. To send an electronic greeting, senders pick a virtual greeting card from a web site and have the option of typing a personal message. Senders provide the recipient's name and email address and their own name and email. Recipients get an email message announcing that somebody has sent them an electronic greeting. The announcement includes a web address and a number where the recipients can go to receive the greeting by viewing the picture and the message [2]. Fig. 2 presents a conceptual map of the ordering process for free electronic greetings.

A review of the electronic greetings literature showed that the leader of the traditional card manufacturing industry — Hallmark — was not very popular in cyber-space, despite having the highest brand name recognition in paper cards and a web site featuring free greetings [2]. $^{1}$ We wondered if there were usability problems in Hallmark's web site that would contribute to its low acceptance. A pilot study (described below) confirmed that there were problems with Hallmark's web site, so we conducted an independent appraisal of this site using the protocol analysis technique. $^{2}$

In the first quarter of 1999, the selected web site (http://www.hallmark.com) featured free and paid options for electronic greetings and required about six different screens to send an electronic greeting. The process is described in Table 2. The site required customers, even those ordering free greetings, to register as members of Hallmark's club by signing in with a username, email address and password.

At the time of this study, the graphic design of the site was based on two frames. The left frame listed different occasions or categories of greetings, and the right frame featured a list of topics presented on index tabs. One of those tabs was Electronic Greetings. The tabs were presented in two horizontal rows on the top of the right frame but were not clearly identifiable as hyperlinks. The rest of the right frame featured text and graphic product descriptions.

## 4. Research method

Before conducting the protocol analyses, we became thoroughly familiar with the site and the process of sending greetings. We also conducted a pilot study with undergraduate students totally unfamiliar with e-greetings' web sites. Four sites were evaluated in the pilot (AmericanGreetings, Blue-Mountain, Egreetings Network and Hallmark). Each student was assigned to evaluate a site that was new to him or her. They were asked to use the site, state what they liked and disliked about it and if they would consider using it in the future.

Half were under 25 years of age, although the ages ranged from 22 to 35. All the subjects were experienced computer and web users, but none of them had ever used this particular site before. Therefore, they were representative of the typical user population of a commercial web site. Three participants had completed or were in the process of doing graduate work, two were still pursuing undergraduate studies and the rest were full-time employees of different organizations. Each subject was studied in an individual session. Subjects were given very little detail about what the process would entail. They were merely told that they were being asked to test a web site. The observer used identical scripts to introduce the task to the subjects. The task consisted of sending a free electronic greeting using the selected Web site. The users could select any category or occasion, as long as they picked a free electronic greeting.

The script describing the task and the think-out loud method is presented in Appendix A. It is worthy of note that the script did not describe the steps needed to send an electronic greeting through this particular site, because one of the purposes of this research was to learn if the site was usable enough to let new users discover the process by themselves.

Each session was tape-recorded. The observer took notes of the user's keystrokes and facial expressions or any other event that could not have been captured by the tape-recorder, and reminded the subjects to talk out loud.

## 4.2. Data analysis

For the analysis, each session was transcribed and pieced together with the observer's notes. The transcripts included exact verbalizations by the subject, observer's reminders, records of the user's actions (sequence of keystrokes, or mouse-clicks) and the screen that was used at each point. About 40 pages of transcripts were generated (an average of five per subject). A cross-session summary of the transcripts is presented in Table 3, where the subjects and their interactions are briefly described. The table presents a snapshot of each session and highlights the critical episodes $^{3}$ that took place in each session.

To analyze the transcripts, we used a combination of modeling, scanning and scoring techniques. In the modeling phase, we developed a code to identify each screen and step necessary to send a free greeting. Screens were labeled with letters and steps were identified with numbers (as shown in Table 2). The starting point was the conceptual model of how to send an electronic greeting depicted in Fig. 2. This model was tailored to the particular steps required to carry out the process through Hallmark's web site. Fig. 2 and Table 2 together represent the “a priori” coding scheme used to analyze the verbal protocols.

In the scanning phase, the transcripts from the verbal protocols were segmented and structured according to the step and screen that the subjects were using. Then each structured protocol was scanned to identify critical points (screen/step) where users were having problems or registering complaints.

For the scoring phase, the problems were isolated and the frequency of recurrent difficulties or complaints was tabulated. In this step, we generated a list of problematic episodes and grouped them according to the screen or interface characteristic causing them. This evidence was used to derive recommendations to improve the design of the web site.

## 5. Results

Six of the eight subjects completed the assignment in an average time of 18 min. One subject took a very short time (only 5 min) because she had a hard time verbalizing despite the repeated reminders from the observer. Another subject took about 40 min because the session took place during a peak time on the web. $^{4}$ Pre-experimental trials showed that the task could be completed in 5–10 min without concurrent verbalization. The increase in the average time for time completion is due to the fact that verbalizing takes time and that some users were engaged in the exploratory browsing of the site as they were completing the task. The more efficient users were more task-focused and more effective in verbalizing their thoughts.

Finding the link for electronic greetings in the main homepage was difficult for five of the eight subjects. From their comments, it can be inferred that they found the page cluttered and confusing. Most of these five users did not perceive the tabs featured on top of the home page as hyperlinks. After being reminded to verbalize their thoughts, some of these users made comments indicating that they were trying to locate the link or button for electronic greetings (“how do I get to the e-greetings part”, “where is the button for e-greetings?”).

Two subjects looked for instructions and took the time to read them. Both subjects found the instructions helpful. But at the end of the session, one of these subjects commented that there was no mention of the membership in the instructions, and the requirement to join Hallmark's club was totally unexpected.

Four subjects sent St. Patrick's Day cards, two sent greetings for April's Fools Day, one sent an Easter digital card and another sent a thank you note. In all, three subjects had trouble finding the free greetings. Two of the subjects had difficulty finding the free cards because the choices were listed after the paid ones. Another subject did not realize that the price tag of US \$0.00 was the indicator for the free greetings.

Four users found the process longer than expected (e.g. “there’s a lot of screens here,” “this system takes too long to send a free card”). Some of these users found this process unnecessarily long, because they had to go through six different screens in order to send their greetings. In addition, at least two users had problems identifying the buttons they should click on in order to proceed “How do I continue here?”.

There were also some individual difficulties. For example, one of the subjects took a “detour” and tried to preview the card and got lost (“Whoops, what’s that (preview screen), how do I keep sending my greeting?”). The observer had to intervene to put the user back on track.

All of the subjects were dissatisfied with the need to become members of Hallmark's club $^{5}$ in order to send a free greeting ("Why do I have to be a member to send a free card?", "What if I do not want to become a member?"). One subject had problems setting his own user name and password, apparently because there was another user with the same name already registered. This subject also mentioned that there was no information about the maximum length for user name and password. Another subject suggested compiling the sender's information without forcing the visitor to join the club.

## 5.1. Discussion of results

The coding and analysis of the verbal protocols show that the site had problems in the three evaluation parameters identified by Scharl and Bauer [19], namely: content, navigation and interactivity. The usability principles identified by Tilson et al. [21] along with the mapping provided on Table 1, facilitate the process of making specific design recommendations in each case.

In terms of content, some users mentioned that the homepage was “too crowded”. In fact, the cluttered design of the main homepage may have affected the ability of some users to identify the hyperlink to e-greetings. This is an example of complicated product menus that impair the ability of the users to select the desired items.

Another type of content problem was the way in which free-greetings were presented. For example, one user was unable to identify the free greetings as the ones showing US \$0.00 in the price field. Interestingly, the free greetings were not clearly labeled as “free,” therefore, it was hard to identify them in the list of choices. This problem illustrates another usability principle, the need to support users with helpful categorization of the merchandise. In this case, free greetings should be clearly marked.

Navigational problems included the difficulty in finding free greetings in some categories, and the difficulty in identifying the appropriate buttons to continue in several screens. For some occasions the free greetings were displayed after several pages of paid cards, and users had to scroll through several pages of choices, by clicking on “next” several times until they reached the page displaying the free greetings. There were two problems with this approach. First, the “next” was displayed as a hyperlink at the bottom of each page and the user had to wait for the page to load and then scroll down to find it. Second, this sequential navigation strategy was frustrating to some users because they did not know how many more screens they had to go through “This is yet another screen with paid greetings... where are the free ones?”.

The issue of having to navigate sequentially through several screens of unwanted greetings to find the desired ones illustrates the lack of two usability principles, namely: simplicity of the path to products and support for narrowing down the product list. Thus, in order to solve this problem the site should provide a direct link to the free greetings within each category or allow users to pre-select products based on specific conditions (in this case price equal to zero), instead of forcing users to browse through all the offers in each category sequentially.

Another navigational problem was the non-obviousness of the hyperlinks or buttons to get to the next step. In order to address this issue, the buttons to continue should be prominently displayed and easily identifiable in each and every screen.

In the area of interactivity, half of the users found that the process of sending a greeting was long and comprised many steps and different screens. Most of the interactivity problems and complains occurred in the membership screen. This screen did not provide information about the maximum or minimum length of user name or password and the feedback was not visible on the screen when it alerted the users about name duplication. The error message was provided outside the visible portion of the page and the users had to scroll in order to view it. Since the users were not aware that an error had occurred, when they received the same screen they had just filled out, they could not understand how to proceed or why the site did not process their request.

All subjects were dissatisfied with the need to become a member in order to send a free greeting. Some of them thought that it was unnecessary because the site was already collecting the name and the email address of the sender and could not understand the value added of becoming a member of the club. Explaining to the users the benefits enjoyed by the members and offering the possibility to opt out of this requirement could address this issue.

As a general design recommendation, the membership or “join club” procedure should be more informative and helpful, especially for users who had become members before but forgot their usernames or passwords, or for users whose name is already registered by another person. The membership requirement should be disclosed and explained in the instructions and in order to reduce dissatisfaction, users should be able to skip this step.

The preview card option was an attempt to enhance content by providing support for reading and learning about products, but was not well implemented and produced navigational problems. In fact, the only user who experimented with the preview button was unable to return to the ordering process. Part of the problem was that the preview option was available in the ordering process rather than in the greeting selection sequence. Thus, one way to solve this problem is to incorporate the preview button along with the selection of the electronic greeting.

Shortly after this project was completed, Hallmark redesigned its web site. In the new version, it was still necessary to become a member in order to send a free greeting. The membership application procedure was more informative and featured help for users whom had become members before but forgot their user-names or passwords. The overall process was still long and required more than half a dozen screens. Although the design recommendations may seem specific to Hallmark's site at the time of the study, there are general usability lessons than can be extended to other commercial sites.

## 5.2. General usability lessons

Content problems such as cluttered designs, poor readability and lack of comprehension, and navigation issues such as non-obviousness of buttons to continue, impaired the efficiency of the users interacting with the site. However, the interactivity factors such as the length of the process and the need to become a member negatively affected the users' opinions about the site. These elements may affect user preferences and the probability that users return to send electronic greetings through this site.

Efficient interaction with a commercial web site seems to be a function of clear path to products and transparent ordering process. Path to products is the way in which the visitors examine or browse the offerings of the site. The easier the navigation through the product choices the better the chances that users find what they are looking for and buy something. Ordering process refers to the steps required to successfully request products from the site. An easy and clear process also improves the likelihood of users making purchases.

In addition to efficient interaction (ability to complete the purchase efficiently and without errors), users are also affected by factors that determine pleasant interaction or lack thereof. In this particular study, the main sources of complaints were the length of the process and the need to become a member. It is interesting to note that when Hallmark redesigned the site, most of the factors affecting efficient interaction were addressed while the ones influencing satisfaction were not changed.

The TAM [24], and the antecedents of perceived ease of use (Fig. 1) clarify the distinction between efficient use factors and satisfaction-influencing elements. Efficient use refers to objective usability (user interaction free of error and misunderstandings), while satisfaction-influencing factors point to the characteristics that make a web site pleasant to use. Therefore, it is possible that a site that ranks well in objective usability is not accepted or preferred, because users are annoyed at some restrictive features (e.g. the requirement to become a member of Hallmark's club).

## 6. Implications

This research has theoretical and practical implications. At the theoretical level, the application of protocol analysis to evaluate the usability of a commercial web site represents a systematic attempt to collect data about the direct experience of the user and understand how objective usability factors affect perceived ease of use. In terms of the antecedents of ease of use of the TAM (Fig. 1), protocol analysis seems to be one of the best techniques to examine the interaction among the following three constructs: objective usability, direct experience and perceived ease of use.

Practical implications for companies launching commercial web sites and attempting to conduct business online highlight the importance of performing usability tests to identify and fix their web-based systems, and increase user productivity and satisfaction with the web site. User interaction studies based on the analysis of verbal protocols can identify specific usability problems and features that elicit negative opinions or customer dissatisfaction.

Building usable web sites is crucial. Confusing web sites, excessive graphics, and complicated order forms and procedures can turn customers away and threaten the profitability of online e-commerce initiatives $[12]$ . Conversely, clear interaction requirements and comfortable selection of products can improve customer satisfaction and site profitability. A satisfied and loyal customer base increases the likelihood of purchases and return visits. A site that is more pleasant for the customer is more profitable for the firm.

## 7. Conclusions

In commercial web sites, whose use is discretionary and there is no possibility of user training, usability testing is of the utmost importance. Better usability will result in more successful interaction between the user and the site and may help the user to make a purchase. However, traditional usability tests can be difficult to conduct when the user population is widespread and diverse, as it is the case for commercial web sites.

This study has shown how think aloud protocol analyses can be used to discover and illustrate the nature of the usability problems encountered by typical users. Protocol analysis offers a wealth of information that is generally not available through other methods and due to the richness of the data collected, protocol analyses do not need to be conducted over a large sample of users.

In addition, this research has documented how to evaluate the usability of commercial web sites in terms of content, navigation and interactivity and specific usability principles. A usable web site promotes efficient interaction through clear path to products and transparent ordering process. These factors affect how visitors use and perceive web-based systems and may explain why some sites are more accepted and used than others. Future research directions should include the comparative examination of commercial web sites and the use of protocol analysis in conjunction with other usability testing methods.

Script

"This activity is intended to evaluate a commercial web site. We have solicited your help because we wish an independent view of how well the system operates. Your role is to perform a task (that will be described to you shortly) and at the same time to give a running comment on what you are attempting to do, what procedures you are using and all other task-related thoughts. In other words, you are being asked to "think aloud". Since this does not come naturally to most of us, I will give you a demonstration of what I mean by think out loud."

<Demonstration of thinking aloud method, while signing into the online catalog of the library>

As I told you, all your words will be recorded for later analysis. So it is important that you remember to voice all your comments. My job is to observe the steps that you take in performing your assignment, to record your words and to remind you to speak, in case you forget that. I can not help you in using the site.

In reporting the results of this session, your name or identity will not be disclosed at any time. Only your profile (age, gender, occupation, etc.) will be revealed.

Your task is to send me a free electronic greeting using this web site [URL]. My e-mail address is.... You can select any category or occasion but the greeting must be free. After you send me the free greeting, I will ask you a few general questions about the site.

Please feel free to ask me any questions you might have in mind regarding the purpose, procedure, or task of the Protocol now. Since the system is new to you, you can take as much time as you wish to complete the task. Remember that, during the session, I will be a silent observer. I will not talk to you or ask any questions while you are interacting with the site, unless you forget to verbalize your thoughts. If you forget to do so, I will remind you to talk out loud".
