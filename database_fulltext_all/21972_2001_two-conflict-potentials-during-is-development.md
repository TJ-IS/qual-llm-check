# Two conflict potentials during IS development

Quey-Jen Yeh $^{a}$ , Chih-Ling Tsai $^{b,*}$

$^{a}$ Department of Business Administration, National Cheng-Kung University, Tainan, Taiwan, ROC $^{b}$ Graduate School of Management, University of California, Davis, CA 95616-8909, USA

Received 4 February 1999; received in revised form 15 November 2000; accepted 3 February 2001

## Abstract

Within the context of human functioning, this study proposes two conflict potentials that possibly occur during IS development — users' substantive dissension and emotional hostility. Results show that negative outcomes from the process are not due to the conflict between users and developers, but rather are linked to the two potentials. That is, the construct for conflict potential is distinct from the construct for conflict itself. Our results also indicate that the negative effects of user substantive dissension may be ameliorated by user participation. We qualify the results by pointing out that societal and environmental differences between the Taiwanese sample population and analogous populations from countries with more developed information industries may affect some key factors. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Conflict potentials; Emotional hostility; Substantive dissension; Adequate user participation; IS development

## 1. Introduction

Intergroup hostility, poor communication, and negative perceptions of other parties have been noted in the information system (IS) literature as symptoms of conflicts between users and IS developers (e.g. [4,40]). While, for any given conflict, competition between participants can increase their motivation and creativity, a high level of conflict can damage the development process, since it may be used merely to justify adversarial positions rather than to seek answers [9]. At first glance, these conflicts may seem more interdepartmental than interpersonal. However, theorists on conflict (e.g. [25]) propose that perceptions of intergroup conflict are also affected by the quality and frequency of interpersonal interaction. Personal biases over in-group/out-group differences develop and grow more pronounced as conflict increases. Therefore, a conflict structure that can be interpreted in terms of personal functioning is appropriate for our study.

Social scientists have demonstrated that, through adequate communication, conflict can be resolved constructively (e.g. [42,45]). In the IS field, much work has shown the relationship between user participation and the resolution of conflict (e.g. [3,35–37]), and the success of IS development (e.g. [12,13,24]). However, these studies were conducted in western countries with strong research traditions. Relevant issues for Management IS in other countries may vary, due to differences in local societal environments and the pace of technological progress. In Taiwan, for example, inadequate user participation and poor communication between users and IS staff are key MIS issues [44]. Unlike their US counterparts, which are primarily facing technology infrastructure issues, Taiwan's problems are more management related [8]. Conflict and communication issues are undoubtedly linked to local contexts.

Ware and Barnes indicate that most conflicts originate from two distinctly different types of issues: substantive, which involve disagreements over organizational practices, such as policies, procedures, roles, and responsibilities, and emotional, which involve highly personal perceptions and feelings about other people and about the substantive issues at hand. Emotional conflict is hard to detect, since, due to social norms, people often rationalize and express it as a substantive issue. Both issues are important to conflict research, however; while the expression of emotional feelings may be justified, it often results in distorted memories of events, and these may prevent in-group members from rejecting stereotypes of an out-group, and thus may eventually become a source of future conflicts.

This paper has two primary objectives: firstly, to quantify the effects of the two conflict potentials on the IS development process — user emotional hostility and substantive dissension. Other than the direct effect of the two potentials on the project outcomes, we also attempted to test, when users are assigned to a team, whether the two potentials will increase conflict with developers and increase “flaming” in team meetings as a way of defusing the conflict. Secondly, if project outcomes in fact are negatively affected by the two potentials, to test whether such venting will ameliorate negative effects. The sample tested consists of end-users from businesses in Taiwan, where users appear to be less involved in the IS development process than their counterparts in the US.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Conflict potentials

Conflict potential can be viewed from several perspectives. As causes of conflict, Barki and Hartwick suggest looking at organizational factors, such as divergence of departmental interests, interference in the attainment of goals, and interdependence among parties. Banner [1] suggests consideration from the levels of human functioning. In discussing the transformational perspectives for conflict resolution, he points out that serious conflict is mainly due to people's insistence on something at either the physical or mental level. When people are in a world where consensual agreement over the use of scarce resources is needed, the possibility of conflict at the physical level is high. Alternatively, when people become attached to their own ideas, beliefs, concepts, values, and think that they have the “truth” in a given situation, they conflict, at the mental level, with others who disagree. Banner’s theory of two-level conflict potentials is analogous to the two conflict issues proposed by Ware and Barnes, who suggest that conflict arises over either the substantive or emotional issues. Substantive pertains to situational or external characteristics, such as organizational rules and procedures, or competition for budgetary funds, staff and other scarce resources; emotional relates to personal or internal characteristics, such as goals, styles, and abilities of people in conflict. Conflict occurs when people perceive that others oppose their preferred outcomes, behaviors, or worth.

Basically, the levels or types of issues differ in behavior and perception. For emotional hostility, when groups involved in conflict attempt to evaluate each other, typical results include perceptual biases, stereotyping, tendency to favor the in-group, and prejudice towards the out-group, etc. (e.g. [33]). These are acts of perception rather than viewable responses or behaviors, like disagreements over substantive issues. Nevertheless, whether the conflict is openly hostile or subtly covert, strong personal feelings are often involved. As conflict increases, individual prejudices develop, group members begin to emphasize the differences between the groups, and an in-group/out-group bias grows more pronounced. Over time, these biases become self-enforcing, personalized, and tend to fuel further conflict.

The IS development process is a social process involving interactions of people from different backgrounds, so confrontations over issues resulting from varying personal perspectives are inevitable.

## 2.2. Conflict and user participation during IS development

Several sources of conflict between IS and end-user personnel have been identified. Smith and McKeen, for example, described four sources from their in-depth interviews with 150 IS and business managers: disagreement about computerization control, differences in goals and time frames, lack of measurable benefit, and disagreement over roles and responsibilities. These conflict potentials may relate to personal judgments but are primarily substantive issues. User participation may be defined as the behaviors, assignments, and activities that users or their representatives perform during IS development [2]. Within group meetings, user participation refers to the extent to which users contribute either statements or questions to the group. Other actions include preparation of meeting information, review of proposals and documents, and discussions outside formal meetings. The effect of user participation on IS outcomes is positive, as measured in terms of system success [19,28], user satisfaction [15,29,30], outcome satisfaction [18], organizational impact, system performance [7] and data quality [47], etc. Hunton and Beeler's findings [17] also suggest that users' attitude toward the system are positively associated with their desire to participate in the development of the new system.

User participation in the IS development process can resolve conflicts. Robey and Farrow have studied the influence of the participatory dynamic on conflict and its resolution during IS development. They found that conflict impacts negatively on project success. However, through user participation, user influence can be enhanced, which in turn results in conflict resolution and project success. Barki and Hartwick extended Robey and Farrow's work by interpreting user participation as a three-dimensional construct and arguing the appropriateness of separating conflict into two constructs: disagreement or divergence of opinion, and manifest conflict. They suggested that, as a key cause of conflict, disagreement may increase manifest conflict, and due to greater interpersonal contact and more occasions for personal problems (such as jealousy and past unpleasantness), manifest conflict also may be elevated by user participation. Robey in response to Barki and Hartwick indicates that the “disagreement” factor should, in fact, be treated as an antecedent to conflict, rather than a dimension of it.

These works indicate that conflict resulting from the user participation process can be resolved constructively. When settled through bargaining or constructive confrontation, it can actually enhance communication, stimulate competitive spirit, unify the team, provide early warning signs of future problems, and clarify persistent underlying organizational problems. However, excepting Barki and Hartwick's separation of “disagreement” from conflict, no works have studied the conflict process in IS development by separating conflict potentials into physical and mental levels, or substantive and emotional issues.

## 2.3. User participation in IS development in Taiwan

Taiwan's information industry was formally started in 1978, when the government first declared a long-term plan for the development of computer facility manufacturing and associated applications. Since then, Taiwan has successfully cultivated its high-tech industry. Some firms, mainly Taiwanese homegrown, have become significant players in the related world markets; Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC), for example, is the world's largest dedicated semiconductor foundry providing the world the most wafers [43].

Taiwan, while succeeding in the high-tech manufacturing industry, has encountered obstacles in the development of its information service sector. Environmentally, the problems include small operation size, small domestic market, low software prices, inadequate R&D ability, deficient intellectual property law, and lack of marketing plans and services. Apart from these environmental barriers, it also faces a number of MIS issues. Among them, Wang and Turban indicate inadequate user participation and poor IS-user communication as two key issues, and Chou and Jou indicate user/senior management communications and users' needs, all related to users, as the three top problems. End-users in Taiwan are usually found to have lukewarm attitude toward participation $[27]$ , and, like their counterparts worldwide, to be unable to specify their precise needs $[11]$ . The situation may hurt opportunities to upgrade the information industry. Wang and Turban, for example, indicate that the improvement of software quality in Taiwan is often compromised by the inadequacy of user participation in IS development. The current MIS issues in Taiwan, as encountered in other newly developed countries, such as Singapore, are similar to those of the US in the 1980s. While Singapore is focusing more on a mix of managerial and technical issues, the US, Europe, and Australia focus more on technology infrastructure and strategic planning issues $[5,46]$ . The US, Europe, and Australia also have longer IT development histories. The inadequacy of user participation in Taiwan may be in part due to the environmental limitations of its high tech sector, and/or its shorter history in IT development.

We see that effective user participation is essential for the success of systems development and implementation. It helps resolve conflicts between IS-staff and end-users. One of our focuses is to examine its impact on the conflict process of a sample from Taiwan's business, where the MIS issues currently faced are, comparatively, more user- and management-related than their counterparts in the US.

## 3. Research models

## 3.1. The propositions

Proposition 1. User emotional hostility and substantive dissension increase manifest conflict between users and developers during IS development.

User emotional hostility is defined as the extent of user animosity towards an IS project team or its members, and user substantive dissension as the extent to which users disagree with system developers, or are dissatisfied with IS-related activities during a development of project. While the two terms may be related, the former describes a mental state or attitude, and the latter describes objective concerns. They are reasons for manifest conflict at the physical and mental levels of human functioning, respectively.

Proposition 2. User emotional hostility and substantive dissension have a negative effect on satisfaction with conflict resolution and the final system, and on the perception of project success.

Conflict resolution is defined as the extent to which manifest disagreements are replaced by agreement and consensus, or a solution that agreeable to all parties in divergence is achieved $[38]$ . Project success is defined as the extent to which a project team is perceived as having been productive in its task, effective in its interaction with non-team members, and in compliance with budgets and schedules. Project success is an important element in the outcome of an IS task. It has major implications for the social satisfaction of people involved in the development, and has been demonstrated to be positively linked to satisfactory conflict resolution. The success of a system has also been characterized by the degree of user satisfaction $[32,41]$ . Positive connections between satisfactory conflict resolution, perceived project success, and user satisfaction with the system generally have been assumed.

Proposition 3. User emotional hostility and substantive dissension will significantly advance the venting of user participants in IS-related meetings.

Although the benefit of user participation in enhancing users' partnership and responsibility for a project's success has been questioned [6,20], most researches should agree its effect in providing users with opportunities to vent their emotion. Participating users may engage more energetically and spend more time in relevant activities. The potential conflicts, either emotional or substantive, may intensify user motivation to participate in project activities in order to achieve consensus among adversaries.

Proposition 4. The negative effects of user substantive dissension and emotional hostility on conflict resolution, project success and user satisfaction can be moderated by users' venting during the development process, and this is more significant for those participating in the process than for those participating in implementation only.

The development stage in which users participate affects the outcome. The stages of IS development are traditionally characterized as a series of steps. Edstrom $[10]$ defines four phases: initiation, feasibility study, system design, and changeover and implementation. He believes that user influence is particularly important during system analysis and in determining the scope of the system, but relatively less important during changeover. Keen and Gerson $[23]$ and Henry $[16]$ also indicate that users involved in stages prior to implementation are more able to negotiate on design issues and create a feeling of vested interest. In practice, Taiwanese users are assigned to an IS development team according to project needs and users' knowledge. The phases in which they participate will here be classified into two general levels: participation in implementation only, and participation in the whole process, including design and implementation, and perhaps initiation.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Variable operationalizations

Seven variables were constructed: user emotional hostility, user substantive dissension, manifest conflict, user venting/participation, conflict resolution, project success, and user satisfaction. The two conflict potentials, user emotional hostility and user substantive dissension, were developed specifically for this study. Manifest conflict, user venting/participation, conflict resolution, and project success constructs were modified from those of Robey et al. Except for user satisfaction, each variable consists of several components (items), and these items are phrased in statement form. Responses to the items were measured on a 7-point unipolar scale ranging from “very low” (1) to “very high” (7):

\- Emotional hostility was a three-item construct consisting of: (1) the extent to which users were unwilling to cooperate before the IS project team was established; (2) the extent to which experiences between IS and functional managers had been unpleasant in the past; (3) the level of unsatisfactorily resolved conflict already existing between users and developers. It was intended to measure users' perceptions and feelings about the IS system and staff.

\- Substantive dissension was a four-item construct: (1) the extent to which users were dissatisfied with the allotment of duties; (2) how much extra work was assigned to users as a result of their participation in IS development; (3) the extent to which users disagreed with developers on technical and system performance issues; (4) the extent to which users disagreed with developers on the procedure of IS related activities. It measured users' physical dissatisfaction or disagreement over an IS development process.

\- User venting or participation was a three-item construct: (1) the frequency with which users voiced opinions or problems in IS-related meetings; (2) the extent to which problems proposed by users were discussed in meetings; (3) the time users spent participating in IS-related meetings. Its purpose was to assess the extent to which users vent in IS meeting occasions during development, rather than to assess the extent to which users ask for participation.

\- Manifest conflict was a three-item construct: (1) the extent to which users and developers debated on important issues during IS development; (2) the frequency of overt conflicts between users and developers; (3) the extent of general disagreement between users and developers. It measured apparent conflicts or arguments between users and developers.

\- User satisfaction was a single-item variable measuring the extent of respondent satisfaction with the system for which they participated in design or implementation. It assessed the overall satisfaction of users with the finished system.

\- Satisfactory conflict resolution was a three-item construct: (1) the extent to which conflict events were resolved to mutual satisfaction; (2) the extent to which the responding user was personally satisfied with the resolution of conflicts as a whole; (3) the extent to which both users and developers compromised to resolve disagreements. It measured the extent to which users perceive that manifest conflicts and disagreements were replaced by consensus during development.

\- Project success used a six-item construct: (1) budget control of the IS team; (2) schedule adherence of the IS team; (3) productiveness of the IS team; (4) operational efficiency of the IS team; (5) interaction of the IS team with other departments; (6) quality of the resulting system furnished by the IS team. It measured the extent to which users perceived the IS project team to have succeeded.

## 4.2. Sampling

A questionnaire was developed to include project information, personal background, and the seven construct items. To insure the validity and reliability of the questionnaire, a pretest was conducted before the formal survey. One hundred and fifty students in two Executive Education Programs offered by a leading business school in Taiwan were asked to participate with the pretest. The students were all working full-time, with more than 70% of them holding a management position. In the beginning of the pretested questionnaire, one question, asking whether the respondent ever participated in a system development as a user, was added. Those who never participated were asked to ignore the rest questions. Thirty-two useful responses were returned. The statistical analysis showed that the six construct items all had an internal reliability values over 0.60, and their correlations to each other were in the directions with statistical significances as expected. Specifically, the three outcome variables — conflict resolution, project success and user satisfaction — were correlated positively with user participation, while negatively with the two conflict potentials; also, users participation was correlated positively with manifest conflict.

In the formal survey, the questionnaires were distributed to the same 150 students. Each was asked to give two questionnaires to two different end-users in their organization who had participated at some point in an IS development project. Each questionnaire stated the general purpose of the research and that participation was voluntary. To ensure that the results were confidential (not reviewed by the respondents' managers), prepaid postage was provided for returning the questionnaire directly to the authors. A total of 300 questionnaires were distributed, with 93 usable responses returned for a response rate of 31%.

The results of the returns show that, at the time of the study, the user sample had a diverse industrial background. The average age was 29.1 years, and 60% were female. Experience with using computer ranged from 1 to 20 years, with an average of 4.25 years. At the time, 24.7% were at the management level and 73.1% were not (2.2% missing values). To test whether the development stage in which a user is assigned to participate will affect the degree to which the negative effects of conflict are moderated in Proposition 4, respondents were divided into two groups according to their participation conditions. In Taiwan, users can be classified into “whole process” or “implementation only”. Respondents who participated in both the design and implementation stages were (43 respondents), and those who only participated in the implementation stage were User 2 (50 respondents). However, there are two significant differences with respect to the IS project being developed. The first is user perception of who initiated the projects. ## 4.4. Analysis method

Path analysis or structural equation modeling (SEM) has been used to examine the social process of IS development. However, both methods are inappropriate in test of causal relationships that have not been confirmed theoretically. Since the current purpose is more exploratory than confirmatory, we apply the multivariate multiple regression (MMR) method for our analyses. This is a general regression model that has multiple predictors and multiple responses, with each univariate response assumed to follow its own individual regression model (e.g. [22]). However, the error terms associated with the different responses may be correlated. The model is solved simultaneously for the multiple linear systems of equations. MMR is appropriate, since it allows for correlation between response variables and it also allows us to adjust for the effects of the two project-related variables. By treating them as dummies, their effects can be adjusted. Using MMR, the models were tested separately for the two user groups.

## 5. Empirical results

## 5.1. Construct factorization

All component items were input for the factorization of variables other than user satisfaction. The component analysis with varimax rotation extracted six factors with eigenvalues larger than 1, which altogether account for approximately 74.4% of the common variance. Table 3 reports the rotated solution for loading coefficients larger than 0.3 in magnitude. Hair et al. indicate that factor loadings in the range of 0.55–0.60 or above are considered significant for a sample of size of 85–100. This guideline suggests that each of the six factors be interpreted as one of the constructs designed for the study, indicating a validity of the instrument in measuring the constructs. Validity is the “ability of a construct’s indicator to measure accurately the concept under study,” and that “the original definition of the construct or concept… matched to the selected indicators or measures” [14].

A further indication of the appropriateness of the factors is the internal reliability value of Cronbach's alpha. This, which estimates the reliability statistics for the components of a multiple item additive factor, tells us how much correlation we expect between our scaled factor and all other possible scales measuring the same thing [31]. A value of 0.70 is the common threshold for acceptable reliability of a factor, but values below 0.70 have been deemed acceptable if the research is exploratory in nature. Except for one factor which has an internal reliability value of 0.67 (Factor 6/User 2, see Table 3), all reliabilities for the entire sample, and for both subsamples (Users 1 and 2), are over 0.70. Therefore, we assume all variables are reliable.

## 5.2. Descriptive statistics

The ratings, gauged on a 7-point interval scale, of each respondent for the individual items composing each variable were averaged to obtain a score that represents the respondent's perception of that variable. The higher the score, the greater the extent to which the respondent perceived the variable. The means and standard deviations of the scores are reported in Table 4, from which we see that there are no significant differences in mean between the two user groups. However, for the three variables regarding conflict — emotional hostility, substantive dissension, and manifest conflict — the mean ratings are all below 4, the median, and for the three outcome variables — conflict resolution, project success and user satisfaction — the ratings are all over 4. The ratings for user venting/participation cluster at the median for both groups. Thus, it seems that user attitude toward IS development is inclined to be positive over both groups. This suggests that comparisons between the groups be made in a relative rather than an absolute sense.

## 5.3. Correlations between the responses

Correlation analysis was performed to determine the directions and degree of correlation between the five response variables. The results are reported in Table 5. The three outcome variables — conflict resolution, project success and user satisfaction — are positively correlated, and the correlations between each of them and user venting/participation are all positive for both groups. This is not surprising, since they represent positive outcomes of user participation. However, the correlations between manifest conflict and the three outcomes are all very slight ( $|r| < 0.1$ ), so these relationships were ignored in our analysis. The only variable that is significantly correlated with manifest conflict is user venting/participation, and this correlation is more significant for the group User 1, those who were involved in both design and implementation.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Data characteristics

Two user conflict potentials during IS development are defined in the study: substantive dissension and emotional hostility. The variables tested for the conflict process include user venting/participation, manifest conflict, and three outcome variables — satisfactory conflict resolution, project success, and user satisfaction. Since the development stage, in which users participate affects the outcome of that participation, respondents were assigned to either User 1, a whole-process participation group including design and implementation, or User 2, an implementation-only participation group.

The data indicate that the two groups differ significantly in their perception of project initiator (user or IS staff) and type of system developed (low or high-level). In particular, User 1 perceived that they had more projects initiated by users (90–64%), and developed more DP/MIS systems(80–57%). This situation, where users perceive that projects initiated by users are mostly lower level, may relate to Taiwan's businesses. Because of the relatively small market and high cost, shorter IT development history, and poor user capability, it is difficult for Taiwan's enterprises and users to foresee the benefit of putting a high level system into practice. Therefore, establishment of a high level system often has to have support from high level management or the IS department, who acknowledges the value of new information technology. However, our findings suggested that users are more likely to participate in the IS development process, including the design phase, when they initiate the projects. This is not surprising, since, when users initiate a project or perceive that they have, they generally will gain occasions to participate in the subsequent design and development phases to share their ideas with developers.

## 6.2. Effects of the conflict potentials

MMR analyses reveal that the two conflict potentials positively affect manifest conflict for both user groups, as hypothesized in Proposition 1, and negatively on all three outcome variables for both user groups, as hypothesized in Proposition 2. Nevertheless, the effects of substantive dissension for User 1 on the three outcome variables are not significant.

One other interesting finding regarding conflict and the IS outcome process is that manifest conflict does not significantly correlate with any of the three outcome variables, despite their significant regression relationships with the two conflict potentials. This may suggest that negative effects on the outcome process result from the two conflict potentials, rather than directly from the manifest conflict itself. This result underscores the necessity of identifying situations that may cause conflict, rather than simply dealing with conflict once manifested.

The link between substantive dissension via user venting/participation to resolution and outcomes may imply that, when users gain opportunities to participate, their dissension over substantive issues may elevate their desire to vent or participate in bargaining toward agreement. These occasions may in turn provide them a way to moderate any negative perceptions of IS outcomes possibly caused by the divergences. This inference is also supported by data from User 2, the group who participated in implementation only. For them, substantive dissension only relates slightly to user venting/participation, but significantly with the negative of the three outcome variables. The results also confirm the importance of providing users adequate occasions to vent or participate in IS development, including the design and implementation phases.

Compared to substantive dissension, the negative effect caused by emotional hostility seems less likely to be reduced by user venting, whether the occasion for venting is involved with both design and implementation, or implementation only. According to our data, users' emotional hostility is only slightly related to their perception of venting/participation, but significantly and negatively related to manifest conflict and the three outcome variables for both user groups. Such a result may be due to the fact that, inhibited by social norms, people often cover up their negative emotions. They instead rationalize personal biases into substantive issues, or drum up disagreements over trivial incidents to provide justification for an emotional conflict with other parties. The consequence is that conflicts over real substantive issues may be solved but not those for which the root is personal emotions. Such a result also implies that simply giving users venting opportunities is not enough to deal with user hostility.

## 6.4. Taiwan's MIS context

The findings may owe much to the context of this particular sample. Taiwan differs from its counterparts in more advanced countries, which focus primarily on IT infrastructure issues. Thus, for Taiwan, all primary issues are user related, and further, the attitudes of end-users toward participation in an IS development project are quite lukewarm. Taiwanese companies often have to put off their attempts to improve software quality because of the insufficiency of user participation. Insufficient participation may reduce users' enthusiasm and raise negative feelings toward IT; on the other hand, users' emotional opposition to IT may lower their interest in engaging in an IS development project. However, users' internal biases toward IT are more dependent on local cultural and technological environmental factors. Users may need to adjust by means of user re-training, school education, and enforcement of using IT, in order to modify their attitude. Adoption of an efficient user-participation or IS development method, for example, the prototyping method (e.g. [21,39]) which has been commonly used by the Western information industry, is also helpful. Laudon and Laudon [26] break down prototyping into four steps: identify user's basic requirements, develop a working prototype, use the prototype, and iteratively revise the prototype. Users are encouraged to participate in the development stage and work with the system in repetitive use and revision phases in order to refine and enhance the system. In this way, users are able to interact with the system much earlier in the development stage and more easily to defuse their substantive or emotional dissatisfactions.

## 7. Conclusion

IS development is a complex task, the final success is dependent not only on technical efficacy but on how the personal interactions during that process influence its outcomes. While manifest conflict between users and developers often occurs during development, it appears to be a necessary result of the participative process; unavoidable, yet neither good nor bad. Any negative effects on outcomes appear to have their source in the potentials of conflict. Recognizing and addressing situations that have the potential to cause conflict is, therefore, more crucial to the management of conflict than simply reducing the manifest conflict during IS development.

This study proposed two conflict potentials contributed by users — substantive dissension and emotional hostility toward IT. Our results show that these two potentials are separate from, and possibly antecedents of, manifest conflict and some negative outcomes of IS development. Giving users occasions to vent/participate during the development process, including design and implementation phases, appears to moderate the negative effects generated by user substantive dissension, but not those by user emotional hostility. IS managers must take particular care to prevent past unpleasantness becoming persistent animosity. Our findings, however, are derived based on a sample from Taiwan's information industry. The US, Europe, and Australia, for example, all have a longer history of IT development, and thus, focus more on technology infrastructure and strategic planning issues. Whether our findings are specific to our particular sample, or can be generalized to other newly developed or developed countries, remains to be explored.
