# Designing systems that support learning and use: a customer-centered approach

Karl L. Smart $^{a,*}$ , Matthew E. Whiting $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Brigham Young University, 567 TNRB, Provo, UT 84602, USA

$^{b}$ Microsoft Corporation, One Microsoft Way, Redmond, WA 98052, USA

Received 27 January 2001; accepted 21 February 2001

## Abstract

As the importance of customer satisfaction has grown, a corresponding emphasis on customer-centered design and end-user computing has emerged. However, little research has attempted to show the application of user-centered design for information-based products, specifically with how customers use information to learn about their software systems and how information assists users when problems arise. This article reports the results of a 2-year study of a cross-functional design team within a major software company; they were monitored while they used a contextual design process to gather information about users. The paper discusses the contextual design methodology, outlines the development of the cross-functional team and research focus, discusses the collection and interpretation of customer data, and shows how the development group translated data into design ideas and prototyped and tested those ideas. Constraints affecting the implementation of design ideas are discussed, with implications explored. This article provides an interpretive case study that demonstrates the contextual design methodology as it applies to the design of information. Additionally, it provides insights into customer use of information and the application of the methodology to the design of information systems, computer applications, and their documentation. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Contextual design; Customer-centered design; Information systems; Participatory design; Documentation; Software and system development; End-user computing

## 1. The need for customer input

As recent research has focused on end-user computing, successful organizations have come to understand the need to design products and services to meet customer requirements and expectations $[11,29]$ . User-centered design has become a business strategy that many companies use to gain competitive advantage and maintain economic viability $[34,40]$ . One hears managerial mantras, such as, “Get close to the customer” and “Listen to the voice of the customer” [27]. As Norman observes,

“Modern industry must distinguish itself through its consideration of the needs of its customers. . . . As companies design more for usability and understanding, they will discover a competitive edge, for these principles save customers time and money while increasing morale” [33].

Organizations, however, realize that they cannot rely on designers, developers, or specialists to know how to design products and services to meet customer needs. Since designers are seldom the primary users of the systems or products, their own biases, rationalizations, and views often interfere with assessing what customers truly need or want. Studies show that the more customer contact a project has, the more likely it is to be successful [25].

The increasing complexity of technology and of products requires a corresponding increase in awareness of customer needs and how humans interact with systems. As Hackos and Redish observe, “Good design happens only when designers understand people as well as technology, . . . Designs that do not meet user’s needs will often fail in the workplace or in the market, resulting in high costs in productivity, frustration, and errors that impact users and their organizations, . . .” [20]. Although an increasing amount of research has appeared on user-centered product development, especially in the computer industry [10,13,41], little of it shows how users deal with information [1] or how to create user-centered information systems [12,15,16].

The importance of information and knowledge management has grown through the past decade, as organizations have reassessed their need for and support of its role and place. An increasing amount of research has focused on the value of using information strategically within organizations $[4,26,36]$ . But little research shows how customers interact with information or how they use it to perform tasks or to overcome difficulties. This article reports the results of a 2-year study of a development team within a major computer software company as they worked with users to explore how users learned to operate new software systems and how they behaved when encountering problems within software applications. Specifically, the team explored methods of user assistance and documentation, with the goal of finding improved ways of helping users. This study uses contextual design methods, as refined by Beyer and Holtzblatt $[3]$ , to gather and analyze information on users' wants, needs, and work habits when using computers.

## 2. Contextual design methodology

In conducting this study, a contextual design process was used to gather information about computer users. The data gathering portion of the methodology (sometimes called contextual inquiry) is a field studies method, where design team members work with users in the context of their own work and work environments. Beyer and Holtzblatt define contextual design as “a field data-gathering technique that studies a few carefully selected individuals in depth to arrive at a fuller understanding of the work practice across all customers.” Previous work details the methodology we adopted [37]. Contextual inquiry provides techniques for gathering data in the context of the user’s own work: of users performing real tasks in their own environment. Researchers play an active role in engaging users in conversations about their work practices and corresponding implications for supporting systems. This approach forces designers into the user’s world as well as allows users to participate in design activities [31].

## 2.1. Team organization and research focus

Since the study emphasized information and the design of information to support users, the company delegated the leadership of the team to the technical documentation department. Since the group had struggled with the challenges of documentation to meet customer needs [19,23,38], a documentation group manager was selected to lead the team, with three others from the documentation department assigned to work full time on the team. From the project's inception, it was understood that a documentation department could not independently effect the changes that should result from the study, so the team was designed as a cross-functional group with additional members that included a lead software developer, a usability specialist, a user interface (UI) designer, and several other software developers that rotated in and out during the project.

Although the team (designated here as the Rousseau group) ultimately wanted data that would help in designing the information component of an application, they sought to understand how users learn and interact with applications and how best to support this. As a result, the team did not set out initially to design a new style or type of documentation but rather to better understand users — their work practices and needs — and to determine how the applications and not just the documentation could best assist users. This focus led the Rousseau group to determine how users learned new software, how they reacted to problems they encountered within computer applications, and how documentation (defined broadly as any information from menu structures and long prompts to printed manuals and online help) interfaced with their learning and problem solving strategies.

The Rousseau group realized that many assumptions (e.g., about how and why individuals use or do not use information or documentation) have not been verified. The team's work attempted to understand the work structure of the individuals using the software and how product documentation supports and assists users in completing their work. The major focus (how do people learn to use a software program and how do users get help when confused?) centers on two primary instances when product information supports a user.

## 2.2. Gathering user data

Beyer and Holtzblatt's recommend interviewing 15–20 customers from at least four to six work sites; the Rousseau group, therefore, first identified potential organizations and individuals for the study. To make certain, the interviews captured a heterogeneous group, the team selected a representative number of male and female users from different types of organizations in different regions of North America. The group conducted 17 interviews at 10 different sites, involving 7 men and 10 women. Four interviews were conducted with individuals working at government agencies, eight in businesses, and five at educational institutions, with sites located in six regions of the United States and Canada: Seattle, Washington; San Francisco, California; Salt Lake City, Utah; Chicago, Illinois; Durham, North Carolina, and Banff, Canada.

One or two members of the team went to every interview: one usually asked questions and worked with the user while the other took detailed notes. In addition, each interview was tape-recorded. Part of the data gathering included collecting artifacts from the site — such as copies of pages from print documentation, online help screens, internal documentation, and user-created tipsheets. The team also gathered detailed notes about the work culture and environment of each user.

Site interviews with individual users are the foundation of the contextual design process. The setting of the interviews attempts to explore work practices and work environments rather than to test predetermined hypotheses or respond to validated interview questions. Interviewers as participant–observers, seek to understand the context of work by actually going to customers' workplaces and watching them do their own work. The interviewer becomes a partner with the user as they discuss the user's work and uncover unarticulated aspects of it. As Holtzblatt and Beyer explain,

Contextual inquiry provides techniques to get data from users in context: while they work at real tasks in their workplace. In a contextual interview, the interviewer observes the user at work and can interrupt at any time and ask questions as an outsider: “What are you doing now?” “Is not there a policy for this?” “Is that what you expect to happen?”

Confronted in the moment of doing the work, users enter into a conversation about what is happening, why, and the implications for any supporting system. The user and interviewer discover together what was previously implicit in the user's mind. Talking about work as it happens, artifacts created previously, and specific past projects reveals the user's job beyond the work done on that day.

The interviewers watched users using a variety of systems, with the interviews centered on observing users actually working. To get users performing tasks in the context of their work environment, interviewers posed questions that forced users to do, not just recollect their work: when was the last time you had a problem in an application? What did you do? How did you respond to the problem you encountered? Can you show us the process by recreating what you did? These questions helped get users actually working, and, as a result, revealed work practices frequently hidden or forgotten by the user. The average interview lasted 2–3 hours.

After collecting data from the interviews, the interview teams returned to meet with the entire Rousseau group, which held interpretative sessions as soon as possible after the return. The night before an interpretive session, the interview team would listen to the tape recording and review the notes. As the Rousseau group gathered, one person captured information on a computer in note card format as the interviewers discussed the interview. Each note card identified the user (U1, U2, etc.) and sequence of the information in the interview (1, 2, etc.). ## 2.4. Creating an affinity diagram

After capturing information from the interviews on note cards and in work models (over a series of months), the Rousseau group followed an inductive process of bringing together all the data. The first step involved creating an affinity diagram, which is based on the quality principles and processes developed in Japan $[8,24]$ . The intent of the affinity diagram is to organize data across all customers. The process helps reveal the scope of the data and identifies any holes or weaknesses in the data. Organizing the data makes key issues stand out, emphasizing significant knowledge about the customer in an easy-to-share format. The process also helped the team decide what the data meant.

The affinity diagram was built from the bottom up, finding common themes and structures from individual note cards. For example, a member of the team would select a note card that identified a particular issue or idea, and then other team members would look for additional note cards that captured similar information. After the notes were grouped, the team created a name or succinct phrase to represent the set of cards. Then the team looked for relationships and structures among the groups. This resulted in a hierarchy that organized the data into manageable chunks. Fig. 2 shows a portion of the affinity diagram developed from the study.

The first level (how I get information to solve problems) is a major group label identifying one of the foci of the study. The second level (what I do first) lists one of the major subheadings that summarizes a set. Other issues in the second level included

\- What alternate strategies are used when the first strategy does not work?

\- How do users handle information overload?

\- What strategies are available when learning something new?

\- Why do users fail to learn in some situations or for a particular application?

\- What do users do after they learn something new?

\- How do users determine if the information is what they need?

\- What perceptions do users have of manuals and online help?

\- How do people use documentation?

The third level (e.g., I go to a book when I need information to solve a problem) shows group labels that reflect the common affinity of a collection of individual note cards. The individual note cards appear under each group title. Additional initial user strategies for restarting that are not listed in the abbreviated portion of the affinity diagram include

\- try to figure out the problem myself, even though I know another strategy could better and more quickly help me;

\- try some standard trouble-shooting strategies (even though I do not understand the problem);

\- check for simple things first;

\- remember information from the training session;

• continue with a faulty hypothesis;

\- talk to myself as I think through the problem;

\- go to other people or places for information;

\- try things I am told, even when I do not think they will help.

The affinity diagram helped in providing organization to the knowledge and insights to the team. It revealed some of the common issues and themes, as well as the scope of problems and issues that users encounter. Along with the work models, the affinity diagram showed key elements of work practice and became a crucial foundation for identifying design requirements. The completed diagram covered the entire wall of a large conference room and served as a constant reference for supporting decisions. When members of the Rousseau group made a claim about a user or design idea, they frequently referred to the diagram to validate their claim. Additionally, the diagram provided a good mechanism for telling the Rousseau-team story to new team members and internal visitors.

The affinity diagram allowed the Rousseau group to envision design and information support solutions from particular problems that users encountered. The diagram became the rationale for innovative design ideas.

## 2.5. Consolidating work models

After creating the affinity diagram, the team set out to consolidate the work models of each of the users. This created concise visual representation of and statements about the customer population, showing common structures without ignoring variations across customers. Through the models, the Rousseau group created “schematics” of the customer population, their work practices, and environment. What follows is a brief description of the consolidated models and some of the key issues identified with each.

## 2.5.1. Sequence models

Although the affinity diagram reflected specific thoughts, values, and behaviors of users, it did not represent specific user actions. To capture these, the Rousseau group created sequence models during initial interpretive sessions from detailed notes on specific tasks that users performed. Consolidating individual sequence models demonstrated specific task structures and showed strategies common among the user population. For example, one consolidated model captured what users do with information they find to assist them in learning or restarting after a hiatus.

Such a sequence model showed various strategies that users employ when they are trying to locate information; it additionally identified potential breakdowns in finding or using information. In looking at redesign, the team analyzed each of the breakdowns and looked at ways the system or its information could assist users who encountered problems. The consolidated sequence models provided rich data about users and specific tasks they perform, defining the work a system must support and where system information could assist users in accomplishing tasks.

## 2.5.2. Flow models

Flow models show an individual user's relationship with others within the organization, specifically showing the flow of information between individuals. Consolidating the flow models revealed basic patterns of communication and work practices and how product information interfaces with roles and communication. As the Rousseau group analyzed and consolidated flow models, they discovered ways that applications and information could assist users in performing their jobs.

For example, many organizations have production workers, important workers, and formal internal answer givers. An important worker (defined by status and function) often expected the formal internal answer giver to come to his or her desk to help solve basic problems. This frequently interrupted the answer giver from regularly assigned responsibilities and caused production workers (individuals with less status or seniority) to have less help when needed. In looking at redesign, the team discussed design ideas for creating a system that provided accurate and timely user information on basic problems for all types of workers, freeing the help desk personnel to concentrate on addressing mission-critical or enterprise-wide issues and questions. A key insight was that help provided by the system needed to be as good as the formal answer giver in providing accurate and timely information. Users turned more frequently to individuals for support than to system information and documentation because of the reliability and pertinence of the information provided.

## 2.5.3. Cultural models

With the consolidated cultural model, the Rousseau group wanted to show the effect of the cultural environment on the ability of users to get help in learning new tasks or finding how to continue when problems arose. The model shows a knowledge worker in the center, with arrows radiating to individuals and groups inside and outside the organization. Users find information from many sources, such as office-mates, informal answer givers, formal internal trainers, its support staff, help desks, external support sources, vendors, and training. Information becomes less applicable, less reliable, and more costly as it is located farther from the knowledge worker or user. This insight led the team to look at documentation and support options that remained near users and their workplace. If the system could provide appropriate support and information, it would probably be more reliable and less costly.

## 2.5.4. Artifact models

Artifacts are items that the interview teams collected while working with users. Many of these were pages from manuals (company-produced user's guides that shipped with products and third-party manuals purchased separately) and printed copies of help screens. Additionally, the team saw frequent use of user-created tipsheets; i.e., informal documentation created to provide help or assistance in specific circumstances. The intent of consolidating artifacts into models was to see similarities and differences in the information users found helpful. The team created consolidated artifact models of each of these types: user's guides, third-party manuals, windows help screens, and user-generated tipsheets.

In comparing the consolidated model, the group found commonalities and differences among the types of information customers used; e.g., many claim that users prefer third-party manuals over those that ship with the product. In analyzing the differences as captured in the artifact models, however, the Rousseau team found that other than a more friendly tone adopted in the third-party manuals, few differences exist between these two types of documentation. The content and organization were strikingly similar.

Tipsheets proved to be a common artifact, and this suggested that users needed to create documentation, that captured information to match their internal schema (or mental model). As a result, users create their own documentation or support for future reference and for sharing solutions to problems encountered with others. Considering the importance and prevalence of tipsheets to users, the Rousseau team looked for ways that applications could help in generating and distributing user-created tipsheets.

## 2.5.5. Physical models

The consolidated physical model reflected the general physical environment. Although the team observed differences among the work sites, they found striking similarities. A central focus of an office was a computer, with a phone, books and reference material, and a work area. The model suggested that the closer the reference material was to the computer, the more important it was to the user. Since notes with process steps and user-created tipsheets were often attached to the monitor, they were considered the most important information to the user. Physical models confirmed data from the cultural model: users often seek assistance from colleagues in close proximity to their workspace. It also demonstrated that users seek assistance through easily reached sources (company help desks and product support lines). Findings from the consolidated models and affinity diagram include

\- users create their own support information and documentation;

\- the interface is part of the documentation;

\- short, context-sensitive tips are important in helping users complete tasks and get help when they have encountered a problem;

\- indexes are crucial entry points to accessing information;

\- users need explanations that they understand;

\- users do not care if the methods are inefficient;

\- users get angry when a new version of an application changes the knowledge base by “improving” the software;

\- error message recovery is very important;

\- users have little time for formal learning or training;

\- tutorials are seldom used;

\- some users — such as answer givers or macro programmers — need a printed manual.

In many ways, these insights impact both the design of systems and the creation of information to support systems.

## 3. Using data to inform design decisions

Recent research has focused on ways to bridge the gap between user information gathered and actual design [45]. The following section shows how the team built upon existing research and methods to convert the collected data into design ideas.

## 3.1. Visioning

Realizing that they were not just designing a new type of manual or help system, the Rousseau group tried to think creatively to come up with creative solutions. The data reflected a diversity of users from which the group categorized the following profiles of learning styles:

\- experimental users who like exploring on their own;

\- model building users who want to see the big picture and who build mental models;

\- users who learn by doing;

\- users who want to learn a concept first, then to try it out;

\- users who want to see a task completed by someone else first;

\- users who like to follow explicit instructions step by step;

\- accidental or indifferent users (users with little motivation or desire to learn).

Some users employ combinations of these learning styles, or they vary their learning style to meet the task.

In addition, the group identified the following strategies utilized when trying to recover from errors or solve problems:

\- try the same procedure or steps;

\- restart (usually involved rebooting the computer or starting the whole process again);

\- ask an office-mate or anyone within earshot;

\- consult the documentation;

\- call the help desk.

Complex problems would cause some users to employ multitude strategies. For example, a user would try to “un-gray” a grayed-out option (i.e., options that have been dimmed on the screen when they cannot be used); the user might try the procedure repeatedly, then consult the documentation; if that proved futile, the user would call the help desk. These learning styles and strategies substantiate existing research $[6,28,32,35]$ .

With such diversity in learning styles and problem-solving strategies, the team used a “visioning” process to invent possible responses to different types of users and problem solving strategies. This focused user needs and then looked for appropriate technology to help. Although not all of the vision ideas could be implemented in the subsequent application release, the ideas became critical for developing innovative solutions in the systems development process. For instance, the group envisioned a system that was aware of users and their differences and that could customize user activity and provide assistance when needed. In discussing and refining this, the group broke the idea into two parts: gathering information (the watcher) and communicating to users (the communicator). The watcher analyzed the set-up of a user's computer and monitored the user's actions to identify possible problems. If users, for example, tried the same sequence unsuccessfully three times, the watcher would automatically note that there was a potential problem.

Information gathered by the watcher was then transferred to the communicator, the part of the application that directly interacted with users. It helped users understand and operate the application, warn the user of problems, step the user through tasks, and help recover from errors. It could interact with users through voice, online text, or “pointing” to pictures or other parts of the interface. The team realized that one of the primary strategies that users turned to was asking others, as has been substantiated in previous research $[17,18]$ . The communicator created an electronic “neighbor” that could provide needed information. The reliability of the information from the communicator would be high because it helped the user due to information gathered by the watcher.

The watcher and communicator, as well as other methods of assistance suited to individual needs and preferences, suggest the need for process support as well as domain-related knowledge when using intelligent help and assistance $[5,44]$ . In generating this list, the Rousseau group did not focus on the immediate technical or financial feasibility of the ideas; rather, they realized that they needed a vision of how an application could implement ideas for a series of product releases. The vision of user assistance then became a basis for building an actual user environment.

## 3.2. Building an user environment design and storyboarding

The involvement of developers and systems specialists became critical, since many of the concepts required the development of fundamental system features. The Rousseau group realized that their work had to be long term and ongoing. For example, effective use of a watcher could only work if there was greater system awareness via the operating system. Windows — the primary operating system considered — lacked the necessary awareness. Additionally, the Internet was just coming into play. Ideas like the communication center and easy access to third-party information needed a majority of users to be Internet-connected so that the system could easily provide online resources. Moreover, technological constraints — processor speeds, hardware, connectivity, and download speeds — still limited some ideas. Nonetheless, the team developed a user environment containing many of the ideas, knowing the implementation would have to occur over several releases.

To make the vision ideas concrete, the Rousseau group created an initial prototype. It showed how the various types of assistance relate to one another. The group set about redesigning several of the consolidated sequences in a storyboard format, integrating innovative aspects. For example, in a storyboard of a redesigned “Learn to do” sequence, the user trigger is “I have time to learn,” should users want to explore and learn about the application. In the new user environment, a user could go to the communication center to access and engage in an online discussion. Or, the system could generate a list of contact individuals who could provide individual assistance. Additionally, users could turn on a “safety switch” that allowed them to play with the application at will, but would not save any work or allow the user to harm any existing data.

If users preferred more immediate interaction, they could go to options within the application. If users wanted to get a general overview of the application, they could access a demonstration, a freestanding quick-tour video. Although the data suggested users seldom used tutorials, many liked watching someone perform an action before trying it themselves. Another way to learn would be to explore the application's structure through maps, which are graphical representations that depict the way features and tasks fit together, with system, task, feature, comparison, menu, or button maps.

For example, new users wanting to learn about the task of creating a new document could select a task map entitled, “Make a document,” showing a visual depiction of the process and a visual glossary of individual elements. Clicking on options would show how to use application features to complete the task. The map would include a visual index that displays a finished document, with specific aspects labeled to assist users in trying those features, — such as borders, bulleted lists, headers and footers, or graphics.

The storyboards visually resembled the consolidated sequence models, with the addition of features from the redesigned user environment integrated into the sequences. They provided the first opportunity for the Rousseau group to test their envisioned redesign. The storyboarding provided actual structure to the vision and provided the starting point for prototyping.

## 3.3. Prototyping information support

The Rousseau group then validated the design ideas with actual users of the prototypes. The paper prototypes allowed users to test the new design ideas in scenarios of actual work situations. Prototyping design ideas allowed the team to identify additional problems that users encountered with the preliminary design. It also allowed the team to create several scenarios and options focusing on different types of learners and learning styles. The Rousseau group performed numerous iterations of prototypes on each of the major design ideas.

## 3.4. Writing specifications

Once the team had adequately tested and refined the design of the user environment, they selected features that they could integrate in the next product release. With the assistance of several developers, the group formalized the user environment and developed corresponding detailed design specifications. Due to constraints, the team realized that several ideas could not be implemented until future releases, but the formalized user environment became the blueprint from which development efforts ensued. Fig. 3 shows the formalized user environment.

Each feature or type of user assistance was given a function number (e.g., F360, F363, etc.) that corresponded to design specifications. In addition, if the function related to a specific user observed by the Rousseau team, the user designation was included (e.g., U21). The design specifications provided more detailed information, with additional function numbers assigned to each aspect of the feature for referencing. The specifications provided a concrete plan for the software, interface, and documentation requirements of the next release. With the formalized user environment and design specifications, the group could share a complete vision of the system with development to help prepare for future releases.

## 4. Dealing with constraints and resistance

As Norman observes, design ultimately must “to take into account real issues in cost, manufacturability, and aesthetics.” In the end, the Rousseau group envisioned more than user assistance: it included a broader concept of the entire user experience, where assistance is seamlessly and unobtrusively integrated into the application. The most significant factors affecting the implementation of the vision were (1) technological constraints; (2) potential user resistance; and (3) corporate constraints and resistance.

An important part of the user experience that the group envisioned required an intelligent system with both pushing and pulling of information $[5,21,22,39]$ . Several technological constraints prohibited both the pushing and the pulling of information. For instance, the team still had hardware constraints. Additionally, the operating system was not effective in following user events — a system limitation that applications could not work around. Also, the use of “agents” or “bots” was limited $[14,30,42]$ .

Even if all aspects of the vision had been technologically possible, the team was aware that parts of the envisioned interface might not be palatable to all users. For example, users may view a system that is constantly “watching” them as a tool for management to monitor their use. To functional effectively, the communicator needed the watcher to gather information on the terminology users preferred when asking questions. This information is critical in determining user interaction. The group was concerned that users would not like this passive data mining. The team realized these types of potential user resistance would need to be addressed.

The Rousseau group also encountered corporate constraints and resistance: development resources were needed for building a communicator, watcher, tipsheet manager, etc. Other constraints include cultural and organizational issues. The contextual-design process was relatively new to the company, and acceptance by the company took time. The process required a new way of thinking and doing, allowing individuals and groups to be involved in the process in new ways. For instance, individuals from technical documentation and information design had never had such a significant role in the development process $[2,7,9]$ .

Perhaps the major organizational constraint affecting the work resulted from a business event totally independent of development and the data: the company was sold. Although the new company ultimately endorsed the work, new managers and personnel needed to be convinced of both the process and value of the approach. The organizational transition slowed the implementation of the vision.

Ultimately, some envisioned changes were implemented in the next product release. Although the vision of the total user experience was not realized, several significant changes were made. The release included a limited intelligent agent that assisted users in certain tasks. Information designers also did work at redesigning error messages and implementing pop-ups to display critical information. Additionally, elements of a natural language interface were integrated, particularly in the help system.

## 5. Implications

The purpose of this paper has been two-fold

1. to demonstrate the contextual design process applied to information and information systems in a detailed manner; and

2. to report some of the results of the contextual inquiry process, reporting on some of the insights that the team learned.

The proprietary nature of much of the data prevents us from detailing every aspect of the process or its results.

One of the significant insights gained by the group was the need to expand the definition of documentation and the role and place of information within systems. Too frequently, information support is viewed as a printed manual and online help. Information in documentation is mainly about user education or user assistance, but it should involve the entire user experience. Truly user-centered applications require the collaboration of developers, interface designers, usability specialists, and information designers.

Although the Rousseau group did not start out to create a new manual, help system, or type of documentation, their understanding and appreciation of user experience, nonetheless, grew as they worked with customers. They realized that the needed material is integrally linked, that the way things are named and ordered is integral to the user experience, that menus are a first level of information support, and that error messages and system prompts are significant parts of system interaction. The Rousseau group realized, as Hackos and Redish claim, that genuinely successful applications need to reflect the workflow of users, support various user learning styles, and remain compatible to the user working environment and language $[20]$ .

The design team found that the contextual-design methodology provided a valuable method for gathering customer data in a systematic, usable way, and that information about users and their work is best gathered through the process of observing and working with users in their environment in the context of their work. The team found that if they truly wanted to be user advocates, they needed direct contact with users, and that the contextual design process provided a method for finding out what users actually do, not just what they think they do or want. As many have advocated, those involved in designing information need to be willing to learn new skills and be more involved in the whole development process $[7,9,43]$ . As organizations attempt to increase customer satisfaction, the design of information systems will become more significant in impacting customer's experience.

The insights derived from our work have important implications for how we develop assistance for users. Natural language interfaces work toward this end, allowing users to type queries in their own words. Additionally, indexes are needed to cross-reference terms, building on feature and task synonyms of users. Also, visual indexes that show elements of the program or results of tasks using the application can identify key features for users. Since users create personalized information and documentation according to varying contexts and needs, applications need to assist users in creating, saving, and sharing the documentation they create. Increased awareness of users, their preferences and learning styles, can help provide personalized assistance.

The Rousseau group also realized that even with great ideas, they needed others in the organization to have understanding and “buy-in” or acceptance ideas. Design teams need to assess what ideas can immediately be implemented and which can wait until later versions. Additionally, participatory or user-centered design must include the participation of stakeholders and internal management as well as end-users to be successful. As the participation of stakeholders and users increases in the design of systems, system designers and developers have a unique opportunity to better understand how humans interact with computers and machines and to facilitate that interaction. Implementing contextual design or other participatory design methods ultimately will increase the value of systems $[18,29]$ and help organizations remain competitive.
