# A model of Internet adoption by SMEs

Jenni Mehrtens $^{b}$ , Paul B. Cragg $^{a,*}$ , Annette M. Mills $^{a}$

$^{a}$ Department of Accountancy, Finance and Information Systems (AFIS), University of Canterbury,

Private Bag 4800, Christchurch, New Zealand

$^{b}$ Microgarphics Inc., 1925-A.N.W. 2nd Street, Gainesville, FL 32609, USA

Received 13 March 2000; received in revised form 15 February 2001; accepted 15 February 2001

## Abstract

The study discussed in this paper used evidence from seven small firms to determine the significant factors that influenced their Internet adoption. Evidence from four firms was used to create a preliminary model of Internet adoption by SMEs. An additional three firms were then examined to refine the preliminary model. The study concluded that three factors significantly affect Internet adoption by small firms: perceived benefits, organisational readiness, and external pressure. The study identified both similarities and differences between Internet adoption and EDI adoption in small firms. The findings have important implications for managers and service providers. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Small firms; Internet adoption; Case studies; Adoption factors; Model

## 1. Introduction

Opportunities for electronic commerce and global marketing have suggested that the Internet could be very important to small and medium sized enterprises (SMEs). There is already evidence that some SMEs have benefited $[18]$ . However, despite numerous studies about Internet use by SMEs, there is a dearth of research identifying the organisational rationale behind Internet adoption. Thus, this study aimed to improve our understanding of Internet adoption by SMEs.

Adoption of the Internet can be viewed as an innovation for a firm, based on Damanpour's argument [6] that an innovation is something that is “new to the adopting organisation”. An organisation’s decision to adopt an innovation is influenced by a number of factors. Some factors are based on attributes of the innovation and include: the relative advantage the innovation has over the current situation, the compatibility of the innovation with respect to all aspects of the organisation, the innovation’s complexity, the innovation’s trialability, and the observability of the innovation [20]. Pressure from within the organisation and externally are also factors that encourage adoption of technology [8]. Some argue that organisations must perceive the Internet as having relative advantage, i.e. it has to be better than what it supersedes [26].

## 2. Adoption of technology by SMEs

Studies of the adoption of other forms of technology indicate that SMEs are likely to consider numerous factors when contemplating the adoption of the

Internet [14,24]. Lefebvre et al. [14] identified four categories of factors that influence adoption of a new technology by SMEs:

1. characteristics of the firm,

2. competitiveness and management strategies of the firm,

3. influences of internal and external parties on the adoption decision process, and

4. characteristics of new technologies adopted.

The CEO often has a significant role in the adoption of IT by SMEs. An SME that is likely to adopt IT will most often have a CEO who has a positive attitude towards IT adoption, who is innovative and who is knowledgeable about IT $[2,25]$ .

Some studies of small firms have focused on particular types of technology. Electronic data interchange (EDI) is an important example as it has similarities to the Internet in that EDI provides an electronic link with customers. Iacovou et al. [11] identified the following three factors that affect the adoption of EDI by small businesses:

1. perceived benefits of EDI by the organisation (including direct benefits, e.g. operational savings, and indirect benefits, e.g. impacts on business processes),

2. organisational readiness (financial resources and technological readiness), and

3. external pressures on the organisation to adopt the technology (from competitors and imposed by trading partners).

There is some evidence that these factors may apply to Internet adoption by small firms. For example, the Internet offers many potential benefits, including its global reach [12]. Many studies have discussed the advantages of using the Internet for publicity, advertising, online selling, communication and collaboration [3,4]. Furthermore, the Internet offers the opportunity for the organisation to study competitor's products, organisation, and related matters [5].

External pressure is another factor within the Iacovou et al. model that has been argued to apply to Internet adoption. For example, “your customers will begin to demand it”. Also, there can be pressure from competitors for a firm to adopt the Internet. “if your competitors have it, that could spur you to get connected (to the Internet)” [13].

## 3. Method

The research aimed to develop a model of SME adoption of the Internet. Stated as a question, this is “why do SMEs adopt the Internet?”

The contemporary nature of the Internet favoured the case study approach. This has been considered particularly appropriate, where the theory in the area is not well developed $[7,22]$ . The case study can capture ‘reality’ in substantial detail and is particularly useful when a natural setting or a focus on contemporary events is needed.

Case studies can be used to generate, describe and/or test theory. This study did not aim to provide a prediction of future actions. Rather, the focus was towards providing a description of the reasons for adoption that could subsequently form the basis of a predictive model. Therefore, the study had the objective of explaining the phenomenon of Internet adoption by SMEs in the form of a model.

Case study research has been criticised for a perceived lack of scientific generalisability $[23]$ . However, it has been argued that the objective of case research is not to draw inferences about some larger population, but rather to generalise back to theory. This is achieved through replication logic $[27]$ . Replication logic is composed of two components: theoretical replication and literal replication. Within literal replication, cases are selected based on their similarities, and are chosen because they specifically support the theoretical explanation. Theoretical replication occurs when cases are chosen because they “produce contrasting results … for predictable reasons”.

The study, thus, aimed to adopt a multiple case strategy, in two stages.

Stage 1: four SMEs in the IT industry which had adopted the Internet.

Stage 2: three non-IT SMEs, of which two had adopted the Internet and one had chosen not to adopt the Internet.

The first stage was designed to produce a literal replication. The cases were all in the IT industry and were expected to provide similar conclusions. The second stage was designed to provide theoretical replication. The change of focus from the IT industry to non-IT firms meant it was possible to present theory that could be extended to a wider set of circumstances. One non-adopter provided a “critical case” to strengthen the theory already developed from the previous six adopting organisations $[23]$ .

The study used seven cases, which is within the range from four to 10 cases recommended by Eisenhardt. However, after each case, an evaluation with respect to the necessity for further case studies was undertaken. In accordance with Eisenhardt no new cases were added when theoretical saturation $^{1}$ had been reached.

The unit of analysis was the organisation. The definition of an SME for the current research was an independent organisation with 200 or less employees. It was also important that the organisations had adopted at least one of the following parts of the Internet: e-mail, Internet browsing, or a web site. With respect to the non-adopter, the organisation had made a conscious decision not to adopt the Internet.

The major method of data collection was through interviews with personnel who had been key to the decision regarding the adoption. Where possible, interviews were recorded and subsequently transcribed. Two interviews were not recorded so these were written up immediately after the interview. Triangulation of evidence was achieved by examining each web site and interviewing others who had been involved in the adoption decision.

Data analysis drew heavily on the “conceptually clustered matrix” approach suggested by Miles and Huberman $[17]$ . This approach brings together items that “belong together”. The analysis commenced with a ‘within case analysis’ that examined the transcripts from each organisation, noticing patterns and coding them accordingly. Once coded, the quotations were summarised into ideas and concepts and grouped into a table. The within-case analysis was followed by cross-case analysis, where three tables were created; one for the first four companies (the IT firms), a second for two non-IT firms, and a third table for the non-adopter firm.

## 4. The four IT case firms

Evidence from the first four firms in the IT industry was used to create a preliminary model, which was subsequently tested by evidence from three firms outside the IT industry.

The four firms consisted of a computer parts and systems retailer, a computer service company, a software development company, and a company with a variety of activities in the IT arena. In general, e-mail was adopted first, usually in conjunction with Internet browsing. Web site development and use followed much later in most organisations.

The firms ranged in size from three employees (including the owner) to about 80 employees. Their organisational structures were typically informal, except for the largest firm. All the CEOs were involved in the day-to-day running of the business. Furthermore, the CEO was often, but not always the advocator for the Internet in the organisation. For more detailed descriptions of the firms, see Appendix A.

As expected for firms in the IT industry, the CEOs were IT aware, skilled, and knowledgeable. The firms had knowledge of the technology. Typical views were “it did not cost (the organisation) anything to set up the Internet” and “it was set up by a staff member”. As a result of their IT skills, all four firms used employees to install e-mail systems into the organisation. However, web sites were found to be more complex and required more time and resources. Two firms created their own web site, although initially one had intended to outsource the design.

Staff attitudes towards the Internet were positive. Also, the speed and capacity of the existing networks in all the organisations were at the high end of computer technology, so Internet use did not require much additional hardware.

## 5. A preliminary Internet adoption model for SMEs

The first four cases were used to derive a preliminary model of Internet adoption by small firms. Each part of the Internet, in terms of email, web browsing and web site development, were considered separately. This within-case analysis showed that many factors were common to the various adoption decisions. Thus, these three components of Internet adoption are treated as one in much of the subsequent discussion, with some exceptions.

The cross-case analysis showed that the form of the model could be borrowed from the EDI Adoption model of Iacovou et al. The key adoption factors were therefore named: perceived benefits, organisational readiness, and external pressure. Each of these is discussed in the next sections.

## 5.1. Perceived benefits

Each part of the Internet, in terms of e-mail, web browsing, and web site development, were considered separately during the generation of the meta-matrices. However, these proved to be very similar, so the presentation of the results and subsequent discussion treats them as one. Based on reasons offered by the four IT firms, three types of perceived benefits fell out as natural categories: relative advantage, communication, and as a business tool.

Firms often gave examples of the relative advantage of the Internet compared to traditional methods of communication, such as telephones and faxes. The concept of global sources of information also formed part of the relative advantage of the Internet. Furthermore, an important aspect of the Internet was the advantage a web site had over traditional forms of advertising and retailing. For example:

“the online shopping facility on the web page removes the ‘glut’ of people in the shop who know exactly what they want”.

The communication aspect of the Internet was identified as having an advantage over traditional methods. Furthermore, the opportunity to present information on a web site was considered a good reason to adopt the Internet.

“the use of e-mail arose out of our need to develop stronger communication links with our distributors”.

The Internet was also viewed as a business tool. The technology was seen as something that should be used as an integral part of every business day to support business objectives. It was felt that the Internet should not be ignored. For example, cases A and D were early adopters that aimed to be at the leading edge. This is a popular reason to adopt a new technology and in particular to be an early adopter $[9]$ .

“the web page is just a resource, a tool for [the manager responsible for overseas recruitment] to use, to give our company a presence on the web”.

The above evidence provided strong support for including perceived benefits as a factor in the preliminary model of Internet adoption by small firms. As with many innovations, perceived benefits was often the characteristic that most influenced its adoption $[21]$ .

## 5.2. Organisational readiness

The four firms helped identify three forms of organisational readiness as highly relevant to the adoption of the Internet: (a) the level of IT knowledge among IT professionals; (b) the level of IT knowledge among non-IT professionals; and (c) the level of IT use within the organisation.

## 5.2.1. Level of IT knowledge among IT professionals

The case evidence supported the idea that the Internet was adopted by organisations that have the personnel who understand the technology $[2]$ . All four companies used employees to install the e-mail component of their Internet adoption, without the need to outsource. However, when it came to a web site it was apparent that it was more difficult to find an employee with the right set of skills to produce a professional web site for the organisation. Case B considered the outsourcing option, but resorted to internal sources to set up their web-site. Case C used an outside contractor to develop their web site, partly as a web designer made an attractive offer in exchange for goods. Also, employees at Case C were not sufficiently interested in learning HTML.

## 5.2.2. Level of IT knowledge among non-IT professionals

All four IT cases had an owner who was particularly cognisant of the Internet and its advantages. Often these owners were the champions of the adoption. This evidence is supported by literature that indicates an adopting organisation will have an innovation champion $[1]$ . The results also point to the champions of the Internet as being people with a reasonable amount of knowledge of the specific technology and technology in general. These champions were not necessarily IT professionals, rather they were people with an interest in technology.

## 5.2.3. Level of IT use in the organisation

All case firms had sophisticated networks of computers, requiring no additions for Internet adoption. Cases B and D had ISDN connections making Internet use and adoption more simple. This evidence supports the view in the literature that organisations who have high levels of information technology are more likely to adopt the Internet since technology is not the only consideration.

Thus, three aspects of organisational readiness were identified within the four IT cases: the level of IT knowledge among IT professionals; the level of IT knowledge among non-IT professionals; and the level of IT use within the organisation. The first and third were similar to the EDI adoption model. The case study evidence indicated an additional type of readiness: the level of IT knowledge among non-IT professionals.

Importantly, no significant support was found for other aspects of readiness, particularly organisational size and financial resources. Attewell claims that larger organisations tend to be more adoption oriented than the smaller organisations in the same industry. This contention was not supported in the current study as the larger case firms did not adopt the Internet before the smaller ones. Williams et al. noted that the size of the organisation is irrelevant due to the levelling effect of the technology. This indicated that firm size should not be part of the Internet adoption model.

Financial resources was also a part of Iacovou's organisational readiness for EDI adoption. However, financial resources was not raised as an issue during the interviews, possibly because most of their Internet adoption was accomplished in house with no appreciable expenses incurred.

## 5.3. External pressure

Unexpectedly, the four IT cases provided evidence of external pressure to adopt the Internet. For EDI, many large organisations have insisted on trading partners being EDI capable. The IT firms felt pressure to adopt the Internet from other Internet users, typically from customers or potential customers.

There was an expectation rather than a pressure that the organisation have an e-mail address and have a web site. The issue of credibility was raised as an important motivator for adopting the Internet; credibility in the eyes of potential employees, clients, and suppliers. Moreover, there was an expectation that the organisation was active on the Internet, including regular browsing and being as up to date as clients. This finding provides support for statements made by Klein and McCollum [16], that customers would begin to demand organisations to be on the Internet.

“A lot of people were ringing up for drivers [and saying] e-mail it to me, and you feel really stupid when you say well I still don’t actually have an e-mail address and you’re a computer shop for crying out loud”.

Case A felt significant pressure to use the Internet in addition to the expectations of customers. They sensed a risk that their customers would know more than them and this would result in lost respect. None of the other three IT firms mentioned this additional pressure during the research and this form of pressure has not been mentioned in other literature.

The literature also offers the actions of competitors as a pressure for firms to adopt an innovation $[13,16]$ . The cases failed to confirm this assertion as none of the four IT cases indicated that they had been influenced by the actions of their competitors.

## 6.1. The two adopters — Cases E and F

The three types of perceived benefit were easily confirmed by both organisations as their reasons for adoption were similar to those of the previous phase of the research. The evidence provided additional examples of the definitions. Importantly, the cases suggested that the previously identified perceived benefits of the Internet were not unique to the IT industry.

When it came to developing a web site both firms recognised the lack of in-house expertise and looked outside for assistance. Case F was able to run a competition to solve their outsourcing requirements. Case E took the conventional route and hired a web developer, after accomplishing the simple pieces of the Internet installation themselves, such as e-mail. This supports the contention that the Internet is an innovation that removes the need for internal IT experts $[19]$ .

Thus, the lack of IT expertise within the two organisations did not appear to affect the decision to adopt the Internet. These firms did not feel greatly disadvantaged by their lack of IT skills. This evidence, therefore failed to support the assertion that the level of IT sophistication held by IT professionals within the firm influences the adoption of technology, such as the Internet.

Both organisations had innovation champions who held influential roles in the organisation, which meant less resistance to the adoption of the Internet. There was a management awareness of the Internet and the potential benefits the Internet could offer the organisation. This supported the inclusion of IT knowledge by management in the model.

Both firms gave support for the inclusion of technological readiness in the model as both firms had adequate computer systems to run and access the Internet. Case F had recently upgraded their computer system to provide employees with faster access to the Internet to ensure the best results from the investment.

While Case E had felt little pressure to adopt the Internet from their external environment, Case F had felt considerable expectations from their clients. This supported the inclusion of external pressure within the model. As with the four IT cases, pressure from competitors was not in evidence, thus, provided further support for this factor to be excluded from the model.

## 6.2. The non-adopting site

The final part of the data collection stage involved one firm that had made a conscious decision not to adopt the Internet after trying a web site out for 3 months.

Case G had a relatively sophisticated network of computers, all of which contained modems. Thus, they were technologically ready for the Internet. The firm also had an owner who was particularly innovative and enthusiastic towards technology. The firm had many technologies both in place and intended for future use. Thus, the firm seemed organisationally ready for the Internet. However, their overall sentiment towards the Internet was that the Internet was “tailor-made for particular industries” and that it was not suitable for their industry. There was no feeling that the Internet could provide a better service than the traditional methods of doing business, namely the telephone and the fax. In particular, they felt the Internet failed to provide the opportunity to confirm a job:

“Clients want to know we have received their order and be assured we are going to turn up when and where they want us to”.

Based on a lack of response during the trial, the manager considered the Internet to be a waste of finances and not worth considering. However, on the rare occasion the owner needed to search the web or send an e-mail for his business, he would wait until he was at home, where he could use his personal account.

## 7. The final model

The final model is presented in Table 2. Although, it is reasonably similar to the previous model (Table 1), it has incorporated the observations made in the final three firms. Throughout the analysis, the definitions for the content of the Internet Adoption model were reconsidered. If further support was found in the non-IT firms for the particular definition it was retained in the model.

However, if no support for the inclusion of the definition was found then that aspect was excluded. These decisions were based on the concept of the second phase of the research acting as a replication of the model. The final version of the model is different from the preliminary model in the column headed “organisational readiness”.

The organisational readiness factor lost one definition in the final model. It was found that the level of IT knowledge in IT professionals was not an influence on the adoption of the Internet. This was shown by Cases E and F not permanently employing IT professionals. The IT work required for the adoption of the Internet was carried out by either contractors or people in the organisation with some IT knowledge.

## 8. Discussion

This study provided a clear understanding of the influences on Internet adoption by small firms. Internet adoption was influenced by three major factors: perceived benefits, organisational readiness, and external pressure. Adopting firms seek benefits, which can come in numerous forms. Typically, firms seek relative advantage. Firms are also influenced by pressure from Internet users outside the firm who want to communicate via the Internet. Firms need to be ready for the Internet, through non-IT managers being knowledgeable about the Internet, and by possessing appropriate hardware and software.

The model seems to adequately describe the factors that influenced decisions to adopt email and web site development. These decisions were influenced by all three factors, i.e. perceived benefits, organisational readiness and external pressure. However, there was little evidence to suggest that web browsing was influenced by external pressure. Thus, a subset of the model applies to web browsing.

The relative simplicity of the final model is a strength that should benefit other researchers, but also provides a more complete understanding. Many prior studies of the Internet and small firms have focused on benefits. The final model shows that three very different types of influence exist; all three influences are important and deserve both recognition and further study. The definitions for each of the factors (Table 2) are an important part of this better understanding and provide a basis for further study.

This study found the EDI adoption model helpful in examining the adoption of the Internet by SMEs. Many similarities were found between Internet adoption and EDI adoption. The major factors have similar titles, however, the definitions of each factor were quite different, particularly for organisational readiness and external pressure. This study found little support for Iacovou's financial resources. However, knowledge among non-IT professionals was identified as significant for Internet adoption. Also, instead of Iacovou's external pressures from both competitors and trading partners, Internet adoption is influenced by expectations of Internet users, who are typically customers. The transferable nature of the EDI adoption model is an addition to the body of knowledge as it had not previously been used to explain adoption of a technology other than EDI.

It is important to recognise the model's limitations. The experiences of seven firms formed the primary source of data for the model. It is possible that the focus on firms in one city in one country (New Zealand) is a source of bias. Also, much of the evidence was collected using interviews and interpreted by a team of researchers. The data analysis may have been influenced by researcher subjectivity. Importantly, the EDI adoption model was used as a base for the study. It may have influenced the interpretation of the case evidence. Further, it was not possible to interview more than one person in all of the firms due to the decision process being undertaken by only one person within the organisation. This reduced the opportunity to triangulate the information with subsequent interviews within the same organisation.

## 8.1. Implications

The findings have considerable significance for policy and for firms that depend on Internet commerce for revenue through products or services. Many small firms, even those with internal IT expertise, have taken their time in devising an Internet strategy. Managers need to be convinced of the benefits before they fully embrace the Internet, although benefits can take many forms, including improved company image. Importantly, it does not seem that a lack of financial resources are at the heart of this delay. Also, IT expertise to develop a web site can be bought in. Instead, small firms lack managers with sufficient knowledge of the Internet. To devise an Internet strategy, small firms need at least one manager to recognise the potential of the Internet and the threats and opportunities that it presents for their business. Although support agencies are running seminars to help raise awareness, the message takes time to be translated into an Internet strategy. Other parties can help, but managers are unlikely to rely on others to tell them how their firm should be run.

Even the IT firms within the study lacked sufficient knowledge to rapidly adopt the Internet. Thus, the great mass of small firms without such IT awareness can be expected to take even longer to adopt the Internet. This suggests that there are business opportunities to assist small firms to adopt Internet technologies. For example, Lockett and Brown $[15]$ indicate the potential role for firms to act as intermediaries to assist the formation of ‘eClusters’. They suggest that eClusters are a relatively new business model enabled by the Internet, where one or more intermediaries do much of the computing for a group of related small firms. The intermediaries save the small firms within the eCluster from numerous IT management tasks, including determining needs, selecting, implementing and running hardware and software. Thus the small firms can concentrate on their core activities rather than have to carry out IT management themselves.

The model also has implications for managers of small firms. Those that have not adopted the Internet must seek a business benefit. For example, they may pursue advantages over traditional methods of advertising, or communication with customers or suppliers, or disseminating product information. However, managers must consider the fit of the Internet to the firm's objectives. To some, the Internet is an exciting innovation, but it has to be suited to the industry and the firm's desired way of doing business. To a lesser extent, this part of the model also has relevance to firms that are already using the Internet. Different types of benefits can be sought from the Internet, so the model may suggest additional benefits to pursue.

The external pressure factor provides evidence that many Internet users expect small firms to be on the Internet. Small firms may be able to exploit this, particularly if competitors have not.

Furthermore, a firm must be ready for the Internet. This may require the firm to acquire new technology, e.g. to upgrade their computers. Importantly, at least one manager within a small firm to be sufficiently aware of the potential of the Internet and the threats and opportunities that it presents for their business. Managers should seek advice, but they must decide the best ways for their firm to exploit the Internet. It is more important to have an Internet savvy manager than to have an IT expert or IT department.

## 8.2. Opportunities for future research

The model could be tested and extended with SMEs in other industries or countries. Another opportunity to test the model is to focus on the decision not to adopt the Internet. Alternative research approaches could use the model to help improve our understanding of Internet adoption by small firms. For example, in-depth longitudinal case studies of individual firms could follow the process from the decision to adopt the Internet, through to implementation. Each component of the model could be examined. Such studies could focus on individual factors within the model. For example, a study of organisational readiness could determine how a firm reaches the required level of IT use and IT knowledge for Internet adoption. Alternatively, a survey could determine the importance of each factor and develop a predictive model. Such a model may have to be industry specific.

The current study indicated that the EDI adoption model was transferable to other technologies, although some definitions were different. Future research could determine if the general form of the EDI adoption model was applicable to technologies other than EDI and the Internet.

## 9. Conclusions

The study used evidence from seven small firms to determine their reasons for Internet adoption. Evidence from four IT firms was used to create a preliminary model. Three non-IT firms were then examined to refine the preliminary model. The study concluded that three factors significantly effect Internet adoption by small firms: perceived benefits, organisational readiness, and external pressure. These factors were consistent across the different Internet innovations of email, web browsing, and having a web site. The final model was similar in form to the EDI adoption model. However, some significant differences were identified between Internet adoption and EDI adoption, particularly for the definitions of organisational readiness and external pressure.

The resulting model has added substantially to the understanding of the decision by small firms to adopt the Internet. Although, many factors influence Internet adoption, the small firm has to be prepared. The development of an Internet strategy takes time and it seems likely that new methods of support could greatly assist this complex process.

## Appendix A. The seven case firms

Case A was in the computer servicing industry with four employees. The focus of the business was designing, installing, and servicing computer systems for companies of varying sizes. The firm had only two competitors in the local market. Their philosophy towards technology was that they must be proactive rather than reactive to maintain their position in the industry. Being in the computer industry itself, the staff had maintained a high level of computer awareness and literacy. In addition, they were experienced on the Internet and its uses. The firm used the Internet for e-mail and Internet browsing only.

Case B was a software development company, had an annual turnover of over US\$ 10 million, and employed approximately 50 staff. The company's customers were large corporations and small banks located both domestically and internationally. A major, international accounting firm was mostly responsible for sales and distribution of the product. Staff competency in terms of using the Internet was high. The company had an Internet network of computers running Windows NT and was connected to the Internet via an ISDN connection. The firm used the Internet for e-mail, Internet browsing and had the beginnings of a web site.

Case C described itself as a computer wholesaler/retailer, specialising in the provision of computer components. There were five employees in addition to the owner, and an annual turnover of between US\$ 5 and 10 million. Their customers were the general public with the occasional small business. The company had a number of competitors with respect to computer systems, but only a few for the parts division of the business, which was the area of the business that generated most revenue. All computers were networked to run the firm's accounting system, but only two had Internet connections. All staff were, to varying degrees, competent with computers and their use. The firm had a web site and also used the Internet for e-mail and browsing.

Case D had undergone many changes in its focus over its life. It began as a computer training facility, targeting the home user. From there it expanded to encompass database, web site and web instrument development. It had recently launched a web-based insurance brokerage package. Annual turnover was around US\$ 100 000 with three employees. A client, later to become a partner, produced an intriguing database problem that ultimately led the company to develop the web-based brokerage package. The company's computers were mainly on a UNIX network, although one was a Windows NT machine. The network was connected to the Internet service provider via an ISDN connection. To varying degrees, all members of this company were competent in the use of computers and the Internet. The firm had e-mail, web sites and web browsing.

Case E were in the clothing industry, where they designed, manufactured and retailed. It employed 80 people, 50 in the manufacturing area and 30 in retail. It had an annual turnover of between US\$ 5 and 10 million. The company created and sold its own clothing line as well as carrying out contracts for its clients. The main market for its retail product were tourists. The company had several computers used for various purposes. Two computers had Internet access — the Macintosh in the art department and a PC accessed by the organisation's accountant. Access was by dial-up only. The competency of staff with computers varied greatly; from no skill at all to the ability to install and maintain software. The firm used the Internet mainly for e-mail, although some browsing was done and a web site was under development.

Case F was in the communication and entertainment industry and considered music, information, entertainment, and advertising as its products. It had recently changed its status from a privately owned organisation to a public one. Annual turnover exceeded US\$ 10 million. The corporation employed 200 people, with Case F employing approximately 25 to 30 people. They ran two computer networks, one for on-air work and another network for general use which was connected to the Internet. This system had recently undergone a serious upgrade to enable easier Internet access. There were three PCs that could access the Internet — previously there was only one. Most personnel were computer literate, with skill levels ranging from simple word processing to more advanced usage. Internet literacy in the firm was limited to those who needed to use the Internet. The firm used the Internet largely for browsing, e-mail, and for providing information through their web site.

Case G was a transport service organisation with approximately 90 employees. Competition was fierce in their industry, with 13 similar companies operating in the local area. The company prided itself on its quality of service, a fact highlighted by the comment “we built this company on service”. It relied heavily on personal contact with clients. The company had been innovative with technology, including a computerised dispatch system. Computer users were highly literate with the technology. The firm made a conscious decision not to adopt the Internet after trialing a web site for a period of three months. The Internet was seen to be a technology that was unsuitable for their particular industry due to the personal nature required to be successful in the industry.
