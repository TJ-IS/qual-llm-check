# Collaborative assessment in education: an application of a fuzzy GSS

Ron C.W. Kwok $^{a,*}$ , Jian Ma $^{b}$ , Doug Vogel $^{b}$ , Duanning Zhou $^{c}$

$^{a}$ School of Management, State University of New York at Binghamton, P.O. Box 6000, Binghamton, NY 13902-6000, USA

$^{b}$ Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, Kowloon, Hong Kong

$^{c}$ Accounting and Information Systems Department, Eastern Washington University, Spokane, WA 99202-1660, USA

Received 5 November 1999; received in revised form 3 February 2001; accepted 28 February 2001

## Abstract

Educators are increasingly pressed to deal effectively and efficiently with multiple criteria evaluation in a variety of circumstances as students become more actively involved in assessment. This paper demonstrates that a “fuzzy set” collaborative assessment approach implemented with a group support system (GSS) can be successfully applied in university classroom contexts. Student learning was significantly enhanced, as was satisfaction, when a collaborative assessment approach was taken. This approach has broad-based applicability in dealing with assessment complexity encountered in a wide variety of business and government contexts, as well as educational institutions. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Assessment; Business education; Fuzzy set; Technology supported learning; Group support systems

## 1. Introduction

The need for assessment is pervasive in organizations. All types and sizes are increasingly trying to integrate multiple criteria in arriving at a conclusion with regard to the impact of various activities. Among these are the evaluation of education and training programs. Such programs are offered in a variety of forms on numerous topics from different sources. Organizations are continually challenged effectively and efficiently to evaluate the various programs to ascertain which deserve corporate support: not only to compare and contrast offerings but also to define ways in which such programs could be improved to serve the needs of the organization and its constituents better.

Unfortunately, few approaches have been demonstrated that effectively and efficiently integrate opinions from multiple stakeholder groups in the assessment processes. Evaluators are often left with varied bits and pieces of hard data and other evidence that somehow must be integrated to arrive at a sensible and defensible conclusion. The result is often a compromise, at best, or capitulation, at worst, as evaluators recognize their inability to arrive at a conclusion that solidly represents the needs of the institution and its stakeholders. Little attention has been given to the assessment of multi-stakeholders, multi-criteria assessment issues, and the development of robust solutions.

Academic institutions are a form of organization within which assessment is commonplace. Instructors assess textbooks and students. Students assess courses and instructors. Instructors assess peers striving for tenure. Institutions assess programs and curriculum. Regents and legislatures assess institutions. Assessment often involves a variety of stakeholders, both internal and external. The problem becomes particularly acute when the potential of legal sanctions are present. As noted in the Carnegie Report on Scholarship Assessed [13], institutions frequently encounter situations where evidence (or lack thereof) is being outweighed by a concern for litigation.

Here, we report on the development and application of a “fuzzy set” approach to collaborative assessment; it integrates multiple criteria from multiple stakeholders. Results from student groups are presented to demonstrate how a group support system (GSS) combined with fuzzy set theory in a problem based learning context can be successful.

## 2. Background

Assessment is difficult, especially when embracing broad-based stakeholder input. It is not impossible, however, and thus represents a challenge. At a high level, it has uniformly applicable constructs. For example, the Carnegie Institute of Teaching developed standards that cut across a variety of assessment environments, e.g. teaching, research, and service as well as institutions and reward systems. These standards are: clear goals, adequate preparation, appropriate methods, significant results, effective presentation, and reflective critique. Thus, at a high level, assessment approaches and corresponding criteria can be developed with the potential to be applied to a wide range of institutions and organization.

Assessment of teaching using multiple criteria is commonplace. In the Carnegie survey, teaching assessment was accomplished using one or more methods of evaluation: student evaluation of classroom teaching (98%), self-evaluation or personal statement (82%), peer review of materials (62%), peer review of classroom teaching (58%), evidence of continuing student interest (34%), alumni opinions (31%), student evaluation of advising (24%), evidence of student achievement (24%), evidence of the impact of teaching on research (15%), and evidence of the impact of teaching on applied scholarship (14%).

Assessment of learning from a student perspective has received considerably less attention and presents more challenges. Assessment of student learning has often been based on teacher assigned grades associated with various in-class and out-of-class activities; e.g. class participation, exams, homework, and projects. Students are rarely given the opportunity to provide input into the process, in spite of evidence that student involvement is effective. Student involvement has extended to interaction in design and course creation. Banta et al. [3] note that the aim of assessment over time is to involve people from across the educational community and that “Assessment’s questions cannot be fully addressed without participation by students”.

Diamond [11] notes the feasibility and importance of weaving assessment into the design of courses and curricula. Assessment can become part of the learning process and is not seen as a burden or overhead. Students want to have a definite and consistent voice about what goes on. They want to give input and not just answer a few questions at the end of the course or when they graduate. In some cases, students have been involved in working with faculty on every phase of assessment, from planning and selecting methods to interpreting findings and suggesting improvements.

As we move from behavioral observation-based learning theories towards a more cognitive orientation with a constructivist approach to instructional design, learning assessment presents special challenges $[22]$ . A constructivist approach focuses on learning through posing problems. For example, in a problem-based learning situation $[9]$ , students are exposed to a practical problem in the form of a project. They are required to work in groups resulting in cooperative learning (e.g. $[20]$ ). Students are encouraged to take a deep approach to learning $[26]$ within which they attempt to relate ideas and construct their own meaning. Such an approach exemplifies the type of learning that employers hope students demonstrate on the job. Alternative assessment methods including student portfolios, product evaluation, open-ended questions, mini-essays, modified essay questions, laboratory tests, and teacher observation are commonplace $[12]$ .

The introduction of technology in the creation of learning environments further complicates the assessment process. Increasingly, academic and business organizations are turning to computer technology as a way to extend the traditional classroom environment in dimensions of time and space to create virtual classrooms [1]. In an ideal case, a virtual classroom provides a rich variety of stimuli including access to people as well as information and tools to facilitate communication between students and to the instructor [31]. A virtual classroom also tends to have both synchronous and asynchronous components — some within a fixed on-campus facility and others relying on connectivity from work or home where students might have only a single phone line. Extensive virtual classroom development is currently under way in a number of global academic and organizational settings [17,34].

Virtual classrooms stretch traditional assessment approaches, especially in the context of constructivist approaches $[21]$ . In a full-blown implementation, support for multiple learning styles and a wide variety of content and process attributes characterize the learning environment. Technology provides degrees of freedom and equifinality in educational approaches that make assessment and determination of cause and effect especially difficult. On the other hand, technology provides support to facilitate complex assessment demands.

## 3. Fuzzy set collaborative assessment approach

Collaborative assessment is a multiple attribute decision making (MADM) problem. In it, lecturers and students are encouraged to participate in the process. It involves discussion and negotiation of methods between students and lecturers $[16]$ . It also involves the contribution to and agreement on the assessment criteria according to a list of attributes; e.g. course objectives and industrial requirements.

MADM differs from multiple objective decision making (MODM) in the sense that it deals with continuous decision space, primarily modeling by mathematical programming with several objective functions. Hwang and Yoon [18] classified a group of over 10 methods according to the type and salient features of information received from the decision makers. These MADM methods may be compensatory or non-compensatory, depending whether tradeoffs among attributes are permitted. Non-compensatory MADM methods include the “conjunctive” method and “disjunctive” methods, the “lexicographic” method, and “elimination by aspects”. The conjunctive method considers that each alternative is acceptable as long as the corresponding attributes meet the minimum cutoffs. Compensatory MADM methods include simple additive weighting (SAW), TOPSIS, ELECTRE, median ranking method, and analytic hierarchy process (AHP) [37].

These methods tend to reflect an ideal decision making environment in which decision makers can rationally consider all aspects of the problem, think through them, get precise needed information, and then provide a consensus solution. However, in collaborative assessment, because of incomplete and uncertain information, as well as lack of common interpretation and different opinions, it is difficult to obtain an “optimal” solution. In the assessment process, lecturers and students frequently confront human subjective judgment with vagueness. For example, they often use “good”, “excellent”, “poor” to evaluate their quality of learning outcomes. But what do these words really mean? The terms do not constitute well-defined measures.

Fuzzy set theory [38] was developed to solve problems in which descriptions of activities and observations were imprecise, vague, and uncertain. A fuzzy set is a class of objects with a continuum of membership. A membership function, which assigns to each object a grade of membership, is associated with the fuzzy set. Usually, the membership grades are in probabilities [0, 1]. When the grade of membership for an object is one, it means that the object is absolutely in that set. When the grade of membership is zero, it means that the object is absolutely not in that set. Borderline cases are assigned values in between.

Fuzzy set theory was applied in decision making by Bellman and Zadeh in 1970 [4]. They note that “Much of the decision making in the real world takes place in an environment in which the goals, the constraints and the consequences of possible actions are not known precisely” and fuzzy set theory can be used to deal with imprecision in decision making. Numerous publications (e.g. [7,8,10,14,25,36,41]) have discussed fuzzy decision making. In recent papers [39,40], Zadeh emphasizes that information granulation is one of three concepts of human cognition, and that, fuzzy information granulation underlines the remarkable human ability to make rational decisions in an environment of imprecision, partial knowledge, partial certainty, and partial truth.

Collaborative assessment inevitably involves human thinking and human subjective judgment. Therefore, combining present MADM methods with fuzzy set theory can better and flexibly reflect the natural decision making process. Research at City University of Hong Kong (CityU) has resulted in the development and evaluation of a fuzzy set approach to collaborative assessment that is particularly appropriate for constructivist approaches to instructional design in student-centered learning environments. Assessment is particularly important in problem based learning (PBL). It has a strong influence on students' approaches to learning and to outcomes.

In order to provide an open and fair method of collaborative assessment on the outcomes of PBL, a fuzzy GSS was developed $[24]$ as a prototype of GSS. The system makes use of mathematical decision methods based on fuzzy set theory for collecting assessment criteria and determining corresponding weights from students and instructors $[23]$ . The assessment is initiated with a brainstormed set of criteria, developed through an iterative process with faculty involvement (both the instructor in charge of the course and colleagues teaching similar courses) with student interaction. Based on the set of criteria gathered through group interaction, students and instructors vote for their preferred assessment criteria and corresponding weights. The criteria are then used to evaluate the students' learning outcomes on a fuzzy grading scale. For more mathematical details, see Appendix A.

## 4. Application results

The fuzzy set collaborative assessment approach was applied to classes using problem-based learning at our University. In particular, data was gathered from 203 students with a project assignment in a course of Client–Server and Distributed Information Systems in the Department of Information Systems. Students were first exposed to the practical problem of administrating research projects. They were required to work in groups of three to 4 to develop a client–server database system for research administration (a detailed description of requirements on project assessment can be found in Appendix B). In order to encourage students to participate in the whole process, students were invited to contribute their opinions and evaluate their own learning in a collaborative setting. The 203 subjects were randomly formed into four treatment groups as follows:

1. not using a collaborative assessment approach (traditional assessment setting) (61 subjects);

2. using a collaborative assessment approach without fuzzy GSS support (42 subjects);

3. using a collaborative assessment approach with the help of a two hour same-time/same-place fuzzy GSS (41 subjects);

4. using a collaborative assessment approach with one week of different-time/different-place Internet-based fuzzy GSS support (59 subjects).

These treatments were to test salient aspects of the fuzzy GSS's impact on the collaborative assessment process. Our primary focus was on groups using the collaborative assessment process with or without fuzzy GSS support in a same-time/same-place meeting format. A variety of data were gathered in an effort to determine the impact of the fuzzy GSS support. Participants of the non-fuzzy GSS supported groups completed the procedures of collaborative assessment, given in Appendix A, in a traditional face-to-face setting, while participants of the fuzzy GSS supported groups completed the procedures with support of an Internet-based GSS with the following functions: (1) brainstorming, (2) voting, and (3) weighting. The fuzzy GSS also supported anonymity in order to enable the students to voice their opinion and preferences freely.

After completing the collaborative assessment task, students were interviewed separately for 20 min and answers were taped. During the interview, each student was asked to illustrate their level of consensus and satisfaction of the project assessment scheme of his or her group. The student was also required to explain the assessment scheme and describe his or her perception of it. At the end of the semester, the academic results of the project deliverable and learning approach of each student group were obtained.

Assessment of learning outcomes was evaluated in terms of: academic results of students' project deliverables and demonstrated degree of deep approaches to learning. Understanding of the learning task was determined from the taped interviews. The level of consensus and satisfaction was ascertained. Results are summarized in Table 1.

## 4.1. Assessment of learning outcomes

Comparison of project deliverable scores revealed that the average score of the fuzzy GSS-supported group was higher than the non-fuzzy GSS-supported group $P = 0.036$ based on a 100 point grading scale. Similarly, the fuzzy GSS-supported group had higher scores in deep approaches to learning than the non-fuzzy GSS-supported group $P = 0.024$ as measured by the study process questionnaire (SPQ) [5]. Biggs identified deep approaches to learning as indicative of more thoughtful self-directed problem-based learning and contrasted this with surface learning typified by memorization and regurgitation of traditional material.

SPQ is a self-reporting questionnaire, in which students are asked to rate themselves on a 5-point Likert-type scale for 42 items addressing learning motives and strategies. The reliability and validity of the SPQ was verified in a number of studies [35]. Each item of SPQ is a self-report statement of a motive or a strategy, e.g. “I find that studying gives me a feeling of deep personal satisfaction” corresponds to deep motive; and “While I am studying, I think of real life situations to which the material that I am learning would be useful” relates to deep strategy. The respondents rated themselves on a 5-point Likert-type scale, from 5 (this item is always or almost always true of me) to 1 (this item is never or only rarely true of me). The related item-ratings were added up for the deep motive or strategy. The ratings of the motive and strategy were then added up for deep approach to learning. The range of scores for deep approach to learning is from 14 to 70. Measurement of deep approaches to learning conducted prior to the treatment showed no significant differences between groups.

The audio taped interviews were analyzed by two different instructors of the course to determine levels of understanding of the learning task. The response of each student was assigned a score from 1 to 5, according to the structure of the observed learning outcome (SOLO) taxonomy used frequently in Hong Kong contexts to measure knowledge acquisition [6,19]. This has five structural levels of the observed learning outcome.

1. Prestructural — based on irrelevant information;

2. Unistructural — focused on one relevant aspect;

3. Multistructural — based on a number of unconnected relevant information;

4. Relational — based on interrelated relevant information with meaningful interpretation and

5. Extended abstract — not only based on logical and related relevant information but also beyond demand of the questions.

In order to become familiarized with the scoring scheme and maximize inter-rater reliability, the two instructors performed a pilot test with 10 sets of audio data. Afterwards, they separately interpreted the audio data and graded the students' responses. If the scores of the two did not match, the instructors were asked to redo the grading without being told each other's scores. If, after the second round, the two scores still did not match, the case was rejected: only 2.56% of the cases were rejected. The results of the statistical analysis show that the average score of the fuzzy GSS-supported group is significantly higher (P = 0.022) than the average score of the control group.

## 4.2. Consensus and satisfaction

Each student was asked to rate his or her consensus level on the assessment criteria using a 6-point Likert-type scale (strongly disagree = 1, quite disagree = 2, slightly disagree = 3, slightly agree = 4, quite agree = 5, strongly agree = 6). The means and standard deviations for the level of perceived consensus of the selected assessment scheme of the fuzzy GSS-supported and the non-fuzzy GSS-supported groups were obtained. The mean score of the consensus level of the fuzzy GSS-supported group was not significantly different from the mean score of the control group, as determined by a t-test at the 5% significance level.

In order to measure the level of satisfaction of the selected assessment scheme, each student was required to rate his or her satisfaction level using a 6-point Likert-type scale (strongly dissatisfactory = 1, quite dissatisfactory = 2, slightly dissatisfactory = 3, slightly satisfactory = 4, quite satisfactory = 5, strongly satisfactory = 6). The means and standard deviations for the level of satisfaction of the selected assessment scheme of the fuzzy GSS-supported and the non-fuzzy GSS-supported groups were obtained. The mean score of the satisfaction level of the fuzzy GSS-supported group was significantly higher (P = 0.032).

## 5. Discussion

Although the focus of this study was on groups using the collaborative assessment process with or without fuzzy GSS support in a same-time/same-place meeting format, additional data was collected from groups not using a collaborative assessment approach (traditional assessment setting) and using a collaborative assessment approach in a geographically and temporally distributed fashion. These data were analyzed to differentiate whether the GSS effect was a function of support for group interaction or application of the fuzzy set approach to collaborative assessment. We concluded that the primary value of the group support aspect is to enable data to be collected efficiently and shared effectively to support decision. This is not surprising.

In terms of learning assessment, the fuzzy GSS same-time/same-place treatment outperformed the others, and was more satisfied with the choice of assessment criteria. Groups that did not use a collaborative assessment approach, or used one without fuzzy GSS, or used the distributed fuzzy GSS failed to show a deeper approach to learning. We feel that the fuzzy GSS was able to sustain a structured approach that complemented the process by which students interacted in face-to-face problem based learning, from which they demonstrated a deeper approach to learning.

This suggests that the use of collaborative assessment when supported by fuzzy GSS technology is beneficial and well received and aligns with similar organizational experiences of GSS. Numerous laboratory and field studies (e.g. [15,27,28,32,33]) have demonstrated that GSS enhances problem solving and results in high levels of participant satisfaction. This indicates to us that this collaborative assessment approach can be successfully applied in a variety of other educational contexts. The educational problems faced in business and government are very similar to those in university environments. In our approach, it is especially important to involve multiple criteria and multiple stakeholders. It is also important, however, to make the collaborative assessment process efficient and fluid. We feel that the approach has demonstrated success that warrants attention and extension to broader domains. It is also important, however, to consider aspects of innovation diffusion. As noted by one assessment professional, “unless the details are worked out so that organizations and individuals can use a process easily, I doubt that many will be interested”. Towards that end, it becomes essential that the fuzzy set approach be evaluated to show that it is sufficiently robust and can be applied efficiently without requiring extensive expertise or continual rework to fit a particular situation.

## 6. Conclusion

The need for assessment is pervasive in organizations and especially integral to educational activities. Here, we have reported on the development and successful application of a fuzzy GSS approach to a collaborative assessment that integrates multiple criteria from multiple stakeholders. The fuzzy GSS was tested with student groups engaged in problem based learning who demonstrated a deeper approach to learning than would otherwise normally occur. The results are extremely encouraging with respect to broader organizational application. Different programs need to be assessed and compared. Organizations of all types and sizes are increasingly trying to integrate multiple criteria in effectively and efficiently evaluating programs to ascertain which deserve support.

## Appendix A. Procedures of fuzzy set collaborative assessment

The fuzzy set collaborative assessment can be organized into the following four stages.

## A.1. Generation of basic assessment criteria

Students are first exposed to the course objectives (e.g. components for knowledge, skills and attitudes) and industry expectation for development of an information system. Together with the instructors, they are invited to brainstorm and contribute the initial basic assessment criteria set as shown in Fig. 1.

## A.2. Selection of assessment criteria

Let $C = \{c_{1}, \ldots, c_{m}\}$ represents the basic assessment criteria set. We assign different rates $W = \{w_{1}, \cdots, w_{n}\}$ to n individual decision makers (i.e. individual students and instructors) depending on their roles in the assessment task, where

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} = 1
$$

Thus, the steps for selecting the assessment criteria can be shown as follows.

Step 1: Define a set of linguistic terms that contains the various degrees of preference the decision makers require. For example, the set {very important, important, not very important, not important}, are depicted by the following fuzzy numbers as shown in Fig. 2. All values of the linguistics terms are between 0 and 1.

Step 2: Assign linguistic term to every criterion $c_j$ ( $j = 1, \ldots, m$ ). An individual decision maker's preference can be denoted by a vector. Then the $n$ individual decision makers' preference form the matrix $(v_{ij})$ ( $i = 1, \ldots, n; j = 1, \ldots, m$ ).

Step 3: Aggregate the matrix $(v_{ij})$ ( $i = 1, \cdots, n; j = 1, \ldots, m$ ) into the group selection vector $V = (v_{1}, \ldots, v_{m})$

$$
\begin{array}{l} v _ {i} = w _ {1} \cdot v _ {1 i} \oplus w _ {2} \cdot v _ {2 i} \oplus \dots \oplus w _ {n} \cdot v _ {n i}), \\ i = 1, \ldots , m \end{array}
$$

where the method for aggregation is similar to that of Baas and Kwakernaak [2].

Step 4: Rank the vector V. About top seven criteria, for convenience, also denoted as $C = \{c_{1}, \ldots, c_{n}\}$ , are then selected as final agreed assessment criteria.

## A.3. Determination of the assessment criteria weights

Analytic hierarchy process (AHP) method [29,30] is used to determine the corresponding assessment criteria weights according to different importance of the assessment criteria. The quantified judgments on pairs of criteria $c_{i}, c_{j}$ are represented by a $n \times n$ matrix $A = [a_{ij}], (i, j = 1, \ldots, n)$ . The matrix is a reciprocal matrix $(a_{ij} = 1/a_{ji})$ with all $a_{ij}$ being positive. The ratio $a_{ij}$ represents the relative importance of $c_{i}$ over $c_{j}$ . The comparison scale ranges from 1 to 9, each representing the concepts of — 1: equally important; 3: weakly more important; 5: strongly more important; 7: demonstratively more important; 9: absolutely more important; 2, 4, 6, and 8 are intermediate values between adjacent judgments. For example, $a_{ij} = 5$ means $c_{i}$ is strongly more important than $c_{j}$ . Thus, the weights $W = \{w_{1}, \ldots, w_{n}\}$ on the criteria $C = \{c_{1}, \ldots, c_{n}\}$ can be computed according to the paired comparison matrix contributed by the students and instructors in the class.

## A.4. Fuzzy grading system

Self-assessment, peer assessment and group assessment are commonly used in student centered learning environment. Assume there are t participants of instructors and students, denoted as $P = \{p_{1}, \ldots, p_{t}\}$ , involved in the evaluation process. Different rates, denoted as $R = \{r_{1}, \ldots, r_{t}\}$ , are assigned to the individual participants depending on their roles in the assessment task, where

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {t} r _ {i} = 1
$$

In this paper, the evaluation results on projects are marked using a 100-point scale. For example, 80 indicates the learning outcomes to be 80% satisfactory according to the commonly agreed criteria. Thus, the evaluation results given by participant $p_{i}$ form a vector $(m_{i1},\ldots,m_{in})$ , where each $m_{ij}\in[0,100]$ , $(j=1,\ldots,n)$ is the assigned mark with reference to the criterion $c_{j}$ .

For t participants involved in the evaluation process, an evaluation matrix $M = (m_{ij})$ ( $i = 1, \ldots, t; j = 1, \ldots, n$ ) is formed. Thus, the evaluation results can be obtained by

$$
\begin{array}{c} M ^ {\prime} = (r _ {1}, r _ {2}, \ldots , r _ {t}) \left( \begin{array}{c} m _ {1 1}, m _ {1 2}, \ldots , m _ {1 n} \\ m _ {2 1}, m _ {2 2}, \ldots , m _ {2 n} \\ \vdots \\ m _ {t 1}, m _ {t 2}, \ldots , m _ {t n} \end{array} \right) \\ = (m _ {1} ^ {\prime}, \ldots , m _ {n} ^ {\prime}) \end{array}
$$

where $m_{i}^{\prime}=r_{1}*m_{1i}+r_{2}*m_{2i}+\cdots+r_{t}*m_{ti}$ and “\*”, “+” are the normal mathematical operators for multiplication and addition, respectively.

In the case of one lecturer assessing students' project according to the agreed assessment criteria, a vector of evaluation results $M' = (m_1', \ldots, m_n')$ is given.

Letter grades are commonly used to reflect the students' performance in the University. Let letter grades $G = (g_1, \ldots, g_d)$ . Membership functions are defined to transform mark grades into letter grades. The fuzzy letter grade $g_i$ can be represented by a membership function $\mu_{g_i} (i = 1, \ldots, d)$ . Hence, given a 100-point scale mark $m', \mu_{g_i}(m')$ represents the degree that the mark $m'$ belongs to the letter grade $g_i$ .

A fuzzy mapping can be defined which combines the membership functions together to form a comprehensive letter grade

$$
f: c _ {i} \rightarrow (\mu_ {g _ {1}} (m _ {i} ^ {\prime}), \mu_ {g _ {2}} (m _ {i} ^ {\prime}), \dots , \mu_ {g _ {d}} (m _ {i} ^ {\prime}))
$$

where $m_{i}^{\prime}$ is the mark given to $c_{i}, m_{i}^{\prime} \in [0, 100]$ , $i = 1, \ldots, n$ . With the membership functions $\mu_{g_{1}}, \ldots, \mu_{g_{d}}$ , the fuzzy mapping f gives each mark $m_{i}^{\prime}$ the degree which belongs to the letter grades $g_{1}, \ldots, g_{d}$ , respectively.

For simplicity, we use $(e_{i1},\ldots,e_{id})$ to represent $(\mu_{g_{1}}(m_{i}^{\prime}),\ldots,\mu_{g_{d}}(m_{i}^{\prime}))$ . Thus, the fuzzy evaluation matrix E is expressed as $(e_{ij})$ $(i=1,2,\ldots,n; j=1,2,\ldots,d)$ .

The final letter grade of the students' learning outcomes can be determined by the vector $Y$

$$
\begin{array}{l} Y = W E = (w _ {1}, w _ {2}, \dots , w _ {n}) \left( \begin{array}{c} e _ {1 1}, e _ {1 2}, \dots , e _ {1 d} \\ e _ {2 1}, e _ {2 2}, \dots , e _ {2 d} \\ \vdots \\ e _ {n 1}, e _ {n 2}, \dots , e _ {n d} \end{array} \right) \\ = (y _ {1}, y _ {2}, \dots , y _ {d}) \end{array}
$$

where $y_{j} = (w_{1} \cdot e_{1j}) \oplus (w_{2} \cdot e_{2j}) \oplus \cdots \oplus (w_{n} \cdot e_{nj})$ and “.”, “⊕” are defined as

$$
a \cdot b: c = a \cdot b = a b
$$

$$
a \oplus b: c = a \oplus b = \min \{1, a + b \}
$$

According to the principles of fuzzy classification, we have $y_{i} = \max(y_{1}, y_{2}, \ldots, y_{d})$ . Thus, the corresponding letter grade $g_{i}$ is the students' final grade.

## Appendix B. Requirements on project assessment

## B.1. Aims and objectives of the project

The aim of the project is to develop IS professionals in the field of distributed and client–server systems, through the application of knowledge and skills provided and not provided by the module to a specific business problem. In undertaking the project the student should demonstrate a clear grasp of the chosen subject matter, a full understanding of the principles being applied, and the ability to manage, evaluate and present the project in a coherent and precise manner.

B.2. On completion of the project the students should be able to

1. Plan, schedule, monitor and control a substantial piece of project work.

2. Apply methods, tools and techniques learned during or outside the course to analyze the required problem area.

3. Design and implement the IS solution for the required problem area emphasized on distributed and client–server system, and user access security.

4. Communicate effectively with all the stakeholders of the project and present clearly the project outcomes, orally and in writing.

5. Develop their self-critical skills by getting them to consider how the project should be assessed.

## B.3. Deliverables

1. The conceptual database schema for the application using entity–relationship methods.

2. The conversion of the conceptual database schema into normalized relations. The design of the global and local schema for different user groups (identify the different groups of users for the system).

3. A suitable network layout which shows how the computing devices at various sites will be interconnected and managed. (Suppose that there is no budget limit and we are flexible to switch to other systems if they are available, and justify your decision with reference to the decision factors you propose.)

4. A report which indicates where the data are to be stored so as to allow users to retrieve information efficiently.

5. Implementation of your database design using SQL-Server (assume that data are distributed).

6. Implementation of the user interfaces of the client application for student users using MS-Access or Visual Basic.

7. Documentation of the other evidences which show how well your group has achieved the assessment criteria (maximum two pages for each documentation).
