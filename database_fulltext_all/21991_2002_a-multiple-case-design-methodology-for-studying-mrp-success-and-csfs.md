# A multiple-case design methodology for studying MRP success and CSFs

James S.K. Ang<sup>a,\*</sup>, Chee-Chuong Sum<sup>a</sup>, Lei-Noy Yeo<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Decision Sciences, National University of Singapore, 10 Kent Ridge Crescent, Singapore 119260, Singapore <sup>b</sup>Keppel Bank of Singapore, Singapore, Singapore

Accepted 23 March 2001

## Abstract

We used a multiple-case design to study materials requirements planning (MRP) implementation outcome in 10 manufacturing companies in Singapore. Using a two-phased data collection approach (pre-interview questionnaires and personal interviews), we sought to develop a comprehensive and operationally acceptable measure of MRP success. Our measure consists of two linked components. They are a satisfaction score (a quantitative measure) and a complementary measure based on comments from the interviewees regarding the level of usage and acceptance of the system. We also extended and consolidated a seven-factor critical success factor (CSF) framework using this methodology. CSFs are important, but knowing the linkages between them is even more important, because these linkages tell us which CSFs to emphasize at various stages of the project. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Materials requirements planning (MRP); Critical success factors (CSFs); Multiple-case design; MRP implementation outcome

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. Motivation of research

It is common practice to combine weighted measures of attributes into overall quality aggregates and then use these for decision making (e.g. [14,15]). For example, Federal Express uses an aggregate of quality attributes, such as missed packages pickups, lost packages, and complaints of customers to evaluate its performance and as a basis for management compensation [7]. However, most MRP studies measured success with single item measures such as degree of satisfaction with the system [22,31] or improved performance [6,9]. Single item measures cannot faithfully reflect the extent of MRP success. We learned from companies that have implemented MRP; it is a complex process and its success should be gauged from a multi-dimensional perspective. A more embracing, operationally acceptable definition of MRP success is needed to allow companies to assess their MRP implementation meaningfully. MRP systems represent major investments [11,27] and involve major changes in organizational procedures, discipline, and focus [8,12,30]. Companies implementing MRP systems want to know if their systems pay off in productivity and competitive advantage. Single item measures, therefore, can be conceptually misleading and inadequate.

Besides the need for a comprehensive, multifaceted measure of MRP success, we wanted to have a better understanding of its implementation process. Existing treatment of critical success factors (CSFs)

[1,2,5,13,16–19,24–26,28,29], though informative and useful for practitioners, can be improved on. Saraph et al. [23] developed an eight-factor framework for quality management, and Black and Porter [3] a 10-factor framework for total quality management (TQM). Both of these used factor analysis (a well-received statistical procedure) to reduce many variables using responses gathered from surveys. Sum et al. [28] used Alternating Conditional Expectation algorithm [4] to determine the CSFs affecting MRP success. Ours represented a methodological departure in that we developed our CSF framework using multiple case studies [32]. Instead of questionnaires, we focused our attention on the interviews conducted with the participants. These were particularly useful, since they provided opportunities to probe beyond the responses, resolve ambiguities, and even overcome unwillingness to answer particular questions. Hence, the multiple case study methodology allowed us to study the complexity of the implementation process, and also consider the relationships among the CSFs. Given the complexity of the implementation process, it appears logical that they are not independent and that they do interact, but most studies on MRP implementation treat CSFs as independent variables, without examining their interaction effects.<sup>1</sup> For example, CSFs such as top management support and training/education are usually examined separately. But it is meaningful to examine interaction effects because CSFs could be inherently related; e.g. the presence of good training/education might depend on the extent of top management support. Understanding the CFS interactions could provide insights into how companies could best meet all their CFSs.

## 1.2. Objectives of research

Given the motivation of our research, we used multiple case methodology to

1. develop a multi-dimensional measure of MRP success;

2. consolidate a CSF framework for MRP implementation; and

3. extend the study of CSFs in isolation to CSFs in interation.

## 2. Methodology

## 2.1. Subjects of case study

## 2.1.1. Criteria for selection

For consistency and ease of comparison, it was decided that all companies should come from the same industry. The electronics industry was chosen for two reasons. First, the electronics industry is one of the biggest and most important industries in Singapore. In terms of percentage contribution to total manufacturing output, it ranks top, with 43.4% in 1999 [10]. Secondly, MRP is more readily found in this industry as materials management and scheduling are the primary operational concerns. MRP systems can be conceived as providing an enterprise with a foundation layer upon which other pieces of software and functional systems (e.g. Computer-Aided Design, Customer Relationship Management, Business Intelligence System, Logistics) stand.

We defined an MRP company as: one that has installed at least the basic modules from the MRP package. Furthermore, the system should be in an operational stage. This ruled out companies that were still in the process of implementing their system. For each participating company, interviews were conducted with at most three key persons involved in implementing and/or using the MRP system. These key persons were preferably: (i) a member of the executive steering committee; (ii) the project leader of the MRP implementation team; or (iii) a major enduser of MRP (e.g. the Planner). Within each company, interviews were conducted with more than one interviewee.

## 2.1.2. The search for participating companies

The target companies were contacted from recommendations from the National Computer Board (NCB) (now renamed the InfoComm Development Authority of Singapore). Several weeks were spent working with the staff from NCB, and waiting for them to provide a list of companies. The 10 companies they helped to select represented a balanced mix of size (based on number of employees and annual gross sales), ownership (local, government, and foreign), and success in terms of MRP implementation.

## 2.2. Data collection process

## 2.2.1. Phase one

All interviewees were required to answer the preinterview questionnaire (which was slightly modified for each interviewee, depending on the role in the MRP implementation). The pre-interview questionnaire was designed to provide background information about the company and its MRP implementation status, and to help us formulate the relevant questions in the interviews.

For each company, the questionnaires were sent separately to the individual interviewees, with a covering letter requesting them to answer the questionnaires separately and independently from interviewees of the same company. The people were assured that their company’s and their own identity would not be revealed. They were also asked to return (either by facsimile or mail) the completed questionnaires directly to us. These steps were taken to encourage candid answers.

## 2.2.2. Phase two

Based on the information gathered in Phase one, an interview guide was developed and tailored to each interviewee. Proceeding in this manner, the sessions were much more effective and fruitful. Any ambiguous answer given in the pre-interview questionnaire could be clarified during the interview. Items in the interview included measures used to assess MRP implementation outcome, the company’s characteristics (e.g. company size, the production process, BOM/product complexity), its MRP implementation status (e.g. year company started implementing MRP, degree of integration of computerized modules, stage of implementation), and the CSFs.

The interview process followed the focused interview format, where the interviewer follows a set of developed questions, but with the interview still remaining fairly open-ended so that the interviewee might be asked to give his or her own opinions and insights into certain issues. Each person was interviewed for about 1 h. During this time, notes were taken as documentation by an experienced research assistant. Whenever necessary, we asked to receive company documents (e.g. organization charts, turnover figures and graphs). Some interviewees gave brief demonstrations on their MRP systems, while others took us on tours around the factory sites.

## 2.3. Multiple-case design and multiple sources of evidence

The literature review had shown that the bulk of case studies conducted on MRP implementation followed a single-case design. However, such studies are usually descriptive in nature, with minimal analysis, and they are thus limited in usefulness for answering more exploratory ‘‘how’’ and ‘‘why’’ research questions.

The evidence from multiple cases, however, is more compelling and the overall study is, therefore, more robust. Multiple-case designs follow a ‘‘replication’ logic (as opposed to ‘‘sampling’’ logic). Therefore, contrary to common misconception that case studies provide little basis for scientific generalization, multiple-case studies are generalizable (though not to populations or universes). Here, the researcher’s goal was to expand and generalize theories (analytic generalization) and not enumerate frequencies (statistical generalization). Given our objectives, a case study with multiple-case design was the appropriate methodology.

Our design permitted us to collect multiple sources of evidence at three levels. The advantage of using multiple sources of evidence is the development of converging lines of inquiry, a process of triangulation. The idea is to follow a corroboratory mode, so that any finding or conclusion in a case study is likely to be more convincing and accurate. In this way, the construct validity<sup>2</sup> of the case study could be addressed, since multiple sources of evidence essentially provide multiple measures of the same phenomenon. As Miles and Huberman [20] put it: ‘‘. . .findings are more dependable when they can be buttressed from several independent sources. . .’’.

Having multiple sources of evidence also helps overcome the commonly cited limitations of interviews (bias, poor recall, and poor or inaccurate articulation). Additionally, paraphrasing and summarizing were done intermittently during each interview to ensure that the interviewee’s answers were interpreted accurately. After the interview, clarifications (if any) were made by follow-up phone calls.

## 3. Background information of the companies

A total of 27 interviewees from the 10 companies were interviewed. Five companies provided us with three interviewees, while three companies provided us with two. Due to time constraint one company could only provide one interviewee. One company, besides providing three interviewees, also permitted us to conduct impromptu interviews with two extra people who happened to be available.

The interviewees spanned various hierarchical levels, and were mainly from Materials, Planning and Logistics, Production Control, and MIS Departments. However, the titles and department names varied widely from company to company.

The characteristics of the companies are presented in Table 1. Both local and foreign companies, with sizes ranging from 95 to 1200 employees, were represented. Five of the companies implemented MRP before the year 1990, while the other five implemented it after 1990. Table 2 gives the details of the modules that were installed in each company.

## 3.1. Multi-dimensional measure of success

Past studies usually used some quantifiable measures of success, but these alone may not result in accurate classification of the companies. For example, one interviewee indicated a low degree of satisfaction because the system did not have certain features he deemed desirable. But as far as the current system was concerned, he was very satisfied, because the system was widely accepted in the company. His subordinates (planners and master schedulers) were actually very creative and innovative in building subsystems using simple spreadsheets and in-house programs to get around the shortcomings of the MRP software. To him, the greatest benefits were the cultivation of discipline (to follow the MRP plans) and the development of his subordinates’ problem-solving skills arising from the shortcomings of the system. As another example, an interviewee indicated a high degree of satisfaction, but upon further probing, it was discovered that the system could have been more extensively used and accepted by the people in the company than was currently the case. As yet another example, an interviewee felt that there was a mediocre benefit level but the company had only just implemented the MRP system: he was, in fact, very pleased with the smooth implementation and was optimistic that the benefits would further increase in future.

Resulting from the analysis of all the responses, we developed a framework for success measures:

1. The degree of satisfaction with the MRP system (satisfaction score).

This is the average of a company’s interviewees’ responses to a question item in the preinterview questionnaire on the degree of satisfaction with the MRP implementation, measured on a seven-point Likert scale. This score reflects the interviewees’ composite feeling of the level of implementation success (such factors as performance improvement, class status, achievement of objectives and so on).

2. The level of acceptance and overall usage of the system (complementary measure).

These are comments culled from the interviews; they serve to reinforce the satisfaction score. A high satisfaction score should be reinforced with a high complementary measure. There must be a high level of usage and acceptance. MRP systems are tools and the trust that users have in them and the information they generate, make the difference between successful and unsuccessful implementation. Therefore, if a high satisfaction score is not reinforced by a high complementary measure, then something has not been revealed.

Complementary measures captured elements like degree of acceptance, degree of usage, level of improvement of discipline and adherence to MRP procedures. ## 3.2. Companies successful in MRP implementation

A satisfaction score of 6.3 indicates that the interviewees of $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ were very satisfied with the system. In $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 } ,$ the acceptance of the system had reached a level where the people trusted and used the system, and were disciplined enough to follow the procedures. The reasons given by the interviewees for their high degree of satisfaction are:

1. The system had forced all areas to follow procedures.

2. The people had maintained high discipline.

3. The people were using the system very well.

4. The way MRP was being used made the planner’s job much easier.

5. The computerized modules were fully integrated.

6. The inventory accuracy was over 99%.

7. They were consistently able to meet 89% on-time delivery.

The ${ \bf S } _ { 1 } \ ' { \bf s }$ interviewees said that the replacement of MRP plans by informal systems (i.e. human intervention) was rare. MRP was also used as the foundation for implementing other programs, such as JIT and TQM. There was also a high level of user acceptance —users trusted the data and reports generated by the system. One of the interviewees mentioned that this was possible due to the excellent management of MRP. A high satisfaction score was reinforced by a high complementary measure.

The $\mathbf { S } _ { 2 }$ (satisfaction score of 5.7) implemented the system very recently, and had a fairly smooth implementation. The interviewees were all very satisfied because the system was working well and they were optimistic that benefits would be achieved. The reasons for the high degree of satisfaction are:

1. The system was working well.

2. The people were using the system well. They knew how to work with and make use of the system.

3. The people were optimistic that they would enjoy more benefits in the future.

4. The system was fulfilling their needs.

5. All modules were integrated and used.

Again, a high satisfaction score was underpinned by a high complementary measure.

## 3.3. Companies unsuccessful in MRP implementation

The MRP at $\mathrm { U } _ { 1 }$ (satisfaction score of 4) had been facing many problems. Some reasons given by the interviewees were:

1. There were many problems in using the system.

2. There were many features in the MRP software, but the people were not using them effectively.

3. The people were not confident enough to use the system.

4. Most of the time, the people did not believe the numbers.

5. The system was not fully integrated.

6. Many parameters and files had not been set up properly.

7. The storeroom was not tracking all the parts.

The company $\mathrm { { U } } _ { 1 }$ implemented MRP in 1990, and by 1991 the implementation was almost completed with all the modules installed. Except for inventory control, many of the modules were still not being used. The interviewees’ dissatisfaction, and the low usage level of the system, indicates that this company was unsuccessful in implementing MRP.

The usage and acceptance level of MRP in Company $\mathrm { { U } } _ { 2 }$ (satisfaction score of 4.5) was also very low. The system was not fully integrated. In fact, everything in the Production Department was still done manually. Some of the reasons for dissatisfaction were:

1. There was still room for improvement.

2. The system was not being used extensively.

3. The reports generated by the system were not totally accurate. Double checking was necessary.

4. People did not really believe the system and many relied on their own judgments.

5. The system was complicated if they wanted to trace something.

At both $\mathrm { U } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { U } _ { 2 } ,$ low satisfaction scores were matched by corresponding low complementary measures.

Since, we are interested in comparing the extreme cases in which the success of MRP implementation has or has not occurred, only $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 } , \mathrm { S } _ { 2 } , \mathrm { U } _ { 1 }$ , and $\mathrm { U } _ { 2 }$ are further discussed. Developing the CSF framework requires that we look at these extreme cases.

## 3.5. Within-case analysis

Within-case analysis involves discussing the findings of $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 } , \mathrm { S } _ { 2 } , \mathrm { U } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { { U } } _ { 2 }$

Company $\mathbf { S } _ { 1 }$ took about 1 year to complete the implementation. Problems with support from top management, company-wide support, education and training, data accuracy, and the meeting of project schedule were very low there. Top management did not merely pay lip service to giving support. They showed their support through actions like providing the necessary resources (manpower, training, etc.) and showing interest in the project. Education and training (an extremely important CSF) not only increased the knowledge level of the staff, but also communicated the importance of the project. This translated into company-wide support, high discipline, and therefore, data accuracy. Good and committed leaders also provided the necessary direction and leadership.

The company $\mathbf { S } _ { 2 }$ took 1.5 years to complete their transition from an old, customized system, to their current system. What was striking about $\mathbf { S } _ { 2 }$ was its elaborate project organization for the implementation. Its successful implementation could be attributed to the effective project management strategy, top management support, good training programs, company-wide commitment, and data accuracy (a feature even before MRP implementation). An example of top management support was the speed that the newly installed hardware was replaced after it was found to be incompatible with the MRP software.

At $\mathrm { U } _ { 1 }$ , shortly after the implementation was completed in 1991, there were many changes in the products and production control became complex. At the same time, they were downsizing and restructuring the company by shifting some of the production to other countries. The personnel turnover was also very high during this period.

In 1994, 3 years after the modules had been completely installed, the master production schedule (MPS) and material requirements planning (MRP) modules were still being implemented manually. It was at about this time that the management began to pay more attention to the MRP system and a project team was set up to rectify problems in the MRP system. Top management support, though forthcoming, was directed more at organizational restructuring. There was insufficient training on the logic and concepts of MRP. Other problem areas were data accuracy and hardware and capacity constraints.

The company $\mathrm { { U } } _ { 2 }$ started implementing MRP in 1993. However, they encountered tremendous problems with the software vendor, and there was an internal of education and expertise. Data accuracy was also a problem. Though top management was supportive, it did not follow through by setting up of a steering or project team to oversee the MRP implementation. They opted for a cheaper MRP system and the vendor did not provide good support. Users were keen on computerization but many lacked computer knowledge. The lack of education and expertise indirectly resulted in over-dependence on the software vendor, who did not and could not provide good support.

## 3.6. Cross-case analysis

The analysis of $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathbf { S } _ { 2 }$ suggests that when CSFs such as top management support, company-wide support, data accuracy, and education and training are present and well managed, the implementation is likely to be successful. That of $\mathrm { U } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { { U } } _ { 2 }$ suggests the reverse, that when some CSFs are missing, the implementation is not likely to be successful.

The findings of $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathbf { S } _ { 2 }$ thus represent a literal replication, while that of $\mathrm { U } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { { U } } _ { 2 }$ represent a theoretical replication.<sup>3</sup> In totality, these two sets of results to seven CSFs whose presence and good management is a necessary condition for MRP implementation success. This means that companies should fulfill these CSFs. For example, if software vendor support is lacking, then management can insist on their MIS Department being active with sufficient internal experts to meet the technical needs of the users.

Though, there are many fine survey studies on CSFs in MRP implementation, we used multiple case studies based on replication logic. The seven CSFs are:

1. Top management support. Top management support and commitment to the implementation project should be present.

2. Effective project management. This refers to having an effective management strategy for the MRP implementation project, such as forming an executive steering committee and a project team, selecting a good project leader, and having a good project plan.

3. Education and training. The existence of a good staff education and training program is critical. Such a program would address the problem (if any) of a lack of MRP expertise in the company.

4. Data accuracy. Data accuracy is highly correlated to MRP implementation success. It is a sine qua non for the operational effectiveness of MRP.

5. Company-wide support. This refers to the human management aspect and includes gaining the support of various departments, communication of the implementation goals, coping with resistance to change, and discipline.

6. Suitability of hardware and software. Managing MRP implementation requires the careful selection of both hardware and software.

7. Software vendor support. This is also positively correlated with implementation success: the more supportive the software vendor, the higher the probability of MRP implementation success.

## 3.7. Interaction effects among the CSFs

The interviewees’ opinions reveals the following:

1. Not all CSFs are of equal importance. Some are more important than others.

2. The importance of CSFs changes over time.

3. There exists some form of causal relationship between them.

## 3.7.1. Some CSFs are more important

Top management support is most frequently cited as the most important factor. If so, everything else follows. To quote one interviewees, ‘‘If top management support is not present, then the people would not take the implementation seriously.’

One interviewee felt that education and training is the most important. An inadequately trained team of users would definitely result in usage problems.

Instead of singling out one factor and labeling it as most important, some interviewees highlighted the more important factors and suggested that emphasis should be placed on them.

## 3.7.2. The importance of CSFs changes over time

Another group felt that their degree of importance of CSFs and the emphasis placed on them changes over time. One interviewee from $\mathrm { S } _ { 1 }$ noted that top management is particularly important at the beginning, to encourage the project team and the participants. Once, the implementation has gained momentum, top management need only monitor the progress, with involvement as and when necessary. A similar point was also noted at $\mathbf { M } _ { 6 } .$ . He said that top management is important at the preliminary stage of the implementation such as the feasibility stage, but less important once the project is approved.

## 3.8. The interaction model

On the basis of the consolidated analysis of CSFs, we developed an Interaction Model (Fig. 1) to explain the interaction effects among the CSFs. The Model essentially combines and reconciles the hierarchical, sequential and causal relationships. Top management support would lead to the formation of an effective project management team. An effective team would ensure that suitable hardware and software are selected, adequate training is provided, and company-wide support is achieved. Company-wide support is further reinforced by the interest and support shown by top management. The committed users, equipped with adequate training and support from the project team, would produce accurate data.

Note that, the absence of any one of the factors would upset the whole network of relationships, which would result in unsuccessful implementation. The factors are, therefore, equally important in achieving implementation success. At the same time, top management support is the most important impetus behind the whole project. Data accuracy is placed at the end of the ‘‘sequence of events,’’ meaning that data accuracy could be treated as one of the implementation goals.

The Interaction Model also portrays some factors as being more important that others (hierarchical relationships). Top management support and effective project management are more important because they are needed for the fulfillment of the rest of the factors (e.g. good training, company-wide support).

## 4. Discussion

We argued that single item measures of MRP success cannot accurately reflect the actual state of affairs. Success is a multi-dimensional construct. Our main contribution here is in developing a comprehensive and operationally acceptable measure of MRP success. Our measure consists of two linked components. They are a satisfaction score (a quantitative measure) and a complementary measure (based on comments from the interviewees regarding the level of usage and acceptance of the system).

We also extended and consolidated a seven-factor CSF framework using multiple case methodology. CSFs are important because they represent necessary conditions for MRP success. Despite the research on CSFs in MRP implementation, a more useful direction would be to link the CSFs in some meaningful way, as we have proposed in our Interaction Model. Just knowing what the CSFs are does not tell the whole story. Improving MRP implementation success deserves serious attention because it affects overall organizational performance.
