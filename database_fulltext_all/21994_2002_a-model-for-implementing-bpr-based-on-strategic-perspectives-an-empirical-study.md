# A model for implementing BPR based on strategic perspectives: an empirical study

Ing-Long Wu

Department of Information Management, National Chung Cheng University, 160 San-Hsing, Ming-Hsiung, Chia-Yi 621, Taiwan Received 10 February 2000; accepted 17 March 2001

## Abstract

Business process reengineering (BPR) efforts have been reported successful in many firms. However, the failure rates were as high as 70%. Various reasons for this have been given in past research. One major reason was that the efforts had not been connected to the corporate goals. In addition, the steps in implementing BPR in past discussions were, however, incomplete and the papers merely reported a part of the process. This study proposes an integrative approach based on a strategic perspective. Basically, this framework involves three steps that: (1) identify corporate strategies based on analysis of a strategic thrust/strategic target matrix; (2) select strategic paths for BPR based on analysis of a functional coupling framework with the IT application; (3) implement BPR based on analysis of project characteristics to decide on a suitable version of the composite methodology. Furthermore, this framework is illustrated by using an empirical study to demonstrate its application in some business units. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Business process reengineering; Competitive strategy; Reengineering methodology; Information technology

## 1. Introduction

Business process reengineering (BPR) has been considered as an important way to reshape business organizations for achieving breakthrough improvements in performance. In fact, BPR involves relatively extensive work, not only in emphasizing the concept of processes, but also by requiring understanding in parts of the organization on topics such as: adoption of technology, organizational structure, job design, human resource, etc. [12]. In particular, information technology (IT) is essential in the innovative redesign of business processes [3,4,10]. IT’s strategic role in BPR should be included from the very beginning, otherwise the results will be much less than hoped.

Studies of BPR projects have reported very large failure rates. Various reasons have been given, most connected to the mismanagement of projects [34]. While it has been suggested that BPR should be connected to the firm’s goals, actual practice seems to be different. In many cases of failures, it appears that BPR has been viewed and applied at an operational or tactical, rather than strategic level. The failures may be because BPR cannot keep up with the pace of environmental changes, resulting in failure to meet organizational requirements. The solution proposed here aims at setting long-term goals and then bases BPR on the needs. This would help planners to identify a set of critical processes that are aligned with the firm’s strategic performance and then become candidate processes for further implementation.

The process of implementing BPR is also often incomplete and fragmented in past literature. The solution to this problem might be achieved in two steps. The first step can be considered from two aspects. First, to achieve substantive reengineering performance, a business process should be first analyzed by certain characteristics from a high-level perspective [33]. These pertain to how different functions are coupled to each other and orchestrated to produce a common process outcome. Second, IT is viewed as a powerful enabler in BPR, and thus BPR efforts would be difficult to implement without the support of IT. Past studies for strategic use of IT in organizations are abundant [30,38]. Thus, many researchers have tried to extend the viewpoint of IT’s strategic use to business processes.

The second step is in physically implementing BPR tasks for candidate processes. Various project characteristics call for different methodological choices and there are numerous reengineering methodologies for selection. This makes BPR project planners difficult to physically proceed for BPR. Thus, the identification of a suitable methodology is an important consideration for implementing BPR.

In conclusion, it is therefore imperative to approach BPR with an integrative aspect of the three steps discussed above. Furthermore, the analytical techniques used for each step are also discussed. Finally, this framework is examined by an empirical study.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. IT and BPR

When the business environment becomes both complex and changeable, the traditional functional architecture and the division of labor no longer satisfy the organizational needs. Thus, an integration of cross-functions based on a process perspective is expected to increase organizational efficiency [17,18]. IT is a major facilitator of BPR and it must be considered in the process [11,36]. In particular, the introduction of IT into an organization should be associated with the empowering aspects of the jobs held by the organizational personnel, indicating that IT and BPR are closely interrelated [1,26]. The application of IT always accompanies BPR, and as a result there is some degree of improvement in performance [14,15]. Without the involvement of

IT’s role in BPR, the efforts will be destined to failure [9,16].

## 2.2. Operational aspects of BPR

BPR is the examination of the flow of activities and information that make up the key business processes in an organization with a view to simplification, cost reduction or improvement in quality or flexibility [24]. BPR focuses almost exclusively on improving the firm’s internal operations such as product distribution and delivery performance to the customers [31]. BPR is defined as a one-time process innovation effort to achieve radical business improvement [7]. BPR is defined as a fundamental rethinking and redesign of operational processes and organizational structure, focused on the organization’s core competence, to achieve dramatic improvements in organizational performance [22].

## 2.3. Identification of corporate strategies

The identification of corporate strategies must be able to predict the future development, take the lead in business process thinking, and draft plans for emergency, to meet the future and uncertain challenge [21]. These methods to develop strategic opportunities include: a five competitive force model, analysis of the value chain [25], the strategic thrust/strategic target matrix [37], critical success factors [28], the customer resource life cycle [19], and McFarlan’s framework [23].

More specifically, the adoption of these methods to identify strategic opportunities and further the application of IT to achieve the strategic opportunities were not very clear for the performance in practice, except for the value chain and strategic thrust/strategic target matrix [2]. The first considers the analysis of internal functions and hopefully achieves competitive position through the changes of internal functions, while the second is directed to the analysis of external environment, for which the proper strategies are developed toward certain targets, as indicated in Fig. 1. This matrix constitutes three strategic targets: suppliers, customers, and competitors, and five strategic thrusts or competitive strategies: cost, innovation, growth, strategic alliance, and differentiation.

## 2.4. IT and functional coupling of processes

Basically, the ways that various functions are orchestrated to accomplish a process, can be differentiated along two dimensions: degree of mediation and degree of collaboration [32]. The extent to the sequential flow of input and output among the participating functions in a business process constitutes the degree of mediation of the process. The degree of collaboration of a process is related to information exchange and mutual adjustment between the functions in the process. The identification of different coupling patterns can then be analyzed as indicated in Fig. 2. Furthermore, the applications of IT can be incorporated to change the degree of mediation and collaboration through alternative strategic paths, as presented in Fig. 2.

The two horizontal Paths X and $X ^ { * }$ are suitable for processes with lower collaboration and higher potential for collaboration enhancement, and they are primarily through application of communication technologies to redesign the processes. On the other hand, the two vertical Paths Y and $Y ^ { * }$ are suitable for processes with higher potential for mediation reduction where many sequential steps can be eliminated, and they are primarily through application of shared information resources to change the mediation. Finally, the diagonal Path Z is suitable for processes with higher potential for both collaboration enhancement and mediation reduction and is primarily through application of communication technologies and shared information resources to achieve the objective.

## 2.5. Reengineering methodology

To put BPR into action, methodological choices play a key role for the success of BPR. Wastell et al. [35] proposed a PADM methodology (process analysis and design methodology) having four phases: process definition, baseline process selection and representation, process evaluation, and target process design. Davenport and Short [6] presented a methodology containing five steps: develop business vision and process objectives, identify redesign processes, understand existing processes, identify IT levers, and build a prototype of the process. Furthermore, every methodology has its own specialties and properties, leading BPR project planners to be confused by the choice of methodologies.

Thus, Kettinger et al. [20] derived a composite stage-activity methodology for the reengineering processes based on the descriptions of 25 BPR methodologies, as indicated in Fig. 3. Basically, it includes six stages (S): envision, initiate, diagnose, redesign, reconstruct, and evaluate; each stage is further subdivided into certain activities (A). This methodology only provides project planners with a methodological archetype. However, no two BPR projects are exactly alike, because of the unique characteristics of projects and the amount of changes sought in organizations. This creates confusion for how to fit the characteristics of processes with the various versions of the methodology. They conducted field study interviews of BPR experts and proposed a contingency approach that used four major project characteristics in BPR project planning: (1) project radicalness; (2) process structuredness; (3) customer focus and (4) the potential of IT enablement. BPR practitioners are able to diagnose the characteristics of reengineered projects based on this approach and then decide which stages and activities in the methodology are most applicable for the specific project. ## 3. Theoretical framework

Thus, past research was not really aware of the importance of corporate strategies in BPR and discussed it literally and superficially. Then, the steps for implementing BPR were incomplete, simply discussing strategic paths for BPR or defining BPR methodologies. This paper proposes an integrative approach to implement BPR projects with a strategic aspect. Basically, the theoretical framework constitutes three steps: (1) identify corporate strategies; (2) select strategic paths for BPR with IT application and (3) implement BPR; this is indicated in Fig. 4. Moreover, the techniques used to analyze the three steps are also discussed.

## 3.1. The first step: identify corporate strategies

The strategic target/strategic thrust matrix is more appropriate for this analysis. Firms can specify their competitive strategies toward certain targets. For example, a firm can adopt a cost strategy at its customers or an alliance strategy at its suppliers. After that, a set of candidate processes can be identified accordingly in terms of the firm’s strategic performance.

## 3.2. The second step: select strategic paths for BPR with IT application

This step uses the functional coupling framework with IT application as a basis for analyzing the critical characteristics of a process that may be redesigned through application of IT. The strategic path for reengineering a process can then be selected from one of the alternative paths.

## 3.3. The third step: implement BPR

This step is based on the methodology proposed by Kettinger et al. [20] However, the potential of IT enablement has already been considered for its influence in the second step. Thus, the contingency approach only considers the other three project characteristics, i.e. project radicalness, process structuredness, and customer focus.

This framework is derived from literature review, so further validation using empirical data is necessary. The first part of this validation explores the relationship between the first and second steps. In practice, when business organizations specify their corporate strategies, the choices of processes for BPR will be influenced accordingly, and the effect on selecting strategic paths for BPR may be recognized. Thus, Hypothesis 1 is hereafter presented for this. It is further elaborated as three sub-hypotheses for the three strategic targets. The second part of this validation discusses the relationship between the second and third steps. In practice, when business organizations select different strategic paths for BPR, the methodological choices for performing BPR will be affected accordingly. However, the methodological choices are based on the criteria of the three project characteristics. Thus, Hypothesis 2 is then proposed for this.

Hypothesis 1. For all targets, competitive strategies are not significantly related to strategic paths for BPR in terms of candidate processes.

Hypothesis 1a. For suppliers, the relationship between competitive strategies and strategic paths for BPR is not significant.

Hypothesis 1b. For customers, the relationship between competitive strategies and strategic paths for BPR is not significant.

Hypothesis 1c. For competitors, the relationship between competitive strategies and strategic paths for BPR is not significant.

Hypothesis 2. Strategic paths for BPR in terms of candidate processes are not significantly related to all characteristics in methodological choices.

## 4. Design of research

An empirical study is conducted by using a survey to collect data.

## 4.1. Instrumentation

The instrument contains a five-part questionnaire as below. The first three and the fifth use nominal scales, while the fourth uses a five-point Likert scale.

## 4.1.1. Basic information

This collects information about the organizational characteristics including industry, annual revenue, and number of employees, and the respondents characteristics including education, age, experience, and position.

## 4.1.2. Corporate strategy

Respondents are requested to choose one of the five competitive strategies considered to be most suitable to the company in terms of each of the three strategic targets.

## 4.1.3. Strategic paths for BPR with IT application

Respondents choose the strategic path for BPR that they consider to be most suitable to the company. However, since Paths Z, XY<sup>-</sup> and YX<sup>-</sup> involve both the considerations of mediation reduction and collaboration enhancement, one question is asked about the main purpose of the selection of the three paths, i.e. either mediation reduction or collaboration enhancement.

## 4.1.4. Project characteristics

This part requests selection of the project radicalness, project structuredness, and customer focus. The first is adapted from the instrument developed by Kettinger et al. [20], comprising seven items. The second is adapted from the material in the studies of Donna and Sirkka [8] and Hammer [14], including seven items. The third is adapted from a survey by Richard et al. [29], constituting five items.

## 4.1.5. Reengineering methodology

Respondents then identify the most suitable version of this methodology according to the degree of the three previously defined characteristics in BPR projects.

## 4.2. Pretest

To measure reliability and validity, a pretest is performed by using a representative sample of 30 companies, comprising 17 manufacturing firms, 9 service firms, and 4 banking firms. Generally speaking, a measure is reliable to the degree that it supplies consistent results, and its reliability is measured by coefficient alpha. In this study, validity is of two types: content and construct validity. The first is the extent to which it provides adequate coverage of the topic under study. The second attempts to identify the underlying construct(s) being measured and determine how well the test represents them [5]. A principal factor analysis is used for the analysis of construct validity.

As the first three and the fifth parts of the questionnaire are defined by well-documented techniques in literature, they definitely should have high reliability and validity. For the fourth part of the questionnaire, coefficient alphas are ranged from 0.72 to 0.77 as in Table 1. According to Price and Mueller [27], a standard coefficient alpha of 0.60 or higher is generally viewed as acceptable for using a measure. This part is adapted from existing literature and is also reviewed by practitioners carefully, content validity should be relatively acceptable. Construct validity is examined by principal component analysis. The rule of Eigenvalue greater than 1 was used as the criterion to extract the factors [13]. The results are shown in Table 1.

## 4.3. Sample organizations and respondents

Since this study explores BPR based on strategic perspectives, it may be assumed that larger firms would be more likely to have this experience. A study sample comprising first 800 manufacturing firms, 500 service firms, and 100 banking firms selects from the 1998 listing of Commonwealth 1000 manufacturing firms, 500 service firms, and 100 banking firms. Furthermore, based on this sample, CIOs or IS top managers are selected as the respondents. This is because this study investigates competitive strategies and the relationship between IT and BPR, and therefore CIOs or IS top managers are likely to be the managerial personnel most familiar with or involved in this topic. Thus, a total of 1400 surveys is finally sent to these managers. In order to improve survey returns, telephone calls or letters are used to follow up after 3 weeks. The 181 replies, with 7 incomplete responses deleted, resulting in a total sample of 174 respondents, a 17% response rate. The responding sample includes 99 manufacturing firms (56.8%), 62 service firms (35.6%), and 13 banking firms (7.6%). The sector distribution of the responding sample is approximately similar to that of the sample frame.

## 5. Analysis and findings

This section is discussed by the sequence of the three steps in this framework based on the empirical data.

## 5.1. The first step: identify corporate strategies

## 5.1.1. Suppliers

The data for firms in adopting the five competitive strategies toward suppliers is summarized as the third row of Table 2. The results show that there is a high proportion of firms in Taiwan that recognize cost strategy as the major consideration for negotiation with suppliers. Obviously, these companies still maintain traditional partnerships rather than modern ones, such as the adoption of growth or alliance strategy to develop the relationship of mutual trust and benefit. However, the adoption of other competitive strategies toward suppliers occupies a relatively high percentage (65%). This implies that most firms are moving toward the direction of close mutual trust and benefit.

## 5.1.2. Customers

The data for customers is collected in the fourth row of Table 2. The results indicate the facts that to effectively meet the constant change in the requirements of customers, firms in Taiwan consider the adoption of innovation strategy as the first priority, and conversely, cost strategy as the least. This implies that the traditional competitive style, based on the cost-oriented strategy toward customers, has not satisfied the requirements of the modern market. For instance, customers would consider the other characteristics as their major concerns when purchasing a product in the market, such as product innovation and quality, rather than low cost. Thus, most firms are able to adopt more competitive strategies toward customers for maintaining a competitive position.

## 5.1.3. Competitors

The data for competitors is collected in the fifth row of Table 2. There is a relatively high proportion of firms considering alliance as their major competitive strategy; that is, the proportion is twice as much as the next. The results may be explained from the facts that the competitive strategies used toward competitors for the intra-industry have shifted from the traditional relationship of confrontation to a focus on the relationship of mutual trust. ATM network provides the best example of an IT-supported alliance in banking industry. Here, banks are able to offer customers nationwide banking at more than just the branches of the customers’ banks. They gain benefits to each other from strategic alliances, and thus avoid confrontative competition within the industry.

## 5.3.2. Choices of the methodologies

To effectively implement BPR, it is necessary for us to help BPR project planners customize a methodology for the project at hand. The objective is to determine the stages and activities in the composite methodology (see Fig. 3) for the project. The effort here suggests, as indicated in Table 7, a guide for BPR project planners. If project radicalness is high, the activities are focused on change management and design of new process. If a process is more structured, the emphasis should be placed on the activities of process capture and modeling. If a process has high customer focus, emphasize those activities related to analyzing external processes for customer requirements and identifying IT levers.

## 6. Conclusion

Today, the functional organization and its division of labor no longer fully represent the requirements of the organization. The idea of emphasizing the integration of cross-functions based on the perspectives of business processes accordingly becomes a means of increasing enterprise-wide efficiency. Past implementation of BPR projects had high failure rates, may be due to focus on the operational rather than the strategic level. Basically, different firms identify different competitive strategies for their suppliers, customers, or competitors. Consequently, a set of candidate processes can be developed in terms of a firm’s strategic performance, and in turn, the selected strategic paths for BPR deviate from each other. Furthermore, the methodological choices for BPR also differ due to the deviation of the strategic paths for BPR. The implications for these finding are two-fold. First, if BPR is not based on strategic perspective, there will be no obvious way to proceed. This causes a high percentage of failures. Second, functional coupling framework associated with IT applications helps BPR project planners set high-level reengineering paths before BPR implementation. This may help to guarantee the success of the BPR because reengineering directions and guidelines are set in advance.

Subsequent research could be founded on this work. First, this study focuses on developing a theoretical framework and further validating it. Future research can be done by applying this framework to reengineer a business project and then verifies its performance. Second, this empirical survey is sampled from a spectrum of industries, and thus the conclusion is more general and comprehensive. Future research can be targeted toward individual industry, for instance, the banking industry, to determine its particular differences and similarities. Third, the method adopted for the analysis of corporate strategy is not unique, and other techniques can also be used for future research to understand the different effects on BPR work.

Finally, although this study has produced some interesting results, it still may have some limitations. First, IS managers or chief information officers from larger firms are primarily chosen to be participants in the survey. However, some of the questionnaires may have been completed by subordinates, and as a result the data may have some biases. Second, this research does not look at the activities in each stage and thus suggest best techniques for analyzing each activity, such as the value chain for $\mathbf { S } _ { 1 } \mathbf { A } _ { 2 }$ or brainstorming for $\mathrm { S } _ { 4 } \mathrm { A } _ { 1 }$ .
