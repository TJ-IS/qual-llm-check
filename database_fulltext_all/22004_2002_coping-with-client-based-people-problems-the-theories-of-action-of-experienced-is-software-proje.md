# Coping with client-based ‘people-problems’: the theories-of-action of experienced IS/software project managers

Tony Moynihan

School of Computer Applications, Dublin City University, Dublin 9, Ireland

Received 26 December 1999; received in revised form 17 January 2001; accepted 16 March 2001

## Abstract

Through interviews with experienced IS and software project managers (PMs), I identify the strategies that they would recommend to others for addressing a range of serious client-based ‘people-problems’. The problems I focus on include: unrealistic customer expectations; lack of real ownership of the project; disagreement on project goals; personal deficiencies on the part of the customer’s PM; reluctant users; and the presence of hidden agendas or ‘nasty’ politics on the customer’s side.

Many of the strategies are based on the same underlying principle: ‘Protect yourself through explicitness, clarity and formality’.

I show that a number of the strategies are rational when judged in the light of agency theory and the literature on interorganisational trust. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Software project management; Agency theory; Theories-of-action

## 1. Introduction

The notion that a project manager (PM) should ‘tailor’ aspects of a project to achieve a fit with important situational characteristics seems to be unassailable. Indeed, it is hard to imagine how a PM could survive otherwise! When tailoring a project to meet the circumstances, a manager must obviously draw on some principles or ‘rules’ for guidance.

The principles/rules/tactics which a manager uses to guide his/her actions have been termed theories-ofaction: ‘‘A full schema for a theory of action would be as follows: In situation S, if you want to achieve consequence C, under assumptions $\mathbf { a } , \ldots , \mathbf { n } ,$ , do $\mathbf { A } ^ { \prime \prime }$ [1].

In this paper, I investigate the theories-of-action that experienced IS and software PMs claim to use when shaping-up new projects for new, external clients. Specifically, I try to identify the theories-of-action that PMs draw on to help them cope with non-technical, ‘people-centred’ project risk.

The risk drivers that I focus on include: unrealistic customer expectations; lack of real customer ownership of the project; disagreement amongst the customers’ people on project goals; personal deficiencies on the part of the customer’s PM; user resistance; and the presence of hidden agendas or ‘nasty’ politics by the customer.

The work described here has a relationship to agency theory [3], which is concerned with transactions between the principal and the agent. The principal is an individual or organisation that has a need that it wants to satisfy. The principal delegates the task of satisfying the need to the agent, who attempts to satisfy the principal’s need, usually in return for a reward.

Agency Theory focusses on the nature of the exchange between the two parties. In particular, on contractual, or other mechanisms, that the principal can use to protect himself/herself against opportunistic behaviour on the part of the agent. The possibility that the agent will behave ‘badly’ is seen, in agency theory, to be especially high when there is an information asymmetry that favours the agent [7]. In other words, in situations where the agent possesses (or is believed by the principal to possess) the special knowledge and skills needed to satisfy the delegated need. The risk of opportunistic behaviour by the agent is seen to be high in this situation because, not possessing the necessary knowledge and skills himself/herself, the principal would find it difficult to assess the quality and quantity of the agent’s work.

Where there is information asymmetry, agency theory proposes means by which the principal might limit the possibility of opportunistic behaviour by the agent. Examples are as follows.

\- Insistence on an outcome-based, as opposed to a behaviour-based, contract. In other words, ‘‘payment by pre-defined results rather than payment on the basis of the level of effort expended by the agent’’.

\- The employment, by the principal, of a third-party who has the necessary expertise to monitor the work of the agent.

\- Ensuring the agent’s on-going commitment to the project by forcing the agent to invest resources in the creation of the assets (e.g. intermediate deliverables) that have deferred-value to the agent in the context of the project, but have little or no value outside the project [8].

Agency theory views the exchange primarily from the perspective of the principal. But what of the agent’s perspective? What strategies can agents use to protect themselves from potentially opportunistic or other unfavourable forms of behaviour on the part of the principal?

## 2. Method

In an earlier study, the author identified the situational variables that experienced PMs claimed they considered when planning and managing IS development projects for new, external clients [6]. The technique of personal construct elicitation was used for that purpose [2]. A personal construct is a bi-polar distinction that a person uses when contrasting different people, objects, situations, etc. For example, for myself, an important distinction between dogs is the likelihood that a dog will bite me! So, when comparing dogs, or thinking about a particular dog, I am likely to think in terms of ‘will or won’t he bite me?’. People have multiple sets of many interacting constructs to help them make sense of the world. The task of identifying the set of constructs used by a person in a particular context is called personal construct elicitation.

In the previous study, each PM was asked to list the systems development projects he or she had worked on as PM over the past year or two. I then selected three projects randomly from the list and asked the PM: ‘‘In what important ways are any two of these three projects the same but different from the third in terms of important situational factors that you had to consider when planning the project?’’.

The PM was then asked to repeat this task with different triads of projects, until no new situational constructs were being elicited. Using this process, 113 different constructs were elicited. These reflected most of the situational variables that have been identified as project ‘risk-drivers’ by IS and software project risk researchers, and reflected some additional situational variables not previously identified in the literature. Content analysis was used to build a classification scheme for the constructs and 20 classes were identified. Of these, three examples are as follows.

\- Constructs relating to the client’s knowledge/understanding/clarity about the requirements to be met or the problem to be solved.

\- Constructs relating to the existence/competence/ seniority/commitment of the project patron/owner.

\- Constructs relating to the level of IT competence and experience of the customer and the users.

## 2.1. The latest study

For the study, 34 constructs were selected from the full set of 113. This sub-set was chosen with a view to including at least one from each of the 20 classes and at least two constructs from the most highly-populated classes. Those constructs selected are shown in Table 1.

Five hypothetical project profiles were then constructed. A project profile consisted of 34 poles, one drawn randomly from each of the 34 chosen constructs. To construct a project profile, random numbers were used to decide whether the left-hand pole or the right-hand pole of the construct should appear in the profile. Within any one profile, a fixed probability of selecting the right-hand pole (in the case of most constructs, the ‘risky’ pole) was applied. But this probability was systematically varied across the profiles. In this way, I generated large variation in apparent ‘riskiness’ across the five hypothetical projects. For this procedure, the questions could be asked: ‘‘Why build more than one project profile?’’ as well as ‘‘why vary the level of apparent ‘riskiness’ across the profiles?’’. The answers to these questions relate to the potential generalisability of the study findings. Had just a single project profile been used for the present study, or had multiple profiles been used (all generated using the same probability of selection of the right-hand pole), it could have been argued that the range of strategies elicited from the PMs might have been constrained by the choice of the particular profile(s) and probability used.

The subjects were 20 IS developers. All managed software-intensive IS development projects for external clients. All 20 had at least 6 years experience of running projects. All were between 30 and 60 years of age. Almost all were owners or directors of their companies. The numbers of developers employed in their companies ranged from 2 to 20 people. The scale of the projects that they typically managed fell within the ranges as follows:

project duration: 3–36 months;

project effort: 3–250 man-months;

project team size: 1–9 people.

All worked with mainstream, current technology. All were in the business of providing information systems ‘solutions’ to commercial clients.

The writer spent about 1 h discussing issues with each PM. In outline, the procedure used involved as follows.

\- The PM was told that the purpose of the exercise was to learn more about the ‘recipes’ that experienced PMs use to cope with project risk, and that a hypothetical project would be used to help with this task.

\- Permission of the PM to tape-record the session was then sought. In all cases, this was forthcoming.

\- The writer gave the PM a set of 34 index cards, one for each of the 34 constructs. The top, visible face, of the card showed both poles of the construct. The bottom face, which the writer asked the PM not to look at, showed the pole that belonged to the hypothetical project profile that had been randomly chosen for that PM.

\- The PM was asked to sort the constructs into three piles. The first to contain constructs that, depending on which of the two poles applied, could make a VERY BIG DIFFERENCE to the ‘riskiness’ of a project. The other two piles to contain constructs that could make a BIG DIFFERENCE or SOME/ LITTLE/NO difference, respectively.

\- I then asked the PM to focus on the VERY BIG DIFFERENCE pile and start by choosing the constructs, singly or in combination, which could make the most difference to project risk.

\- The writer then asked the PM why he/she had chosen these constructs.

\- The PM was then asked to turn over the card(s) to reveal the pole(s) that applied to the hypothetical project, and to give a verbal reaction to this.

\- I then explored the strategies, tactics, tricks, and so on, that the PM would use to cope with that pole. This procedure was repeated until all of the VERY BIG DIFFERENCE constructs had been turned over.

\- At intervals, the PM was asked to sum up his or her feelings about the unfolding project, and to describe the strategies/tactics/‘ploys’ (s)he would advise a PM faced with managing that project to use.

\- The session was concluded by asking the PM to rate the likely outcome of the project on a set of scales.

When all 20 interviews had been completed, the process of analysing the interview transcripts began. The PMs’ verbatim descriptions of the strategies that they recommended for coping with the risky poles were extracted from the interview transcripts. A total of 303 descriptions were obtained. I sorted these verbatim descriptions into piles. To each pile, I assigned those descriptions that seemed, in their essence, to be referring to the same, underlying strategy. Then, for each pile, I tried to capture that common underlying strategy by creating a synthesis of the PMs’ descriptions (a ‘label’). A number of iterations of this sorting and labelling process were required. The goal was to achieve piles and labels which, in a semantic sense, maximised ‘variation’ across the piles, and minimised ‘variation’ within the piles. By the conclusion of this process, the 303 descriptions had been broken into forty-two piles.

## 3. Results

3.1. The composition of the PMs’ VBD, BD, and LND piles

In Table 1, for each construct, I show the numbers of PMs who placed that construct in the VERY BIG DIFFERENCE, the BIG DIFFERENCE, and the LITTLE/NO DIFFERENCE piles. The constructs are shown in decreasing order of numbers of PMs placing that construct in the VERY BIG DIFFERENCE pile.

In characterising their VERY BIG DIFFERENCE piles, the PMs used phrases which are as follows.

\- The VBD pile is about things that are not directly controllable by us. Things that could come back and bite us.

\- These are the real risk generators in my view. These are the ones that could cause you to lose control . . . these generate uncertainty . . . possible black holes.

\- The VBD pile are the key factors, some of which may not be in your direct control . . . like the client not having realistic expectations. You can only manage so many of these factors simultaneously . . . the items in the BD pile are important, but generally I would say these are all manageable.

\- The VBD pile has to do with control and familiarity. These are success versus disaster things.

The ‘people-centred’ constructs that I have chosen for this paper have been italicised in Table 1. I chose these particular constructs because, of all the ‘peoplecentred’ constructs in the entire set of 34 constructs, these six were the most frequently assigned by the PMs to their VBD piles.

## 4. Discussion

## 4.1. The strategies

Firstly, some general observations on the strategies. Many seem to be based on a common principle: ‘selfprotection by means of explicitness, clarity and formality’ (#2, #4, #5, #6, #7, #10, #14, #16). The literature on trust in organisations lends support to this principle. In the context of interorganisational relations, trust has been defined as: ‘‘The expectation that an actor (1) can be relied on to fulfill obligations, (2) will behave in a predictable manner, and (3) will act and negotiate fairly when the possibility for opportunism is present’’ [9].

Zaheer et al. [9] also states: ‘‘Under conditions of low trust, lengthy and difficult negotiations over unforseen contingencies are likely to take place between exchange partners because of the possibility of both ex ante and ex post opportunism. In addition, contractual and structural safeguards are put in place to protect investments in the relationship. Under high trust conditions, in contrast, firms are less inclined to rely on elaborate safeguards for specifying, monitoring and enforcing agreements’’.

Where a project is subject to the sorts of ‘peopleproblems’ that are the focus here, it seems likely that the level of trust that the PM would invest in the client organisation would be low. Thus, one would expect the PM’s theories-of-action to reflect the above generalisation. This seems to be the case.

The need to ‘manage’ the client’s expectations from the very outset of the transaction is reflected across a number of the strategies. The interview transcripts show that, rather than tailoring the project to meet the client’s (probably unrealistic) initial expectations, the PM scopes the project to reflect what he/she feels to be achievable and then tries to ‘re-calibrate’ the client’s expectations accordingly. This he/she attempts to do primarily through a process of education (#9). Where the client’s expectations are unrealistic, the PMs also advocated the need for an increased level of formality and explicitness in the transaction (e.g. #2, #5, #7).

Most of the individual counts shown in the body of Table 2 are low. So, one must avoid the danger of overinterpreting the data. However, two strategies in particular were mentioned by PMs in the context of practically all of the seven ‘nasty’ poles. These two apparently ‘broad-spectrum’ antidotes are the introduction of formality/explicitness/bureaucracy into all aspects of the project (#5), and obtaining client signoff at every stage and on each deliverable (#7). The clear intent of these strategies is to provide selfprotection for the PM.

Taking some of the strategies individually, further observations can be made. The most frequently mentioned strategy (#1) relates to the PM in his/her role as a change-agent. Most of the PMs appeared to be very wary of becoming embroiled in the client’s peopleproblems. Where there are such problems, the great majority of the PMs believed that they should act as unaligned (neutral) educators and facilitators, as opposed to acting as unquestioning tools of the client’s management. In terms of the change-agent models proposed in the IS literature, these PMs seem to see themselves as filling a combination of the Facilitator and Advocator roles [5]. It must be said, however, that a small minority took a more ‘Rambo-ish’ view of their role (‘‘If users don’t want it, get someone further up-the-line to steam-roller things through’’). This latter group seem to espouse values which are close to those that Zaheer et al. [9] term the traditional IS change-agent model.

At least two of the strategies have a resonance in agency theory, which predicts that where there is an information asymmetry that favours the agent, the principal may try to assure the agent’s ‘good behaviour’ by compelling him/her to expend his/her own resources in transaction-specific assets which have a deferred-value to the agent, and only then in the context of the ‘successful’ completion of the transaction. Strategy #6 seems to be an attempt by PMs to avoid this trap. In other words, to avoid the early investment of his/her own resources to create an asset (the requirements/functional specification) that may subsequently have no value to the PM in the event, for example, of project termination, or some other negative outcome. As one PM put it, in the context of disagreement amongst the client’s people: ‘‘. . . if you spend a lot of time producing a good specification, they could then walk up to [a] competitor and say ‘There’s the spec . . . give us that’. Then you are just giving away the shop . . . doing your competitor’s research for him . . . so’, you need to say ‘we will have a formal charge for agreeing things . . .’ ’’.

Agency theory also suggests that, in situations of information asymmetry which favour the agent, the principal should insist on outcome-based payment to the agent. In other words, to base payment on actual results achieved relative to the principal’s expectations, rather than on the level of effort expended by the agent. Strategy #8 (‘‘Insist on a time and materials deal . . .’’) is a counter to this principle and is seen by PMs to be salient in situations in which the PM does not have full control over project outcomes.

Three strategies constitute direct ‘fixes’ to project ‘ownership’ problems and to any inadequacies on the part of the client’s PM (#3, #11, #12). Strategy #15 is an attempt to manage around any such problems. In essence, it advises ‘‘Don’t put all your eggs into a onerelationship basket, e.g. the relationship with the client’s PM. Reduce your dependency on him/her by building strong relationships with the users and with other key-players in the client organisation’’.

## 4.2. Comments on the research method

This paper has dealt with PMs’ espoused strategies. In other words, with what PMs say they would do in certain circumstances. But obviously, what PMs would actually do may differ from what they say they would do. This is clearly a potential weakness in the research method. Also, the study used a grounded research approach: the study was not theory-driven and used very open-ended, non-directive data collection techniques. For these reasons, the study may have understated the range of strategies that PMs would espouse. For example, had the writer prepared lengthy checklists of possible strategies and prompted the PMs from these lists, more strategies might have been elicited. It is safest to assume that by espousing a strategy, a PM is indicating that the strategy is one to be applied ‘vigorously’ and in a ‘pure’ form. By failing to recommend a strategy, it is safe to assume that, if used at all, the strategy would be applied less vigorously or in a ‘diluted’ form. It is not safe to assume that it would not have been espoused had the PM been prompted.

The focus of this study has been on the strategies that experienced IS/software PMs say they would use to cope with a selection of ‘people-centred’ problems when working on development projects for external client organisations. The PMs were all located in

Ireland. This raises the question: ‘‘Would IS/software project managers in other countries have the same theories-of-action as Irish project managers?’’ There is empirical evidence to suggest that IS/software PMs working in developed economies, but in different countries and in different cultures, share their thinking on IS/software project risk and its management. For example, [4] conducted a Delphi study with software PMs in the USA, Hong Kong, and Finland. All converged on the same project risk-drivers, and all ranked these in roughly the same order of importance. In my past study, I describe a number of other studies that support the proposition that Irish IS/software PMs construe project risk in much the same way as PMs in the USA, Canada, and a range of European countries. So, it is probably safe to assume that PMs in other countries would have the same theories as Irish PMs.

Would the theories-of-action held by the PMs in this study be held by PMs of larger projects? Analysis of the interview transcripts suggest that the PMs’ theories-of-action are driven by such motivations as the desire to do a good job for the client, the necessity to provide self-protection against potentially damaging or arbitrary client behaviour, the desire to deal fairly and ethically with the client’s people, the need to turn a profit, etc. Aims such as these are shared by managers of projects of all sizes. The strategies for achieving these aims appear to be quite generic, and to be based on management principles of wide efficacy. Also, it is hard to construct a convincing argument that the PMs strategies for small or medium sized projects would not be applicable to larger ones. So, it is likely that the theories-of-action identified here would be shared by PMs of projects of all sizes.
