# The role of communication in organizational change: an empirical investigation

Hulya Julie Yazici

Management Department, College of Business, University of Wisconsin-La Crosse, 418G Carl Wimberly Hall, 1725 State Street, La Crosse, WI 54601, USA

Received 10 August 2000; received in revised form 9 January 2001; accepted 22 May 2001

## Abstract

Despite their widespread availability, the role information technologies (IT) play on organizational processes is not clear. This paper, investigates the causal links between organization structure, communication media, and task complexity for better organizational communication. A field survey was administrated in a leading international manufacturing company, followed by a causal path analysis there of communication determinants and outcomes.

The findings indicate the significance of organization structure for cross-functional information access, delivery, use, and sharing needs. IT was perceived to be somewhat important, but its effect on communication depended on task complexity. In all, traditionally rich communication media is used and preferred. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Communication; IT; Organization structure; Change; Information flow; Task complexity; Communication media; Sharing

## 1. Introduction

The promise of emerging IT is to enhance productivity, communication, and decision-making. It has been reported that 50 percent of capital budget expenditures of manufacturing companies today are IT investments [18] and nearly 40 percent of the business reengineering expenses of companies in 1997 went to pay for information systems [14]. This is evidence that many firms are willing to devote considerable investment dollars in attempts to realize the promise of IT initiatives. IT encompasses production and coordination oriented technologies, ranging from client-server applications to executive information systems (EIS) and web-based conferencing [20]. Although we assume that new IT investments will accompany any organizational change to enhance communication, we have little conclusive research to explain the role of IT in such process change.

Successful implementation of process based change management is a key indicator of value in today’s global business. However, the change process is painstaking, slow, and often unsuccessful due to lack of fundamental management change. Radical organizational changes, introduced in the more excessive implementations of business process reengineering (BPR) [15], lend themselves more to process management and ownership. Nevertheless, the transition from traditional to a process based management is difficult and requires the full attention and commitment of the organization [16]. A recent study found an influence of nontechnology management issues, such as organizational involvement and team structure, on reengineering outcomes [13]. It seems that clear communication of a new vision, strategy, and process at all hierarchical levels can ease management change.

IT is believed to be an enabling factor for organizational change [5,24], by allowing work to be done in new and different ways. This perspective suggests that IT is a production technology rather than a coordination technology. However, whether this characteristic leads to successful organizational change is not clear. Some researchers have suggested that an organization’s structure depend on the technology it uses [28,32]. This line of research is focused on predicting changes that occur in response to using IT. IT is also viewed as a tool to enhance information-processing in an organization with implied standards of data and language [9]. A study examined the relation between IT investments and financial success, and concluded that only the ones who successfully integrate IT into their business processes are able to add value to the company [30].

Furthermore, IT is seen as having a mediating effect on communication, leading to new patterns of communication or changes in the content and quantity of existing kinds of communication: a coordination technology which encourages participation among workers and controls the way a decision is made, monitoring results, improving productivity of meetings, moreover enhancing the team’s decision-making [17]. Coordination is also argued as necessary for business processes that, exhibit interdependence, which is a fundamental principle in designing organizations [10]. Similarly teamwork is a representation of reciprocal task interdependency which may require intensive technology. Therefore, a link exists between organizational structure, task interdependency, and coordination technologies.

Cooperation is a key characteristic of process management in creating processes by crossing functional boundaries and allowing people with diverse knowledge and skills to form teams and perform together. These problems expand in global process teams where different culture and language occurs [26].

## 2. Prior research

This study focuses on research related to the influence of organization structure and coordination technologies to access, use, and deliver information with changing complexity. Fig. 1 shows the framework.

## 2.1. Organization structure and IT linkage

Allen and Hauptmann [2] investigated the influence of communication technologies on organizational structure for various tasks ranging from R&D to marketing and financial. They generated a conceptual model for organizations by investigating task interdependencies, duration, and communication technologies, such as e-mail, bulletin boards, and computer conferencing. Combining structure with technology, their emphasis was on understanding how the communication technologies improve knowledge transfer and coordination in a project team structure versus a traditional functional structure. They related coordination of subsystem with project organization and proposed the use of bulletin boards for project status and reporting. However, for the functional organization, knowledge transfer was needed and it is fulfilled by document search and retrieval systems, expert systems, and bulletin boards.

Kendall classified the technologies under two groups, production- and coordination-oriented. According to his classification, animation, hypertext, and client-server technologies fall under the production-oriented class, while communication media, group support systems (GSS), EIS and web-based conferencing are coordination-oriented technologies. Kendall pointed to the uncertainty concerning the technology’s value as a significant barrier for entry to these systems.

GSS are designed to improve organizational effectiveness by reducing communication barriers. Williams and Wilson [31] investigated the users perceptions of the impact of GSS on power and influence within the organization. They found that GSS are perceived to increase participation, improve access to information, reduce power distance to key individuals, and provide increased opportunities to influence the opinions of others.

Lee and Leifer [22], focused on information sharing as linking organizational with IS structures. Organizational technologies and the nature of work teams determined the level of information sharing requirements. Cross-functional unit integration and development of inter-organizational network systems were seen as achieving high levels of information sharing. George et al. [12] proposed three different architectures for organizational DSS tailored to downsizing, a focus on teams, and outsourcing. They argued that no single architecture could fully satisfy the support requirements of every organizational situation and change. Each architecture relied on communication, coordination, filtering, decision-making and monitoring technologies.

In a similar study [19], researchers examined the fit between decision type, organizational context characteristics, and decision processes as a determining factor for the best IS type. IS effectiveness criteria were determined for four different organizational design paradigms and an attempt has been made to align them with IS support, e.g. in a highly structured paternalistic paradigm, data processing systems will be more appropriate. The limiting factor of DSS utilization is further explained by differentiating cultural and management structures along organizational boundaries [29].

## 2.2. Study of individual and task differences for IT use

Previous research on the effective use of IT recognized the influence of individual difference. Orlikowski [27] suggested that organizational structure and culture, as well as an individual’s mental model influences implementation of groupware. Among the structural elements reviewed, reward systems, policies, work culture, and norms were found significant. Furthermore, Davenport [7] emphasized a need for human-centered information management in contrast to the standard IT view.

King and Xia [21] investigated communication media choice. Their study revealed that individual learning experiences of communication media influence a persons perceptions of media appropriateness. Also, their findings indicated that the traditionally rich media, such as face-to-face, group meetings, and telephone, are perceived to be more appropriate than emerging new media, such as groupware, e-mail, and electronic meeting systems. This agrees with an earlier study [11], where media choices and responses to communication constraints were examined. Consistent with contingency theory, face-to-face interaction is preferred for integrative projects. In the same study, researchers also indicated the significance of the malleability of IS and how contingency theory fails to explain the impact of interactive communication on group performance. The relationship between user participation and system success was examined and a positive link found between user participation and user satisfaction.

The influence of managerial incentives and control over the IT use has also been investigated [3]. The effectiveness of these incentives was found to depend on users’ ability to distinguish between various forms of incentives and control.

These studies suggest that, both organizational and individual differences influence the design of information and communication systems. Similarly, a recent study found significant influence of culture on the EIS use by senior management observing Mexican and Swedish managers [23].

The successful use of communication technologies rests on contributing to the needs of the organization structure and complementing process changes. Among the individual difference variables, ease of use and usefulness beliefs, length of tenure in workforce, level of education, the extent of prior experience, and participation in IT training have been related to IT acceptance [1].

## 3. Research model

Effective communication requires seeing others point of view. Effective communication through knowledge of those being contacted also involves being aware of and sharing different perspectives; this results in better coordination of activities and adapting [4,8]. This study analyzed information sharing, access, and delivery as significant determinants of communication effectiveness. The perceived relationships of IT, organization structure and procedures, as well as individual differences, were studied. Furthermore, the influence of verbal, written, and electronic modalities, i.e. communication media, was also analyzed.

Effective communication can be described as a combination of how well information is delivered, accessed, shared, and used.

The indicators of communication were studied under two groups of information flow, within departments or functions, intra-functional, and between functions, cross-functional. The first group of independent variables of the study are change factors and they consist of organization structure and procedures, information/office technologies adopted by the orga nization, and individual differences. The intermediate independent variables were task complexity and communication modality, i.e. media used to communicate. The effort investigated how these variables can be used to explain the perceived intra- and cross-functional communication effectiveness, which is defined as information delivery, access, sharing, and use.

Fig. 2 shows the research model. A survey was designed to determine which change factor is perceived to influence intra- and cross-functional communication. Then subjects were asked to value information delivery, access, sharing, and use with respect to task complexity. Subjects also stated the communication media they use for each type of task.

## 3.1. Independent variables of the model

## 3.1.1. Organization structure and procedures

Structure encompasses organizational structure, hierarchy, organizational culture, job requirements, and reward policies for empowerment. Although all these factors were defined, this study focused on the change in organization structure, from a functional to a cross-functional project structure and to a matrix organization. The survey question designed for this purpose is generic (Appendix A), and asks whether organizational factors beginning with organization structure have an influence on the communication. Since, at the time of the survey, the company was going through a restructuring, the subjects indicated that they answered the question with the organization structure in mind.

The procedures constitute the written guidelines and standard documents that exist within the organization. They explain how things are done and how the rules are passed on to staff. It was expected that a change in the organization structure from a functional to cross-functional would result in effective communication. Therefore,

Hypothesis 1. Cross-functional organization structure promotes access and sharing of information for high as well as low complexity tasks.

## 3.1.2. IT/office technology

In the study, IT/OT consisted of web-based technologies and collaboration support systems as well as hardware. Availability of IT was expected to ease and enhance information delivery, access, share, and use. This combined measure of communication effectiveness would, in turn, increase performance and efficiency of the work processes. IT availability should favor the high complexity tasks, these are the essence of all work processes.

## 3.1.3. Individual differences

Individual differences and personality traits have been also found to influence organizational communication. One of the traits investigated in a recent study is resistance to change influenced by self-interest as well as differences in race and ethnicity. As the individualists will not likely to share information and tend to be less cooperative, it is worthwhile to investigate these traits. Individual differences have found to affect the acceptance of new technologies. The research model recognizes attitude toward sharing information, information-processing, and decisionmaking styles, as well as training in communication and MIS as among the individual differences. However, the related survey question is generic and questions the overall influence of individual difference.

Hypothesis 3. Individual differences, specifically attitude towards sharing and training in MIS enhance information sharing and the quantity and format, i.e. information use.

## 3.1.4. Task complexity

The most complex processes encompass several functional boundaries of an organization [25]. Most often, integrating communication between cross-functional teams and the functional departments of origin has been found to be a key problem. Both administrative and operational exchange represent high complexity decision-making tasks, whereas low complexity tasks consist of news, announcements, and meeting schedules, this is also formal and informal information [6]. It is expected that communication effectiveness between high and low complexity tasks will differ. High complexity tasks are used for tracking progress, reporting, and knowledge transfer within and between project groups, and they are dynamic in nature. All means of delivery, access, sharing and use are important. However, low complexity tasks, such as announcements and administrative references, are discrete and available to the entire organization.

Hypothesis 4a. Information delivery, access, sharing and use are significant for high complexity tasks.

Hypothesis 4b. Information delivery and access are effective communication needs for low complexity tasks.

## 3.1.5. Communication media

Modality through which information is delivered plays a vital role in communication effectiveness. Verbal ways include face-to-face, telephone, group meetings, and word of mouth. Written refer to documents, fax, and company newsletter. Electronic communication includes e-mail, central database, service request system, and computer listing of purchasing information. The study, undertaken in the last 5 years of the 20th century, included the evolution of the Internet. Although aware of them, users are not fully engaged with electronic media, specifically when conveying and using information for operational tasks. Verbal and written methods are used extensively, whereas electronic ones are not fully used.

Hypothesis 5. Communication media interacts with organizational tasks, electronic modality is perceived to aid information delivery, access, sharing and use of high complexity tasks.

## 4. Research methodology and validation

The company selected for this study was a Turkish home appliance manufacturer with a billion dollars (US) in sales revenues. Its manufacturing facilities included five plants in the same country. The company produces and distributes a comprehensive range of home appliances internationally. It is also responsible for the design and manufacturing of its products. Downstream functions of distribution are performed with vertical integration. Some upstream functions of production however are supplied by others, mostly European businesses.

A survey was undertaken of a sample of the work force to determine the organizations communication awareness and determine possible IT requirements. It was designed by a cross-functional quality improvement team, with members from marketing, quality, IS, engineering, R&D, and service. Years of experience of the team and number of interviews conducted affected the design of the questions. A total of 104 individuals at the operational level and 15 middle managers involving 19 departments and seven organizational functions participated in the study. Employee demographics, such as years of experience and position, were also collected. Table 1 shows subject’s profile. Technical and nontechnical personnel in the sample have 3–5 years of experience on average, versus an average of 8 years of experience found in administrative personnel.

## 4.1. Criterion validity

Employees’ year of experience, job position, and work unit/department were used as external variables to measure the communication effectiveness. An analysis of variance revealed the main as well as interaction effects of these criteria. Work experience had no effect on information delivery, access, share, and use. Position, on the other hand, was associated with individual differences (F: 2.30, P > F: 0.01). The relationship between work unit (department) and organization structure was also found significant (F: 2.62, P > F: 0.001, F: 2.29, P > F: 0.006). The analysis also showed that inadequate system support varied for work units (F: 1.53, P > F: 0.09). Furthermore, procedural factors were significant for intra-functional communication of work units (F: 1.85, P > F: 0.03).

## 4.2. Construct validity

The survey was designed with four main sections, allowing employees to comment on the main causes of the intra- and cross-functional communication problems, the degree of ineffective communication encountered for varying tasks, and the communication media or information modalities used for communicating information related to operational, administrative (high complexity), and general (low complexity information. Each group of questions were to be answered using a 5-point Likert scale. For the influence of organization structure, IT, individual differences, and organizational procedures on intra and cross-communication, the two end points were very much influential and not influential at all. Prior research, indicates the use of these constructs. Internal consistency of these questions was found computing Cronbach’s alpha, as shown in Table 2. Moreover, internal consistency of communication effectiveness measures, i.e. the delivery, access, sharing, and use of information, was also computed. A total of seven communication effectiveness measures was used. Respondents scored the problems of communication effectiveness with the endpoints, very frequently encounter and never encounter.

Finally, internal consistency was computed for the verbal, written, and electronic communication modalities. Employees are asked to score their use of these with a 5-point scale ranging between very frequently use and never use one. The Cronbach alpha indicated a strong correlation of variables, with the exception of written communication. The reliability of written communication is 0.58. The reason may be related to the tools of writing, which vary from fax to wallboard, or to monthly newsletter, etc. each delivering information of varying complexity ranging from operational to general.

## 5. Data analysis and results

To test the hypotheses, SAS/GLM (general linear model) was utilized for the maximum likelihood estimations. Pearson correlation coefficients with probabilities ranging from 0.001 to 0.05 are considered statistically significant. Pearson coefficients for individual differences are not shown on the Fig. 3 due to probabilities over the threshold of 0.05. All correlation coefficients and associated probabilities between the four independent variables (organization structure, procedures, IT, individual differences) and communication effectiveness variables are shown in Table 4.

## 5.1. Organizational factors (structure and procedures)

Organizational factors (structure and procedures) are perceived to have the most influence on crossfunctional communication of especially high complexity operational tasks. Except for delivery time and excess information, all communication effectiveness measures, i.e. information delivery rate, information access and sharing are perceived to be associated with cross-functional organization structure. The company was going through a restructuring towards matrix structure and this survey showed that the work force, including middle management, strongly valued crossfunctional communication (solid arrows, which indicate cross-functional communication). Speed of delivery (delivery time) and excess information, which is represented in the questionnaire by receiving the unneeded information, were perceived to be associated mostly with IT and somewhat with procedures. The first hypothesis relating cross-functional structure with information delivery, access, sharing, and use is accepted, except for two sub-variables.

Subjects perceived organizational procedures to be the second most influential factor in intra as well as cross-communication. Procedures, rules of information flow, etc. are associated with most of the determinants of organizational communication effectiveness, such as delivery time, hierarchical filtration, information sharing of operational and administrative tasks, and accessibility of low complexity or informal information. Apparently, organization structure needs to be coupled with understandable and easy to follow procedures, rules, and policies. Easy access is mostly desired.

## 5.2. IT

IT: The GLM procedure shows overall significance of IT on cross-functional communication $( F = 5 . 6 6 ,$ $P > F \colon 0 . 0 8 )$ . The role IT plays differs between intraand cross-functional communication. Although information use was associated with all communication, this was partially true for other variables, such as information delivery and access.

Therefore, the Hypothesis 2a linking IT to information delivery, access, sharing and use is only partially accepted. Again here, besides being traditionally managed, the fact that developing countries began adapting computer based technologies after 1990s put them behind the developments in the West until the Internet revolutionalized the situation by becoming attractive and affordable to all nations.

Another finding was the interaction of task complexity and IT. For instance, while delivery time of operational information (personnel communication: 0.166, P ¼ 0:10) was perceived associated with IT, the same cannot be concluded for the other task types and other communication variables. Therefore, we partially accept Hypothesis 2b expecting the aid of IT for all high complexity tasks.

## 5.3. Individual differences

Individual differences was not perceived to affect communication effectiveness with the exception of excess information and delivery time (both above 0.05 probability threshold) for intra-functional communication. For the company involved in this study, cultural differences as well as lack of training are expected to be relevant. The fact that only delivery time and information quantity are related to individual differences shows that information is delivered to a department manager whothenforwardedtheinformation to his (therewere no female managers prior to restructuring) staff.

Therefore, the third hypothesis relating individual differences to information sharing and use cannot be fully confirmed. This finding contradicts previous research, where individual differences played a role in IT use. However, it is also equally important to note that those studies examined US businesses.

## 5.4. Task complexity

Information delivery rate is perceived important for high complexity tasks and delivery time for both low and high complexity tasks. This is a reflection of how delivery of information is restricted to some individuals or others may have to wait a long time to obtain it. Another communication issue is information accessibility, defined as hierarchical filtration and ease of access for all three types of tasks. Personnel as well as managers perceive information access important for organizational communication but individuals have to go through an exhaustive search to determine its location. This creates a frustrated and inefficient work environment.

Hypothesis 4a is accepted; information delivery, access, sharing, and use are significant for high complexity tasks. And Hypothesis 4b is accepted for low complexity tasks.

## 5.5. Communication media

When subjects were asked to comment on the degree the communication media used, verbal modality of operational, administrative and general information, i.e. high and low complexity tasks was found to be the most frequently used communication mode (mean 2.67; S.D. 1.07). Written modality was the second most frequently used. Electronic modality, on the other hand, was the least utilized media mainly due to its limited availability.

The survey reported that for communication of low complexity task information, the monthly newsletter, telephone, and face-to-face meetings were among the most often used. For the communication of high complexity tasks, i.e. administrative and operational information, the telephone was found to be very frequently used, along with written documents, face-to-face meetings, and fax. Table 5 shows that all three modalities are found significant for the communication of high complexity (operational, administrative) and low complexity (general) task information.

An analysis of variance using SAS/GLM MAN-OVA procedure showed the effect of each communication modality on organizational communication parameters of delivery, access, sharing and use. Overall effects of verbal, written and electronic modalities on communication effectiveness were also analyzed using four criterion tests (Wilks’ lambda, Hotelling, Roy’s Maximum Root, Pillai’s Trace). The MANOVA results for the effects of communication media on information delivery, access, sharing and use with corresponding F values are presented in Table 6. The effects with F values significant between 0.01 and 0.13 probabilities are shown as shaded blocks. F values and corresponding probabilities P > F, are reported above the blocks.

Operational information delivery was found to be related to verbal and written modalities, while use of operational information was associated with written and electronic modalities. Subjects perceive electronic modality to ease the excess information by allowing the users to receive only what they need. However, verbal, written and electronic modalities influenced adminis trative information delivery, access and use, while sharing was influenced by the use of verbal and written modalities. Similar results were found for general information use and access, emphasizing the influence of verbal and written communication modalities. Therefore, Hypothesis 5 is partially accepted.

Subjects were also asked about their preferred communication modalities. Communicating operational information, all three were preferred. For the administrative information ranging from a specific accounting information to various approval forms, verbal and written modalities were preferred, telephone, news board and written documents were the most commonly used. Finally, when communicating low complexity informal information, all three were preferred. These results show that our hypothesis of task type and communication modality interaction does indeed exist.

## 6. Conclusion

This study, based on a real case from a non-US business, illustrates the importance of organizational structure as well as the interaction of task complexity with communication media and IT. IT alone was not perceived significant for effective communication. A common perception was that electronic and written modalities improved the way that information was represented. Information could also be filtered better using electronics. Overall, IT was associated with information delivery and use, i.e. speeding up and enhancing the use of the information.

Individual differences were not associated with organizational communication. Furthermore, crossfunctional communication was positively associated with organization structure, signalling a need for restructuring: more hierarchy in the organization will result in more communication problems. Communication effectiveness is found dependent on task complexity and also the influence of communication media. Employees prefer to use all communication media: verbal modalities, such as telephone, face-toface, and group meetings are frequently used for formal communication of operational tasks.

Survey results reported here were presented to the company’s top management. The traditional functional based organization needed to be modified to a process based and cross-functional organization structure. As the competition grew, and the economy became unstable management realized a change was needed.

The work force was amazingly receptive to the change. The critical organizational processes were identified, based on global market competitiveness and company’s strengths and weaknesses. The company’s current status and its analysis were discussed among the restructuring team consisting of functional managers, product development engineers, and specialists from various functions. When presented and shared with other managers, this sharing of rich information created ownership, enabling management to re-evaluate their views and opinions. The organizations product development department was restructured to allow a multifunctional project team approach in every independent product center located at different manufacturing sites.

The study indicated IT as an influencing factor for effective communication following organizational structure and procedures. A mainframe based IT had been in use by the company for many years, but new forms of organizations required more and more information as well as resource sharing. This organizational change showed the importance of network technology for fast and accurate delivery, ease of access, and sharing of information.

Electronic communication created awareness and efficient use of operational, administrative and general information. It also substantially reduced paperwork and reduced duplication of organizational reports generated by different functions. Besides the formal communication that IT provides, informal communication means are also important, group meetings among cross-functional groups have increased following the change efforts. This created exchange of ideas, involvement and awareness, all necessary for an effective communication. In fact, 2 years after the above changes, Human Resources reported a 25 percent decrease in the personnel turnover and increased job satisfaction. Brainstorming and decision sessions between management and staff played a significant role in these outcomes.
