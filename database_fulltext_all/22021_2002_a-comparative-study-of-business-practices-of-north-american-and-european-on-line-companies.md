# A comparative study of business practices of North American and European on-line companies

Nicholas Protogeros \*

Heletel Ltd., Thessaloniki Technology Park, 57001 Thessaloniki, Greece

Accepted 21 May 2001

## Abstract

During the last year, a large research study was funded by the European Commission; its aim was to determine on-line company characteristics and their Web business strategies while assessing their Web site efficiency. A comparative comparison of North American company practices and comparable ones in Europe was also made. Some of the interesting results are presented here. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Electronic commerce; Internet; Statistical analysis; Comparative study

## 1. Introduction

Electronic commerce (EC) is having a tremendous impact on the business environment [13]. According to The Economist [15], more than 90% of top managers believe that the Internet will transform or have a major impact on the global marketplace. Forrester Research, Inc. [10] shares this view, by forecasting that business-to-business EC in America alone will double every year over the next 5 years, surging from 40 billion euros to 1.2 trillion euros in 2003. Goldman, Sachs & Co. predicts that business-to-business EC will be worth 1.4 trillion euros by 2004. Business-toconsumer EC is forecast to rise from 7.5 billion to 115 billion. Britain and Germany are expected to reach similar growth patterns about 2 years later, while France and Italy will lag behind for another 2 years.

It is evident that countries and firms taking rapid steps towards EC will have a clear competitive advantage in future. The interest of many firms focuses on this new technology, searching for ways to participate in the new era of the digital economy. This is not only because it is more efficient and easy to use but also because it creates a totally different new arena where companies are challenged to innovate. This new type of business brings the promise of high revenue.

To have an effective Web presence, small and medium enterprises (SMEs) need guidance and information. They need to identify the best practices, the strategic choices, the problems and the requirements of a Web site. However, there is a severe lack of resources as regards information and decision support tools tailored to their needs. This paper is such a tool and is the result of the Best Business Web Sites study, which originated from a call for tenders of the Enterprise DG of the European Commission. It was conducted during 1999. The objective was to deduce the effective Web business strategies and identify the key characteristics and best practices that make a Web site successful.

This study analysed 200,000 business Web sites to get information on their structural characteristics. It also obtained accurate sectoral information for 80,000 of these sites, while 2129 answers to a questionnaire survey were collected in two ways: an automated process collected information on 200,000 business Web sites, and a questionnaire survey registered 2129 responses of Web business site owners/Webmasters.

## 2. History and current status

EC embraces electronic data interchange (EDI) transactions [12] as well as simple e-mail communications. EC is increasingly adopted by SMEs, using the whole spectrum of available technologies, with the prominent use of the World Wide Web.

The scope of this study was not limited to the sales potential of the Web, but also considers other ways business is being revolutionised by EC. Many companies that are not using it for direct transactions are using electronic communications to transform their internal systems. Businesses are constantly reinvented, achieving lower costs, more rapid response, greater customisation and the development of new products and services.

Global Reach [11] reports that the number of people using the Internet world wide has passed the 200 million mark. The Computer Industry Almanac [14] forecasted that there would be over 327 million Internet users by year-end 2000, of which 102 million would be European, 22.9 million from Germany, 17 million from the UK, 12.6 million from France, 10.6 from Italy, 5.4 from The Netherlands, 4.4 from Spain, and 3.7 from Sweden. The total on-line population is expected to rise to over 717 million users by the end of 2005; 202 million of these will be Europeans.

Businesses are also taking steps to establish their presence on the Web. Although the majority of on-line companies had only one domain, the average was six registered domains per company. Much of the growth in numbers occurred outside of America, as sites organised globally. Consumer-oriented Web sites comprised half of all publicly-listed Web sites.

Andersen Consulting shared a similar view by reporting that European companies dramatically increased their use of EC in the 12 months prior to summer 1999. The report estimated that the Western European Internet market will be worth 400 billion euros by 2003, with 170 million users.

There are differences between companies though, small businesses (10–99 employees) are less than half as likely to have a Web site as medium-sized businesses, and micro-businesses are almost a sixth as likely.

## 3. Methodology

Three different datasets were employed in the various analyses presented in this report. The first was collected by the Java robot (an automated process), and referred to approximately 200,000 sites on the Internet. These data include sites from all over the world. The examined variables were: the freshness of information of each site, the number of available pages, the traffic of the site, the number of available links to other sites, and the ‘speed’ of the site.

The sites for which it was possible to collect additional information comprised a smaller dataset (80,000); this was also collected automatically by the Java robot. The additional variables were the ‘nationality’ and the sectoral ‘identity’ of each site.

The last dataset was generated from an electronic questionnaire, which was available at Heletel’s Web site. Companies accessed by the Java robot were invited to complete the electronic form.<sup>1</sup> Some replies were discarded, due to the fact that very few of the questions were answered (what percentage was enough—any special fields needed?).

The questionnaire had five basic sections: the first on the organisation of the Web sites; then the Internet strategy of the firm; third, the overall impact of the Internet investment; and the fourth and fifth parts contained questions on basic parameters of the firms.

Those who completed the questionnaire had the opportunity to choose between several potential answers. These can be classified into two general categories.

1. In multiple choice questions, when a specific reply was chosen then any other answers were ignored because it made no sense. For instance, the question: ‘‘Is your company a branch in a group of companies?’’. Possible answers were - ‘‘yes, owned more than 25% by a big corporation’’, - ‘‘yes, but owned less than 25% by a big corporation’’, or

\- ‘‘no’’. (There was always also the option not to answer the particular question.)

2. When there was the possibility of multiple answers.

For instance, ‘‘Does the Internet investment improved communication’’, could be answered as: ‘‘We have replaced existing communication media (phone, fax, telex, etc.)’’, or ‘‘We have developed internal communication’’, or both these answers. (Again, there was also the alternative not to answer at all.)

Several types of statistical analysis were employed to study the characteristics of the Internet sites [1–9], as well as of the most successful business sites.

The basic statistics included the calculation of the frequencies, percentages, and averages for each option of each question of the questionnaire, as well as of the technical characteristics acquired by the Java robot process.

## 4. The profile of the on-line company

An examination of the customers’ profile of the Internet firms shows that a little more than half of the

Web sites are targeting the consumer (54%), while the rest 46% are business-to-business Web sites (Fig. 1).

Nevertheless, the customer profile of the European firms operating on the Internet (based on the questionnaire results) is significantly different from that of North American firms (USA and Canadian). More than 50% of the American enterprises have individuals as customers (57% versus 39% for the European firms), whereas the clientele of firms located in the European Union are other companies (the respective percentage is 61%, where the American companies have 43%). This difference may be an indication that the EC type in North America is more ‘business-toconsumer’, whereas EC in Europe is more ‘businessto-business’.

Moreover, the American firms have more customers than the European companies. This fact corroborates the difference between the EC types followed. As the American firms are more ‘consumer’ oriented it is a matter of course that they have more customers (Fig. 2).

The European companies on the Internet also appear to have higher turnover than American firms. In addition, European firms employ more people. Sixty percent of European firms employ fewer than 10 employees, while the figure for the American companies is 67%. In general, most of the on-line companies are ‘‘micro,’’ small in terms of employment (Fig. 3).

## 5. Business characteristics of Web sites

Even though companies are taking fast steps to implement EC technologies, they are still in the early stages. The companies that have integrated their Web site in their operations comprise only 14% of the sample. Most companies are still using their site mainly as an electronic brochure (41%). However, this is more likely to be true for the EU, where 45% of the companies follow this pattern in comparison to 39% for the US. American sites tend to be more in the e-business gallery category. This is another indication of the orientation of the American sites toward on-line sales and the consumer (Fig. 8).

![](/api/attachments/5VSK27RC/fulltext/images/ac4553928180f842746dca40808bcf3c114fea478191aa2e9f9cf5b55f92abf2.jpg)  
Fig. 8.

The results are in accordance with other surveys, such as that conducted by Booz Allen and the EIU (http://www.bah.com). They report that survey respondents know that they must transform their companies and that most still have a long way to go to capture the benefits of the Internet. Most companies (89%) have simple Web sites offering general information today, but only 56% are providing some customer service via the Web, and still fewer (37%) have an ‘‘e-business’’ capability, whereby they can conduct transactions on-line.

More conclusions were drawn by the initial motivation/reason for the Web venture of a company. Most of the companies on the Internet had, as their primary target, to establish their presence in this new arena of competition (77%), or to explore new media (47%). The creation of a Web site was also deemed to be a necessary tool for penetrating international markets (30%), while often it was the initiative of one person in the company (38%). This should, however, be kept in perspective, since more than 60% of the companies analysed had fewer than 10 full time employees, and so it is normal for one person to take the initiative in setting up a Web site.

There are some significant differences between American and European companies. The number of American companies that went on the Web because of the competitive pressure is almost double that of the EU companies. On the other hand, EU companies cite the urge to compete in international markets more than American ones, e.g. the French market is considered ‘‘foreign’’ to a UK firm, and thus the need for further European economic integration is clearly demonstrated (Fig. 9).

A more detailed analysis was made of the expectations of the Web site owner. The most widely expected result was the promotion of products or services, and the ability to be noticed and identified. Almost half of the respondents wanted to achieve sales; in general, they were successful.

What is more striking is that, companies that did not anticipate a particular benefit from their Web site nevertheless achieved it. For example, companies that anticipated breaking into international markets accounted for 29% of the total, while 53% of the total actually did. Also though only 30% expected to satisfy customers demands, 49% did. On the other hand, companies were bad at achieving an interaction between sales and customers, interaction between R&D, marketing, and suppliers, and the developmentof a new Web business.

Apparently, a domain name is not necessary for exploiting Web opportunities. Even more striking is that 91% of the companies achieved a business deal as the result of their Web site: indeed, many companies went on-line only a few months before the study and did not have the time to conclude a business deal.

The majority of companies (64%) have an annual in-house Web site budget of <5000 euros (taking all expenses, such as hardware, software and salaries, into account) (Fig. 10).

The annual Web site budget contracted to other companies presents a similar picture (taking all expenses into account): 81% of the companies spent less than 5000 euros (Fig. 11).

These results do not differ greatly from the results in The World Wide Webmaster study by YellowWindow (http://www.yellowwindow.be), which took place in March–April 1998 and found that the cost of design and maintenance of a Web site was relatively low (71%over<5000euros,andonly2%over50,000euros).

European companies appear to spend more money on their Web sites, regardless of whether this refers to in-house Web budgets or whether the Web site construction was subcontracted to other companies. This finding agrees with ActivMedia Research (http:// www.activmedia.com), which reports that European firms spend more than twice as much per Web site than North American firms. Data collected for the ‘‘Real Numbers Behind Net Profits 1999’’ study shows that Europe will average 73,500 euros’s investment per site while North America reported an average investment of 31,000 euros.

A point where there is significant divergence between companies is the level of the person(s) in charge of the Web site. The general manager of the firm is most commonly in charge of the Web site. However, for non-SMEs, the first choices are the communication or marketing manager of the firm, or a full time Webmaster. Companies providing Web-based services are more likely to have a full time Webmaster in charge. Industrial companies tend to appoint the Marketing manager to be in charge of their Web sites. Furthermore, there is a difference between European and American companies. In most of the European companies, the CEO of the company or a committee manages the Web site (almost 60% compared with 41% for US and Canadian firms). The role of a fulltime Webmaster is more common in American companies (22%, compared with 17% of the European businesses) (Fig. 12).

Almost half of the companies’ Web sites are maintained with less effort than the equivalent of one person working full-time on their maintenance.

The non-SMEs, in contrast, have more than five fulltime persons working for the Web site. There are no significant differences between the North American and European firms on this point (Fig. 13).

More than 85% of Web sites today are available in only one language, almost always English. However, a company can get a lot more attention by catering for people in other countries in the native language and actively draw them to its Web site. As would be expected, European sites appear to be much more multilingual than American ones. ## 6. Specific characteristics of the different on-line business strategies

On-line companies have four different business objectives on the Web. The following specific information was derived for each.

## 6.1. Promote/advertise/create brand awareness

Companies have many means to promote their business and to strengthen their brand name on the Web. Almost half that actively promote their Web sites engage in some kind of paid promotion, while almost all engage in some kind of free promotion. It is noteworthy that the American companies are slightly more willing to pay than the Europeans.

The European companies utilise methods like targeted e-mail, co-operating on-line with trusted third parties (such as banks, government initiatives, etc.), e-mail newsletters, or participating in newsgroups more intensely than their American counterparts. American companies seem to be more wary about e-mail campaigns, probably because of the magnitude of the spamming problem there (Fig. 15).

## 6.2. Increase turnover/market share/achieve sales

One of the crucial differences between European and American firms, according to the results of the questionnaire, is the payment system for on-line sales. More than half (55%) of the European companies do not use the Internet for sales. The figure for the American businesses is 8% lower. One restricting factor for on-line sales for European firms appears to be the low percentage of credit card use on the Web. Only 18% of the EU firms use credit cards for on-line sales, whereas more than 32% of the American firms do. Needless to say, this under-development of such a convenient means has a heavier impact on small and medium-sized enterprises.

Security is a major issue. Nevertheless, we found that only 12% of the sites have a secure port configured to handle encrypted communications. Nonetheless, it is highly possible that many sites that offer information redirect their visitors to another site with secure port capabilities to complete a transaction (Fig. 16).

## 6.3. Improve interaction with external partners (customers/suppliers)

When it comes to customer service and support provided by the companies, most firms respond to a potential query or a problem within a day. Almost all of these responses are personalised (98%). There are no significant differences on either the average time or the type of response between the European and American companies.

## 6.4. Improve interaction within the company (processes/organisation)

Companies that aim at improving interaction within them account for only 57% of the total questionnaire respondents. Almost 40% of the companies aim at developing a new business specifically for the Web. Other practices that utilise this strategy have low popularity, since they presuppose the existence of a well defined company structure and formed business processes. This was not true for more than 60% of the sample analysed. However, larger companies have this objective for their Web operations (Fig. 17).

## 7. Conclusions

In general, on-line companies are still in their early stage of development with low revenue and only a few employees. However, there are differences in the USA. Specifically, the average European firm was a larger company (or belonged to a group of companies) with more employees and higher turnover than the average American firm. The European firms also had fewer customers, on average, than the American firms.

From the above, it was concluded that the European Web is more business driven, while the American Web is more consumer driven. Business-to-business Web sites accounted for 61% in the EU, compared with 43% in North America. The figures stood at 39% for business-to-consumer Web sites in the EU and 57% in America. It seems that most American Web sites are targeting consumers with on-line sales, while most EU sites are targeting other businesses.

The examination of the questionnaire answers confirmed our belief that businesses are not only using the Web as a shopping outlet. The business strategies that were identified were classified as follows:

1. promote/advertise/create brand awareness;

2. increase turnover/market share/achieve sales;

3. improve interaction with external partners (customers/suppliers);

4. improve interaction within the company (processes/organisation).

Of course a company might have more than one of these strategies. It came as no surprise that 52% of the surveyed companies follow all of these strategies. The only one that is not followed widely is the Interaction within the company (57%). The other three strategies are each followed by more than 90% of the companies.

The Web business environment is evolving so rapidly that it is difficult to identify the best business practices. Companies cannot wait, but try to identify what works by doing it. Learn and ‘launch’ has become ‘launch’ and ‘learn’.
