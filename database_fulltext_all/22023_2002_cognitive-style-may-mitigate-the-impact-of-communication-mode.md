# Cognitive style may mitigate the impact of communication mode

Reza Barkhi\*

Department of Accounting & Information Systems, Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Polytechnic Institute & State University, Blackburg, VA 24061, USA

Received 7 February 2000; received in revised form 20 February 2001; accepted 29 May 2001

## Abstract

With the proliferation of Internet-based applications for collaborative work such as group decision support system (GDSS), traditional face-to-face (FTF) interactions are being augmented or replaced by screen-to-face (STF) technology. Extant research results find that some individuals prefer FTF mode for interaction and would feel frustrated under an STF mode, but others may feel very comfortable using electronic STF applications. This paper evaluates the influence of cognitive style on the perception of individuals negotiating over FTF or STF communication modes. Cognitive style explains how individuals prefer to receive information and what methods they use to process that information. The results suggest that when individuals are faced with a mixed-motive negotiation task, some cognitive styles mitigate the effects of communication modes while other cognitive styles are not sensitive to it. Hence, cognitive style and communication modes have interaction effects. © 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Communication channel; Cognitive style; Negotiation; Mixed-motive; Group decision support system

## 1. Introduction

Group decision support system (GDSS) research has produced a large body of theoretical and practical findings $[16]$ . Much of the research has compared face-to-face (FTF) groups that use or do not use group decision aids $[23,33,36]$ . Today, the traditional FTF interactions are being augmented or replaced by screen-to-face (STF) interactions enabled by Internet-based applications that provide decision support tools for individuals in a “group”. This has prompted research on the impact of communication modes $[9,11,14,17,21,26,30,31,35,41,57,58]$ .

One set of theories suggests that communication mode influences group behavior and outcomes [12,39,

51]. The “rich” FTF mode provides high social presence and enables a personal and warm interaction, while the STF mode seems cold and impersonal. Another set of theories suggests that communication mode does not influence group behaviors and outcomes. Social processes determine communication patterns rather than media characteristics and meaning is socially constructed $[18,19]$ . Individuals interpret lean messages to construct rich meaning or critically evaluate lean messages to produce rich meaning $[32]$ .

A major activity of interacting groups is communication. Individuals may perceive the same communication mode differently. For example, while some prefer using email for personal messages, others prefer an FTF mode, where they can observe facial expressions, body gestures, and other non-verbal communication cues. For these individuals, non-verbal cues provide critical feedback about each individual's reaction to the information exchanged.

Two factors motivate the research reported here: first, there are opposing schools of thought on the influence of communication modes, and second, the theoretical and anecdotal evidence suggest that perceived properties of media vary by user and are subjective $[63]$ . I propose that individuals working on a mixed-motive task perceive each communication mode differently, depending on their cognitive styles. I limited the scope of this study to mixed-motive tasks $[38]$ . In such tasks, group members often have to compete with others but they also have to cooperate to find a solution that is acceptable by the group. These tasks are representative of many tasks in organizations and can be influenced by communication strategies $[5,6]$ .

This effort studied the impact of communication modes on perceived frustration, as well as on perceived trust. The frustration level may affect the likelihood and frequency of future use of the technology. Trust is a governance mechanism in exchange relationships, characterized by uncertainty, volnurability, and dependence $[4]$ . Trust is based on perception $[20]$ . The level of perceived trust may influence the truthfulness of the information and the level of cooperation between members of a group. Both trust and frustration levels experienced by individuals using different communication modes may be influenced by individual differences in cognitive styles. These variables may therefore be critical factors in explaining how individuals use GDSS for mixed-motive negotiation tasks.

## 2. A review of cognitive styles

Cognitive style describes how individuals prefer to receive information and what methods they use to process that information. The preferences are indications of the type of environment in which one feels most comfortable and works best. The theoretical work in this field originally developed by Jung [27] has been used to construct a reliable way to classify individuals in the Myers–Briggs type indicator (MBTI). The MBTI has been used by many decision theory researchers to study decision patterns of managers with their cognitive style [10,28,47,49,50].

MBTI classifies people according to the way they perceive (become aware of things, people, occurrences, and ideas) and the way they judge (come to a conclusion about what has been perceived) [59]. People can perceive by sensing (S) or intuition (N). The S type prefers to receive information about the environment by using the five senses to become aware of the environment. This individual uses hard data that deals with specifics and concrete items. On the other hand, the N type is primarily interested in ideas and concepts and looks for information that describes hypothetical possibilities and, therefore, accepts qualitative and subjective information. This individual prefers to receive information by intuition—an indirect way of looking at things by using unconscious ideas or association. While the S type is usually accurate in catching details in the external world, the N type usually misses the detail and may even lose track of externals while he or she concentrates on the vivid and complex inner voice. Given this description, the N type is expected to be less frustrated with missing details in a virtual STF interaction and perceive it to be similar to FTF interaction, while the S type would possibly be frustrated by it. While the reality for the S type is what is observed through the senses, the N type interprets the reality with internally enhanced thought or knowledge.

Individuals process the information they receive to make a decision in different ways. Two approaches to making a decision are thinking (T) and feeling (F). The T type prefers to use a logical and impersonal process. On the other hand, the F type considers the subjective values in personal terms, as well as the personal values of the people affected by the decision. While the T type pays more attention to the logic of the task and finds it difficult to confront others to express his or her feelings, the F type pays more attention to how he or she feels about the outcomes and is sensitive to the feeling of others more than the logic of the task. The F type shows emotions and can frequently affect and arouse others' emotions, while the T type is embarrassed and usually hesitates to display emotions. Hence, the T type may prefer STF communication that reduces the effect of personalities involved, while the F type may feel more comfortable with a FTF mode.

Individuals can also be classified according to their relative interest in outer or inner worlds. Introverts (I) focus on their inner worlds of concepts and ideas, while extroverts (E) are more involved with the world of people and things. Extroverts are talkative, impulsive, and gregarious, while introverts tend to work alone, dislike distractions, and think before acting. Hence, the I type is likely to prefer STF while the E type is likely to feel more comfortable in a FTF mode.

## 3. A review of communication modes

Some individuals may perceive electronic communication mode as natural, efficient, and appropriate; others may perceive it as restrictive, unnatural, and lacking the capacity to be expressive. “Media richness theory” [55] classifies communication media along a continuum of low to high “richness”. Media richness is defined by four criteria that include feedback, multiple cues, language variety, and personal focus. The basic tenet of the theory is that “rich” media provide a better fit for tasks that involve high uncertainty, and high equivocality and “lean” media provide a better fit for tasks that involve low uncertainty and low equivocality.

One of the consequences of media richness is “presence”. The level of “social presence” varies with the level of richness that the communication media provide. Social presence is the aggregation and cognitive synthesis of such factors as physical appearance and posture, facial expression, direction of looking, mutual gaze, and the feeling of intimacy and trust as perceived by the individual to be present in the medium. Media providing low social presence are characterized as unsociable, insensitive, cold, and impersonal. Social presence may affect the way individuals perceive their discussions and their relationships with others. Research indicates that FTF mode is highest in social presence followed by video, multichannel audio, speakerphone, and written text. STF communication diminishes social presence by reducing the number of cues that are available in FTF interaction.

FTF contact seems to help in resolving conflicts and the development of trust $[60]$ . Trust may promote cooperation and may enhance the level of truthfulness in information that is exchanged among members of a group. Low social presence in a STF communication may make it harder to develop the mutual trust needed for cooperation for individuals: the “human touch”.

On the other hand, low social presence may filter expressive signs of competition among members who prefer to focus on the logic of the problem rather than the personalities involved (i.e. thinking type).

“Social influence theory” postulates that an individual’s media perception is, in part, socially constructed. The theory assumes that: (1) perceived properties of media vary by user and the social context and hence are subjective; (2) not all features of media are equally salient to individuals and (3) making choices about media is retrospectively and subjectively rational. Research has proved some support for the social influence theory. However, it has been shown that the subjective perception of individuals about the richness of the media is consistent with the objective characteristics of richness defined by “media richness theory”. In other words, consistent with the objective definition of richness, empirical research has found that individuals perceive FTF and computer output as the extremes of the richness continuum.

The idea that “richer” is better has been challenged by the argument that it is the social processes surrounding media use that determine communication patterns rather than the media characteristics. While much of the prior research has been positivist, research has also used the interpretive approach to show that richness and leanness is not an inherent property of electronic communication; richness is an emergent property of the interaction of the electronic mail medium with its organizational context. Communicators interpret rich meaning from lean electronic messages, given that they are not passive recipients of data but are active producers of meaning. A different perspective to study electronic communication is “critical social theory” (CST) [43]. This perspective focuses on the listener’s or reader’s critique of the validity or correctness of what is being communicated and, if needed, the listener’s or reader’s emancipation from distorted communication. The hermeneutics and CST approaches can generate significant findings as the researcher studies communication within an interpretive paradigm. However, when the objective is to measure the differences in perception of different individuals about various communication modes, a positivist approach that is not subject to interpretation and does not extend the results beyond measurable criteria is insightful.

Communication issues have generated much research, because of the importance of communication in organizations. Theoretical arguments on media effects are conflicting—one school of thought suggesting media matters and another that media does not matter. There is also empirical evidence to show that different individuals “perceive” the same media differently [61]. The empirical research on media richness theories provides inconsistent results. Hence, there must be other factors that can explain the conflicting theoretical and empirical results. This paper suggests that while some individuals with certain cognitive styles “perceive” richness positively, others perceive it as being distracting. The impact of communication modes on individuals with different cognitive styles in computer-supported group decision making literature has received scant attention. This paper reports the results of an experiment that tests if cognitive style mitigates the effects of communication modes for individuals engaged in a mixed-motive negotiation task.

## 4. Hypotheses

## 4.1. Introversion/extroversion

Extroverts are talkative, impulsive, and gregarious while introverts tend to work alone, dislike distractions, and think before acting $[54]$ . These characteristics have lead researchers to conclude that extroverts prefer to express themselves using an FTF communication mode that is more expressive than the STF mode. This may suggest that extroverts will be more frustrated in an STF group and introverts will be more frustrated in an FTF group.

Such a generalization is likely to oversimplify the complexity of the situation, as it ignores the underlying task. This is one of the most important factors that affect group interactions. The impact of communication mode on introverts and extroverts may be different for different types of tasks.

In a mixed-motive negotiation task, members compete to protect their stakes. As extroverts express themselves in an FTF environment to protect their stakes, their expressive body language, facial expressions, and tone of voice are likely to intensify the competitive climate. The STF mode, on the other hand, can reduce the expressive capacity and filter signals, reducing the competitive intensity. It has been shown that high “presence” amplifies things: making a “good” situation better and making a “bad” situation worse [52]. Hence, in a win–lose competitive negotiation the capacity for self expression may be related to increased levels of frustration. In an STF group, extroverts will be less expressive and are limited to the written text on screen to express themselves. The STF mode blocks the cues that may intensify competitiveness, preventing a “bad” situation from becoming worse. Hence, the STF mode should be less frustrating for extroverts engaged in a mixed-motive negotiation task. This suggests the hypothesis as follows.

Hypothesis 1. An extrovert (E) faced with a mixed-motive negotiation task will perceive less frustration in an STF group than in an FTF group.

Introverts are generally engaged in their inner worlds of concepts and ideas. They can easily ignore environmental noise in an FTF group as they concentrate on their inner world of concepts and ideas. They will not be as affected by the communication mode, given their developed ability to ignore external noise. Hence, the impact of communication mode will not be so significant on introverts faced with a mixed-motive negotiation task. This suggests the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2. An introvert (I) faced with a mixed-motive negotiation task will perceive equal frustration in an STF group as in an FTF group.

## 4.2. Feeling/thinking

Given that an F individual tends to personalize the situation, his/her frustration can be positively correlated with how well the communication mode allows personalization of the frustrating situation. The F individual may not be able to “feel” and personalize the conflict as well in an STF group as in an FTF group. Not being able to “feel” and personalize the win–lose negotiation situation in an STF group, the F individual should perceive lower frustration in an STF group (i.e. lower capacity to personalize the conflict) than in an FTF group. The F individual becomes more frustrated in an FTF group, as this individual “feels”

his or her decision will affect who loses; personalizing such a negative environment can be frustrating and the FTF mode may amplify things, making a “bad” situation worse. This suggests the hypothesis as follows.

Hypothesis 3. An F individual faced with a mixed-motive negotiation task will perceive more frustration in an FTF group than in an STF group.

The level of trust between members influences the way they exchange information; this can have a significant impact on the effectiveness of the GDSS. Hypothesis 4, therefore, studies how the level of perceived trust is mitigated by communication mode for F individuals. In a mixed-motive task, it becomes important to develop trust in order to identify a mutually beneficial solution. The F individual prefers to personalize the interaction and will be better able to develop trust in an FTF group and is likely to perceive a higher level of trust in an FTF group than in an STF group.

Hypothesis 4. An F individual faced with a mixed-motive negotiation task will perceive more trust in an FTF group than in an STF group.

The T individual tends to emphasize the impersonal logical cues. Although the STF mode blocks body language, facial, and verbal cues, and may affect the level of personalization, it will not affect the logic of the negotiation task. Hence, the richness of the communication mode and the level of social presence should not affect the perception of the T individual who focuses on the logical cues. The communication mode should not significantly influence the individual's ability to focus on the logic of the problem because he or she has an inherent ability to downplay the personalities involved and emphasize the logic. This suggests two hypotheses as follows.

Hypothesis 5. A T individual faced with a mixed-motive negotiation task will perceive equal frustration in an FTF group as in an STF group.

Hypothesis 6. A T individual faced with a mixed-motive negotiation task will perceive equal trust in an STF group as in an FTF group.

## 4.3. Sensing/intuitive

The S type prefers to use sensory abilities to “sense” the environment and become aware of things, people, occurrences, and ideas. Such senses are partially blocked in STF groups relative to FTF groups. This suggests that an S individual will be more frustrated in an STF group than in an FTF group.

On the other hand, “presence” amplifies the situation. When the environment is negative, conflicting, and win–lose, the STF mode used to sense the environment is likely to reduce frustration. The richness of an FTF mode should intensify the win–lose negotiation, in turn, increasing the perceived frustration. Given that this reasoning suggests higher frustration in FTF groups as well as STF groups, Hypothesis 7 is non-directional.

Hypothesis 7. An S individual faced with a mixed-motive negotiation task will perceive equal frustration in an FTF group as in an STF group.

The S individual prefers hard data. STF mode forces members to “write” their messages. Written text relative to spoken words requires one to reflect longer on what is being communicated. Also, STF data is hard and cannot be denied once a message is sent. An S individual may find information communicated in an STF mode more trustworthy and credible than in FTF mode. In addition, the S individual may feel the higher level of competition and lower level of trust in an FTF group than in an STF group. The perception of higher competition may result in lower perceived trust in an FTF group. These two arguments suggest as follows.

Hypothesis 8. An S individual faced with a mixed-motive negotiation task will perceive more trust in an STF group than in an FTF group.

Unlike the S individual, who becomes aware of things directly through his/her senses, an intuitive (N) individual uses indirect perception by way of the unconscious. The intuitive type looks for information that describes hypothetical possibilities and accepts qualitative and subjective information. The N individual will “see through” the five senses in an FTF setting and intuitively understand that individuals engaged in a win–lose negotiation are self interested and competitive. The cognitive dissonance between individual's intention (to compete) and action (try to cooperate) in an FTF environment can increase the frustration of the N individual. This suggests the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 9. An N individual faced with a mixed-motive negotiation task will perceive more frustration in an FTF group than in an STF group.

The N individual relies on strong intuition to perceive the degree of trust in the group and is generally so engrossed in pursuing the possibilities that he or she seldom looks intently at the facts. A mixed-motive negotiation task may induce members not to trust their true preferences and payoffs. However, as the N individual constructs a hypothetical reality, his or her intuitive sense will help incorporate essential elements of reality. This should help develop an accurate estimate of the level of trust, irrespective of the communication mode. Hence:

Hypothesis 10. An N individual faced with a mixed-motive negotiation task will perceive equal trust in an FTF group as in an STF group.

## 5. Research design

A total of 104 senior and junior students in several sections of a business course made up the subjects. The course was required for all business students, and thus the subjects represented a cross section of all “business school” majors. Subjects were randomly assigned to FTF or STF groups without a priori knowledge of the subjects’ cognitive styles. Members of FTF groups were seated in a meeting room and could communicate both verbally and via a computer during the problem solving session. Members of STF groups were physically separated, did not have FTF contact and communicated via the communication subcomponent of the GDSS (i.e. STF).

Dependent variables were the level of perceived trust and the level of perceived frustration. Trust is a critical factor when one party does not have direct control over the actions of the other, the decision is important, and the environment is uncertain [37]. Trust is promoted as an essential ingredient to a functioning group in virtual organizations and can affect the effectiveness of virtual structures [42]. It is an important aspect of interpersonal relationships, can affect information exchange, perceived risk, and the effectiveness of groups [40,44]. Communication media used in a negotiation task can influence trust, and hence, affect the decision process and outcomes [56].

The level of frustration influences the level of comfort with technology, and hence, the future use of a particular technology. Independent variables were communication mode and cognitive style. Communication mode has two values (FTF and STF) and cognitive style components have six values (I/E, F/T, S/N). Individuals were randomly assigned to either an FTF or an STF group. After the experiment, the subjects answered questions that measured their perceived trust and perceived frustration using a 7-point Likert scale instrument. About 2 weeks later, the subjects filled out a validated cognitive style questionnaire that identified the cognitive styles of the decision makers [46].

## 5.1. The task

A mixed-motive negotiation task was used $[2]$ . In the experiment, the managers of three departments of a hypothetical company formed each group. Members try to negotiate with other members to develop a production plan. The task is constructed so that a solution that is optimal for one department is not acceptable by others. Hence, to find a comprehensive solution acceptable to all where everyone has to give up something, group members need to negotiate.

The groups have a GDSS, specifically developed for this task to satisfy task–technology fit requirement $[62]$ , to aid them in making the decision. Although the GDSS provides models with logic of the problem and can aid each decision maker to find an optimal solution, the information necessary to find the pareto optimal solution is divided among the members. The members thus have different preferences and asymmetrical information: they need to exchange information to resolve equivocality. The members negotiate and exchange information depending on the level of trust that develops in the group.

## 5.2. GDSS features

Both FTF and STF groups were supported by the GDSS, which provided modeling and optimization capabilities, information exchange facilities, “what-if” capability, and the ability to capture group memory. The GDSS is a level two GDSS $[13]$ because of its modeling capability. It provides process support (i.e. information exchange via pre-defined templates), task support (i.e. group memory and “what-if” capability), and task structure (i.e. problem formulation and optimization capability) $[45]$ . The GDSS provides communication support (real-time chat, and pre-defined templates), some process structuring (group memory), and capabilities for information processing (evaluate information, cross-impact analysis). A pilot experiment identified some features that pilot subjects requested and the GDSS was modified accordingly. Subjects confirmed that there was a task–technology fit between the GDSS technology and the task.

The measures in this study were collected at the individual level of analysis, given that the nature of research question deals with individual cognitive style and individual perceptions of frustration and trust about the communication mode.

## 7. Discussion

Two factors motivated this research. First, there are opposing schools of thought on communication media effects. Second, there is theoretical and anecdotal evidence to suggest that different individuals have different preferences for the same communication mode. Given that there is a proliferation of Internet-based applications that reduce the need for FTF interactions in groups, I felt a need to conduct this research. Today, software interfaces for collaborative groups can be designed and configured to individual differences. Hence, there is theoretical and practical interest in individual differences and how they perceive communication modes to design GDSS systems that understand their users. One of the main results of this study is that communication mode, and cognitive style have interaction effects for a mixed-motive negotiation task.

The results indicate that some cognitive styles are more sensitive to communication modes than others. The level of frustration and the level of trust for individuals with certain cognitive styles were significantly mitigated by communication mode used.

As depicted in Table 3, the influence of communication mode on the frustration of introverts is not significant. Introverts may filter the rich environment to make it lean by ignoring what they perceive as noise. On the other hand, extroverts are more frustrated in an FTF group than in an STF group when faced with a mixed-motive negotiation task. Extroverts may not be able as effectively as the introverts to construct richness when they use lean media.

The result that for a mixed-motive (i.e. win–lose) negotiation task, extroverts are more frustrated in a FTF group than in a STF group is different from what cognitive style theories may suggest.

The S individual perceives higher trust in a negotiation task when he or she uses an STF mode than an FTF mode. The S individual is better able to sense the competition in a FTF setting. This results in differences in perceived trust.

The T individual is able to overcome the effects of communication mode and can do a better job focusing on the logic of the task instead of the personalities involved than can the S individuals. Hence, the T individual is not affected by the communication mode.

## 8. Conclusion

Cognitive style has captured our imagination and there is evidence that it explains decision choices, preferences, and perceptions $[1,3,8,15,22,24,25,29,34,48,53]$ . This study links cognitive style with communication mode used by individuals to communicate with others. Figs. 1–4 display the relationship between communication mode and cognitive style on the level of frustration and trust. The figures illustrate that cognitive style can mitigate the influence of communication modes. There is an interaction effect between communication mode and cognitive style for Figs. 1, 2 and 4 where the lines cross. Although there is no interaction in Fig. 3, the figure shows that an F individual's perceived trust is sensitive to communication mode while the T individual is not sensitive to communication mode.

This research is not without limitations. First, one has to be careful about extending the results to other tasks. Second, the composition of groups was not studied here and it may affect the perception of individuals. Third, we only studied one measure of individual differences (i.e. cognitive style) and many other ways exist to study individual differences that may be linked to the use of communication modes. Also, we only used two perception measures (i.e. level of frustration and level of trust) and there are many other perception variables.

Given that experience with communication modes has determining effect on the perceived appropriateness and use of media $[7]$ and that different individuals perceive communication modes differently, experience may mitigate the influence of cognitive style. For example, an extrovert engaged in a negotiation task may need to develop a higher level of experience with computer-mediated communication than does an introvert.
