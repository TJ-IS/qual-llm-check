# Specification of a capability-based IT classification framework

Paul Mulligan $^{*}$

Tomasso Hall #325, Babson College, Babson Park, MA 02457-0310, USA

Received 7 July 2000; received in revised form 3 February 2001; accepted 26 May 2001

## Abstract

The effort discussed here examined the issue of information technology (IT) classification within the financial services industry. Phase I utilized a Delphi study, involving 31 participants from 11 organizations, to provide initial specification of a capability-based typology of IT. Phase II of the research was a multiple case study that further refined the construct specifications and explored issues in technology application. Results of the Delphi and case study analysis suggested that differentiation within the IT construct occurred along a capability-based continuum that incorporated four primary IT attributes. These attributes included system scope, system focus, system accessibility, and the level of IT integration. © 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information technology; IT classification; Capability-based IT differentiation

## 1. Introduction

Service industry proponents frequently cite the sector's expanding contribution to the US GDP and burgeoning employment figures [32]. Critics respond by noting the reputation of service as contributing to lagging productivity growth rates, disappointing quality performance, and restrictive delivery systems [3]. Diagnosing the pathologies of the service sector is a complex and challenging endeavor. A promising starting point is the intersection between information technology (IT), the factory of the service sector, and the underlying service operation task, which propel service delivery.

Many believe that IT can resolve critical service sector competitive issues, including productivity, quality, delivery, and flexibility [33,36]. Services annually invest over US\$ 100 billion in IT, a rate that results in the sector's aggregate ownership of $85\%$ of the installed information- and communication-based technology in the United States [34]. However, the returns on technology investments are mixed, at best. In fact, over investment in IT is cited by some as a cause of poor service productivity growth, while others have gone so far as to call IT a 'service sector sinkhole' [15].

In the past, IT managers had few options when selecting what type of IT to deploy and the existing choices where typically scale constrained—large systems for large tasks, small systems for small tasks. Managers have many IT options today and the choices are no longer scale dependent. Early results from this research program suggest that organizations only benefit from having additional IT options when managers can make informed decisions regarding IT selection. Results also suggest that the culture of many organizations appears to encourage either selection of the status quo, the IT currently present in the organization, or acquisition of the latest ‘hot’ technology.

Researchers in the manufacturing sector expend considerable effort and thought in studying the match between task and technology. This relationship proved to be significant in improving several measures of performance in process management and in new product development [42]. No similar research that broadly links the underlying task to technology selection is available in the service sector. Recent research revealed the significance of task–technology fit in a service environment but did so by comparing two systems in a single task environment. This research establishes a technology typology that will facilitate broader comparisons of technology across diverse service task environments.

Development of such a typology is valuable to both researchers and practitioners. Both continue to grapple with the perplexing problems of return on technology investment, the role of IT in process design and the strategic application of IT in the service sector $[1,11]$ . Practitioners can utilize this typology to specify technology choice decisions better and to comprehend the intricacies of melding legacy and emerging technology more clearly. These factors become increasingly important as organizations continue to commit resources to Internet technology and redirect maintenance-focused spending to more strategic IT initiatives. Parallel research resulted in the development of a corresponding service task typology, which, when combined with the IT typology, facilitated examination of task–technology fit issues and the exploration of the interaction between task type and IT capability $[26]$ .

## 1.1. Research questions

A long-range objective of this program is to observe and analyze the interaction between service task and IT capability. But performing this type of analysis mandated the development of a framework for classifying IT within the context of the service enterprise. Most existing technology typologies focused on production technology or the tangible components of the machine technology associated with IT. Advances in IT range and flexibility encourage the development of an IT-based typology that focuses on capabilities rather than the physical components of the underlying technology.

## 1.2. Research question #1

What are the critical differentiating dimensions of information technology capability?

The importance of balance between an organization's chosen IT and the organization's structure, processes, and strategy is well established [23,38]. This observation suggests that certain forms of IT are more appropriate for certain types of tasks. There are similar references to task–IT compatibility in the reengineering literature, however, researchers have yet to identify the critical components of the task–IT linkage [8,16].

## 1.3. Research question #2

What are the dynamics of the interaction between service tasks and IT capability?

## 2. Literature review

A review of extant literature reveals a gap in the classification of IT capability. A 1982 review of technology–structure research cites several operations-based conceptualizations of technology [12]. These include perspectives based on dimensions of technical complexity [43], operations technology and operations variability [17,31], interdependence [41], and the balance between routine and non-routine transactions [29,30]. These early conceptualizations of technology focus on machine technology and appear more applicable to manufacturing than services. A service-specific review notes that knowledge technology, principally IT, is the dominant one of the service sector [25].

The MIT, ‘Management in the 1990s’, research program reported that most IT-based classification frameworks focused on elements of structure and scale [37]. Structural frameworks promote segregation of hardware and software or differentiation among processors, databases, and communication technology. Scale-based IT classification facilitates differentiation among large mainframe systems, mid-range systems, and micro or personal computers.

Typologies that categorize systems based upon their structure or scale do not support the objectives of our research. Crowston and Malone suggest that

A series of interviews with experts and two pilot case studies confirmed the inappropriateness of machine–technology frameworks for our research. This suggested the need for a new, capability-based model for IT classification.

The concept of IT capability has been discussed more frequently in practitioner-based literature than in academic journals. Early depiction of it explored the gap between capabilities and their utilization, identifying critical capabilities as: (1) a lever to lower costs; (2) a means of building customer and supplier dependency and (3) an approach to discouraging new rivals and a spur to define new products [28]. Similar research focused on its ability to improve internal operations and influence external markets [4].

Another body of research used individuals or groups as the unit of analysis, examining such issues as the impact of IT on an individual's potential power and influence within an organization through a survey of users of IS [21]. These power bases include attributes such as resource provision, irreplacability, network centrality, expertise, and authority. Additional group-focused research used survey data to demonstrate the impact of IT on work group structuring [22], including attributes such as standardization, specialization, locus of decision making, and centralization of decision control.

While producing significant, informative results, these group-based studies are, however, primarily observing the second-order effects of underlying, yet unidentified, IT capabilities. Of greater interest is the supposition that information accessibility, data availability, and network centrality are the underlying IT capabilities that drive work group structuring activities. These first-order IT capabilities that directly affect the service task.

A review of existing theory on the impact of IT identifies five basic forms: they include effects on organizational structure, team formulation, boundary spanning, process integration, and the management of interdependence $[35]$ . The effects on organizational structure and team formulation are, again, second-order effects. In contrast, boundary spanning and integration appear to represent first-order IT capabilities that affect intra-organizational communication and process design. The management of interdependence has the potential to affect the design of work processes, intra-organizational communication, and inter-organizational integration.

An alternative approach to the issue of IT capability explores the role of IT in business process redesign $[9]$ , which identified nine basic IT capabilities, suggesting that these capabilities drive a recursive relationship between IT and the redesign process (Table 1). The recursive relationship suggests that IT capabilities will not only enable known forms of redesign but also create new process design options. The IT capabilities available to an organization would then define available options in the operating environment. This suggests that each new application of IT introduces an opportunity to develop new methods.

A review of capability-based IT literature confirms our contention that no single typology exists to differentiate between various kinds of systems. Researchers examine IT capability from multiple perspectives, including work design, process transformation, power relationships, and coordination. However, no integrated view identifies the key differentiating IT attributes or facilitates classification of IT systems.

## 3. Research models and methodology

The call for empirical operations and technology management research began during the early 1980s and continued into the 1990s [24,40]. The preliminary stages of the research combined literature reviews with expert interviews and pilot case studies to define the research questions and refine the research model.

## 3.1. Industry and unit of analysis—action

A single industry study was performed; it focused on financial services, defined as all forms of banking, brokerage services, mutual fund processing, trust, custody, and insurance. Prior research demonstrates that single industry studies are appropriate for exploratory research $[13,20]$ . Studies have demonstrated that single industry perspectives eliminate the complexity of cross-industry variability and can, while limiting generalizability, enhance internal validity and reliability. Our selection of financial services as the target industry was based on this industry's strong reliance on IT to support their operations and the current pattern of aggressive investment in IT as the industry seeks to replace aging IT infrastructures and integrate legacy infrastructure with new technology.

The effort focused on recently completed applications of IT. This highlights the relationship between IT and task—a unit of action (the IT project) that transforms a service task by applying technology. The explicit pairing of individual tasks with IT capability eliminates the ‘blurring’ problem encountered when applying organization-level typologies to diverse task environments. The choice decisions studied here are then typically made at a functional or task-level, rather than at the organization-level, further justifying the selection of this unit of action.

## 3.2. Phase I Delphi study

Delphi methods employ panels of experts to order and define dimensions of complex phenomena $[39]$ . We utilized both the definitional and ranking capabilities of the Delphi method, where participants first identify the critical underlying dimensions of the IT construct and then rank order them. This research study used Delphi principles that stress panel anonymity, response summarization, and iterative cycles in the response-analysis process until consensus is achieved. The Delphi panel consisted of 31 senior IT managers from 11 organizations. The use of experienced managers with over 10 years of financial services–IT experience contributed to the content validity of the Delphi study $[5]$ .

## 3.3. The Delphi instrument

The draft Delphi instrument incorporated the 21 IT capability attributes identified in the literature reviews and expert interviews. Step one in the construction of the Delphi instrument was the conversion of the identified IT attributes into 5-point Likert-type statements. An industry advisory panel then reviewed the draft Delphi instruments. These reviewers recommended several minor changes in wording and question order. The panel also recommended the partitioning of system security into two independent dimensions (system access and data security) and the addition of an open-ended question at the start of the instrument. This question asked each respondent to list his or her opinion of the four most important dimensions of IT capability.

The final step in the instrument development process was a pre-test of the instrument with two IT managers who, while not members of the actual Delphi panel, were comparably skilled and experienced. The pre-test assessed the respondent's ability to interpret the Delphi instructions and comprehend the terminology. It resulted in several changes to enhance question clarity.

## 3.4. Phase II case studies

The next phase included a series of comparative case studies that further refined the construct specification and explored the dynamics of interaction between the service task and IT capability. The case study incorporated a combination of accepted explanatory and exploratory procedures that address all facets of design, data collection, and data analysis $[44]$ . Case site selection was purposeful, rather than random, in order to ensure representation across the dimensions identified in the Delphi study. Data collection involved a combination of structured interviews, archival data analysis, and direct observation. Data analysis and reporting followed a series of structured methods that include pattern matching, explanation building, and inductive development of research propositions. The case analyses involved nine cases across six organizations (Table 3).

## 3.5. Pilot cases

Two pilot cases allowed us to assess the face validity of the research model and associated constructs. They mapped completed task–IT initiatives to the original research model to determine what issues may arise in the application of the models. The pilot case study sites were comparable in size, structure, and focus to the actual case sites, but they were not included in the final case analysis. They were used solely to validate the model and pre-test the case protocol

## 3.6. Case design and site selection

The case design follows Yin's type 3 approach for a multi-case, holistic design. This utilizes multiple cases for the purpose of replication, and incorporates a single unit of analysis. The multiple case design is similar to a quasi-experiment. In this manner, the case selection process supports a combination of literal replication, the observation of like outcomes from similar IT forms, and theoretical replication, the occurrence of dissimilar outcomes due to explainable causes, specifically the variations in IT.

## 3.7. Data collection

The research used two forms of data collection to enhance the accuracy and reliability of the data $[18]$ . First, the case design it used multiple forms of data collection, including interviews, archival data, and direct observation. Second, the design used multiple sources of data in each data collection type, such as multiple interviewees for each case and multiple sources of data. This provides safeguards that maximize the probability that observed variance is truly due to the trait under study and not an artifact of the methodology. The use of structured interviews serves multiple purposes, including maintenance of the researcher's focus, preservation of internal validity, and enhancement of reliability $[10]$ .

## 3.8. Data analysis

Analysis of the case data followed established guidelines, which recommend conducting case data analysis through a combination of pattern analysis, explanation building, and within-case, modified time-series analysis. The constructs under study are strongly linked to theory, which makes pattern matching a valuable tool. However, the lack of established theory to predict outcomes precludes total reliance on pattern matching analysis procedures. The expected outcome variability dictates the adoption of alternative data analysis methods to accommodate the more ambiguous components. Explanation building seeks to establish causal links for case observations.

## 4. Results, discussion and managerial implications

## 4.1. The Delphi administration, results and analysis

The Delphi study involved three rounds of data collection, analysis, and redistribution. The Round 1 response rate was 81%, 25 of 31 instruments returned. The open-ended question asked in Round 1 did not produce any additional items. The size of the Delphi panel and the high Round 1 response rate enabled the generation of a correlation matrix to test the independence of the dimensions. The results indicated that the dimensions are only weakly correlated, validating the decision to treat them independently.

The next major decision was the selection of items for inclusion in subsequent rounds of the Delphi study. A natural break appeared between attributes 9 and 10 for the IT capability construct (Table 4). Therefore, in Rounds 2 and 3 the distributed Delphi instrument included only the top nine attributes. The objective of Rounds 2 and 3 was to confirm or re-order the ranking of the key dimensions from Round 1.

## 4.2. Phase II: case study construct specification and interaction analysis

The case analyses confirmed the vital role of the integration attributes and redefine the scale attribute in terms of system scope. The security, availability and contingency attributes were shown not to be primary differentiating attributes of the IT construct but second-order effects emanating from the primary construct. The technology focus attribute proved to be a critical defining dimension of the IT capability construct as well as a fourth, previously undefined, attribute that incorporates elements of system accessibility.

## 4.3. Integration: intra- and inter-departmental

The Delphi respondents ranked the two integration dimensions as the first and third most critical attributes. The case data supported this finding and confirmed the role of integration as a key determinant. The case analyses further assisted in refining the definition of the integration continuum, suggesting a progression from independent to dependent and interdependent systems.

Examples of each integration level exist within the case sample. The collateral assignment system is highly independent with a self-contained data infrastructure in which the previous cycle's output becomes the current cycle's input. The reorganization system demonstrates limited, serial dependency on other processing systems. The global trading and mutual fund trading systems display greater levels of integration with numerous data and processing dependencies. These dependencies create a processing environment in which completion of a predecessor systems invokes a new initiation of the application systems. The corporate actions network system displays the highest level of integration, interdependence, in which applications share common data and functions. The processing sequence in these systems is random and largely controlled by external events, as partner systems deliver new information to the application.

## 4.4. Scale: transaction flow and data storage

The scale dimension, represented by transaction flow and data storage capacity in the Delphi study, proves to be more of a condition than an attribute of the IT systems. Scale is important in system design, however, the case analyses indicated that scope is a more salient attribute of IT capability. The rapidly expanding processing capabilities and scalability of virtually all hardware components mitigated the impact of transaction volume on the system's design and development procedures. IT managers participating in the case studies were more inclined to associate system size with the number of functional areas that the IT must support rather than the number of transactions processed. Similarly, data storage capacity was highly scalable with current storage technologies. The concern for IT managers was not the volume of stored data but, rather, the breadth of utilization patterns that the databases and associated access protocols must support.

The concept of IT scope centers on the number of tasks, functions and distinct user groups supported by the IT system. The low-end of the scope continuum comprises highly task-specific systems. The mid-continuum, function-specific systems support a broad range of tasks and, although they cross organizational boundaries, their functional scope is constant both within and across organizations. The scope of task-independent systems is independent of any specific set of tasks or functions. These IT systems provide data acquisition and management capabilities that support a wide range of unrelated tasks and users, thus delivering generic IT functionality that allows the system user to define usage patterns for the application and its associated data.

## 4.5. Technology focus: production, work flow management and communication

The technology focus attribute examines the type of work accomplished by the IT system. The three categories of work studied were: task execution or production, communication, and work flow management. The Delphi study treated technology focus and the concept of “automate versus informate” $^{1}$ as distinct IT attributes [45]. Later work extended the automate–informate continuum to include a third option, network, which occurs via the interconnection of diverse IT systems [6].

The case data support the presence of the three levels of IT focus. The execution of production tasks generally involves the automation of manual procedures. Communication-focused systems address the delivery of information to service providers and customers. Work flow management systems provide decision support and decision making logic that perform a variety of work management activities including task scheduling and task-to-worker job assignments.

## 4.6. Supplementary IT capability attributes

The case analyses endorsed several of the Delphi study findings and dismissed several others as non-differentiating. The case data also identify one additional attribute: accessibility. There are two dimensions to this: access and distribution. Access refers to avenues through which system users may view data. Distribution relates to the available channels through which users may receive data.

Owner accessibility is the lowest level of IT capability for this. These systems attributes limit application and data distribution access to those individuals directly responsible for the underlying business process. The highest level of accessibility is open access.

These systems allow access and distribution to a broad spectrum of direct users and non-participant interested parties through varied access methods, including EDI and the Internet.

## 4.7. The IT capability continuum

The empirical evidence indicates that four key differentiating attributes define the multi-dimensional IT construct: integration, system scope, system focus, and accessibility. This suggests the presence of a continuum that contains three levels of IT capability (Table 7).

The continuous nature of the IT attributes precludes explicit boundaries between the levels. However, the capability levels do demonstrate observable differences relative to the approach to IT development and organizational utilization (Table 8). The lowest IT capability level, information management, includes systems that focus on the execution of specific production tasks. Such systems typically demonstrate low levels of IT integration and often are highly independent processing systems. The principle functions of information management systems are data conversion and computation.

The mid-continuum network coordination systems display moderate levels of system integration in the form of cross-system dependencies. The scope of these systems is function-specific, combining multiple tasks or task outputs in support of a common business function. The primary focus of network coordination systems is communication, though some elements of production execution may be present. The network participants often include members of multiple organizations working within the same general business function.

Enterprise management systems represent the highest level of IT capability. These systems display elevated levels of IT integration in the form of processing interdependence and may incorporate elements of task execution and communication but the primary focus of these systems is on knowledge and work flow management. The scope of enterprise systems is task-independent. The provider does not dictate, and may not know, the utilization patterns of the system users. Access to these systems is open to system owners, participants and other non-participant interested parties.

The principal function of enterprise management systems is the analysis and interpretation of data. As such, knowledge management systems would fall into this category. Enterprise systems invoke emerging IT paradigms such as text manipulation, multimedia and the World Wide Web to assimilate and distribute information. The span of influence for these systems is at the organization level.

## 5. Summary

Our work is an attempt to identify the critical attributes of IT capability in an effort to close the gap in our understanding of utilization-based classification of the various kinds of systems. The specified typology is a continuum encompassing three principle levels of IT capability: information management, network coordination, and enterprise management. The subsequent analysis of the IT capability framework identified development and implementation characteristics of the three IT capability levels and mapped the nine cases to the underlying attributes and the primary levels of IT capability (Table 9).

Organizations continue to struggle with numerous IT issues including technology cost containment, methods for the strategic deployment of IT, the productivity paradox and the technology choice decision $[14,19]$ . It is reasonable to expect that different kinds of technology will be more or less appropriate to achieve different objectives in the management of a business enterprise.
