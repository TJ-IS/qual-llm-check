# A model of computerization of manufacturing systems: an international study

Hsin Hsin Chang<sup>\*</sup>

College of Management, Graduate School of Management, National Chaiyi University, 151 Linshen East Road, Chaiyi 600, Taiwan, ROC

Accepted 22 May 2001

## Abstract

This paper introduces a model of best practice in the computerization of manufacturing systems, by drawing on the practical experience of senior managers of both manufacturing and information management in Taiwan and the UK. The design used both qualitative and quantitative data analysis techniques to examine the implementation and integration of information systems support for manufacturing (ISSM). The investigation was conducted in three main phases. A series of qualitative case studies was conducted on the use of ISSM in 21 Taiwanese companies and 3 government organizations. Within-case and cross-case analysis showed that the six most important elements of ISSM implementation and integration are: the nature of manufacturing systems, ISSM and related software subsystems, barriers to ISSM, facilitators to ISSM, measured benefits from ISSM investment, and the level of ISSM integration. A preliminary development of a model of ISSM was then used to design a questionnaire for case studies analysis to examine ISSM in Taiwan and the UK. A comparison of ISSM in the two countries suggests that Taiwanese manufacturers have created platforms on which to build advanced manufacturing systems, are aware of the problems, and have plans to overcome them. UK firms had more experience with ISSM, and more organizations had achieved highly integrated manufacturing systems. Both countries appear to be moving toward similar designs for their enterprise resource planning and supply chain management systems. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information technology (IT); Information systems support for manufacturing (ISSM); Computer integrated manufacturing (CIM); Supply chain management (SCM)

## 1. Introduction

Integration of manufacturing systems and supply chain systems in modern industrial enterprises embody complex decisions and systematic actions. In order to operate successfully and profitably in rapidly changing global markets, highly flexible manufacturing systems (FMS), build to order (BTO), configure to order (CTO), optimized distribution modeling (ODM), enterprise resource planning (ERP) and supply chain management (SCM) techniques must be implemented. ISSM involves the application of IT to support these complicated manufacturing and organizational decisions. It systematically tracks relevant attributes, such as speed, response time, accuracy, and customer relationships. This is facilitated through an analysis matrix that relates data units and various system components. Measures of these attributes can then be used to analyze potential improvements to the system under consideration.

This paper is a comparative study of the implementation and integration of ISSM in Taiwan (one of the emerging ‘‘little dragons’’ of southeast Asia) and the UK. The manufacturing performance of southeast Asian economies, where firms appear to be rapidly developing sophisticated manufacturing systems, is of strategic competitive importance to Western manufacturers. The research involved preliminary case study development followed by a postal survey in both Taiwan and the UK, and was conducted in the following phases.

1. An analysis and tentative theory development based on a series of case studies on ISSM development, in 21 Taiwan companies and 3 government organizations.

2. Within-case and cross-case analysis showing that the six most important elements of ISSM implementation and integration are: the nature of production systems, ISSM and related software subsystems, barriers to ISSM, facilitators of ISSM, measured benefits from ISSM investment, and the level of ISSM integration.

3. A model of ISSM implementation was developed from the case study findings, and this model was used to design a questionnaire to examine integration in Taiwan and the UK.

4. A comparison of implementation and integration related to ISSM in Taiwan and the UK, and a refinement of a model of the ISSM implementation process.

## 2. Information systems support for advanced manufacturing

## 2.1. Definitions

The application of IT in manufacturing began with the concept of integrated planning and operations developed by IBM in the early 1960s, when large manufacturers and computer suppliers promoted concepts and developed software for manufacturing planning and control. To effect commercial results, the implementation of ISSM had to be supported by changes in organizational attitude and decision-making. Maull et al. [19] defined ISSM as ‘‘a set of policies, procedures, systems and knowledge that surrounds the processing of customer orders, to provide information such as work to lists, job schedules, packing lists, back order, and material orders’’. Webster and Williams [25] defined ISSM as ‘‘the set of software technologies concerned with the planning and control of manufacturing resources’’.

Further definitions of ISSM found in the literature, include Bessant and Buckingham [2], Hollier and Barber [15], Childe et al. [6], Clark et al. [9], Muhlemann et al. [21], Maull et al. [19], Monniot et al. [20], Cork [10], and Chang [4,5]. ISSM involves not only computers and the manipulation of data but also the various procedures and people who must carry out operations. ISSM can be defined as ‘‘a managerial information system that supports manufacturing functions and includes technology management, organization, and customer relationship service procedures’’.

## 2.2. ISSM computer subsystems

There are many different ISSM packages available for a wide range of applications. Waterlow and Monniot [24] define ISSM software subsystems as including aggregate planning, master production scheduling, purchasing/capacity planning activities, material requirements planning (MRP), inventory control (raw materials, components, end products), work in progress (WIP) control, order monitoring (production progressing), works order paperwork printing, shop-floor scheduling, operation sequencing and shop-floor data collection, production engineering (bill of materials, process planning, tool control). These applications aid in manufacturing management through: design engineering (specifications, modifications), maintenance engineering (coordination monitoring, fault analysis, maintenance scheduling), quality control (inspection, failure analysis), cost control (products, materials, labor), sales order processing, dispatch (scheduling of deliveries, dispatch note printing system), and works payroll.

Childe et al. [6] defined ISSM systems as ‘‘the networks of human and computerized elements, which regulate the flow of work, control purchasing and delivery and thereby manage the manufacturing function’’. To be successful, manufacturers need a top– down integration strategy covering integration of organization, logistics, and information.

## 2.3. Barriers, facilitators and benefits to ISSM

Manufacturers attempt to implement ISSM because they foresee benefits from doing so. They are sometimes frustrated in these attempts because manufacturing systems are complex and the addition of further technology without sufficient preparation may not lead to improvement. The Taiwanese Economic and Marketing Information Center [22] revealed a number of problems in their summary: lack of a clear hierarchy for computerization, software inflexibility, poorly defined operating processes, lack of systems design, poor applications design, lack of provision for real user demand, incompatible staff, and difficulties with interdepartmental relationships. Waterlow and Monniot [24], Bonney and Head [3] identified obstacles to successful implementation of ISSM as including poor planning for investment decisions, inflexible organizational structures, unclear definition of ISSM systems requirement, limited ISSM expertise and lack of top management support.

Bessant and Lamming [1] pointed out the need to have a suitable organization basis. They use the concept of organizational learning to distinguish between substitution and transformation methodologies, and suggested that ISSM is successful when companies adopt a ‘‘double loop’’ approach to organizational change. ISSM can lead to quantitative and qualitative improvements in manufacturing and improvements due to the benefits of internal and external re-organization. Cork [10] feels a major facilitator and benefit is that ISSM implementation is accompanied by the acquisition of a systemic view of the production process by managers, who should experience the following benefits in individual functions: shorter delivery lead times, more accurate quoted delivery dates, utilization of plant and manpower at the lowest possible cost, minimization of the levels of WIP and stock, flexible manufacturing to meet changing volume and business mix, systematic planning and control of materials procurement.

## 2.4. Level of ISSM integration

ISSM is configured to meet the specific requirements of each manufacturing firm for the planning and control of its production activity [10]. In the 1980s, the Application of Computers to Manufacturing Engineering Directorate of the UK, Science Engineering Research Council (ACME-SERC) sponsored a program of research into ISSM. One outcome of that program was a ranking scale for the level of integration of ISSM. We have adopted this integration scale since we found it to be meaningful to both UK and Taiwanese managers. ISSM is a necessary prerequisite to the development of an SCM system [7,8,14,20]. The integration-ranking model for computer systems applied to manufacturing is represented in Table 1.

The prerequisites and the associated methods for integrating systems are largely technical in nature: in reality human and organizational factors are at least as important. The importance of human factors, stressed by researchers such as Bessant and Buckingham [2] and Gould and Dent [13], is often underestimated by organizations. This explains, in part, why the vast increases in computing power and software sophistication achieved in the last decade have yet to produce the anticipated levels of computer integration.

## 3. Research design

The study examined manufacturing systems, subsystems, the organizational and technological barriers and facilitators to ISSM implementation, and benefits. Respondents included management information systems (MIS) and manufacturing managers. The research involved preliminary case study development in Taiwan followed by a postal survey in both Taiwan and the UK. Eisenhardt [12] stated that the triangulation made possible by multiple data collection methods provides stronger substantiation of constructs and hypotheses. Of special note is the combi-ning of qualitative and quantitative evidence. Qualitative data are useful for understanding the rationale or theory underlying relationships in the quantitative data. The analysis of the data followed the approach suggested by Eisenhardt [12]. The stages in building theory included: theoretic definition and sensitivity, identification research, research design and methodology, conceptual construct, data analysis and interpretation, development of a model of ISSM, second survey, refined model, conclusion and recommendation.

## 4.2. Detail of the interview results

Company A has most experience of enterprise and CIM systems, but had not fulfilled its ambitions at the time of the survey. Even so, its planned financial commitment for automatic monitors and coding to link shop-floor data with computer systems, and ERP, indicates its determination to move forward. In company B, problems have been overcome by automating most data entry and the use of barcodes for shop-floor data capture. Kearney [16] has argued for the development of the sort of cross-trained managers found at company B, in order to make best use of IT. Managers comments clearly indicated the need for the type of hybrid staff that exists in this company. The company integrated its ISSM system within a very short time frame.

In company C, data capture is incomplete, particularly from the shop floor. Company C’s lower level of commitment to IT is shown by its reluctance to expend the effort required to develop data capture systems on the shop floor. In company D, the MIS and manufacturing departments have different strategies, which are not compatible. MIS considers the company group first; the strategic focus is head office goals. The production departments collect shop-floor data, but this data is not linked with the information center.

In company E, production and MIS managers understood each other’s processes, but their staff did not. Products are make-to-order only, with a lead-time of 1–2 months. Production staffs do not want to supply data in real time, so the education of employees in the importance of entering data is a key issue for the company. In company F, ISSM is the MIS (mainly) and production managers’ responsibility. This has given a lot of work to the MIS managers. Full ISSM systems have been integrated, with only some data errors. Company G had difficulties in implementing its MRP system. The main problems included high operator turnover, lack of computer system knowledge, poor production processes, uncertain material quality, and frequent schedule changes. MIS focus is on selecting the right tools for developing information systems. Company H is a government organization, with large amounts of capital and enough MIS and production experts. The company had already implemented EIS, ERP and SCM. The company facilitates interdepartmental integration by developing staff with experience of both MIS and manufacturing.

In company I, the manufacturing director is responsible for ISSM. Production transfers data to the MIS department every day by batch, causing some data errors. MIS had not considered ISSM barriers, and did not support the production department. In companies J and U, the CIM system is linked to all other information systems. Manufacturing is highly automated, with on-line data from SCM processes. The information system is the responsibility of everybody in the company—it is not just the job of a few managers.

Company K is a government organization in the petroleum industry. The company uses linear programming in production systems, and consultants provide support for information systems. The computer system cannot achieve reliable, immediate and certain information. Every department receives benefits from IT, but they operate their systems independently. Difficulties in integrating systems include mismatches between hardware and software, and inability to share data. Company K needs to develop a committee to push forward IT. Company L failed to implement ISSM successfully. Barriers included manual data entry by production operators, not building a hierarchy of the overall computer structure, incompatible staff, selecting the wrong systems, poor system analysis, and lack of top management support.

Company M has the lowest level of commitment to IT and to the development of data on the shop floor. Company M has no MIS department, and external software suppliers developed computer systems. The production department is responsible for ISSM, and this was considered to be a barrier to ISSM. The company failed to implement ISSM due to poor software suppliers, selecting the wrong systems and incompatible goals and personality clashes between manufacturing managers. Company N’s top management strongly believe that they need compatible experts to design, implement and integrate ISSM and CIM, and the main future plans include integrating executive level management and the development of ERP and SCM.

## 4.3. Barriers, facilitators, and benefits to ISSM

The survey results show that all managers mentioned ‘‘frequent schedule alteration’’ most often as a barrier to ISSM. Every Taiwanese company has limited resources, and every department director has to decide how to allocate them. The MIS director of companies B, J and N suggested that these decisions include

1. how to use limited resources to the best effect;

2. how to be compatible with MIS to obtain the best results, and,

3. the software and hardware required (based on return on investment and budgetary constraints).

These questions are very closely linked with the way management operates and its strategy. Whether computerization succeeds or fails is also very closely linked with management structure and ideas. Taiwanese companies have MIS experts, but software development needs definite ROI information and other knowledge, e.g. number of man-hours to develop systems.

The most frequently reported barriers were lack of user involvement, poor system design and frequent production schedule changes. The facilitators mentioned most often were long-term planning for computer systems, top management commitment, production and MIS managers’ mutual understanding, development of a common database, large capital investment in ISSM, educating and training employees, and building a hierarchy of the overall computer system structure. ISSM can lead to quantitative and qualitative improvements in manufacturing and internal and external organizational benefits in Taiwanese manufacturing. The most frequently reported benefits from ISSM were improvements in materials handling, clearly defined solutions, order winning capability, information quality, meeting delivery dates, reduced cost, and lower inventory.

A complete technology audit for an organization includes a comparative analysis, showing the relationship of technology to other, nontechnology-based activities, and a developmental analysis, providing an indication of the scope and likelihood of possible changes in the use of technology by the organization. Careful planning, implementation, and execution will spell success. This relates to benefits, facilitators and level of ISSM integration in the IT and organizational views of the model. ## 4.5. A preliminary development of a model of ISSM

The preliminary research led to a model of ISSM implementation and integration from 21 Taiwanese manufacturing companies. The analyses cover both IT and organizational aspects in Fig. 1. From an IT viewpoint, starting with an ISSM strategy, the implementation process is understood to involve long-term planning and capital investment for computer systems, leading to the construction of an IT infrastructure. The implementation will encounter technical barriers but certain technologies will facilitate integration. Eventually, if the integration of IT systems with production systems is successful, the practical benefits will lead to further pressures to overcome technical barriers and pressures to introduce technological improvements.

From an organizational viewpoint, the ISSM strategy will need to be defined in terms of explicit corporate policies communicated by senior management and enacted with commitment. Analysis of 21 case studies confirmed that many of the barriers and facilitators to ISSM development arose due to the relationship between MIS and manufacturing. Barriers and facilitators, and measured results of implementation are assumed to occupy the same position as in the system view, but are influenced by different variables. The nature of the organization greatly affects successful integration of systems. System failure sometimes arises due to failure to recognize human factors (Dornier et al. [11]). Without proper organization, systems can be created that must be frequently redesigned to accommodate enhancements and changes. A well thought out organization produces an environment for integration and systems growth. Integration of computer systems within manufacturing can be difficult enough without organizational problems. Objective conflicts, internal politics, lack of focus, and inadequate management skills are all a direct result of the organizational structure. Integration is likely to necessitate the re-allocation of tasks between computers and humans until a satisfactory computer package and set of manual tasks are arrived at (Child et al. [6]). As for the IT view discussed above, the barriers and facilitators to effective use of ISSM are different at each successive level of integration (Chopra and Meindl [7]; Waterlow and Monniot [24]). Lucas and Baroudi [17] mentioned that traditional organizations are using technology to make some changes in structure without making major modifications to the entire organization. The movement toward greater exchange of data electronically between customers and suppliers may be creating cases of vertically integrated conglomerates.

## 5. Second survey of ISSM: Taiwan and UK

The model of the implementation process was used to design a questionnaire to examine the development of ISSM in Taiwan and the UK questionnaires were sent to 900 directors of production, MIS, and those with joint responsibility in Taiwan (350 directors) and the UK (550 directors). In order to minimize size effects, manufacturing firms included in the survey matched the criteria of more than 500 employees and annual turnover of more than US\$ 40 million.

Questionnaires were sent to 275 manufacturing and 275 MIS directors in the UK. The MIS directors names were drawn from the membership of a national professional IT organization and questionnaires were addressed to manufacturing directors named in the FAME business information database or the Dunn and Bradstreet directory. About 112 responses were returned to give a response rate of 20 percent. Respondents included 77 MIS directors (69 percent), 26 manufacturing directors (23 percent), and 9 MIS/ manufacturing directors (8 percent). In Taiwan, questionnaires were sent to 175 manufacturing and 175 MIS directors. Names and companies of potential respondents were obtained from the membership list of the Taiwan Information Management Association (IMA), a Taiwanese government publication and through personal contacts. Of the 350 questionnaires distributed, 96 were completed and returned to give a response rate of 27 percent. The respondents included 48 MIS directors (50 percent), 36 manufacturing directors (38 percent), and 12 MIS/production directors (13 percent). In total 208 responses were received, giving a response rate of 24 percent. Respondent firms encompassed several industrial sectors, including electrical engineering (28 percent), mechanical engineering (19 percent), metal manufacture (17 percent), and chemical and allied industries (11 percent).

The majority of firms had either several functions computerized without regard to integration or had several functions linked via common data and coordinated control. The level of ISSM integration reported by respondents was significantly related to industrial sector but not to country or respondent’s function. This would be the expected result if respondents were able reliably to report the level of ISSM integration, independently of their personal responsibilities and nationality.

## 5.1. ISSM experience and future development of advanced systems

Generally, UK firms had more experience with ISSM. Almost half the UK firms had over 15-year experience and over a quarter had at least 20-year experience. In contrast 62 percent of Taiwanese firms had less than 15-year experience and 21 percent had from 15- to 20-year experience with ISSM. When all firms were analyzed, a significant relationship was found between experience and level of ISSM integration. This was also found for Taiwanese but not for UK manufacturers when analyzed separately. In Taiwan, firms tended to build software in-house or to or buy and modify software. UK firms were more likely to buy and modify software. However, no significant relationship between the type of manufacturing system software and the level of ISSM integration was detected.

There were positive relationships between the frequency of feedback from ISSM software subsystems and ISSM integration, as shown in Table 4. All relationships were positive, suggesting that increased frequency of data feedback is associated with greater integration. Feedback from all subsystems except for inventory control was more highly correlated with the level of integration in Taiwan than in the UK. Feedback frequency from all systems except for MRP was significantly correlated with the level of integration when the whole sample was considered. Feedback from all systems except for MRP and inventory control was significantly related to ISSM integration in

Taiwan. An only shop-floor data collection significant relationship was found in the UK. Data feedback frequency was generally more highly correlated with integration in Taiwan than in the UK. Statistically significant relationships were found between feedback frequency for aggregate planning, MPS, capacity planning, shop-floor scheduling and data collection, manufacturing maintenance engineering, quality control, cost control, dispatch, and integration software subsystems in Taiwanese companies.

In Table 5, most firms had plans for CIM and ERP. The high percentage of firms with plans for CIM and ERP implied that most firms had long-term aims for the computerization of production management and that systems’ integration was an important goal. The reported level of ISSM integration was related to the existence of plans for all three types of system in Taiwanese firms but statistically significant relationships were not found for UK firms. The introduction of CIM and EIS systems was associated with highly integrated ISSM systems. Most companies’ senior management felt that the investment capital required for software systems and IT development in ERP and SCM was too large in relation to the available budget, and that this was limiting the rate of progress with ISSM.

## 5.2. Composite barriers and integration of ISSM

An analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to examine the relationships between composite variables representing barriers to ISSM implementation, and the level of ISSM integration in Taiwan and the UK, as shown in Table 6. Left hand columns represent the importance of the barriers to ISSM implementation. The right hand columns represent the probability that the company has plans to remove the barrier. The following conclusions can be drawn:

1. The importance of barriers varies between both Taiwan and UK respondents, and by respondent position.

2. Planned actions to remove barriers appear to be linked as much to the ease of removal as to importance.

3. Barriers are significantly correlated in terms of importance.

4. The importance of barriers is generally negatively correlated to the level of integration.

Respondents were asked to rate the importance of 21 barrier variables (see Table 6) using a 5-point scale. Responses were on a Likert scale of 1–5, where 1 ¼ barriers not important to ISSM; 5 ¼ barrier very important to ISSM in the y-axis. On the x-axis, 21 barrier variable numbers are in Figs. 2 and 3 from Table 6. In order to see more clearly the results of analysis it is necessary to graph the scores of Taiwan minus UK. Fig. 3 shows differences for 21 barriers of ISSM for all respondents. Note that all scores have been recalibrated to give a zero average (center line). The ‘‘limits’’ above and below the centerline correspond to a 95 percent confidence interval (not just an average difference). This means that 95 percent of all responses will fall within the two limits, if results are random.

Significant differences are observed between the two nationalities ranking of the importance of variable BA10 (lack of MIS staff system analysis/design skill) and variable BA20 (high MIS staff turnover). ## 5.5.2. Integration of ISSM

Table 10 shows that, whilst the distributions of firms from levels 1 to 3 were similar in Taiwan and the UK, the proportions of firms in levels 4 and 5 were reversed. A slightly higher proportion of organizations in Taiwan had achieved fully integrated software computer subsystems, but fewer had achieved integration of manufacturing. Taiwanese manufacturers may have leapfrogged from very low levels of integration to very high levels in a very short space of time. The lack of residual systems is important, since many companies are reluctant to dispose of expensive mainframe systems, and thus attempt to improve performance. The relative youth of Taiwanese manufac-turing companies means that they are likely to have newer systems in place.

The cultural differences between the two countries should not be underestimated. Many Taiwanese companies are still owner-managed, and Taiwanese place a high emphasis on education [4]. Senior managers may take decisions aligned with the long-term aim of increasing the value of their companies, such as investment in IT, rather than other short-term priorities. The decision-making process in such companies may also tend to be quicker. A problem noted by Taiwanese managers and not reported in the UK is the relatively low education and position of manufacturing operators—these may be the only staff without degrees in some Taiwanese companies.

## 6. Refine the model of ISSM: IT and organizational views

The analyses supported the general nature of the relationships within the hypothesized model of ISSM. However, differences were found in the significance of relationships when comparing the UK and Taiwan. UK and Taiwanese manufacturers perceive barriers, facilitators, and benefits differently. ANVOA and factor analysis of subsystems suggests that organizations in Taiwan and the UK have adopted a fragmented approach to system design. Subsystems already implemented reflect differences in prioritization in Taiwan and the UK. The model of the process has both IT and organizational aspects as shown in Fig. 8. The model includes: the nature of the manufacturing and information systems and departments, implementation of ISSM subsystems, barriers and facilitators to ISSM, and measured benefits of the substantial investment in ISSM technology. The impact includes both practical improvements in performance, and also the level of ISSM integration [7,8,11,14,25].

From an IT viewpoint, the implementation of ISSM processes involves the development of an information management infrastructure, starting with manufacturing systems and ISSM subsystems management and strategy. During implementation, actions may be taken to reduce barriers or utilize facilitators. Benefits, in terms of both measurable improvements in manufacturing performance, and system integration will accrue. After the initial integration of manufacturing systems, the practical benefits will lead to further plans to overcome technical barriers and pressures to introduce information system developments.

Underlying the IT view of the implementation of ISSM is the organizational view, the links in which mirror those in the systems view. From an organizational view, the implementation of ISSM requires its integration into the business functions and manufacturing strategy of the organization and the coordination of the management of the implementation process between manufacturing and MIS departments. Analysis of the views of senior managers suggests that many of the technical barriers and facilitators to ISSM development arise due to the relationship between

MIS and manufacturing. In the course of integration, it is likely to be necessary to re-allocate tasks between computers and humans until a satisfactory balance between automated and manual tasks is achieved [6]. Moreover, facilitators and barriers to effective use of ISSM are likely to be different at each successive level of integration [7,17,18,20].

## 7. Discussion and conclusion

Data analysis confirms that integration is positively related to practical benefits in terms of improved manufacturing performance. Overall, this study has identified as prerequisites to full integration, the implementation of CIM, ERP, and SCM systems. The above analyses suggest that in both Taiwan and the UK there are still serious barriers to overcome before ISSM is fully and successfully integrated into manufacturing systems. The analysis of the perceptions of senior managers of MIS and manufacturing, suggests that Taiwanese and UK manufacturers have a very similar view of the benefits and facilitators for ISSM integration, but perceive the barriers to ISSM integration differently. Taiwanese and UK manufacturers have set in place the platform on which to build manufacturing systems, are aware of the problems ahead and have plans in place to overcome them.

Barriers and facilitators represent a mixture of both the IT and organizational views of ISSM, and the most important barriers and facilitators are predominantly organizational aspects of ISSM. This suggests that the IT view is underpinned by the organizational view, and that integration of systems is dependent upon good management of departments and structural planning, not just upon technical abilities. Barriers perhaps relate more to lower levels of ISSM integration than the facilitators do. This may explain why they were perceived to be significantly related to the level of integration, whilst facilitators were not. The majority of respondent organizations have achieved a level of integration reflecting the nature of the relationships within the proposed model of ISSM implementation and integration.
