# The benefits of data warehousing: why some organizations realize exceptional payoffs

Hugh J. Watson $^{a,*}$ , Dale L. Goodhue $^{a,1}$ , Barbara H. Wixom $^{b,2}$

$^{a}$ Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602, USA

$^{b}$ McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA 22903, USA

Received 14 March 2000; received in revised form 8 February 2001; accepted 6 May 2001

## Abstract

Data warehousing is one of the key developments in the information systems (IS) field. While its benefits are plentiful, some organizations are receiving more significant returns than others. The types of returns can vary in the impact they have on the organization and the ease in which they can be quantified and measured. This article presents a framework that shows how data warehouses can transform an organization; it also offers a compelling explanation for why differences in impact exist. Case studies of data warehousing initiatives at a large manufacturing company (LMC), the Internal Revenue Service, and a financial services company (FSC) are presented and discussed within the context of the framework. The analysis shows that the benefits that each company received can be tied to the way in which it conforms to the framework. © 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Data warehousing; Benefits; Organizational transformation

## 1. Introduction

Data warehousing is one of the key developments in the information system (IS) field. Only 10 years ago, warehousing initiatives were just beginning in a few visionary firms. Today, virtually all large organizations have warehouses and many are moving to later generations of warehouses [3]. The Palo Alto Management Group provides figures about the size and growth of the data warehousing market. They estimate that the sale of systems, software, services, and in-house expenditures will grow from a US\$ 8.8 billion market in 1996 to a US\$ 113.5 billion market in 2002—a 51% growth rate.

The fundamental business driver behind data warehousing is the desire to improve decision making and organizational performance $[6]$ . It involves extracting data from source systems (e.g. legacy, external), cleaning (e.g. eliminating inconsistencies) and transforming the data (e.g. applying business rules, summarizing), and loading it to a data store (e.g. relational database). It also includes the actual tools (e.g. SQL) and applications that end-users apply to access and analyze the data.

Data warehousing projects are high risk/high return initiatives. In a 1996 study, International Data Corporation studied 62 data warehouses and found an average 3 years ROI of $401\%$ [9]. While precise figures are not

Nearly all of the current literature on data warehousing is written by practitioners who draw on their experience. In order to provide an academic perspective, we began a program of research. Among our projects has been a set of case studies that focus attention on organizational benefits.

In the process of conducting the case studies, it became clear that some organizations are receiving more significant returns than others. This caused us to ask ‘why?’ A compelling explanation is provided by Kotter’s [13] eight-step model for transforming an organization. Companies that use a warehouse as a facilitator for organizational transformation potentially receive exceptional payoffs.

## 2. A framework for benefits

Data warehousing supports a variety of decision support applications that can lead to benefits for organizations, however, as early as the 1960s, researchers realized that the benefits are difficult to define and measure $[10]$ . The difficulty exists because the benefits include intangible impacts. In one study, the Fleet-manager DSS led to a rich blend of qualitative and quantitative benefits, including a reduction in labor time, more effective and efficient decisions, and higher morale among users $[8]$ . Other studies have suggested that DSS benefits can be important, but not necessarily straightforward or easy to quantify $[5,12]$ .

Over the years, techniques and methodologies have been created for rigorously measuring the benefits of decision support applications [1,17]. For example, Conoco's EIS manager and his four-person staff evaluated the benefits of the company's executive IS. The group was able to quantify benefits, such as time savings, improved decision making, and services replacement by using a structured measurement methodology. However, the benefit analysis effort was quite time consuming, requiring the entire group's dedicated effort for 3 months.

Data warehouse applications can have a wide variety of tangible and intangible benefits. IS research as a whole has not been consistent in the way it has conceptualized benefits or measured them. In fact, DeLone and McLean [2] identified over 100 different measures of success that had been applied by researchers over the years. These include benefits that range from time savings to useful information to increased market share (see Table 1).

Because a data warehouse can provide more detailed, integrated, accessible, and historically complete information, it should be possible for an organization to operate very differently, to ‘re-design’ itself. This requires unfreezing the old way of doing business, changing to a new way of doing it, and then institutionalizing the new way $[15]$ .

The greatest benefits from data warehousing applications occur when they are used to redesign business processes and support strategic business objectives. These benefits involve more significant changes to the way that the organization operates.

A data warehouse can be a critical enabler for a major shift in an organization's strategy. This would involve reengineering a particular business process but would probably include a coordinated collection of redesigned processes, all working together to support a new organizational strategy. Guha et al. [7] investigated three companies that were implementing large-scale IS. They found that dramatic changes in business processes that happen for strategic reasons can create tremendous returns. One of the companies, Lucent Corporation, realized a US\$ 250 million turnaround with six consecutive profitable quarters after implementing an IS to change the way the company did business. A paper company that failed to reengineer processes or support the IS with strategic objectives incurred only lower-level benefits, like reduced cost of operations and improved information access.

Based on extensive experience in guiding organizations through major transformations, Kotter developed a framework, shown in Table 2, that describes how organizations can successfully achieve major change. We have found that this framework helps explain many of the differences that we see in our three case studies. We present it first.

At the top of Kotter's list is ‘establishing a sense of urgency’. Organizational transformation is too difficult to attempt if key people are not convinced of its necessity. Once there is a sense of urgency that something must be done, it is necessary to ‘form a powerful guiding coalition’. A group has to be formed with enough power to lead the change effort.

## 4. Three data warehousing case studies

We selected three companies from diverse industries and sectors. While all have been successful with their data warehousing efforts, some are providing a more significant payoff than others. In each organization we spent 1–2 days in face-to-face interviewing of employees, conducting phone interviews, and examining documents and video tapes of key events. Interviews included senior management, middle management, implementers and users. All interviews were taped and transcribed.

## 4.1. Large manufacturing company

This large manufacturing company (LMC) is a leading producer of home appliances. It employs tens of thousands of people who manufacture them under a variety of major brand names in countries around the world. It is information-intensive, with millions of products that are sold and tracked for their lifetime.

## 4.1.1. The need for a data warehouse

In the early 1990s, several LMC business units identified a need for integrated information to support a variety of business unit initiatives. For example, the quality department wanted a single repository of service call data to query and analyze to uncover defects and potential recalls. Until that point, information was scattered across systems throughout the company in different formats, making it difficult for business units to perform their basic functions.

LMC had just recovered from an IT failure in which a high dollar executive IS project was canceled because the data to support the project was spread across too many disparate systems. As data warehousing's popularity increased in general, the IT department saw it as a way to improve the technical infrastructure upon which other systems could be built without the problems that surfaced trying to build the EIS.

## 4.1.2. Champion turnover

The strongest push came from a high-level IT executive who saw the potential of data warehousing and incorporated it into the company's IT strategic plan. Unfortunately, he left shortly thereafter, forcing the team to market the vision to the rest of the company on its own. The group still works regularly to justify its decisions and encourage business units throughout the organization to leverage the information. The manager admits that this is extremely frustrating: ‘every new leadership comes in questioning—why are we doing this, why are we organizing this way? So, we are constantly reeducating people about the warehouse’.

## 4.1.3. Data warehouse projects are approved

As business units recognized how data warehousing could be used to meet their needs, they entered into LMC's formal justification and approval process. All IT-related projects must create a plan that outlines their business needs and the expected benefit from building the new system. The IT group then describes exactly how technology will be used to meet the business unit's needs. The completed plans are reviewed regularly by a steering committee.

When projects cannot be linked to a specific business unit, a plan is written and submitted by IT itself. At LMC, the infrastructure was approved using IT sponsorship, however, each application that the warehouse supports was approved with individual plans submitted by various business units.

Currently, the data warehouse is fed by six external data sources and over 100 mainframe sources, and it supports 12 primary subject areas, including business partners, competitors, products, and business transactions. Additionally, the warehouse provides data to dependent data marts that support marketing, logistics, manufacturing, and procurement applications. Overall, 1700 users have access to warehouse applications, and 400–600 unique users access the applications each month. The amount of queries has numbered as many as 40,000 in 1 month, and this figure continues to grow. Currently, it supports approximately 10 different applications.

## 4.1.4. Business units learn how the warehouse can be applied

The vision for LMC's warehouse has been quite strong at the application level; users are able to communicate quite clearly why the warehouse is in place and how it supports their needs. In fact, the strongest advocates are employees who have incorporated warehouse applications into their daily work routines.

Communication of the data warehouse and its impact has reached some employees holding management positions, and these supervisors and managers agree that the warehouse is helping people access and use information in ways that were not previously possible. However, they also note that they have not yet witnessed a dramatic change in the ways things are done.

## 4.1.5. Providing the right warehouse tools

LMC has had some difficulties finding a tool that can meet the needs of all of its users. Its approach was to deliver a single tool to the company's hundreds of employees who access the data warehouse, however, it appears that the software has been too difficult for the average user. Currently, the tools require the use of SQL queries through a graphical user interface. Many employees feel that this is asking too much. Users tend to make incorrect assumptions or run inefficient queries and be surprised by the results. Consequently, some people do not use the warehouse, while others rely on colleagues who have learned to use the technology well. The ‘power users’ spend much of their time helping other people.

## 4.1.6. Users benefit from the warehouse

Without question, at LMC local impacts of the warehouse are ubiquitous, and users have little problem attaching tangible dollar amounts to their applications. Interestingly, it appears that the benefits are much greater than anyone can tally because of the budgeting process. If a business unit communicates large savings, the budget is reduced for the next budget cycle to incorporate the savings. Thus, most business units downplay their benefits to keep their budget intact.

When prompted, users cited a variety of measurable benefits. For example, a quality engineer who reads and analyzes service call tickets explained that about 30–40 times as many service tickets can be checked. 'We produce 17,000 of a kind of appliance each day. If we find a problem as small as a 0.1% service incident rate, we save 17 service calls per day (17,000 × 0.001). Each call costs US\$ 75 which results in US\$ 1275 saved in service calls per day and in a US\$ 38,250 savings per month'. Many other users can give similar examples.

Through cleansing, aggregating, and possibly augmenting (e.g. external data) processes, LMC's data is also used to create better information and make better decisions. For example, LMC takes great care to plan how its merchandise should be displayed on the floors of retail stores because the display is known to impact the sales of that merchandise. Analysts examined floor planning models against sales and used hard data to support a change in floor policy. A field operations manager actually tracked hard cost reductions of over US\$ 1 million in the first year to the change in policy resulting from the analysis.

Although the company has not yet incorporated the warehouse into the strategy of the company, LMC has found that some business processes are being changed. In one instance, the warehouse has allowed LMC to broaden its organizational boundary by incorporating suppliers into a process that previously was conducted solely by internal employees. Now parts suppliers access quality information about the parts sold to LMC. This allows them to see the entire history of a part failure from the initial customer call to final resolution by a service technician. Suppliers can also look at the performance for all parts of a single type in order to gain a higher-level perspective on parts performance.

Supplier access to the warehouse has affected the cost recovery process for failed parts. Because supplier and LMC personnel have access to the same parts failure data, it is easier to determine and agree upon the reason for a failure; decide who is responsible for it; and decide upon the suppliers' share of the warranty cost payment.

## 4.1.7. Future plans

In addition to the 400–600 current users each month, some business units have strategies in place to encourage use by more of their own members. One manager, for example, trains his engineers to use the applications and refuses to approve their work until they have substantiated information pulled from the warehouse. The level of encouragement varies by business unit.

Ideally, LMC would like to continue developing the data warehouse and keep momentum going by spreading and increasing its use across the company. Unfortunately, the team has had some recent setbacks, including the reassignment of the data warehouse manager to the company's SAP initiative. They hope that change in management will not stop the growth of warehousing, but their future is uncertain.

## 4.2. Internal revenue service

The IRS is the largest single source of funding for the US Government, collecting over a trillion dollars a year from more than 250 million taxpayers. Tax returns and payments are processed at 10 service centers around the country. Thirty-three district offices handle collection activities (when individuals do not pay all taxes due) and audit approximately 2% of returns to discover people who under-report their taxes due.

## 4.2.1. A persistent 14% of taxes never get collected

Historically, about 82.5% of the total tax due is paid voluntarily and 3.5% is collected through enforcement efforts. About 14% of the potential taxes due are not collected. This represents a significant loss of revenue.

## 4.2.2. The audit and politics

The centerpiece of compliance enforcement is the audit and its threat. Auditing is manpower intensive, and the IRS has developed sophisticated means of automatically scoring returns to predict the likely payoff (additional tax revenue) of auditing any given return.

During times of tight budgets and budget deficits, there is pressure to improve its success. However, in recent years there has been a growing backlash against the IRS's enforcement efforts.

## 4.2.3. A new vision: the market segment approach

In 1986–1987, a group of IRS executives generated a new vision, called Compliance 2000. Under this new market segment approach, the IRS would identify whole groups (market segments) of taxpayers that had high rates of non-compliance, come to understand the reasons for this, and take selective actions (other than auditing) to change the behavior of the group as whole. The ‘market segment approach’ represented a radical departure from the ‘audit mentality’ of the past and a significant shift in strategic direction for IRS. It was seen by upper management as a key strategic possibility for the organization.

A first win for the market segment approach addressed taxpayers who claimed too many dependents on their returns. At the IRS' urging, Congress included in the tax bill of 1986 the requirement that social security numbers be required for all dependents. In short order, this removed seven million false dependents from tax returns, without any audit expenses.

The new vision was institutionalized in 1989 with the creation of the Compliance 2000 Office, which gave direction and encouragement to IRS districts to generate and carry out additional ways in which the market segmentation approach could be used to improve compliance.

## 4.2.4. Revitalization: a refined vision

In 1991, the Office of Research recognized that Compliance 2000 efforts had been hampered by two factors. First, the information technology to support the vision was not in place. The IS were all structured around the individual social security number or employer identification number, so there was no way to retrieve groups of individuals with certain characteristics, or to perform analysis on them. This made it difficult to explore the data and look for populations with particular non-compliance traits, etc.

Secondly, the only way most people had ever thought about correcting compliance problems was with audits. There was virtually no precedence for or history of thinking about aggregate populations and their characteristics, or of generating corrective actions.

The Office of Research sought to address both of these factors: they proposed a major investment in the

IRS' information infrastructure, including a data warehouse called the Compliance Research IS or CRIS.

To get beyond the ‘audit mentality’, they proposed a new organizational unit called the District Offices of Research and Analysis (DORA) for the 33 districts. Each DORA would be staffed by 6–8 peoples charged with doing market segmentation research and championing the concept at the district level.

## 4.2.5. The coalition

The Assistant Commissioner, Research and Statistics of Income was a strong advocate for the CRIS system and the market segment approach. He had strong support from his immediate superior. In addition to support at the top, key players in the National Office of Research had extensive experience in various functions in the districts and understood how to sell ideas to that audience.

## 4.2.6. Technical implementation

CRIS has been implemented incrementally. As an early test of the concept, in 1991–1992 field researchers in six districts were given a flat file of data containing 400 attributes from income tax forms for one particular industry. Between 1995 and 1998, different versions of CRIS were incrementally implemented across all districts. In 1998, the full CRIS system was implemented. It has a query processing front end that does automatic weighting (that is, compensates for the stratified sampling of different filing groups in generating figures for combined populations), and it includes sufficient business rules and intelligence to facilitate well-constructed queries.

The data warehouse has 800 GB of raw data (1.3 TB of total data) capturing 1900 data attributes. It is organized into five primary subject areas: business, returns, taxpayer transactions, income sources, and tax payments. Three hundred users from the DORAs and the National Office of Research have access to the data. Over the years, the investment has amounted to about US\$ 25 million.

## 4.2.7. Building the human resources infrastructure

Though they envisioned staffing the DORAs with people who had critical skills (computer research analysts, operations research analysts, statisticians, program analysts, etc.), the National Office of Research had minimal power over who actually filled the slots.

Individuals who lost other jobs in the organization often had first pick. As a result, the DORAs had employees who ranged from well trained and excited about the market segmentation research possibilities, to those much less well trained and enthusiastic. With this population of users, it has turned out that the incremental implementation helped ease the learning process.

To succeed in the market segmentation approach, the DORA analysts need to develop new patterns of behavior. The DORA managers were to provide the incentive structures to make this happen. It is not yet clear how successful this is. It seems clear that unless DORA members are actually rewarded for efforts at and successes in market segment research, their behavior could easily shift back into the familiar patterns they (and their managers) are more comfortable with.

## 4.2.8. Changing the budget process

Early on, the Assistant Commissioner, Research and Statistics of Income persuaded the Chief Operations Officer to move the Budget Planning Office under his control. Many of the market segment approach projects have unusually high expected pay-offs, but under the old budgeting process this did not necessarily result in funding. By gaining control of the budget process and making ‘expected return’ a key factor in funding decisions, the National Office of Research was able to ensure that research results and potential market segment projects got the visibility and the funding they needed.

## 4.2.9. Marketing the vision

The National Office of Research has made regular presentations at the semi-annual IRS executive conferences. Typically they have been given only enough time to summarize their initiatives. However, in 1997, the National Office of Research proposed profiling 11 projects in depth, and in an unusual shift they were given a whole day to do this. The response from the executives was very positive.

## 4.2.10. Managing expectations and delivering intermediate benefits

The National Office of Research delivered the technology and its benefits in stages over time. This has resulted in clear incremental improvements in the ability to access and report on taxpayer behavior. For example, valuable reports that in the past would have taken 1.5 years to generate can now be prepared in about 40 seconds.

One example speaks directly to the potential of the market segment approach. Recall that the 1986 tax law requiring SSNs for all dependents removed seven million false dependents in 1 year. However, analysis by the National Office of Research in the mid-1990s revealed several generic types of error involving social security numbers: parents claiming children who were no longer dependents, divorced parents claiming the same child, and fictitious SSNs. They sent letters to a small sample of the offending taxpayers, noting the exact error and advising them to correct it on their next return. Forty-five percent of the taxpayers corrected their behavior after receiving the letter. Based on these results, in the winter of 1998–1999, letters were sent to 2.5 million taxpayers, and it was expected that this would result in a half billion dollar increase in revenue the following year.

## 4.3. Financial services company

The financial services company (FSC) studied here is headquartered in the southeastern United States. It has approximately 7500 employees and operates in six southern and southeastern states. In addition to banking, FSC is a leading marketer of investment products and processor of healthcare payments.

## 4.3.1. A company in distress

1990 was not a good year for FSC. It lost US\$ 60 million and was operating under letters of agreement with regulators. Throughout FSC there was concern that it would be closed, and the larger banks would ‘come in and pick the pieces they wanted’. In response to this situation, the Board of Directors brought in a new senior management team to turn the bank around. This team’s first step was to tighten controls over costs.

## 4.3.2. A new vision: tailored client solutions

Management recognized, however, that it needed a long-term strategy if FSC was to survive in the increasingly competitive banking industry. FSC needed to identify a niche in which it could excel.

The strategy selected was to be ‘customer intimate’, that is, to know its customers exceptionally well. The name given to this strategy was ‘tailored client solutions (TCS)’. It was based on the premise that products tailored to customers’ needs would lead to stronger customer–bank relationships and long-term sustainable profits. Financial products, distribution channels, sales, and servicing would be integrated in ways designed to meet clients’ needs and preferences while achieving target profitability levels. The data needed to implement TCS would be provided by a data warehouse.

## 4.3.3. The vision is introduced

The TCS strategy was first introduced at the bank's 1995 strategic planning meeting. FSC's senior management used the meeting to discuss and refine plans for moving ahead. FSC was going to be managed in a new way. The focus would be on the customer rather than on products.

FSC concurrently developed the component parts of the strategy with each part providing significant financial impact. Once those component parts had been successfully implemented, the full TCS strategy was formally announced, in a September 1997 meeting. Using meetings and FSC's newsletter, the TCS strategy was communicated to everyone in the company. With favorable financial returns, bank personnel were quite supportive of TCS when it was fully announced.

## 4.3.4. Bringing in the right skills

While senior management had a strategy, it recognized that it did not have the skills in-house to implement it. The TCS strategy required an Executive Director of Marketing, experienced in developing and implementing technology-enabled marketing strategies. A-year-long search found a Director with the required experience. He brought in new managers and professionals to be part of his team.

## 4.3.5. The VISION data warehouse

A data warehouse was a necessary component of TCS. It is termed VISION and contains the following.

\- Transaction data: For every customer, the bank has all bank-related transactions, whether cashing a check, withdrawing money from an ATM, or using a debit card.

\- Demographic data: Through a combination of internally captured data, such as investment goals, gender, age, and zip code, and external data, such as credit ratings, the bank can create a comprehensive demographic understanding of each customer.

\- Behavioral data: Using conjoint analysis with a sample of over 3000 customers, the bank has a good understanding of customers' attitudes and preferences.

\- Revenue and cost data: Data is captured or derived to support calculations of the profitability of all of the bank's products and customers.

VISION was developed in phases with tangible business goals and technical deliverables at the end of each phase (e.g. the first phase identified the top revenue producing customers while a later phase provided profitability information for all of the bank's products and customers). This gave an even more accurate picture of the bank's best customers and most important products.

## 4.3.6. Uses of the VISION data warehouse

The users of VISION include financial analysts, marketing analysts, managers, and front-line personnel, such as personal bankers and tellers.

4.3.6.1. Financial analysis. The financial analysts assess the profitability of current and future products and the profitability of each of the bank's customers. Two applications show how this information is used.

4.3.6.1.1. Senior accounts. FSC offered a free checking account to clients who are 55 and older. Working with VISION, financial analysts discovered that the bank was making money from a few senior accounts but was experiencing a loss with others. Further analysis revealed that the average size of the checking account balance was the key factor in differentiating this. The minimum balance was increased in such a way that there was a low attrition rate but customers were made profitable.

4.3.6.1.2. Waiving of fees. Fees are an important source of income for banks. Yet it is common for customers to ask for fees to be waived. Using data from VISION, analysts decide the maximum amount in fees that can be waived each year per customer and make this information available to front-line personnel.

4.3.6.2. Marketing analysis. Marketing analysts try to forecast, as accurately as possible, the future actions of customers under different scenarios.

4.3.6.2.1. Cross selling products. Banks want to sell additional products to customers. VISION data is used to identify for every customer what product might cross sell. Whenever a customer has a transaction with the bank, whether it is with a teller, ATM, PC banking, or audio response system, the customer is provided with information about a product that should be of interest to him or her.

4.3.6.2.2. Select Awards. FSC's Select Awards program is designed to increase customer loyalty and increase customers' use of the company's products. With this program, a customer earns points for longevity with the bank, the breadth (i.e. number of products), and depth (i.e. average account balances) of the banking relationship. Each month customers receive a report of the number of points that they have earned. These can be redeemed in a variety of ways—discounted banking services, free items from retailers, free meals from restaurants, and free airline tickets. VISION data is used to analyze the profitability of products, set the points that are awarded, and determine the point levels for different awards.

4.3.6.2.3. Distribution management system. FSC's Distribution Management System is used to design channels that meet customers' needs and preferences in a way that is profitable to the bank. Using inputs from VISION, such as household profitability, how customers currently use the bank, customer preferences, and customer segments, the system calculates the optimum way to distribute products.

4.3.6.3. Other uses and impacts. Data from the warehouse is also critical in rethinking how to deploy bricks and mortar, employ staff in the branches, and perceive customers.

4.3.6.3.1. Redesigning bank operations. When the market justifies it, a full service branch is operated, with a full complement of personal bankers, customer and small business relationship managers, and investment specialists. When the market potential is less, a much smaller branch is operated, staffed by a single personal banker. These changes have consi-derable impact on FSC's employees. Consequently, FSC has used a change management process where it analyzes every employee in a branch, their skill sets, their competencies, and what they want to do in their careers in order to mitigate the potential negative reactions to the changes.

4.3.6.3.2. Rethinking who are good customers. The TCS strategy has changed the mindset of the bank's employees. Knowing the profitability of each customer has made employees rethink who are the 'good' customers. Now, a good customer may be one who is frequently overdrawn and pays the associated fees. The greater focus on the bottom line is also seen in a term used by analysts; they refer to their work as 'revenue mining'.

## 4.3.7. The strategic impact of TCS and data warehousing

At FSC, data warehousing is an integral part of corporate strategy. TCS could not be executed without it. FSC's success has not gone unnoticed. In 1998, FSC acquired another bank. The CEO of that bank said that FSC was attractive because he ‘wanted to join up with a financial institutions of the future and not a bank of the past’.

## 5. Discussion

All three of the initiatives are successful—all organizations are receiving important benefits. However, the extent of the benefits differs from organization to organization.

The first step in Kotter's model is to create a sense of urgency. At FSC this was easy to do; the company was facing failure. There was also a sense of urgency at the IRS, though not as severe. The sense of urgency at LMC was not as great: a warehouse was needed to facilitate the building of several important IS, but this was recognized primarily in the IS group.

To change an organization requires a strong coalition. FSC had a guiding coalition of the CEO and the rest of the senior management team. The IRS also had a strong coalition that included the Chief Operations Officer, the Assistant Commissioner for

Research and Statistics of Income, and other key players in the National Office of Research. The guiding coalition at LMC was considerably weaker. The champion for the warehouse was a high-level IT manager who left the company shortly after initiating the project.

There also must be a vision of a different future that individuals can believe in and rally around. There was such a vision at FSC that was reflected in its TCS strategy. Similarly at the IRS, Compliance 2000 was a strong vision for using market segmentation as a better way to get tax payers to pay their fair share, to collect more taxes without a public backlash. The vision for data warehousing at LMC was more modest (and understood only within IS)—build a better infrastructure that could provide support for a wide range of decision support applications.

A vision must be communicated throughout the organization so it can energize action. Interestingly at FSC, the full vision was not communicated throughout the organization initially. Top management was concerned that the vision was perhaps too grand to be believed, especially after the recent poor performance. At the IRS significant effort was given to communicating the vision both at the executive level and at the district level, though the District Offices of Research and Analysis. Finally, at LMC the vision for data warehousing as a key enabler for decision support applications has not been communicated throughout the organization to any extent.

For the vision to become part of the life of the organization, the proponents must actively move to create incentives to act in accordance with the vision, and to remove impediments. At times, key managers who undermine the changes must be ushered off stage. This has been very clear at FSC. A new management team was brought in, clearly sounding the warning that new ways were coming. At the IRS, though there are some obvious changes, the impact is less obvious. Part of keeping the momentum going for a change effort is planning for and creating short-term wins. This has been very clear at FSC, where each phase of the project was associated with clearly identifiable benefits, such as identifying the top revenue producing customers at the end of the first phase, then developing profitability indicators for customers and products at the end of another phase. The IRS also had a clear plan of incremental deliverables, each with a recognizable value to the organization. At LMC, while there are numerous examples of benefits, there is no obvious strategy of generating visible wins as a way of promoting the vision implicit in the data warehouse.

As the organization gains experience and benefits from the new vision, it is time to consolidate improvements and produce still more change. This is clearly taking place at FSC. The IRS is in a sense a bit too early in its cycle to be consolidating and expanding change. At LMC, although some individual managers in user departments are gradually incorporating the system into their procedures, it is not being used as vehicle for achieving a new overall vision for the organization.

Finally, it is necessary to institutionalize the changes by frequently articulating the links between the new behaviors and organizational success, and by developing the organizational structure to ensure continuation of the vision. At FSC, the information is a key piece of discussions about future possibilities for the bank. At IRS, the continued articulation of the connection between market segmentation and increased revenues is clear, especially with the day-long presentation of 11 projects to the semi-annual conference of IRS executives. At LMC, the recent loss of the data warehouse manager to a higher profile project makes the future growth of the system and its impacts more uncertain.

## 6. Conclusion

There is a striking contrast between FSC and the IRS on the one hand, and LMC on the other. In neither FSC nor the IRS is the data warehousing effort seen as anything other than a means to a strategic end. In both of these organizations, the strategic vision for changing the organization came first, and was followed by recognition of the importance of an appropriate IT infrastructure. In both, the effort was driven by the business side of the house. However, LMC has occasionally been mentioned in the trade literature as having a cutting edge IS department and took a technological approach.

FSC and the IRS both worked carefully and methodically to bring about a major organizational transformation. At FSC, it seems reasonable to declare victory—it appears that the hoped for transformation has taken place. At the IRS it is still too early to tell if the transformation will be successful.

At LMC, on the other hand, there was never any attempt to achieve a major organizational transformation, and none was achieved. The warehouse was almost an internal IS department affair. Business managers were never particularly aware of or interested in it, because they never saw any links between it and the strategic goals they were pursuing. The most important value of the data warehouse at LMC may ultimately be an incremental ease in delivering decision support applications. Though this value might ultimately be quite evident to IS managers, it may never be particularly visible to business managers.

Though it is dangerous to generalize from a few cases, these three raise the intriguing possibility that change and the truly momentous benefits are far more likely when the vision comes from the business side of the house rather than the IS side.
