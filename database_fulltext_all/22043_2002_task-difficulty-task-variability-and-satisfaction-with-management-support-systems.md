# Task difficulty, task variability and satisfaction with management support systems

Maarten Gelderman<sup>\*</sup>

Plantage Kerklaan 65/1, 1018 CX Amsterdam, The Netherlands

Received 12 January 2000; received in revised form 01 March 2001; accepted 26 March 2001

## Abstract

From its inception, the notion of management support systems (MSS) has been closely linked to task structure, which influences both the need for management support and the ability to provide it. This article presents the results of an empirical analysis to show the relation between task structure and satisfaction with MSS. Two dimensions of task structure are distinguished: task difficulty and task variability. The results of the analysis indicate that task difficulty negatively affects satisfaction with MSS, whereas the influence of task variability is insignificant

The provision of EIS functionality generally contributes to satisfaction with an MSS. This contribution is larger when task difficulty is high; as a result provision of EIS functionality fully compensates for the negative effect of task difficulty. However, task variability may frustrate this positive influence. Overall the effort indicates that, in researching the effectiveness of MSS success, one should not only look at the information that managers would like to have but also at the possibility of providing such information. The effect of both dimensions of task structure on this ability differs: task difficulty does not lead to problems, as long as the support does not require that cause–effect relations are understood but task variability leads to numerous exceptions when data are missing or not provided timely enough. If more features are build into the system, this situation tends to get worse. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Management support systems; Task structure; EIS functionality; DSS functionality; MSS success; Survey

## 1. Introduction

Task structure is the most intriguing variable in management support systems (MSS) research. On the one hand, task structure has been used to define the tasks that MSS ought to support [25,51], on the other hand, task structure has been used to explain MSS success [22–24,38,40,46]. This paper investigates the influence of task structure on MSS success and its relation with MSS features that the normative design literature deems important. Does drill-down functionality in an MSS really contribute to user satisfaction, or is the potential increase in information value more than offset by the complexities of the user interface? Even more interesting: how do design features of the MSS interact with task structure? May be some features are highly desirable in a structured environment but should not be implemented for unstructured tasks. Maybe functionality like what–if analysis and simulation, prediction, and planning functionality get too complex if tasks are unstructured and will consequently negatively affect MSS success. On the other hand, managers in a relatively structured environment may not need external data, non-financial data, and qualitative data and their provision would be just a waste of effort. In this paper, I discuss theoretical considerations concerning this topic, derive hypotheses and test them empirically on data gathered by a survey among Dutch managers.

## 2. Literature review and hypotheses development

## 2.1. Management support systems

Management support systems (MSS) are computerized systems that support managers in their day-to-day tasks by providing information and decision aids. Here, traditional decision support systems (DSS), as well as traditional management and executive information systems (MIS and EIS) are treated as manifestations of an MSS if they provide management support. This definition of MSS is at least a partial answer to the call of Snitkin and King [49] to revise the definition of DSS based on an ‘in use’ concept, as the original definition has become overworked. Although all systems are included the provision of functionality deemed characteristic for EIS or DSS will be assessed and will be used to explain success of the systems.

MSS support unstructured or semi-structured tasks [5,26,43,44,54], as the nature of managerial tasks is inherently unstructured. Consequently, MSS only support managers; their primary goal is to increase organizational performance by improving managerial effectiveness rather than managerial efficiency [2,9,12,48]. A similar conclusion is reached by Macintosh who observes that a MSS is not complete without a manager [33].

## 2.2. MSS success

The final aim of MSS is to enhance goal attainment of the organization by improving decision performance. Although goal attainment has been employed as a success measure in laboratory studies [8,37, 53,55], it is difficult, if not impossible to assess the contribution of MSS to goal attainment in real world situations. Consequently, in empirical studies MSS success has mainly been operationalized by usage and user satisfaction (US) [18]. The rationale is that a system will only be used if the benefits will outweigh the costs [35,45]. In this way usage is applied as an indirect measure of the users’ judgment of the value of the system. However, Ginzberg speaks of ‘a gap between our ability to develop new managerial technology and our ability to get managers to use it’ ([21], p. 459). Alternatively, usage may be considered as a neutral description of behavior, rather than a success measure [47].

This, however, requires usage to be voluntary; an assumption that is not met when usage is enforced. Furthermore, managers will only use the MSS when they perceive it to be useful for achieving their own goals. An assumption of perfect knowledge or goal congruence between user and organization is not necessarily true.

More problematic, however, is the observation that more usage does not always imply a greater contribution to organizational performance; this makes the amount-of-usage criterion valueless as a success measure. The application of a dichotomous usage measure, on the other hand, lacks sufficient sensitivity; it will only identify systems that are not used at all and not differentiate between systems that are used [7]. Whereas usage indirectly assesses the users’ judgment of MSS success, US directly assesses how well the IS meets the requirements. This approach is conceptually similar to the assessment of user evaluations of task technology fit [22]. However, Goodhue measures satisfaction with information systems and services available to the user, whereas the Doll and Torkzadeh [11] measure used in this study assessed satisfaction with a single application. Furthermore, the dimensionality of both measures is different.

Although US also suffers from the shortcoming that it is based on the match between information requirements and information provided as perceived by the user and may be affected by a lack of goal congruence between user and organization, it does not share the other shortcomings of the usage criterion. Consequently, US is the most widely applied success measure in empirical studies on MSS success [10,27, 39,50].

Gelderman [19], using a data set that also contains the data used in this study, found US to be related to organizational performance but did not find a similar relation between usage and performance. As both the theoretical considerations and this empirical evidence suggest that US is a reasonably adequate measure for the contribution of MSS to organizational performance, used to assess MSS success here.

## 2.3. Task structure

MSS aim at the support of unstructured tasks, the extent to which the tasks supported are unstructured may vary. It has been argued that MSS are more successful in less structured task environments, as the need for support is higher. From a more practical viewpoint, it has been claimed that MSS development is more difficult for less structured tasks and consequently the changes of MSS will decrease when tasks are less structured [13,15,20,32–34,36]. Similarly, it has been argued that although initially a decrease in task structure will imply an increase in MSS success, additional decreases in task structure will make MSS design more difficult and will consequently reduce MSS success. In addition, both Simon and Anthony [4] claim that MSS brought some decisions from the unstructured to the structured area. This may imply that MSS development induced managerial or organizational learning, a generally acknowledged benefit of MSS development. However, it may also represent a mismatch between MSS and the organizational environment.

Empirical material in this area is scarce. Elam and Leidner found that environmental turbulence is an important reason for MSS adoption [14], however, it may be determined by perceived MSS value rather than certainty that this goal will be achieved. McKeen, Guimaraes and Wetherbe found no relation between US and task complexity, although it turned out to be a significant moderator of the influence of user involvement. Sanders and Courtney also found no significant relation between task difficulty and task variability and US but found a positive, significant influence of task newness in their study. The positive influence of task newness on US, however, may well have another explanation than the influence of task structure: MacIntosh indicated that managers who are new in a function appreciate structured budgeting systems more, because those systems reduce their own uncertainty about the task at hand. A similar argument may hold for the presence of MSS. Goodhue found a negative relation between non-routineness of tasks and user evaluations of task technology fit.

A closer look indicates that three separate arguments may be distinguished. The first focuses on the need for MSS; this is negatively related to task structure. If a task is more structured, the need for

MSS is smaller. The second one concerns feasibility: this is positively related to task structure. Morestructured tasks are more easily supported by MSS. The third argument is that MSS may lead to organizational learning and consequently change task structure. Investigation of this reciprocal influence requires longitudinal research; this was not attempted here.

Definition of task structure deserves additional attention. The task of integrating the diverse notions of task structure was undertaken by Van de Ven and Ferry [52] in their seminal work on organizational assessment. In an elaborate study, they distinguish between two dimensions of task structure.

1. Task variability: the number of exceptions encountered in the characteristics of the work.

2. Task difficulty: the analyzability and predictability of the work in an organization unit.

Both of these indicate the extent to which search processes are trivial and programmed at one extreme or the other of the task difficulty continuum: the extent to which they rely upon chance and guesswork.

The general hypothesis about the influence of task structure on US can be applied to both dimensions. The problems of designing a ‘good’ MSS, however, seem to be mainly associated with task difficulty. This leads to two hypothesis — both are represented by the straight arrows from task variability and difficulty to US in Fig. 1.

Hypothesis 1a. Task difficulty has a negative influence on US.

Hypothesis 1b. Task variability has a negative influence on US.

## 2.4. MSS characteristics

Over 25 years ago, Mintzberg [41] pointed out that managers do not only need financial information. More implicitly he also indicated that the source of this kind of information is likely to lie outside the organization. A similar approach was taken in the Balanced Scorecard literature [28–31]. Although Kaplan and Norton implicitly focus on quantitative information, they stress the importance of non-financial measures, of external data, and of process data. This data does not describe results, but rather indicates how results have been achieved. Overall, those authors claim that certain MSS features — provision of nonfinancial, qualitative, and external data and drill-down functionality — will exert a direct positively influence on MSS success. Those features map reasonably well on the functionality of an EIS, these are here termed EIS functionality. The direct influence, can now be reworded as the following.

Hypothesis 2. The provision of EIS functionality positively affects US.

A closer look at EIS functionality indicates that it is likely that there is an interaction between task structure and the contribution of EIS functionality to MSS success. If task difficulty is low, the tasks are well understood and the calculation of unambiguous measures, like profit and ROI, pose few problems. However, if task difficulty is high the shortcomings of such measures become apparent and managers will need non-financial information, etc. Instead of one unambiguous measure, they need information that allows them to form judgments of their own. Ewusi–Mensah indicates that, in an uncontrollable environment, more qualitative and more external information is needed. This should be future oriented and on-time. Simultaneously, the quality of data is likely to be lower, a remark that seems particularly true for information that is processed before it is made available: the user must have confidence in the editing process.

Gordon and Miller discussed the influence of environmental dynamism on desired information system characteristics. They claim that when dynamism increases, MSS should incorporate more non-financial data to provide managers with information on competitor actions, consumer tastes, and shifting demographic factors. They also suggest that systems in such situations should contain detailed financial information; this allows managers to perform in-depth analysis if they wish.

If task difficulty is low, less ambiguous information is preferred, and internal and financial information may suffice. If task difficulty is high the shortcomings of this type of information may become apparent and additional data may be needed, while management simultaneously may feel the need to be able to analyze the data underlying other measures.

Hypothesis 3a. The positive effect of the provision of EIS functionality on US is larger if task difficulty is high.

Hypothesis 3b. The positive effect of the provision of EIS functionality on US is larger if task variability is high.

A key feature of EIS functionality is that task difficulty is not likely to have a negative influence on the quality of the data provided. Task variety does not exert a negative influence either, data may need updating more frequently and situations may be encountered in which the desired information is not available. The only negative influence of task difficulty and variability is that it may be difficult if not impossible to provide this information, if the information can be and is provided, it will contribute to MSS success and this contribution will increase in unstructured situations.

The opposite argument can be made for typical DSS features. Such features are model based and are used for predictions and trends, planning functionality, and what–if analysis and simulation. Although the value of such functionality also increases if task structure decreases the models underlying such functionality are, almost by definition, negatively affected. If task difficulty is high, cause–effect relations are not well understood and the models are likely to be flawed; if task variability is high, exceptions occur frequently and the models are likely to be incomplete and possibly outdated. Ginzberg proposed that, in unstable environments, ‘decisional systems containing highly articulated formal models will be less likely to succeed than will decisional system providing more generalized support.’ MacIntosh and Daft [34] make a similar claim, presenting evidence from a case study, where due to high task difficulty (‘limited knowledge of cause and effect relations’ in their terminology), econometric models were useless.

It should be noted that the opposite claim has been made. Gordon and Miller claim that when environmental dynamism increases, effective MSS should make ‘greater use of forecast information [. . .] to inform managers of trends and important issues before they become difficult problems.’ Again, however, this consideration only refers to a need for information, and does not consider whether it can be provided.

Hypothesis 4a. High task difficulty will negatively affect the influence of the provision of DSS functionality on MSS success.

Hypothesis 4b. High task variability will negatively affect the influence of the provision of DSS function ality on MSS success.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Survey, sample and response

In order to test the hypotheses empirically, a questionnaire was send out to a sample of 550 Dutch managers. The questionnaire, which also contained questions for another research project, was 12 pages long and printed on colored paper in order to increase response rates (as suggested by Fowler [16]). With the questionnaire the respondents received an answer card that could be used to request the results of the survey, a postage-paid return envelope and a letter on university stationary; this explained the aim of the study and guaranteed the respondents confidentiality. One month after the mailing, a reminder was send out to nonrespondents. Of the 550 managers, 87 (15.8%) returned a useful questionnaire, 22 (4%) returned the questionnaire but did not provide valid responses. The majority of the 22 questionnaires were accompanied by a letter explaining corporate policy that prevented cooperation with survey research. Four hundred fourty-one questionnaires (80.2%) were not returned.

Of the 87 useful responses, 34 were filled out by a manager to whom no MSS was available. The remaining 53 managers had an MSS; 34 managers used the system themselves, 14 used it with the help of an assistant and 4 did not use it at all (one manager did not answer this question).

## 3.2. Measurement instruments

Three categories of variables are essential to this study: task structure, US, and the MSS characteristics. The first two categories of variables were assessed using existing measurement instruments, the last category was assessed by asking the respondents about the functionality of their MSS.

To assess task structure the measurement instruments for the assessment of task difficulty and task variability developed by Van de Ven and Ferry were used. These instruments provide the best possible match with the definitions of both constructs used here. Furthermore, these measurement instruments have been extensively validated by their authors.

US was measured by the Doll and Torkzadeh [11] instrument. Contrary to the older and more widely used Bailey and Pearson [6] instrument this measures satisfaction with an individual application and not with information in general.

The validity and reliability of both the task structure and the US instrument were assessed using confirmatory factor analysis to assess the measurement properties of the instrument and discriminant and convergent validity, Cronbach’s a to assess reliability, and a procedure suggested by the American Psychology Association [3] to assess face validity. An expert panel, consisting of three members of faculty and two management consultants, was provided with a list of 34 variable names and a copy of the questionnaire from which headings explicitly indicating the nature of the variables measured were removed. The experts were informed that each question was supposed to measure one variable and that each variable could be measured by multiple questions and were asked to indicate for each question which variable it would measure. The face validity of a scale is defined as the average number of experts that placed the item underneath the right variable name.

$$
\text { face   validity } (\%) = \frac {1}{m} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} \left(\frac {1}{n _ {i}} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n _ {i}} y _ {i j}\right) \times 100\tag{1}
$$

where m is the number of items in the scale, $n _ { i }$ is the number of experts that classified item i, and

$$
y _ {i j} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 0 & \text { if   the   expert   did   not   mention   the   intended   variable, } \\ \frac {1}{q} & \text { if   the   expert   mentioned   q   variables,   including   the   intended   one, } \\ 1 & \text { if   the   expert   only   mentioned   the   intended   variable. } \end{array} \right.
$$

After some small modifications of the instrument<sup>1</sup> all psychometric properties turned out to be acceptable to good. A summary of reliabilities and face validities of the constructs is provided in Table 1. All items loaded on the correct constructs and the assumption of equality of factor loadings (an assumption of Cronbach’s a and a requirement for the usage of summed scales) could be maintained. Discriminant validity was satisfactory as well. Unfortunately, the assumption of equality of observed covariance matrix and the covariance matrix implied by the measurement model should be rejected for the US instrument. Table 2 gives an overview of the functionality available in the systems used by the respondents. Almost every system provided financial data. A large majority also provided quantitative non-financial data. Graphics are available to 64% of the respondents. Slightly less than 50% indicated that the system gave information on performance indicators. The least available elements in the MSS were external data, what–if analysis and simulation, and qualitative data. The EIS functionality provided by the MSS was defined as the number of the options from: quantitative non-financial data, qualitative data, external data, and drill-down functionality. Similarly, DSS functionality is defined as the number of the following options provided: what–if analysis and simulation, trends and predictions, and planning functionality.

## 4. Results

To test the hypotheses MANOVA was used. Regression would not suffice, as US consists of multiple constructs (repeatedly carrying out regression analysis would result in capitalization on chance as each hypothesis is tested five times). LISREL analysis or partial least squares analysis seemed a suitable alternative, but given the limited number of observations and the need to model interaction terms, this approach was practically impossible. MANOVA remained as the most suited technique,<sup>2</sup> but the price to be paid, was that some continuous variables had to be converted to groups that were distinguished by ranking: the subjects were simply divided into high and low task difficulty groups, high and low task variability groups, etc. In all subsequent analyses, the homogeneity of covariance matrices across groups was assessed.

## 5. Summary and discussion

## 5.1. Summary of findings

The tests of the hypotheses show that, by and large, task difficulty has the expected influence on MSS success, but that task variability has not. Apparently the ability to provide support is mainly affected by task difficulty and not by task variability. The increased contribution of EIS functionality in unstructured situations was only found to matter in situations of high task difficulty, but not high variability. If task variability is high, the contribution of EIS functionality to MSS success even decreases. The general characteristic of such functionality—more information as long as it is not interpreted by some kind of model— contributes to US as the need for such information increases due to high task difficulty or as due to similar reasons the shortcomings of traditional information become apparent. However, if task variability is high, the opposite effect is found. Both dimensions of task structure serve a distinct function. Task difficulty affects the knowledge of cause–effect relations. When such knowledge is absent satisfaction increases if unprocessed data are provided. The decrease in satisfaction for increases in variability is mainly caused by satisfaction with content and timeliness and may be explained by the fact that data are too often missing or outdated; understanding of cause–effect relations is not the only relevant issue.

Analyzing the hypotheses related to DSS functionality both dimensions of task structure serve a distinct role. The interaction between DSS functionality and task difficulty is not significant, the contribution of such functionality to US, surprisingly, does not depend on task difficulty. The interaction of DSS functionality with task variability is significant. Task variability negatively influences the effect that DSS functionality has on US. This finding is similar to the interaction between task variability and EIS functionality.

## 5.2. Sensitivity analysis and rival hypotheses

A major question that remains, is: how sensitive are the results presented to alternative definitions of the critical design dimensions — DSS and EIS functionality?

Goodhue argues ‘that managers who deal with routine (repetitive and analyzable) issues will, over time, develop ways to work around weaknesses in the way information systems support those tasks’ and furthermore states that accessing the data will become routine, which may influence perceived ease-of-use of the MSS. A closer look at this argument, however, suggests that such routinization is mainly related to task variability and not to task difficulty. For task variability, however, no direct influence on MSS success was found.

More disturbing is an alternative explanation that claims that the results are just because managers receive less information than they desire. The negative influence of task difficulty on US might be explained by the fact that task difficulty implies that managers have an incomplete model of their decision situation. In 1967, Ackoff [1] claimed that managers with an incomplete model want to ‘play it safe’ and obtain as much information as possible. This may imply that they desire more information than can economically be justified. If their MSS only provides as much information as can be justified, the manager’s evaluation may not reflect the contribution of the MSS to organizational performance. In such a situation, US would no longer be a valid success measure.

Other authors have indicated that high task difficulty and task variability indicate that managers need more information [17,42]. In this situation, US would remain a valid measure of MSS success, but the model presented in Fig. 1 would no longer be accurate, as decreased US scores would be explained by dissatisfaction with the amount of information provided. The influence of task structure on US would then be explained by the model shown in Fig. 2. Unfortunately, a measure for the amount of information provided was not included in the questionnaire. Instead I used a single item measure asking the respondents whether they received the right amount of information to test this alternative hypothesis. Using LISREL, I tested whether after incorporating the indirect influence of task structure through amount of information on US a direct influence of task structure on US remained. After incorporating the indirect effect a significant direct effect still was found.<sup>5</sup>

It has also been suggested that frequency of reporting causes problems in unstructured situations. Providing information more frequently would be beneficial. MANOVA was used to assess whether the daily provision of information would alleviate the problems caused by task structure. The results of this analysis are presented in Table 4. The results indicate that neither the interaction between daily reporting and task variability, nor the interaction between daily reporting and task difficulty is significant. However, those findings should be treated with caution. The assumptions of MANOVA are not met in this case. Investigation of univariate results indicates that daily reporting positively affects satisfaction with format, timeliness and ease-of-use. The interactions with task variability and task difficulty are also not significant for the univariate results.

In the definition used for the analyses in the previous section, the usage of performance indicators was not considered an EIS characteristic. The reason was that the definition of EIS functionality focused on the provision on non-processed information. The usage of performance indicators, however, implies that the data are processed to derive an outcome measure. Such processing may lead to undesirable results if task difficulty is high. This decision, however, does imply that the label EIS functionality is not strictly appropriate, as performance indicators may well be considered a typical feature of such systems. The analysis was repeated using a modified definition of EIS functionality in which performance indicators were added. Although a direct positive influence of EIS functionality is still found, the interaction term is no longer significant.

I then tested what would be the influence of task variability and task difficulty if we just looked at the functionality provided by the MSS, i.e. a functionality measure, consisting of the number of the features provided by the MSS was used. Again the analysis showed that because of the inclusion of processed data in this functionality measure, the influence of the EIS functionality was attenuated and a significant interaction of the new functionality measure and US was not found.<sup>6</sup> The results for variability are significant in this case and suggest that the provision of more functionality increases US, if task variability is high this becomes a decrease; if task variability is low, the increase is larger.

A concluding remark on US is in place. It is noteworthy that the majority of the univariate results that turned out to be significant involved the content and timeliness dimension. Of course this may just be accidental, but it might also be that those two dimensions are most closely related to environmental characteristics, whereas the other subdimensions would be related to development, maintentance, or design of the system.

Finally, it should be acknowledged that an indisputable limitation of this study is introduced by the method — survey research. First, analysis of survey data can only be used to assess correlations and not causality. Second, a zero influence may have been found for variables that have a devastating impact on MSS success. In cases where the system is a total failure, it may fall into total disuse; in this case, the present sample really contains MSS that range from successful to ‘almost’ unsuccessful.
