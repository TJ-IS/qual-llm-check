# Predicting customer behavior in the market-space: a study of Rayport and Sviokla's framework

Hsipeng Lu $^{a,*}$ , Judy Chuan-Chuan Lin $^{a,b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Information Management, National Taiwan University of Science and Technology, Taipei, Taiwan, ROC $^{b}$ Department of Computer and Information Science, Soochow University, Taipei, Taiwan, ROC

Received 10 July 2000; received in revised form 3 March 2001; accepted 22 August 2001

## Abstract

The rapidly evolving phenomenon of the World Wide Web and the creation of a new international arena for electronic commerce have expanded the possibilities for the marketing of products and services. The role played by customer behavior in the market-space is still not entirely understood and calls for investigations into the significance of customer attitudes and loyalty. In this article, a model is developed; it examines the significance of content, context, and infrastructure in determining customer loyalty. An empirical study involving 145 subjects was conducted to test this model and to gain a better understanding of the relationship between customer belief about a Web site (specifically, an e-publishing site) and customer behavior (such as attitudes toward brand and customer loyalty).

The results indicate that customer attitude is influenced by belief about brand equity (value), which is affected by the content, context, and infrastructure. Customer loyalty is determined by attitude and belief about the context in which the products or services are offered. These findings provide a better understanding of customer behavior associated with Web sites in the market-space, also confirm the proposition of Rayport and Sviokla that ‘customer loyalty is developed at the context level’. © 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Internet marketing; Customer behavior; Web site management

## 1. Introduction

The rapidly evolving phenomenon of the World Wide Web (WWW) has resulted in a paradigm shift that has greatly influenced business operations [4,7,22,42,43,45], mainly through its promotion of the growth of electronic commerce. Today's business managers, therefore, must not only seek to retain their competitiveness in the traditional marketplace, but must also seize the opportunities offered by the electronic marketplace $[50]$ . In this market-space, a product or service no longer needs to be physically present. Instead, the information about such products or services can be formatted and presented to customers through electronic media. Most e-companies begin by designing and launching Web sites to gain inexpensive publicity, display their products and services, and provide a method whereby customers may purchase what the company is offering. As a result, the new and evolving market-space already offers a variety of physical goods (e.g. books, personal computers, clothing), digital goods (e.g. software), as well as for e-publishing available through subscription (e.g. electronic newspapers).

To help business managers realize the full potential of the market-space, Rayport and Sviokla developed a framework for creating new values for customers [44]. In this framework, a customer's perceived value of a product or service consists of the three basic elements, content of the product or service that companies are offering, context of how companies are offering the product or service, and infrastructure that enables the transaction to occur. In the traditional marketplace, content, context, and infrastructure are bundled together, usually by the same company, to establish brand equity, a depiction of customer-perceived value provided by the product. However, the separation of these three elements is easily done in the market-space. By disaggregating content, context, and infrastructure, the company can “create new ways of adding value, lowering costs, forging relationships with non-traditional partners, and rethinking ‘ownership’ issues”.

To illustrate this point, Rayport and Sviokla use a newspaper as an example. In the traditional marketplace, news and information are provided to subscribers through physical delivery. The publisher often creates the value for its subscribers by integrating content (news, business, sports, etc.), context (format, editorial style, logo, etc.), and infrastructure (printing plant and physical distribution). However, in the market-space, the content of an on-line newspaper is less restricted and can come from single or multiple publishers. To offer an on-line newspaper, an electronic format is designed; this constitutes the context of the newspaper in the form of a homepage. The infrastructure to enable such an offering includes digital networks, telephone lines or cables, and personal computers or Internet-capable devices equipped with modems or other connectivity devices, none of which are owned by the content or context providers. The characteristics of the Internet provide an impetus to disaggregate these three elements and, therefore, publishers of electronic newspapers need not support the value activities for all three elements, as in the traditional marketplace.

Relying on the concept that content, context, and infrastructure can be disaggregated, Rayport and Sviokla used many existing cases to exemplify the creation of new values for customers. Furthermore, several succeeding reports have extracted this idea to examine the strategies for the new and rapidly evolving channels specific to companies operating within the marketplace $[8,27,34,37,41]$ . However, these studies are confined to the scope of qualitative exploration. The quantitative linkage between the three elements and transactions has generally been ignored.

The purpose of this paper is therefore to investigate the quantitative linkage between Rayport and Sviokla's framework and transaction in the market-space under the premise that the three elements are disaggregatable. In particular, this study attempts to determine how well this three-element framework can explain customer attitude and customer loyalty toward the subscription to an e-publishing service in the market-space. This inquiry is very important for the following reasons. First, it fills the research gap, as stated above, by adding quantitative analysis. Second, it provides another approach to test this framework. Prior studies document, the application of this framework at the level of case studies, yet no notable, extant research examines its descriptive validity at the level of large samples. Third, the results of our study provide suggestions for on-line publishers to develop strategies to compete in the market-space. Thus, we present a model of a Web user's perceptions about a market-space product from a perspective of Rayport and Sviokla's framework. In addition, multiple regression is applied to explore the empirical strength of the relationships in the proposed model.

## 2. Customer behavior in the market-space

Just as in the traditional marketplace, companies need to build customer loyalty as well as attract new customers in the market-space; however, the special characteristics of the Internet have altered the rules for marketing. In fact, customer acquisition and retention are considered the biggest challenges for on-line companies today $[20]$ . The Internet provides a unique marketing channel that not only allows multilateral communications among companies and their customers $[21]$ , but also grants the possibility of establishing a one-to-one relationship between the provider and the buyer $[18]$ . Through the market-space, firms can identify their customers' needs more precisely and thus improve customer satisfaction as well as retention [14]. The customer-oriented marketing concept presented by Kotler [28] is no longer a mirage. In addition, the Internet also provides a suitable environment to develop virtual communities, with strong bonds among the members [19]. According to Hagel III and Armstrong, the company that owns a successful virtual community can retain customers easily and eventually gain true customer loyalty.

The above studies highlight the distinctive features of the relationship between companies and customers in the market-space, yet these studies do not answer some of the following questions.

\- What are the factors that affect customers' attitudes about and loyalty to a company or a product brand offered in the market-space?

\- Is there a relationship between customer attitude and loyalty in the market-space?

\- How can customer loyalty toward a brand be enhanced in the market-space?

To answer these questions, we propose a model to examine customer attitude as well as brand loyalty in the market-space. In doing so, the relative impact of the three elements on customer attitude and loyalty can be assessed to provide insights into customers' behavior in the market-space.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

This study developed a research model by modifying the theory of reasoned action (TRA) [16]. According to Fishbein and Ajzen, TRA advocates that social behavior is influenced by belief, attitudes, and intentions. It has been widely and successfully used to explain social behavior. Yet its validity in explaining the behavioral intention in the newly developed market-space remains an open but interesting issue.

Fig. 1 depicts Rayport and Sviokla's framework representing customer behavior in the market-space using TRA. Customers' beliefs about a particular brand's content, context, and infrastructure have impacts on their attitude toward repetitive transaction for the product or the service associated with it. Further, these attitudes mediate the impact of customers' beliefs on the intention of repetitive transactions with the firm (customer loyalty). Thus, the research model not only follows the belief–attitude–intention link, but also incorporates a linkage among beliefs about content, context, infrastructure, and customer loyalty.

According to TRA, intention is mediated by attitude, and attitude by belief. Therefore, it is our first proposition that customer attitude is determined by belief about the content, context, and infrastructure of a product or service in the market-space ( $H_{1a-c}$ ). Though TRA postulates that the effect of belief on intentions is fully mediated only by attitude, prior research [6,11,12,49] provided evidence as well as theoretical explanations of direct belief–intention links. Since the Internet is a newly evolved media, the effect of a direct belief–intention link needs to be considered. Therefore, our second and third propositions are: that intention of repetitive transaction, customer loyalty, is determined by customer attitude ( $H_{2}$ ) and the beliefs about brand equity ( $H_{3a-c}$ ). Hence, this study proposes the following hypotheses.

## 4. Research method

To test the research model, an empirical study was conducted. We selected China Times Inter@ctive (http://www.chinatimes.com.tw) as the e-publishing Web site to be tested using this model. This is an Internet content provider that provides aggregated news and information from various sources including China Times and its affiliated newspapers and magazines. Thus, it provides a channel not only for China Times, but also for its affiliated newspapers. It also acts as a content provider, furnishing news content to business partners such as www.kimo.com.tw, a portal site based in Taiwan. Thus, China Times Inter@ctive is a good e-publishing site for our study.

## 4.1. Research sample

The sample included 145 undergraduate students (male 92, female 53) who were taking a course “basic computer concepts" at Soochow University in Taipei, Taiwan. Although such a student sample may not represent an unbiased group and thus cause a generalization problem, the study used student subjects for four reasons.

1. According to the survey done by Yam.com, a popular portal site in Taiwan (http://www.yam.com.tw), about 80% of Internet users in Taiwan are college graduates [31].

2. Such students will eventually become the most active Internet users and influential consumers in the market-space. Understanding the needs and preferences of potential customers are important and desirable.

3. The use of students can decrease the effect of computer literacy variances.

4. In addition, although the development of electronic commerce in Taiwan is still in a somewhat nascent stage as compared to that of the US and Europe, the degree of its Internet penetration is one of the highest among Chinese societies. The findings can provide a further understanding of customer perception in the Chinese market-space and offer an impetus for future research.

## 4.2. Operationalization and measurement of the constructs

The five constructs included in our study are: (1) customer loyalty; (2) customer attitude; (3) content-belief; (4) context-belief; and (5) infrastructure-belief.

## 1. Customer loyalty

According to Methlie and Nysveen, loyalty is an intention to keep on using a service, such as an on-line bank [36]. Moreover, Reichheld and Schefter state that as a customer increases repetitive use of functions or services provided by a Web site, loyalty toward the Web site will increase [46]. Therefore, we operationalize customer loyalty as: students' intention to revisit China Times Inter@ctive. TRA states that an individual's intention of performing a behavior has a direct impact on individual behavior. Thus, the stronger an individual's intention of repeating usage of the service provided by China Times Inter@ctive, the higher the loyalty to them. A scale with two items, adapted and modified from Davis [11] to suit the Internet environment, was used to measure the students' intention to revisit the site.

## 2. Customer attitude

We operationalize customer attitude as students' positive feelings towards repetitive use of the site. To develop a scale for measuring this, we referred to Davis with modification to suit the Internet setting. A scale of five items about students' positive feelings toward using China Times Inter@ctive was thus constructed to measure their attitude to it. Since these students had used the Web site before responding to the scale, their responses represented their attitude towards its repetitive use.

## 3. Beliefs about content, context, and infrastructure

In this study, a composition of content-belief, context-belief, and infrastructure-belief is used to represent a customer beliefs about particular brand equity. The definition of these three beliefs is following.

3.1. Content-belief. Rayport and Sviokla stated that content is the product/service offered by the company. China Times Inter@ctive is an information provider, and the quality of information it provides is crucial to its users. Thus, content-belief is defined as the student's perception of the quality of information that the site provides. A scale of six items was developed to measure a student's belief about its content.

3.2. Context-belief. Context is denoted as the means of offering the content. Rayport and Sviokla further discussed the “intelligent front end” as the way that users effectively consume the news content. Providing a good interface is an important task for the on-line newspaper. Thus, context-belief is defined as the student’s perception of the degree to which China Times Inter@ctive’s interface design is effective. A scale of four items was developed to measure a student’s belief about the context.

3.3. Infrastructure-belief. The infrastructure of an on-line newspaper is a collection of assets, such as telephone lines, networks, etc. The efficiency of the infrastructure affects the user's willingness to use an on-line newspaper. Thus, the user's perception concerning the efficiency of the infrastructure (infrastructure-belief) also provides valuable information for managing a successful on-line newspaper. In this study, infrastructure-belief is defined as the user's perception of the efficiency of China Times Inter@ctive. A scale of three items was developed to measure a student's beliefs about its infrastructure.

These items were based on a pilot study using 20 experienced Internet users as subjects. These experts were asked to list items that corresponded to the constructs. The most frequently mentioned items were included in the instrument. To reduce the possible ambiguity of the questions, a pretest of the instrument was performed. The items in the instrument were then modified in response to the results of the pretest (see Table 1).

## 4.3. Scale reliability and validity

In developing the scales for measuring these constructs, we used Cronbach alpha to assess the reliability [10], and used factor analysis to ensure the construct validity [26]. The coefficient alphas for the content-belief, context-belief, infrastructure-belief, customer attitude, and customer loyalty were 0.66, 0.70, 0.72, 0.62, and 0.81, respectively. Since the Cronbach alpha values were above the conventional level [40], the scales for these constructs were deemed to exhibit adequate reliability.

With respect to the scales measuring customer attitude and customer loyalty, factor analysis yielded only one with eigenvalue greater than unity for each scale. To ensure that content-belief, context-belief, and infrastructure-belief are indeed three distinct constructs, the 13 items included in the three belief scales were used for performing factor analysis with varimax rotation. The results yielded three factors with eigenvalue greater than unity (see Table 2). Among the three factors, factor 1 corresponded to content-belief (six items), factor 2 to context-belief (four items), and factor 3 to the infrastructure-belief (three items).

## 5.3. Analytic strategy for assessing the model

The data were analyzed using path analysis. Path analysis is a multivariate analytical methodology for empirically examining sets of relationships in the form of linear causal models $[25,29]$ .

Specifying a path diagram is the first step in path analysis. The hypothetical causal relationships are represented by unidirectional arrows linking two variables together. According to the model, the three elements of belief and attitude are all posited to exert simultaneous, direct, and linear influences on loyalty, while the three elements of belief act on attitude as well. The value of the path coefficient associated with each path represents the strength of each linear influence.

The path coefficients can be estimated in many ways. However, multiple regression analyses (i.e. ordinary least squares estimation) have been used by many empirical applications, including sociology $[13]$ , psychology $[51]$ , and management $[3,24,32,39]$ . The path coefficient has been shown to be identical to the standardized regression coefficients. These are comparable among variables, because they are normalized into the same unit. Path analysis excels conventional regression analysis in terms of the former's ability to extend “the single–multiple regression equation treatment to a network of equations involving more than one equation” (see $[30]$ , p. 135).

## 6. Discussion

This study attempted quantitatively to investigate customer behavior toward the acceptance of an e-publishing product, based upon Rayport and Sviokla's framework. While all the binary relationships between research variables were statistically significant, the path analyses reveal that some of the relationships may have been spurious.

The results of this study confirm hypotheses $H_{1a-c}$ : that customer attitude is positively related to beliefs about brand equity, as measured by content–belief, context–belief, and infrastructure–belief. Moreover, the effect of context–belief and infrastructure–belief are statistically stronger than that of content–belief, as is evident from the path analysis.

Another finding, and perhaps the most intriguing one, is the relative impact of context-belief on customer loyalty. The study indicates that customer loyalty can be predicted by a customer's attitude and context-belief about a brand, but not by content-belief or infrastructure-belief. This finding is not only in line with the results of prior studies that indicated a strong link between attitude and intention [1,2,9,17,23,33,35,47,48], but also coincides with the concept proposed by Rayport and Sviokla that “customer loyalty must be developed at the context level first”.

The insignificance of the direct effect of content and infrastructure on customer loyalty can be explained from a cost–benefit perspective. Since the content provided by China Times Inter@ctive is gratis, the customers tend to be less critical of the benefit gained from a free content. Likewise, provision of an effective “front end” (i.e. context) enables the readers to reduce their search effort and transaction cost. Therefore, context–belief affects customer loyalty more significantly than content–belief.

As for the perception of infrastructure, the general consensus is that infrastructure-related construction is not a responsibility of the content providers. Hence, though efficiency of the infrastructure is important in forming an attitude toward China Times Inter@ctive, it is not a significant factor in generating customer loyalty.

## 7. Conclusion and limitations

Our results supported Rayport and Sviokla's framework: three elements influence customer attitude and loyalty to a particular e-publishing product in the market-space. More specifically, the beliefs about brand equity in terms of content, context, and infrastructure explain $47\%$ of the variance on customer's attitude toward e-publishing. Similarly, content, context, infrastructure, and customer's attitude explain

49% of the variance in customer loyalty. The results lead to a better understanding of the relationship between this framework and customer behaviors in the market-space.

Furthermore, one plausible finding is that the result confirms the proposition that customer loyalty is affected at the context level. On-line publishers, therefore, should put more emphasis upon the provision of an effective context. This, in turn, will develop brand equity and loyalty. Content and infrastructure, however, do not directly affect customer loyalty. Our findings showed that improving the beliefs about brand equity represented by content, context, and infrastructure would have a direct effect on the formation of customer attitude, which exerts significant influence on customer loyalty.

Future studies may investigate potential factors besides the three elements that might be significant in predicting customer loyalty as well as attitude. Despite the strong significance of the three elements in predicting customer loyalty and attitude, other factors, such as subjective norm, peer influence, and enterprise image could exert additional impact as well. Furthermore, demographic factors could also affect customer attitude.

The results of this study should be interpreted and accepted with caution for several reasons. First, statistical analysis only provides numerical relationships. Interpretation of the numbers represents the authors' subjective appraisal. Secondly, it should be noted that loyalty is measured here by intention to reuse China Time Inter@tive, and that other similar products or services were not offered as competitive alternatives. Nevertheless, prior research has indicated a strong tie between “intention to revisit” a Web site and “loyalty” to it. Therefore, despite the possible shortcomings of our measurement of loyalty, we believe that the knowledge gained from this study is valuable. The other difficulty is the limitation on generalizability. Conclusions are based on a single product, electronic publishing, and the subjects are students. Care should be exercised when generalizing these results to other settings. However, results consistent with other studies and theories, such as TRA and the value framework, enhance confidence in the findings. Therefore, this study provides further understanding about the utility of the market-space.
