# Effect of store design on consumer purchases: van empirical study of on-line bookstores

Ting-Peng Liang $^{*}$ , Hung-Jen Lai

Department of Information Management, National Sun Yat-sen University, Kaohsiung 80424, Taiwan, ROC

Received 8 December 1999; received in revised form 2 February 2001; accepted 16 March 2001

## Abstract

Electronic commerce, that allows business to be performed over the Internet, has been a major trend in this century. Many businesses are moving on-line. However, there are neither guidelines nor theories to show how electronic store features affect consumer purchases. This paper uses a consumer-oriented perspective to derive functional requirements for e-store design. These needs are grouped into three categories: motivation, hygiene, and media richness.

An empirical study was conducted on three on-line bookstores to evaluate the relative importance of these factors. The results show that the quality of e-store design has an effect on the consumer purchase decision. Consumers are more likely to shop at well-designed websites. Among the on-line functions, support of transaction and post-sales services play key roles. Hygiene factors are critical when consumers decide whether to shop electronically, while motivational factors play a key role when consumers choose among different electronic stores. Media richness factors are, in general, least important. The implication of the findings is that providing good transactional support and post-sales services to the customer is the key. © 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Electronic commerce; On-line shopping; Consumer behavior; Web system design; Electronic business

## 1. Introduction

The introduction of the Internet, coupled with the rapid proliferation of the world wide web (WWW), has created a challenging arena for business. A surge of interests in e-commerce that allow transactions to be conducted over the Internet has propagated from the US to virtually every corner of the world. Web-based e-stores increase at an unprecedented speed. The number of stores have increased at an annual rate of greater than 50 percent in some industries, such as banking, stock trading, and bookstores [17]. Although some dot-com companies have faced difficulties recently, it is clear that the trend of going on-line is the norm today.

Given its enormous potential, research in the area has increased very fast and expanded to many different lines. For instance, Hoffman et al. [11] and Hamilton [10] examined the opportunities and challenges of the web. Tenenbaum et al. [22] proposed an architecture for Internet commerce. Kambil [14] studied potential electronic business processes. Bailey and Bakos [2] examined the impact of electronic markets on intermediaries. Dreze and Zufryden [7] proposed a methodology for assessing the effect of advertising on the

Internet. Lee and Clark [15] examined the impact of electronic markets on firm competitiveness and market structures. Jarvenpaa and Todd [13] studied consumer reactions to electronic shopping and identified product perceptions, shopping experience, and customer services as major factors for consumers. Bhimani [3] and Denning [6] discussed security in electronic commerce. Panurach [21] and Neuman [19] studied electronic payment systems. Gupta et al. [9] studied merchandise pricing in e-commerce. Liang and Huang [16] found that transaction costs play a key role in consumer selection of electronic channels. Liu and Arnett [18] and Wan [23] examined the factors associated with website success and opportunities that enhance commercial websites. Four factors affecting website success have been identified: information and service quality, system use, playfulness, and system design quality.

The purpose of this article is to examine the effect of store design quality on consumer purchases using on-line bookstores as the test bed. The design of an on-line store plays the same role as the layout of a conventional store. However, design of an on-line store is more influential than the layout of a conventional store. This is because web pages are, in most cases, the only way that customers know which products are available at the store and about other information needed to complete an electronic transaction. Hence, a well-designed store should be more attractive for customers.

Another issue to explore is whether and why certain functions are more important than others. Previous research findings on the relative importance of design functions of virtual stores are inconclusive $[12,20]$ . Building knowledge of the relative importance can generate useful guidelines for designing successful on-line stores.

Here, functional requirements of on-line stores are derived from the customer's perspective, using the consumer choice model proposed by Engel et al. (the “EKB” model) [8]. The requirements are organized to create a set of guidelines for designing and evaluating electronic stores. These guidelines are further divided into three theoretical categories:

\- Motivators, such as having a good search engine, provide direct support to the customer transaction process.

\- Hygienic, such as having good security, protect customers from risks or unwanted events in the transactional process.

\- Media richness, such as providing chat rooms, add more information channels to the process.

The remainder of the article is organized into three sections. Factors that may affect the successful design of e-stores are summarized in Section 2. The empirical design and findings are reported in Section 3. Section 4 concludes the paper.

## 2. Factors for designing successful on-line stores

Designing a good website is a complicated task. Many factors must be taken into account. A good design must provide adequate functional support to meet customer needs at each stage of their decision processes. Jarvenpaa and Todd categorized customer concerns into four aspects: product perception (quality, variety, price), shopping experience (effort, compatibility, playfulness), customer service (responsiveness, assurance, reliability, tangibility, empathy), and consumer risks (economic risk, social risk, performance risk, privacy risk). Palmer and Griffith proposed a model of website design for marketing that includes technical characteristics and marketing functions. Although their findings are interesting, the model did not take the consumer's decision process into consideration. Recently, Huizingh distinguished content from the structure of a website, where content included information, features, or services offered in the website and structure referred to the way web pages were organized. Wan organized features of website design into a process-value framework. The process included promotion, pricing, transaction, and services. The values were information, friendliness, responsiveness, and reliability. Liu and Arnett reported that quality of information and service, system use, playfulness, and system design quality were critical to the success of a website. Four factors were identified as aspects of system design quality: well-organized hyperlinks, customized search functions, high speed of web access, and ease of correcting server's error.

Although existing literature provides insight into design factors, we do not have a complete customeroriented feature list. Because website designers should focus on the way that customers use a website, we adopt the EKB model as a base to derive user requirements for e-stores. The model divides consumer decision processes into five stages: problem recognition, search for information, evaluation of alternatives, choice, and outcome evaluation. When a problem is recognized, demand for products that can eliminate the problem occurs. Product information is collected and alternatives are evaluated. Once an alternative is chosen, the consumer executes the transaction, evaluates the outcome, and saves the experience for the future. Following the model, a sixth stage of on-line shopping can be identified: the on-line transaction (including the filling-out of ordering information, payment, etc.) that occurs after the consumer has chosen a product.

## 2.1. Problem recognition

Problem recognition occurs when a customer feels a need for a product. Factors that help include environmental stimulation, individual experience, and inner motivation of the consumer. In general, it is hard to support individual experience and inner motivation through on-line store design. On-line buyers may also have needs recognized off-line, but developing a stimulating environment may be helpful in some cases, particularly in strengthening existing needs or deriving new ones. Possible features and guidelines for supporting problem recognition include the following:

## 1. Easy to get started

1.1. Consistent interface styles: Consistent interface styles can reduce the cognitive load.

1.2. Hierarchical product organization: Since it is impossible to view or touch actual products in electronic stores, organizing products in a creative way may help.

## 2. Shopping navigation

Most electronic stores have a large selection of items, it is necessary to have a good shopping guide and search engine. The following are a few guidelines for good navigation:

2.1. Proper use of special effects: Special effects on a web page attract immediate attention. For instance, dynamic banners catch customer attention for items that are on sale. Presentation in multimedia, flash, or other rich forms may help the consumer gain more insight.

2.2. Carefully-designed hyperlinks: In the traditional store, shelf management is important. For the same reason, hyperlinks that guide customers through shopping routes must be carefully designed. Since customers are easy to get lost in hyperlinks, it is important to let them know where they are, where they were, and where they can go next.

## 3. Stimulating the desire to purchase

3.1. Using POP: Traditional stores often use point of purchase (POP) advertising to focus customer attention on certain items or events by adding New! or On Sale! It is possible to use sound and animation for certain products.

3.2. On-line broadcasting: Background music or other sound may enliven the electronic environment and help stimulate a desire to purchase.

3.3. Adding auction mechanism: An auction corner that allows people to bid on certain merchandise may attract consumers.

## 4. Providing reference groups

Consumers sometimes rely on friends and neighbors for opinions. For example, people go to popular stores that are more crowded. They may have fun with other shoppers. Two approaches may be used:

4.1. Showing the number of current visitors: This is an index of current shopping atmosphere. Showing this gives customers a sense of store popularity.

4.2. Providing chat rooms: These are important tools that allow consumers to interact with one another to exchange their ideas and experiences.

## 2.2. Search for information

Before making a purchase, consumers often look for relevant information. Therefore, functions that support information search may be useful.

## 1. Allowing for fast product search

Good organization of products and a built-in search engine can aid customers find what they need.

## 2. Providing value-added information

Electronic stores can easily offer value-added information, such as best sellers, headline news, fashion trends, or other activity announcements.

## 3. Providing customized Information

An electronic store can provide customized information. For instance, the system may analyze sales data and consumer preference by data mining or other techniques to create personalized information.

## 2.3. Evaluation of alternatives

A store may provide functions that support customer evaluation of products and help to build trust.

## 1. Support of product evaluation

Customers often compare candidates using attributes, inquiries to staff, and bargaining for a better price [1]. It helps if a store provides the following:

1.1. Comparison of prices and other attributes: Price is a key attribute that most consumers consider in their purchase decision. A mechanism for comparing prices and other features may help.

1.2. Customer interaction: Consumers often seek opinions from their friends or other consumers before making a final decision. Functions allowing customers to access other customers' comments or opinions directly may help.

## 2. Building trust in the electronic environment

For customers who are not familiar with electronic purchasing, it is important to build their confidence. Possible mechanisms include the following:

2.1. Claiming security of transactions: Since the seller and the buyer do not actually see each other in the virtual market, Internet security is a major concern. Therefore, adopting proper security mechanisms, such as secured electronic transactions, and making announcements may help.

2.2. Offering a VIP system: VIPs are a special group of customers. Offering VIP memberships is useful in retaining regular and well paying customers.

2.3. Providing adequate service phone numbers: To reduce the feeling of uncertainties while purchasing, it is helpful to make service phone numbers and other contact information available.

## 2.4. Choice

At the choice stage, consumers need tools to aggregate information and make a decision. Functions must be provided to make their choice easier and suggest possible purchases.

1. Providing shopping carts for easy handling of multiple items

In a traditional store, consumers use shopping carts to carry their merchandise. In the electronic environment, these are also useful.

2. Suggesting substitute products

The system may help customers choose alternative products by offering choice. When a product is rejected because of budget, providing substitute cheaper products may be effective.

## 3. Using online salespersons

Sometimes, having on-line salespersons or intelligent agents to deal with special problems may be necessary. Their main responsibility is to interact, note reactions, and promote products.

## 2.5. Transaction

Once a product is chosen, the customer has to fill out certain ordering, payment, and delivery data. Major steps and their possible support include the following:

1. Easy to sign up as a member: Many electronic stores require membership information in order to complete the transaction. The process for registration should be simple.

2. Placing orders on-line: The customer should be able to place their orders once an item is selected. This reduces the risks that customers may change their mind.

Following the completion of a transaction, the following are essential functions:

## 3. An empirical study

In order to examine the effect of store design on customer purchase decision and the underlying mechanism of effective design, an empirical study was conducted. It was composed of two major parts: the first is to assess the quality of store design in terms of their functions; the second is to analyze consumer choice of electronic stores. Design quality was assessed by judgments of the consumer and expert. Consumer choice was measured in three ways: consumer's purchase decision, their willingness to revisit in the future, and their willingness to purchase again later. The first measure shows where the subjects actually purchased their items; the latter two indicated their willingness to visit or purchase in the future. Therefore, three alternative hypotheses were built. Fig. 1 shows the framework of the empirical study. The hypotheses are as follows.

Hypothesis 1. Better-designed electronic stores will attract more consumers who will purchase.

Hypothesis 2. Better-designed electronic stores will attract more consumers who intend to revisit.

Hypothesis 3. Better-designed electronic stores will attract more consumers to purchase again in the future.

Functions related to the measurement of design quality are classified into motivators, hygiene factors, and media richness factors. A motivator provides support to the customer to simplify the transactional process. A hygiene factor alleviates possible concerns associated with electronic transactions. Media richness is a medium's capacity for immediate feedback [4,5]. The design quality of a store is measured by the extent to which these three types of factors are available. Table 2 shows the classification of the functions under study.

## 3.1. Subjects and experimental design

The business chosen for the study was bookstores, because of their popularity among the products available on-line. Three on-line bookstores in Taiwan were selected. They were Kingston (www.kingstone.com.tw), Books (www.books.com.tw), and Sanmin (sanmin.com.tw). Kingston and Sanmin also have well-known traditional stores, while Books is a purely electronic store. Three experts were initially asked to assess the quality of the on-line stores. They examined the criteria for quality assessment to determine their relative importance, developed a set of measurements, and evaluated the three websites accordingly. Thirty student volunteers (21 males and 9 females) were then recruited and each received an NT\$ 500 (equivalent to US\$ 16) stipend for participating in the experiment. All subjects had experienced in using the WWW and owned at least one email address and a credit card. Twenty-five of them had visited at least one electronic store before participating in the experiment and eight had purchasing experience from electronic stores.

Table 3 shows the profile of the participants. Since purchasing books is a common real-world behavior for students, using student subjects in this experiment is less likely to incur any limitations associated with using students as subjects for studying business issues.

During the experiment, participants were first asked to visit all three electronic bookstores and surf around for at least 5 min in each to find the price of a designated book. This was to avoid possible bias due to unfamiliarity with their functions. After visiting all stores, they were asked to buy two items of their choice from the stores. So, the subjects purchased a total of 60 items. A questionnaire was used to collect three kinds of information from the subjects: their background, design factors considered important by them, and factors that actually affected their purchase decisions.

## 3.2.2. Design quality of the stores

Quality assessment from consumers and experts were collected. The subjects were asked to assess the quality of the three stores on a five-point Likert scale, with 1 to be the worst and 5 to be the best. The result indicates that Kingston was the best, Books was second, and Sanmin was the worst. The ANOVA result shows that their designs were significantly different (F = 12.8; P = 0.00). The LSD multiple comparison indicates that the difference between Kingston and Books was insignificant, but Sanmin was significantly worse than the others (see Table 8).

The expert assessment involved three experts who were invited to perform an itemized evaluation. Seventeen functions considered very desirable or desirable (i.e. their average level was higher than 4.0) by the experimental subjects were used by the experts in examining the quality of each store. They assessed the relative importance of the functions and the quality score of all functions for three stores on a five-point Likert scale. The final score of a store is the weighted sum of scores on all items. The maximum score that a store can get from an expert is 425 $(5 \times 5 \times 17)$ . The result, as shown in Table 9, is consistent to the consumer's assessment. That is, Kingston and Books were significantly better than Sanmin.

If we choose only the top five functions to recalculate the quality from expert assessments, then the difference between Kingston and Books becomes significant (see Table 10). Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that, based on the quality of the store, the consumer's actual purchase, willingness to revisit and willingness to purchase in the future would be the highest for Kingston, second for Books, and the lowest for Sanmin. That means, difference in design quality is a valid factor in this experiment.

## 3.2.3. Effect of store quality on consumer choice

Consumer choice is measured by the actual purchase during the experiment, willingness to visit again, and willingness to purchase in the future. ## 4. Discussions and conclusion

In summary, the paper examined the effect of design quality on consumer choice of electronic bookstores. We adopted a consumer's perspective in web store design and used the EKB model to identify potential design features critical to consumer purchases. These features were then classified as motivators, hygiene factors, and media richness factors based on their support of the on-line shopping activities. Empirical findings indicate that design quality is as important as product price, and more important than store reputation for on-line book shopping. Consumers are more likely to visit and purchase at well-designed stores that provide highly desirable functions. Consumer's willingness to visit again is higher than their willingness to purchase again. This is not unexpected, because there is often a gap between a visit and actually making a purchase.

Among the three tested bookstores, Sanmin and Kingston both have conventional stores and very good reputation, but they have very different outcomes. The former sold the least, while Kingston sold the most in our experiment. The result is also consistent to the actual performance of these bookstores. In the real world, Kingston and Books hold their on-line business actively, but Sanmin's on-line business becomes minor and less successful. Books, due to its success, was acquired by 7–11 chain stores in Taiwan at the end of year 2000.
