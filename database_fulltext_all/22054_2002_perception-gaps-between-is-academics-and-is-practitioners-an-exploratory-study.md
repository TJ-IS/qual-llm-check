# Perception gaps between IS academics and IS practitioners: an exploratory study

## Abstract

It has long been recognized that there are significant perception gaps between information systems (IS) academics and IS practitioners with regards to the required IS knowledge/skills to perform their professional jobs successfully. Unfortunately, there is no consensus about which knowledge/skills are more important in the IS profession. With the rapidly changing IS technology and IS industry, a study was urgently needed to find and understand the gaps between these two groups. We report here on a study to measure the gap

## 1. Introduction

Information systems (IS) professionals are experiencing a rapid change both in the technology they use and the business environment in which they work. The change in IS technology is so fast and dynamic that even the IS industry has and continues to have a hard time catching up. Under this dynamic environment, IS academics have had a hard time to cope with the change and to satisfy the demands of the IS industry. It is not uncommon to find that the computing methods learned at the university have become obsolete by the time that the student is ready to join the workforce. Lack of resources and an incorrect perception held by academics about the required knowledge/skills for entry to an IS profession are often cited as the leading causes of this educational problem.

The graduate of an IS program should possess the required skills and training to perform well at an entrylevel position and the person should have a basis for continued career growth as a professional [2]. However, many researchers report persistent gaps (from a moderate to a very serious level) between knowledge/ skills that are taught in academia and those that are demanded by the IS industry [1,5,9–12]. One of the areas in which the largest gap is reported is the integration—of networks, to existing business applications, or of new technologies with existing applications. To this area, IS curricula usually do not provide sufficient preparation for their students. Another area in which a large gap is reported is the systems development process. It was also found that IS practitioners, such as IS managers, users’ managers, and IS consultants, rate the importance of various IS skills and knowledge differently. A contradiction, although it was a modest level, of vision in terms of what must be offered in IS degree programs was also reported.

Recent developments of IS industry jobs and career paths have made the understanding about the knowledge/skills requirement of an IS professional even more difficult [8]. The old promise of a single career path, programmer ! analyst ! project manager ! IS manager, is being replaced with a new reality in which there are a diversity of IS career paths. IS practitioners’ use of certain knowledge/skills can vary significantly depending on their career and work experience. Even within a specific career path, IS practitioners are required to have different knowledge and skills as their careers progress [4]. So, for certain knowledge/skills, especially at the later stage of an IS professionals career, it may be necessary to educate IS majors more in ways to learn knowledge/skills rather than to teach them the knowledge/skills themselves.

IS academics experience difficulty in finding research themes that are current to industry practice and fail to communicate the results of their research to practitioners over time [6]. Evidence of conflicting contemporaneous trends between academics’ research and practice exists. It is therefore very important to investigate whether academic’s perception of knowledge/skills requirements have gone astray.

The empirical investigation for this study was split into two phases. In the first, the broad spectrum of knowledge/skills required for an IS professional’s career was investigated between IS academics and practitioners. In the second, the specific software tools that an IS practitioner uses to do his/her everyday jobs were scrutinized and an analysis was made of the differences between these two groups. The authors believe that the findings in this study can provide both IS academics and practitioners the perception differences and provide useful information that can lead to a reduction in the gaps between these two arenas.

## 2. Classification of IS knowledge/skills

In order to develop the necessary question items for our survey instrument, a literature review was conducted both in academics’ and practitioners’ publications. The ACM curriculum planning recommendations on IS education were developed over decades based on a taxonomy of knowledge contents classified according to IS technology, IS process, and AACSB common body of knowledge (on Business Management). However, there is no generally accepted classification of IS knowledge/skills nor is there consensus on which knowledge/skills are the critical ones and some are more important than others in the IS profession.

In publishing ACM’s curriculum guidelines for a master’s level program in information systems development, Ashenhurst [1] identified 37 skills and abilities that students in a graduate MIS program should expect to acquire into six categories: people, models, systems, computers, organizations, and society.

Todd et al. [10] classified IS knowledge into seven categories: hardware, software, business, management, social, and problem solving, and development methodology. They reasoned that interpersonal/managerial skills are the most important to IS managers. They found that communication and business skills become increasingly important to programmers and system analysts though technical skills are still the most important for programmers.

Nelson [9] classified 30 items into six groups: organizational knowledge, organizational skills, organizational unit, general IS knowledge, technical skills, and IS product. Organizational knowledge and organizational unit groups denote the knowledge related to the specific organization for which an IS practitioner works. Organizational skills denote general skills required working in a team and they imply interpersonal communication, interpersonal behavior, group dynamics, and project management. He found that IS personnel were deficient in general IS knowledge followed by organizational knowledge, technical skills, organizational skills, IS product, and organizational units (in that order), while end-users were generally feeling deficient in IS knowledge.

Lee et al. [5] classified three abilities: ability to learn new technologies, ability to focus on technology as a means (not an end), and ability to understand technological trends into technology management knowledge and other technology-related knowledge into technical specialized knowledge. They classified non-technology-related knowledge into business functional knowledge and interpersonal and management skills, letting interpersonal and management skills contain some personal traits; for example, an ability to deal with ambiguity and to be sensitive to self-directed and proactive. They also included the ability to teach others interpersonal and management skills. They found that non-technological knowledge is more important than technical skills. They also found that university curricula lag in updating critical new technologies such as networks and telecommunications and that IS graduate require more breadth and depth of education across the dimensions of technology, business, and human relations.

In this paper, critical IS knowledge/skills are classified into two parts. In the second part, the software tools that IS practitioners need to perform their everyday jobs are scrutinized in detail. ## 4. Methodology

For this study, the perceptions of both academics and IS practitioners were simultaneously surveyed. The following sets of questions were included in the questionnaire. First, what general IS knowledge/skills are required for IS professionals in conducting their daily jobs (knowledge/skills data set, Tables 5–7). Then, the perceptions of those two groups about the required level of a particular software-based technical specialty (the software/tools data set) were surveyed.

## 4.1. Data collection

In order to minimize any time bias, the data was collected from both groups at much the same time. Following two pilot tests of the questionnaire, a survey instrument was mailed to both groups. A follow-up mailing was made to non-respondents. Recipients were assured that all data were confidential and that only aggregate results would be reported. Questionnaires were sent to 470 IS practitioners; a total of 92 questionnaires were returned after the second mailing; this results in a response rate of 19.6%. Excluding seven invalid questionnaires, 85 practitioner responses were used in the analysis. For collecting data from IS academics, questionnaires were sent to the chairs of IS departments in 429 schools; 64 questionnaires were returned after the second mailing. This is a response rate of 14.7%. One questionnaire was not usable due to incomplete information. Thus, 63 academic questionnaires were used in the study.

## 4.2. Data analysis

We used two types of data analysis; rankings based on mean values of variables and multivariate analysis of variance using SAS Release 6.12, to investigate the statistical relationships between IS academics’ and IS practitioners’ data. Overall, IS academics rate the variables of the IS technology area as important as or more important than IS practitioners rate them. Furthermore, a statistically significant difference is detected. It is interesting to notice that less experienced IS practitioners (with less than 3 years of work experience) rate three areas (visions about IT competitive advantages, IS technological trends, and hard ware) as less important than the group of practitioners and academics. These mean values increase sharply for IS practitioners with greater experience.

IS academics’ perception of the organizational and society area is quite different than IS practitioners; IS academics rate most of the organization and society variables such as knowledge of specific business functional areas, knowledge of specific industries, and knowledge of specific organizations, less important than IS practitioners. This trend is statistically significant especially in two variables, specific industries and specific organizations.

They are rated average by both IS academics and practitioners. Object-oriented languages, CASE/structured programming, transaction processing systems, and decision support systems belong to this category as well.

Assembly languages, simulation/optimization tools, multimedia tools, expert systems/shells, teleconferencing tools, and data warehouse/mart tools are ranked as the least important tools by both IS academics and practitioners. We are not able to find any significant perception gaps between the two groups in terms of the least required tools. Statistics tools show some difference; ranked very low (24th/29th) by IS practitioners, but ranked 16th by academics. It is very interesting to observe that ERP and data warehouse/mart tools that have recently received great attention in the IS industry are ranked very low by both IS practitioners (21st and 23rd) and academics (24th and 22nd). We presume that these tools are not available to the average IS practitioners yet, since these tools were recently introduced, and accordingly, there may be only a small number of practitioners using them. However, we are not really certain why IS academics also rank them very low.

Result IV. A perception gap in the technical specialties (software-based) based on multivariate analysis of variance.

As shown in Table 10, IS academics rate the software tools required by IS practitioners to perform their daily activities successfully as higher than IS practitioners rate them in all variables except project management. Considering only statistically significant variables IS academics feel these technical software skills are far more important than IS practitioners perceive them.

The largest perception gaps (mean difference > 0:9) are shown in variables such as PC-based database tools, statistics tools, high-level programming languages, fourth generation language, object-oriented language, CASE, Web page production, and telecommunication tools. This implies that IS academics emphasize these tools far higher than IS practitioners. The perception gap becomes minimal in PC-based database tools, object-oriented languages, and CASE, if the perception of IS academics is compared to that of less experienced IS practitioners. The beginning-level IS practitioners feel these technical skills are more important than the experienced practitioners do.

Follow-up conversations with several survey participants were made after completing the study; these were intended to investigate the causes of the perception gaps. There seem to be several potential reasons why significant gaps exist. The lack of resource for computing updates, an unacceptable speed of a curriculum change relative to a speed of a technology change, and/or a lack of faculty knowledge/trainings about a new technology are found to be the major reasons.

## 5. Conclusion

The authors found that there are significant perception gaps between IS academics and practitioners and these perception gaps can be explained, to some degree, by the fact that the IS education system is known to be somewhat ineffective.

It is also interesting to note that IS academics rate most of the variables, in organization and society area, interpersonal communication area, and personal traits area, less important than IS practitioners do, while IS academics deem the variables in the IS technology area as important as or more important than IS practitioners do.

IS academics rate almost all software tools more important than practitioners. And this trend is consistent in the two areas: IS technology of core knowledge/skills and software-based technical specialties.

The authors believe that the findings portray a very important lesson and also convey a very significant indication for IS academics.

Their understanding about the required knowledge/skills/software for the IS profession is not in line with the industry demand.

The findings also suggest that IS academics need a directional change to place more emphasis on nontechnical areas such as interpersonal skills, personal traits, and industry understanding. Without the reduction of perception gaps between IS academics and practitioners, we may not be able to continue to provide an effective IS academic program that can satisfy the demand of the IS industry.
