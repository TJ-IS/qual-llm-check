# Managing risks in IT projects: an options perspective

Ram L. Kumar<sup>\*</sup>

Business Information Systems and Operations Management, The Belk College of Business Administration, The University of North Carolina at Charlotte, 9201 University City Boulevard, Charlotte, NC 28223, USA

Received 30 August 2000; received in revised form 17 September 2001; accepted 5 October 2001

## Abstract

Organizations are making large investments in information technology (IT) projects. However, many are risky and are often considered runaway, because they do not meet original expectations of cost, time, or benefits. Effective management of risks in IT projects is therefore extremely important. This paper emphasizes the need to differentiate between risks that can be resolved by action and risks that require hedging. It presents a framework for understanding and hedging risks in IT projects based on the finance literature on real options. This framework can be used to understand and justify project management decisions. The insight provided by this framework is consistent with practical managerial decisions in IT project management. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information technology; Project management; Risk management; Real options

## 1. Introduction

Organizations are making large investments in information technology (IT) and there is growing realization that IT investments can be a significant source of competitive advantage [11]. Accounting legislation in the US has recognized the importance of IT investments and requires that after 15 December 1998, costs associated with a software system developed for internal use must be added together and accounted as an asset on balance sheets [12]. This legislation has both advantages and disadvantages from the viewpoint of IT managers. On the one hand, it might increase the awareness of the importance of IT projects. On the other hand, competition for capital expenditure dollars is often more intense than for annual budget dollars; companies examine their priorities carefully. Effective management of IT projects is therefore extremely important.

IT projects are often difficult to estimate and manage and some projects are canceled or reduced in scope because of overruns in cost and/or time or failure to produce anticipated benefits [25,32]. Hence, effective management of IT project risks is a challenging task in many organizations. Also, it is becoming increasingly common for large IT projects to be managed by multifunctional teams. Hence, it is also important for the project management team members to communicate well with each other and with project sponsors using business-oriented concepts (as opposed to IT-oriented ones) and to present risk management in IT projects using business terms that senior finance executives can understand.

The purpose of this article is to propose a framework for managing risks in IT projects. This framework is based on research in finance on real options theory [1,42,26,43]. The proposed framework represents a systematic approach to risk management in IT projects. There is empirical evidence to support the fact that managers who are aware of some options-like ideas do a better job of managing risky research and development (R&D) projects [24]. Also, senior finance executives are becoming increasingly aware of the need to view major risky capital investments as options [1,8,33]. Researchers in computer science [40] suggest that the real options framework can be useful for understanding key software design decisions. The literature on strategic management [29] and justification of IT investments [10,22,30] has also recognized the value of conceptualizing decisions in terms of options. Earlier research [16,20] has shown that traditional financial evaluation techniques such as net present value (NPV) tend to undervalue IT investments and has proposed the use of real options techniques for evaluating IT investments. In the MIS literature, Benaroch and Kauffman [2] illustrate the use of real options techniques in the context of a decision to expand a banking ATM network. Taudes [41] explains that software procurement decisions can be considered real options. To the best of our knowledge, risk management in IT projects has not been systematically studied in the context of real options. In particular, this paper suggests that many IT project management decisions can be conceptualized as rea options and illustrates how some common options can be valued.

## 2. Managing risks in IT projects

Risk has been studied from many perspectives. These include a detailed theoretical analysis of the anatomy of risk [38], and risk and uncertainty in the context of the value of information [13]. Research has examined uncertainty in the context of managing IT projects [4,15,37]; these risks include specification uncertainty (due to uncertain business conditions or lack of knowledge), incorrect understanding of specifications, overlooked specifications, unrealistic schedules and budgets, shortfalls in externally furnished components or services, real-time performance shortfalls, and technical uncertainty due to innovative nature of the project. Researchers have also recognized that the problem of risk management in IT projects is accentuated by managerial tendency to continue funding failing projects [18].

Risk management strategies are oriented towards identifying different types of risks, assessing their relative importance for the project, and implementing strategies for managing risk. Many practical techniques are oriented towards making checklists of major risks, assigning probabilities for them, quantifying them, and taking actions to manage them. The information economics literature has examined the concept of ‘‘value of information’’ in the context of decisionmaking, and on the relationship between information and the decision maker’s utility function. Decision theory is often discussed as a method of quantifying risks; it recognizes that individuals may differ in their attitudes towards risk and uses utility functions to capture individual differences in risk taking. Researchers have recognized that real options theory can be viewed as a version of decision theory that uses market information in valuing decisions [39]. Decision trees could be extremely complicated for many decision scenarios and options may provide a more intuitive framework. Researchers and practitioners have recognized that the benefit of using options theory is that it is intuitive, and managers are able to relate to visualizing decision scenarios as options [34].

Risk management actions can be viewed as being of two types. The first is oriented towards reducing the degree of risk; for example, a major source of uncertainty in IT projects is uncertainty regarding the scope or specifications of the project. This can be partially resolved by interviewing multiple stakeholders.

However, since risk cannot be completely eliminated, a second type of strategy, oriented towards hedging risks is important. Risk hedging strategies are insurance-like ones oriented towards minimizing the negative impact of risk, when the associated uncertainty is resolved over time. For example, specification uncertainty in IT projects may be due to uncertain business conditions that may be resolved as the project progresses. In this case, strategies to hedge risks are important. These strategies are well-accepted in finance, and treated systematically in the finance literature [5]. This paper focuses primarily on strategies for hedging risk in IS projects. While, managers may intuitively use some strategies to hedge risks, a systematic analysis of approaches to hedging risks is lacking in IT projects.

## 3. Real options

An option can be described as a right (not an obligation) to do something (such as buy or sell an asset). Financial options are options on financial assets (e.g. an option to buy 100 shares of AOL at US\$ 120.00 per share on 1 January 2002). These are usually traded and one can buy the type of option in the example above for a price (say, US\$ 500). The option in this example is one of many types and is called a ‘European-type call option’ [14]. This will be exercised if the price of AOL shares at the time of exercise is greater than the agreed price of US\$ 120. However, it need not be exercised if the price of AOL shares is lower when the option expires. Recent research in finance and economics has recognized that the concept of options can also be applied to real (non-financial) assets, such as investments in technology.

Real investment opportunities can be viewed as an option to exchange one asset for another. For example, an IT project can be viewed as an option to exchange the cost of the project for the benefits of the system resulting from the project. Real options, such as IT projects, are typically not traded. Managerial (real) options typically result from other management decisions and are not traded-like financial options. For example, exercising an option to invest in a manufacturing plant might result in additional options to expand production or add new products [17]. Alternatively, investing in an R&D project might generate options for commercialization [35]. Similarly, investing in one phase of an IT project may result in an option to invest in the next phase (if it is not terminated after the first phase).

While there are several different types of options and each type of option may have a different valuation method, the value of most options depend on the following parameters:

1. the benefits resulting from exercising the option. In the context of software projects, this would be the benefit resulting from investing in a phase of the project. Higher benefits result in higher option values;

2. the cost of exercising the option. In the context of software projects, this would be the cost of taking up the next phase. Lower costs result in higher option values;

3. the degree of uncertainty associated with costs and benefits. While financial option valuation is typically based on market risk that is reflected in the value of traded securities, real options (such as IT projects) have other kinds of risk. If the uncertainty associated with either the costs or benefits is zero, then increased uncertainty in the benefits or costs (respectively) increases value; for example, if the cost of the software project is known exactly and the benefits are uncertain, then increased uncertainty of the benefits increases the option value. If there is uncertainty regarding costs as well as benefits, then the effect of uncertainty is situation-specific and depends on the correlation between cost and benefit uncertainty;

4. the time remaining until expiry of the option. In the context of software projects, this refers to the time available for making a decision to take up the next phase. In practice, this is likely to be influenced by organizational factors, such as deadlines for completion. The higher the time remaining until expiry of the option, the higher the option value;

5. the opportunity cost of delaying exercise of the option. Decisions to invest in phases of systems development could be delayed. However, these may result in loss of profits or competitive disadvantage. The higher the opportunity cost of delay, the lower the option value;

6. the risk-free rate of return. The lower the risk-free rate of return, the higher the option value.

Option valuation uses a risk-neutral approach. This does not mean that the valuation is correct only if everyone is risk-neutral. Option valuation is the same in the risk-neutral world and our everyday risk-averse world as long as it is possible to build a portfolio of assets that tracks the risk characteristics of the investment. In the event of not being able to build a tracking portfolio, option valuation may still be a good approximation that captures characteristics (flexibilities) of the investment decision that are not considered by NPV.

Financial options are typically traded on exchanges, and individuals as well as corporations can buy and sell them. Trading options is a well-accepted practice in corporate risk management. Options to invest in IT projects (or phases of IT projects) are acquired by prior managerial decisions, e.g. a study of competitors systems by managers may result in an option to invest in an IT project. Even though they are typically not traded, it is possible nowadays to bid on an IT project on the internet. This bidding is, however, different from trading in financial options such as those on stocks. Real options have been studied in a variety of academic settings including finance, economics [9,27], strategy, operations [17,21,23], and MIS. The finance and economics literature has recognized that the concept of real options is useful for conceptualizing and valuing investment decisions where management has the flexibility to alter the course of the project as the project progresses [19]. This literature also emphasizes the fact that the option to delay an investment project has intrinsic value and traditional financial calculations, such as NPV, underestimate the value of investment projects.

Valuation of real options is different from that of financial options since the underlying asset (such as an IT project) is not traded. Also, assumptions regarding the nature of uncertainty, and uncertainty in costs and benefits could be different. Valuation of real options in a variety of settings is an interesting and active area of research and may require techniques such as simulation or dynamic programming. This paper uses the model proposed by Margrabe [28] for valuing an option to exchange risky assets. This model is used because it is relatively general and simple. It has been used in the MIS literature to value investments in new IT. However, the type of problem for which this option valuation model is used here is different. Such models based on financial options are useful since they provide a lower bound on the option value and this is more accurate than using NPV. Alternative option valuation models can be substituted where appropriate.

Each arc (arrow) in Fig. 2(a) denotes a project activity (1, 2, 3, 4). Activities 1 and 3 precede activities 2 and 4, respectively. Fig. 2(b) denotes the time required for each activity. While the ideas described in this paper are applicable to other methodologies [6,7], we will use the spiral development methodology suggested by Boehm [3] for illustrative purposes. This is well-accepted and envisages the IT project as a set of staged investments. Risk analysis is conducted at the end of each stage (milestone) in the project and commitment is made to the next stage of investment. While several books and articles [36] provide some discussion and examples of risk mitigation strategies, they are primarily anecdotal. A systematic, theory-based approach to risk management projects is lacking. We suggest that hedging risks by altering a portfolio of options (creating or dropping options or altering the values of existing options) is a useful and rich framework for understanding risk hedging strategies for IS projects. Management decisions could increase option value by reducing the cost of exercising the option, increasing the benefits from an option, or by increasing the time to expiration of the option.

Fig. 3 illustrates the fact that the project management team has a set of different options. T ¼ 0 denotes the start of the project, T ¼ 1 some intermediate point in the project, and T ¼ 2 the end of the project. Reviews at T ¼ 1 and 2 can either be planned milestones or reviews caused by some change in the scope of the project. The major types of options in each of these portfolios are based on the real options literature.

Prior to the start of the project, the project management team could have the following major options:

1. to defer start of the project. This option is likely to be exercised if deferral would yield additional information that would resolve uncertainty (for example, a more complete specification). However, in many cases, deferral also has a cost in terms of revenue loss or preemptive action by competition, etc. It is important to recognize this option, since financial calculations, such as NPV, which do not consider this option underestimate the value of the project and result in incorrect decisions;

2. the option to invest in phase 1 of the project, which in turn results in an option to invest in phase 2 of the project, which in turn results in an option to invest in phase 3, etc. Each phase is an option because management has the right to invest in the next phase but need not do so. This an example of a compound option. The compound option viewpoint is similar to the idea of incremental commitment to different stages of a project—a well-accepted concept in IT project management.

At any intermediate review point (planned or caused by some environmental factor), the project team has the following options:

1. to defer the start of the next phase if new information is expected;

2. to abandon the project if the conditions are unfavorable for continuing;

3. to contract or expand the scale of the project.

At the end of the project, management may have the option to expand the project.

However, at each review point, the value of the options portfolio is likely to be different, because the options might be different and the parameters that affect option value (costs, benefits, etc.) might be different.

## 5. Hedging risk in IT projects

Managers can hedge risks by holding an appropriate portfolio of real options. Managing IT projects can be viewed as the process of updating this portfolio by adding new options, deleting existing ones, or altering the values of existing options by altering parameters that affect their value.

## 5.1. Option to defer commitment

This is useful in scenarios where waiting would reveal additional information. Based on real options theory, the longer the time available until expiry of the option, the more valuable it is. However, waiting may also have an opportunity cost due to lost revenue, etc. Situations with an opportunity cost of waiting can be modeled as options with possible leakage in value. Managers would therefore need to examine each phase of the project in terms of the benefits of deferral versus the opportunity cost of delay. Managers who expect uncertainty to be resolved by waiting can consider creating or modifying options to defer commitment by

1. being aware of activities or stages of the project where deferral is beneficial and scheduling them as late as possible. Examples include hardware procurement (delay may result in lower price), or some types of technical prototyping (delay may result in the availability of new technology). This amounts to increasing the time to expiry of options to purchase hardware or other technology;

2. gathering information in stages and delaying information gathering that may change. This amounts to increasing the time to expiry of the option to obtain information about the system;

3. deferring project start in anticipation of new technology that may make it easy to obtain the desired technical performance of the system. This is similar to (1);

4. investing in software tools (such as code generators) that speed up later stages of the project. This may allow managers to defer commitment to design decisions without affecting the project time. The cost of these tools would have to be weighed against the benefits of deferring specification or design decisions;

5. using methodologies or software architectures that permit delay of critical design decisions. This is similar to (4).

## Example 1. Acquiring a CASE tool

Consider a scenario where a software project is expected to take 3 years. One year is to be spent on requirements analysis and detailed feasibility study. Application design and application development is expected to take 2 years. Investing in the requirements analysis and feasibility study can be considered similar to buying an option to invest in the design and application development project. Let the expected cost of requirements determination and feasibility study be

US\$ 50,000.<sup>1</sup> Let the expected cost of application development be US\$ 200,000 (s<sub>i</sub>) and let expected revenues be US\$ 300,000 (s ). The NPV for this decision scenario would be US\$ 50,000 (US\$ 300,000  50,000  200,000). This scenario amounts to spending US\$ 50,000 (for requirements determination) in order to acquire an option to invest US\$ 200,000 (for application development) after 1 year in return for a project worth US\$ 300,000. Management has the option not to take up the application development project. The value of this option is US\$ 137,704 (using Eqs. (1)–(3)). The net value of this decision (option value-cost to acquire the option) is US\$ 87,704 (US\$ 137,704  50,000),<sup>2</sup> which is significantly higher than the NPV of US\$ 50,000.

Consider a scenario where investing US\$ 20,000 in an integrated CASE and application development tool would reduce the application development time to 1 year. Assume that, since technology is changing, it would be beneficial to delay the decision on application development. Buying the CASE tool would allow the application development decision to be made after 2 years instead of 1 year. This scenario is illustrated in Fig. 4. Also, assume that the CASE tool reduced application development effort to US\$ 190,000 (instead of US\$ 200,000). Hence, by investing in requirements determination and the case tool (US\$ 70,000), the organization now has an option to invest US\$ 190,000 in application development after 2 years in exchange for a project worth US\$ 300,000. The value of this option is US\$ 168,552. The net value of this decision (option value-cost to acquire the option) is US\$ 98,552 (US\$ 168,552  70,000). The NPV in the case would be US\$ 40,000 (US\$ 300,000 190,000  70,000).

Let us consider the decision to invest in the CASE tool. According to the NPV analysis, the CASE tool results in a US\$ 10,000 reduction in NPV and hence does not seem to be justified. However, the option values indicated that the net value of the decision increases from US\$ 87,704 to 98,552, thus justifying the decision to invest in the CASE tool. This difference is because the NPV analysis does not take into account the value of delaying the commitment to application development, while option valuation does.

## 5.2. Option to abandon the project

Managers and researchers have realized that it might be preferable to contract the scale of the project or terminate a project rather than continue. IT project management decisions can create or alter options to contract or abandon projects. Real options theory provides formal analysis to illustrate that projects with options to contract or abandon can be morevaluable than those which do not have these options [31], especially when project uncertainty is high. Examples include:

1. project reviews provide for an option to abandon the project if conditions are unfavorable. While such project reviews are often undertaken, there is a managerial tendency to continue investing in failing projects, because individuals associated with the project view this as a personal failure. It is important to create a culture that recognizes that some IT projects are high-risk and provide for options to abandon based on expectations of future performance rather than sunk costs;

2. provide for multifunctional review teams that make objective decisions about project abandonment. This is analogous to reducing the cost of the option to abandon;

3. contracts with consultants that provide for the option to terminate.

## Example 2. Abandonment option

Consider an IT project with two phases. Investing in the first phase results in an option to take up the second, which can be abandoned if conditions are unfavorable. In this case $s _ { \mathrm { i } }$ refers to the cost of abandoning the project and may include firing costs and premature lease termination for hardware or soft ware. The $s _ { \mathrm { r } }$ is the salvage value of the project. The abandonment option is an option to exchange a cash flow stream with an expected value $s _ { \mathrm { i } }$ (by incurring the costs of abandonment) for a cash flow stream with and expected value of $s _ { \mathrm { r } }$ If we assume that investing in phase 1 of the project results in an option to abandon the project after 1 year, and $s _ { \mathrm { i } } = \mathrm { U S } \$ 100 ,00 0$ and $s _ { \mathrm { r } } = \mathrm { U S } \$ 95, 000$ , then $\mathrm { N P V } = \mathrm { U S } \mathbb { \bar { \beta } } - 5 0 0 0$ but option value is US\$ 6405.<sup>3</sup>

## 5.3. Option to change the scale of the project

Many projects may have specification or technical uncertainty that can be resolved only as the project progresses. Investment in some part of the project is therefore required. In such cases, the project team typically invests in activities or products that increase the value of options to vary the scale of the project.

## Examples include

1. investing in a simulation or a prototype, which adds an option to change the scope of the project. This is similar to R&D projects and the primary problem is technical uncertainty. In such cases, adding an option to expand or contract can add significantly to the value of the investment;

2. using a methodology that permits a high degree of reuse might permit the system to expand easily. Software development can be modeled as a series of growth options.

## Example 3. Expansion or contraction

Investing in phase 1 of a project may provide an option to invest in phase 2 of the project—an option to expand the project. Investment in phase 2 will be undertaken only if conditions are favorable. In this case $s _ { \mathrm { i } }$ refers to the cost of the second phase of the project. The $s _ { \mathrm { r } }$ refers to benefits from the second phase of the project. An investment of US\$ 50,000 might result in an option to expand the scope of the project after 1 year by investing an additional sum of US\$ 200,000 in exchange for expected value of revenues of US\$ 300,000. The NPV of this project would be US\$ 50,000 (US\$ 300,000  200,000  50,000). Option value of this project can be calculated as before using Eqs. (1)–(3), and is US\$ 137,704.

## 6. Managerial implications

IT project managers typically manage risk by identifying risks and formulating risk management strategies. These risk management strategies may include strategies to reduce risk (reduction strategies) as well as strategies to manage the impact of risk (risk hedging strategies). Managers may often use the term ‘risk mitigation strategies’ to include risk reduction as well as risk hedging strategies.

In practice, managers often use checklists of risks, with associated probabilities to evaluate the risk level of a project. For example, consider a project with a significant risk in terms of changing specifications. It may be possible to assign a probability to a cost overrun as a result of specification uncertainty (say 0.3) and estimate the amount of cost overrun (say, US\$ 50,000). Thus, the risk exposure of this project is $0 . 3 \times 5 0 , 0 0 0 = \mathrm { U S } \$ 15, 0 0 0$ . It may be possible to consider multiple risks in a similar fashion. It may also be possible to reduce the probability of a cost overrun to 0.2 through the use of a risk reduction strategy (such as JAD sessions or spending more time on comprehensive requirements determination). Risk hedging strategies involve building managerial flexibility or options into the project, e.g. a software tool could result in ‘‘buying time’’ to delay implementation.

Table 3 illustrates the fact that managers can think in terms of risk reduction and options-based risk hedging strategies for different types of risks. Risk reduction strategies are traditionally analyzed using checklists and probabilities. Risk hedging strategies can be addressed through the options approach. Thus, the options approach complements the traditional approach.

Conceptualizing risk hedging in terms of options helps to understand and manage project management decisions. For example, consider a decision to invest in a relatively expensive application development tool that generates code from models. Managers may attempt to justify this investment in terms of labor reduction for programming. However, focusing on just this understates the value of the tool, since it does not capture the value of the option to delay as was shown in Example 1. This insight into the value of the tool might help to strengthen the case for investing in it. Using real options analysis might result in accepting decisions that might be rejected based on NPV or simplistic cost-benefit analysis.

Traditional techniques such as checklists of risks, with associated probabilities, used in IT project management do not emphasize risk hedging, which is well-accepted in financial risk and has strong theoretical underpinnings. This paper has proposed that the theory of real options is a useful framework for understanding risk hedging in IT projects. It has illustrated that the qualitative insight provided by real options theory is consistent with intuitive IT project management decisions encountered in practice.
