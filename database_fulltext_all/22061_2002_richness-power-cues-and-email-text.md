# Richness, power cues and email text

Niki Panteli

School of Management, University of Bath, Bath BA2 7AY, England, UK

Received 17 July 2000; received in revised form 28 February 2001; accepted 11 October 2001

## Abstract

The paper presents a study that assesses the level of richness in electronic-mediated communication by taking into account its text-based attributes. Though a key feature of email, only limited research has focused on the text-based attributes of email messages. The underlying assumption is that there is an organisational context that intertwines with email message texts. The study explores the extent to which these attributes differ among senders at different organisational layers. Using textual and deconstruction analysis, it analysed a series of email messages distributed within two academic departments over a period of several months, and found that email, as a communication medium, signals rather than alleviates hierarchical differences. Therefore, even though email is often presented as a lean medium, the way text-based messages are constructed may convey the social cues that are traditionally used to determine status differences in organisations. The study argues that email is a richer communication medium than is reflected in the scale of information richness theory. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Email; Communication; Information richness; Power; Deconstruction; Textual analysis

## 1. Introduction

Text-based and asynchronous activity has been the main feature upon which advocates of information richness theory have concentrated when considering electronic-mediated communication (email) as a lean medium. Unlike, however, other written communication media, email has distinctive text-based attributes that have not been considered in applying information richness theory. Email messages have the characteristics of written and spoken language, characteristics unique to email communication, or a combination of all of these, and the use of these different textual patterns may differ across an organisational hierarchy. Drawing on such issues, our study refutes the value of information richness theory by analysing and deconstructing the text of email messages sent across an organisational hierarchy.

Information richness theory proposes that individuals select communication media depending on their information requirements and, in particular, on the level of ambiguity and uncertainty that exists within a given situation [3]: ‘‘Communication transactions that can overcome different frames of reference or clarify ambiguous issues to change understanding in a timely manner are considered rich. Communications that require a long time to enable understanding or that cannot overcome different perspectives are lower in richness. In a sense, richness pertains to the learning capacity of communication’’ (p. 560). According to this, communication media vary in their level of information richness depending on their capacity for immediate feedback, the number of social cues and channels utilised, personalisation, and language variety. In order of decreasing richness, the media classifications have traditionally been (1) face-to-face, (2) telephone, (3) personal documents, such as letters and memos, (4) impersonal written documents, and (5) numeric documents. Thus, email as a written and asynchronous form of communication does not meet the requirements for rich communication; instead, it is identified as a lean medium in information richness theory. Trevino et al. [31] added to this, by arguing that email is driven mainly by situational determinants (i.e. distance, expediency, structure, role expectations, or time pressures), often by content reasons (i.e. simple and routine messages) and rarely by symbolic reasons, indicating in this way that email, unlike face-to-face communication, does not have the ability to signal meaning beyond the explicit message that it carries.

This rationalistic view for evaluating the level of richness in email communication medium has been criticised by several researchers. According to Schmitz and Fulk [19], the theory views richness as a relatively objective feature that is largely inherent to the medium. Their study found that perceived email richness varies across individuals and co-varies with relational social influences and with media experience factors. Moreover, despite the arguments in Trevino et al. [31], Lee [10] suggests that email messages are carriers of meaning; although email itself may not be a rich communication medium, it is the meaning given to email messages that contribute to its richness. He stresses that information richness is not just a function of the communication medium, but of the interaction between the communication medium and the organisational context in which it is used. Similarly, Markus [12] identified the role of organisational context on the choice of communication media. She found that email was used for communication tasks that involved high degrees of ambiguity and was used more intensively by senior managers. In fact, she also found that email was the preferred and promoted communication medium of senior managers across the organisational hierarchy. This is consistent with other findings on managerial email use [16,22].

The increasing use of email in organisations can be explained by noting that email shares characteristics that are distinctive from other communication media and that make it attractive. This medium aids in changing the way people seek out information from others and the way they interact with their colleagues and superiors [8,24]. Although asynchronous, email is fast and messages sent from all over the world reach their destination almost instantly. Email can also reach multi-recipients and, therefore, enhances opportunities for the simultaneous sharing of information [18]. The use of email in organisations reduces the need for face-to-face communication [27], whilst it increases the likelihood for vertical and horizontal transformation of organisational processes and structures [7]. Moreover, experimental research has shown that electronic-mediated environments provide more opportunities for equal participation in discussions of both introvert and extrovert individuals than traditional face-to-face environments [33].

Although it is a written form of communication, email also has distinctive text-based characteristics [23]. Unlike spoken discourse where what is said cannot be unsaid, written discourse enables writers to take more time to structure their messages and therefore their text could be or appear to be more planned and consequential. Similarly, in email, a text can be written, deleted, corrected, rewritten, and restructured several times before distribution without recipients knowing that it took place. However, email messages are not necessarily formal, structured or well presented as other written media, such as letters and reports. They may have oral and unique characteristics instead of or in addition to characteristics of written discourse. They could be spontaneously written, brief, with typographical and grammatical errors that resemble the conversational speech rather than a well thought textual language. There are also characteristics seemingly unique to email use [32]. Smiley faces, graphic icons built out of punctuation marks, e.g. ;-), :-), as well as typographical characters are some of the expressive innovations in email discourse. Such humoristic patterns have not previously existed in paper-based messages. Uses of asterisks or capital letters for emphasis are additional ways for the codification of language expressions [1].

It follows that email can integrate characteristics found in traditional communication media and enhance communication across an organisational hierarchy. Its richness may, as a result, require different interpretation and evaluation from that of traditional communication media; yet these unique characteristics have been overlooked in information richness theory. This paper refutes the conventional notion of richness by aiming to identify social cues that could be evident in email texts. The following section presents evidence of this by drawing upon the existing literature.

## 2. Email texts and social cues

This section uses the experience of Galle (in Lee [10]) to show that social cues could be uncovered in the text-based attributes of email messages. Lee used this example that took place in HCP company<sup>1</sup> to suggest that the meanings email messages convey are not limited to those of the dictionary definitions of their words. The specific email example involved a request by an employee (Galle) to be provided with further training. The communication that followed between the recipients of this email (EDHCP, CRANE, ALPHA, BETA, GAMMA) showed that ‘‘what was straightforward and innocuous for Galle turned out to be politically sensitive and managerially troublesome for others’’ (p. 152). It was argued that ‘‘(those) who receive emails are not passive recipients of data, but active producers of meaning’’ (p. 154). In his study, Lee paid particular emphasis on the recipients’ viewpoint. However, by looking closely at this example of email communication, another aspect of email use in organisations can be uncovered; it concerns the sender’s side. Traditionally, organisational members at the lower layers differ to those who have higher status and tend to follow their direction as ‘‘speech and demeanour become more formal in the presence of people who have high status’’ ([25], p. 88).

One of the earliest studies on message attributes [24] found that messages from subordinates were no different from those of superiors and this finding was used to strengthen the argument in favour of status equalisation in email use in organisations. Due to the absence of social context cues (i.e. job titles, social importance, hierarchical position, age, and appearance) in email communication [26], email is seen to erode the influence of authority in communication processes [4]. Several studies have reported that status inequalities are significantly reduced when using email (e.g. [5,28]). Pliskin and Romm [15], for example, found that email can create illusions of physical and emotional closeness and can free low-level employees from status related constraints. Therefore, these employees are equally likely, as are senior managers, to come forward with an idea and contribute to a discussion or to a decision making process electronically. However, as explained earlier, messages may have oral, written, and unique characteristics. It is not known how these specific attributes are used and combined in email messages sent by users at different hierarchical layers.

The example of email communication by Lee aids our understanding of such phenomena by making a preliminary investigation into the text-based attributes of email messages across an organisational hierarchy. In fact, Galle is new to HCP and new to the use of email; she sends a straightforward request to five different senior members of the organisation; her message (see Appendix A) shows evidence of a well-edited and well-thought out written text; the use of paragraphs, the sender’s full name and title in the organisation, and thanks preserve the formal style of writing of organisational writing, especially between different hierarchical layers. In contrast to Galle’s formal message, the email communication on the same subject between various senior members is short and to the point, spontaneous and conversational. The following message is provided as an example.

‘‘You need to ask Ray (the executive vice-president) what his feelings are on the need to train in (the branch). One would think with an mis dept there they could do their own training. Dave’’.

The lack of capitalisation at the beginning of the sentences as well as in titles (i.e. mis), the appearance of lowercase names (i.e. dave) and the typographical error (i.e. could to) show evidence of an informal and spontaneous composition of a written message. By looking closely at the patterns of email use between Galle’s message and those of the senior members of the organisation, features of both written and spoken language can be identified and show characteristics that are unique to email. In this example, characteristics of written discourse are seen in the message sent by an employee at a low hierarchical layer while characteristics of spoken language (unique to electronic discourse) can be identified in the messages sent by senior members. This leads to the view that email usage characteristics may differ between different social groups (e.g. groups with different hierarchical status) in an organisation. Such an argument contradicts the view of email as a status equaliser, as presented in relevant literature.

Sherblom’s study [20] found a significant difference in the tendency to add one’s signature to the end of email messages on the basis of hierarchical status and direction of message flow. He found that a redundant signature was added in 67% of upward messages and 87% of horizontal messages, but in none of downward-directed messages in his sample. There are two issues that arise from this. Firstly, status differences do not disappear simply because email as a medium lacks visible participants. Senders may deliberately, for personal, political, or other reasons, reveal their identity and status position. Secondly, Sherblom’s study encourages researchers to closely observe message transcripts as these could reveal important information about senders’ behaviour in electronicmediated environments and the extent to which traditional norms of interactions are sustained. Email is a medium that enables senders to edit their message before sending it. By doing so, they may choose to identify (or hide) their status and title. Furthermore, when using email, senders still have a sense of belonging to a social group [9] even when their hierarchical position and other information may not be available to others. This sense of belonging may consciously or unconsciously affect the construction of email messages. In the case of HCP, Galle did not have to meet EDHCP and CRANE to know that they had senior positions in her organisation.

Accordingly, at least some social cues may emerge in text-based email messages and that it is important to take this into account when assessing the level of richness of email communication. The underlying assumption is that there is an organisational context that intertwines with email message texts. Therefore, even though email is often presented as a lean medium, the way text-based messages are constructed may convey the social cues that are traditionally used to determine status differences in organisations. Our study explores the extent to which these attributes differ among different organisational actors within the same organisation in an attempt to re-assess email richness.

## 3. Research approach

The effort reported here approached email communication as signaling interpretation, framed within the language patterns and structures experienced at a given time and within a certain context. Textual analysis is used to examine the visible characteristics of text-based email messages, notably those of presentation, language patterns, signature, and forms of address, in an attempt to identify whether they differ among senders at different hierarchical layers. A text, however, may also communicate information that is not said in a written form. The textual analysis is thus complemented with the method of deconstruction, which is a suitable method for uncovering hidden messages within texts.

Deconstruction focuses closely on the wording of a text. As an advocate of the approach [6] put it: ‘‘what the text does not say on standard interpretation—is just as important as what it does say, and what is given only marginal importance or perceived as having peripheral impact is as important, perhaps more important than what had been deemed to be central’’ (p. 164). In information systems, this approach was used [2] to examine the level and kind of user involvement promoted in a well-known systems methodology (information engineering). It was found that users are given only a passive role in systems development even though the text of the methodology itself stresses the importance of user involvement. In this way, even though it is not its direct aim to identify authors’ intentions, with deconstruction, the devalued ‘other’ is made visible, contradictions in formal organisational statements and practices are identified, whilst the suppressed interests of members of disempowered and marginalised groups could also be revealed [13]. Uncovering implicit meanings and unacknowledged biases is recognised as being the major strength of this approach. Our study used the deconstruction approach to analyse and interpret an email message from a power relationship perspective, suggesting why it could be difficult to sustain email as a status equaliser in organisations.

## 4. Site selection

Since the aim of the study is to identify how different social groups within the same organisation use email, it was important to select a site where employees have equal access to this medium as unequal access can lead to misleading results. Another important factor is that not all organisations have an open access policy on email use. There are organisations where managers have chosen to limit access (e.g. incoming only) or to charge email costs directly to users. Academic communities have an open door policy (for this reason, they have been previously used for research in this area [17]), and are, therefore, suitable siteparticipants.

The research involved the study of two academic departments (Beta and Kappa) over a two-phased period. During the first phase, all the messages sent to a member of staff (Karen) in a social science department were analysed. In order to maintain anonymity, this was called the Department of ‘Beta’ Studies in ‘A’ Institute. At the time of the study, the department consisted of professorial staff (5), lecturing staff (16), fixed term contract research staff (3), secretarial (13) and other support staff, i.e. information systems support and a librarian (2). The participant, Karen, was asked at the start of the study to store all outgoing and incoming email messages during the period January–October 1996 in a separate folder; this was accessed by the researcher at the end of this period. The names that appear in the study are pseudonyms but none of the other senders’/recipients characteristics (e.g. status, title) have been changed. Karen joined the department as a full-time research assistant in January 1996 on a 2-year contract. She was previously a researcher at another university for 4 years. At a later stage of this study, and in order to increase validity of the results, another researcher of the same rank as Karen, Jane, at the department of Kappa (a science department) was asked to participate in the study. Jane joined Kappa a year before this study took place and prior to that was a researcher at another institution.

## 5. Results and analysis

At the end of the 10-month period, there were 213 email messages in Karen’s departmental folder. The list included those messages related to work (e.g. departmental activities, announcements on forthcoming conferences and meetings, responses to requests) and personal messages from colleagues and friends in the department. There were 71 messages that were addressed personally to Karen (one of these was copied to her) and 142 messages that were addressed to the internal distribution lists (‘allstaff’ or ‘academics’ for academic-members only).

5.1. ‘‘How it is said’’: oral, written and unique forms of communication

Oral and written characteristics were identified in the messages collected. Words used in oral discourse, such as ‘Ta’, and ‘stuff’, were present. Also, some messages resembled conversational features. For example, a message just said:

‘‘OK, Karen, I’ll come just before 12 noon’’, ‘‘OK’’, ‘‘Thanks’’.

Incomplete sentences and also abbreviated words that are not used in oral or written communication, such as FYI (for your information) and k’bd (keyboard), were characteristics that indicated informality: ‘‘Glad to hear that K’bd works’’.

There was no evidence of use of flaming or of discourse features unique to electronic communication, such as smiley faces. The lack of electronic patterns could be explained by the fact that email has not been well-established as a communication medium within this department, even though it had been available for several years before the study was conducted. Only 213 messages were distributed within a 10-months period, while a large number of internal memos had continued to be distributed in paper form. It is therefore possible that the limited use of email has not provided the opportunity for the evolution of new language and textual patterns among members of this department.

Further analysis showed that professors were more likely than other members to write a message without addressing the recipients. ‘Dear All’, ‘Hi Everyone’, ‘Dear Karen’, ‘Karen’ often appeared in the messages sent by the non-professorial members (see Table 1).

Email messages were also analysed in terms of signatures, i.e. whether or not the message was signed by the sender giving at least his/her title (e.g. Dr., Director, Secretary to . . . etc.), full name and department. Overall, 56 emails were signed; 67.3% of these were emails by professorial staff. The presence of signatures also varied by direction and senders. The vast majority of the signed messages to ‘allstaff’ were sent by professors. Thus, most of the signatures appeared in messages directed horizontally and downwards. The least signed messages were those directed upwards and horizontally (see Table 2).

Unlike email message 1, this message appears short and conversational, but also instructive. Firstly, the message gives information about the location of the application forms, assuming that people in the department are interested in attending. Secondly, at the bottom of the message the professor asks his secretary to copy that message to the secretary’s distribution list. By doing so, he also reinforces his seniority in the whole department.

To summarise, email messages sent within the department of Beta were said in oral and written discourse but the extent of their use differs among different user-groups. In particular, it is found that messages sent by non-professorial staff appear more personal and friendly than those sent by the professorial staff, for the former tend to address and/or greet the recipient(s), while avoiding signatures. Though personalised, friendly, and informative, email text, especially when written by staff at the lower ranks (e.g. secretaries and support staff), often appear structured and formal, as in the case of traditional written discourse. The messages by professorial staff also signal oral characteristics—their messages appear short, to the point, and spontaneous. At the same time, their messages show evidence of formality, because they often carry signatures. This plus the lack of greetings sustain social and status distances within this relatively small department.<sup>2</sup>

The characteristics found in the email messages sent by senior members of staff may be the result of several factors, such as lower levels of typing skills or that the higher level of responsibility implies a need to control time spent. Hence, their messages become directive. One may also argue that signatures appear automatically as email systems could be set to sign all emails sent with a formal title and return address information. The same, however, could be said for everyone else in this department. Why do not others use this facility as extensively? Only 14.9 and 16.5% of lecturing and administrative staff, respectively, and none of the research staff signed their messages. Also, since all messages were distributed internally, there was no need to give a return postal address. Clearly, when senders choose to add their signatures to internal messages, there is status difference within their workplace. It is also worth noting that if they wanted to show equality, users could have chosen to write more friendly messages. Either intentionally or unintentionally, therefore, messages carry the signals of power differentials in this workplace.

For the second research assistant, Jane—in department Kappa, hierarchical attributes are also found in email messages, through to a lesser extent and in a different form. This department has been described as having a high email culture. Unlike, Karen, Jane receives between 160 and 170 emails every month from colleagues in her department. A sample of 30 emails sent during the period September–October 1996 was randomly selected. These are generally short, informal, and conversational. There are, however, variations in the level of informality across the departmental hierarchy. Some messages that were sent by Jane to another research assistant (email message 3) and to a professor in her department (email message 4) are:

Beta and Kappa, though they are both departments within the same organisation, show different cultures and communication patterns. Their familiarity and use of computing technology might have a role to play on the extensive use of email in Kappa and the limited use in Beta. Despite their differences, status was conveyed and reinforced through email. Thus, messages could share both oral and written characteristics, but hierarchical differences may set the ‘location’ and type of these characteristics within an email text.

## 5.2. ‘‘What is not said’’: the deconstruction of email messages

The deconstruction approach is used to identify what is not said but is hidden in a text-based message. As an example, the sender, Karen, who had attended a conference applied for reimbursement for her expenses, knowing that the department allocates a reasonable amount of money per year to every member of staff for such expenses. A couple of months after, she contacted the chairman of the department, Richard Smith, who had to approve the reimbursement, to find out why there was a delay (see email message 7). The chairman replied with email message 8.

Email Message 7:

Date: May 17, 1996, 09:38:18

Subject: conference

Dear Richard,

Just a reminder about my request for reimbursement for my participation at the [] conference last february. Has my application been approved?

Looking forward to hearing from you.

Thanks.

Karen

Email Message 8:

Date: May 17, 1996, 15:44:51

Subject: RE: conference

I have discussed and agreed this matter with Philip. Please check with him.

Thanks,

Richard

Professor Richard Smith

Department of Beta Studies

‘A’ Institute

Karen received a reply from Richard several hours after, only to find out that this did not contain the answer to her question. Email message 8 appears short and to the point. In his reply, the chairman of the department simply asked the recipient to contact another person in the department to get the answer. This person, Philip, is the co-ordinator of the project on which Karen works. Even though the chairman knows the answer (‘‘I have . . . agreed this matter with Philip’’), he does not provide the response.

Karen had contacted Richard at the beginning of March to ask about reimbursement for her conference costs and a few days later she had been asked, by his secretary, for copies of the invoices/receipts. Although Karen was optimistic about partial or full reimbursement, she had not heard for more than 2 months whether her application had been approved. It was then that she decided to send another email (email message 7). Maybe Karen’s second email was a surprise to Richard who might have thought that (having discussed and agreed this matter with Philip) she should have been told about the decision. But neither Richard nor Philip contacted her and Karen felt that she needed to get a response. The chairman could have replied at an earlier stage to tell her that he was unable to make a decision and that she had to contact Philip for an answer. However, the text suggests that he had made the effort to see Philip to discuss this specific matter. The enquiry was discussed between the chairman and the project co-ordinator, even though the conference had nothing to do with her current research post. When the chairman replied, he seems to imply that research assistants appointed on particular project with a member of permanent staff should communicate concerns and enquiries with this person. Karen’s attempt and need to extend her communication with other members of the department was not acknowledged, but discouraged by Richard’s reply.

Email message 7 by Karen to the chairman acknowledges interconnections between the various hierarchical layers in an academic environment. This is also shown in email message 8, but the latter gives precedence and significance to the interconnections at senior level. This shows that norms of interactions across the departmental hierarchy are not erased but are reproduced in this email communication. Email messages 9 and 10 reinforce this argument.

Email message 9 was forwarded to Karen by Philip. The email originated with Professor John Hall. Philip had previously asked John to act as the mediator for making an appointment with Chris Martin (John’s friend), who was asked to participate in the project undertaken by Philip and Karen. Email message 9 is John’s reply to Philip after he had spoken to Chris.

Email Message 9:

This is the message I’ve got from Chris Martin. Up to you to decide in the light of his comments whether you want to send karen to see him. If you do, I suggest you or she phones him direct on . . . [number].

\- - -Forwarded message follows- - -

Professor John Hall

The phrase ‘‘send karen to see him’’ indicates a directive and authoritative delegation of tasks between the project managers and their research assistants. This email does not only reinforce but also enhances power differentials by dictating to other senior members of staff how to allocate project tasks.

Email Message 10:

Karen:

I decided to check on the progress of the order for the keyboard only to discover it has not been placed!

Jeremy

Email message 10 by another professor, Jeremy, does not itself contribute nor reinforce power differentials, as is the case with email messages 8 and 9. This text, however, tells a story that involves Karen, the writer (the teller of the story) and some administrative procedures. The story is about the replacement of Karen’s old keyboard. The central meaning of the story is certainly not difficult to identify. Though Karen had reported the fault and was told that an order for a new one was placed, Jeremy found out that actually no order had been placed. This incident in isolation may not be seen to be worth relating to its organisational context, but when put together with the other two emails, the incident confirms the degree of hierarchical relationships in the department. Moreover, a senior member acknowledges this hierarchydependent situation. Another issue is that email messages 8 and 9 seem typical of senior members in their attempt to delegate tasks, but email message 10 appears atypical—it was not common for senior staff to initiate and undertake trivial tasks for lower hierarchical members (such as ordering a keyboard). The phrase ‘‘I will place the order myself’’ acknowledges the power differentials between research assistants and other academic staff.<sup>3</sup> Jeremy uses his power to get the new keyboard, something that Karen on her own could not do despite its low cost.

## 6. Discussion

Earlier studies have shown that email is a richer medium than is reflected in the scale of information richness theory. Our study adds to this argument by taking primary account of the text-based nature of email messages, which has been overlooked in the literature.

The email examples suggest that text-based electronic communication are socially constructed by the context of the organisation in which email is used. The fact that email has not been established as a formal or as an informal means of communication gives flexibility to its users in choosing how to present their messages and what language to use. The degree of flexibility depends, however, on the norms of interactions within a given setting. People at higher ranks speak and write more freely than those at lower ranks. Our study found that signatures were more often added to the messages sent by senior staff for both downwards and horizontal messages indicating complexity and variations in norms of behaviour that exist in different organisations. Such norms have been, however, neglected in the predictions of information richness theory.

Using the deconstruction approach, the analysis focused on email exchanges that took place between senior members of an academic department and a recently appointed research assistant. These examples highlight the persistence of hierarchical differences, which, although hidden, could still be found betweenthe-lines of email messages. The short and unstructured message that Karen received from the chairman of the department reinforced and enhanced power inequality in the department. Despite arguments made in the literature that email enables more democratic participation and better communication across hierarchical levels, senders’ writing style may unconsciously and unintentionally influence users in maintaining a level of formality in electronicmediated communication. Furthermore, unfriendly and impersonal emails from co-communicators may make recipients to seek for alternative media. This is easier done in a low email culture than in workplaces where email is heavily used and encouraged.

Following from this study, it is reasonable to argue that elements of a hierarchical organisation show signs of persistence in email messages. Email, therefore, as a communication medium signals and supports rather than alleviates hierarchical differences. Thus, beneath the surface of email as a medium of communication that promotes the equalisation phenomenon, lies a continuation of communication barriers between the different organisational layers.

## 7. Conclusion and implications

By analysing email messages sent within two academic departments, this study has examined firstly how messages are constructed by senders across a hierarchy and secondly what is not said in email messages. The former is based on the argument that text-based attributes are not homogeneous; messages may have characteristics of written and spoken language or characteristics unique to email communication or a combination of all these and therefore the use of these different textual patterns may differ across the hierarchy. The latter is based on deconstruction to identify what is hidden in email text in an attempt to develop conceptual implications of the authors/senders intent. This highlights the need for rethinking the concept of richness in email communication.

Earlier research [21] found that the absence of social cues may reduce the rate of intra-group communication and consequently lead to a low amount of idea generation in groups using email. Our study has found that some social cues are present in email communication but these may have detrimental effects, since they tend to reinforce existing status and power dynamics. For the effective diffusion of email within an organisation, and thus for developing more opportunities for idea generation and improved communication across a hierarchy, managers need to be particularly careful so that their own email messages do not constrain the potentials of the medium. The communication medium itself cannot alleviate power differentials; however, it provides the opportunity for ‘relaxing’ traditional patterns of interactions by encouraging informal and most importantly informative communication across hierarchical layers.

The question remains whether generalisability of these findings can be extended to other types of organisations. Universities and academic departments exhibit a fair amount of pooled interdependence [30]. For example, that between an operations management lecturer and an industrial relations lecturer is small compared to the interdependence between a Production Manager and a Personnel Manager in a manufacturing setting. It is though worth noting that, academics can be more rank conscious than in industry. Nevertheless, the argument that is emphasised in this paper is not the difference between academia and industry nor is that different departments experience different degrees of email use, but rather the continuation of traditional power differentials in email communication. The findings of this study confirm the propositions drawn from a closed examination of the text-based email attributes. Future research on email richness should investigate other social cues and should also consider the emergence of multi-media communication that combines text, video and voice within an electronic-mediated communication system.
