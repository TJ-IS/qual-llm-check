# Having expectations of information systems benefits that match received benefits: does it really matter?

D. Sandy Staples<sup>a,\*</sup>, Ian Wong<sup>a,1</sup>, Peter B. Seddon<sup>b,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Queen’s School of Business, Queen’s University, Kingston, Ont., Canada K7L 3N6

<sup>b</sup>Department of Information Systems, The University of Melbourne, Parkville 3052, Vic., Australia

Received 15 February 2001; received in revised form 10 May 2001; accepted 8 November 200

## Abstract

A study was conducted to examine the effect of implementing a new system on its users, specifically, the relationship between pre-implementation expectations and their perceived benefits based on post-implementation experience. Disconfirmation theory was used as the theoretical basis; this predicts that unrealistically high expectations will result in lower levels of perceived benefit than those associated with realistic expectations (i.e. where expectations match experience). Support was found for this prediction, refuting the predictions of dissonance theory. In addition to examining expectations of system use generally, six expectation categories were examined to identify the critical categories where managers should keep expectations from becoming unrealistically high. Significant relationships were found for three expectation categories: system usefulness, ease of use, and information quality. The results indicate that creating and maintaining realistic expectations of future system benefits really does matter. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information systems success; End-user satisfaction; User expectations; Disconfirmation theory

## 1. Introduction

User expectations are potentially an important factor affecting the perceived benefits arising from use of new information systems (IS) [28]. Users continue to become more demanding in terms of what they want from their systems and managers struggle to determine how to establish realistic expectations. The technological advances in recent years and the over stimulation provided by the enthusiastic media have together created higher user expectations. Technologically competent users are now expecting appropriate state-of-the-art technology in their applications while lower competence users are demanding user-friendly applications with simple user interfaces [30]. To build support for new projects, managers may oversell the benefits of future systems, thereby either inadvertently or deliberately creating high expectations that may go unfulfilled.

Therefore, it is no surprise that managing user expectations has become difficult but more essential. Managers who develop effective strategies for this can potentially enhance the success of the implementation of new IS. And this is certainly not a new problem. In the late 1980s, user expectations came to the forefront of the significant problems facing IS managers [26,27]. Consistent with this, Conrath and Mignen’s [10] survey of EDP/MIS managers found that user expectations of EDP/MIS products and services were ranked second in a list of 33 items affecting user satisfaction.

What are effective strategies to manage user expectations? What is the impact of having high expectations? A recent meta-analysis of factors that influence end-user satisfaction found several studies that examined the association between expectations and user satisfaction. A moderate positive effect size was found. This suggests that creating high expectations can result in having higher user satisfaction. One very significant limitation of almost all of these studies, as pointed out by the authors, is that the studies were cross-sectional (i.e. measured at one point in time). Expectations were measured after the fact. After the system was introduced, users were asked to recall what expectations they had held before the system was introduced. This method can introduce recollection errors [25], and it does not assess the match between expectation and experience. Szajna and Scamell [42], the only longitudinal study in the metaanalysis, explained the positive association they found in their laboratory experiment using cognitive dissonance theory. Mahmood et al. [28] conclude their meta-analysis by calling for more focused research via longitudinal studies at pre-implementation and post-implementation time periods.

As far as we were able to determine, there are only two field-based studies that have examined the effect of expectations on perceived user benefits by measuring expectations before implementation of an IS and measuring views of actual use after implementation. Ginzberg did this in 1981 [14] and Marcolin did this in 1994 (an unpublished Ph.D. thesis [29]). In both of these studies, these researchers found that unrealistically high expectations led to lower levels of user satisfaction. Marcolin grounded her work in disconfirmation theory and the results supported it (Ginzberg did not theoretically ground his work).

Managing expectations is important, but what should managers do? One set of findings suggests that high expectations are good; another set of findings suggests that managers should avoid creating unrealistically high expectations. This study was intended to investigate the impact of the realism of expectations on perceptions of the benefits of system use and break down this impact in order to offer advice to managers and provide guidance for future research. First, the study examines the effects that unrealistically high expectations have on measures of the perceived net benefit of systems. Next, the paper looks at expectations at a more detailed level by examining individual expectation categories.

## 2. A review of related literature

Ginzberg looked at the effect of unrealistic expectations via an empirical study in the trust department of a large US bank. He classified the pre-implementation expectations of system developers as the standard level of expectations and examined the realism of user expectation by measuring deviations from this standard. Ginzberg’s results showed that users who held realistic pre-implementation expectations of a system had higher levels of satisfaction, rated the system as more successful, and were more likely to use the system than users who held unrealistic expectations. This study set the groundwork for the exploration of the effects of disconfirmed expectations on IS outcomes.

Since then, two studies have explored the mechanism behind expectations by adding a theoretical framework. These measured similar relationships and yielded surprisingly differing outcomes. First, Szajna and Scamell designed a laboratory experiment based on cognitive dissonance theory from social psychology literature. This is based on the idea that unmet (i.e. disconfirmed) expectations will create psychological discomfort. When an individual receives ideas that are dissonant, he or she will try to reduce the discomfort by distorting one or both of the ideas to make them more consonant [23]. Past studies have provided support for the theory in various fields of study [8,36,37]. Applied to the field of information systems, the theory predicts that individuals will change their level of satisfaction with a system to be more in line with prior expectations.

The results of Szajna and Scamell’s study were that users with unrealistically high expectations had higher levels of satisfaction than users with moderate ones, as would be predicted by cognitive dissonance theory. Also, users with unrealistically low expectations were found to have lower levels of satisfaction. These results support the idea that individuals adjust their perceptions of satisfaction to bring them more in line with their prior expectations and to reduce dissonance.

Therefore, contrary to Ginzberg’s findings, higher initial user expectations were found to be associated with relatively higher levels of satisfaction.

The second study was by Marcolin, who used disconfirmation theory taken from the consumer satisfaction literature to explain user satisfaction. The disconfirmation model from marketing literature [22], proposed by Churchill and Surprenant [9], used individual’s perceived expectations, perceived performance, and perceived disconfirmation to predict consumer satisfaction. Confirmation of expectations can be at three possible levels: (1) confirmed when the product performs as expected (expectations match perceived performance); (2) negatively disconfirmed when the product performs worse than expected, and (3) positively disconfirmed when the product performs better than expected [35]. Disconfirmation theory suggests that unrealistically high expectations (i.e. negative disconfirmation) lead to lower levels of satisfaction. Considerable support has been found in the marketing area for this theory. A recent metaanalysis of customer satisfaction [43] found a strong correlation between disconfirmation and satisfaction and concluded that disconfirmation plays a dominant role in satisfaction. The gap between expectations and perceived performance is also a key component of the SERVQUAL research stream [17], again indicating the importance and relevance of disconfirmation (the fit or misfit between expectations and perceptions of performance).

Disconfirmation theory originated from adaptation level theory, which argues that an individual perceives stimuli only in relation to an adapted standard used to guide subsequent evaluations [19]. When this standard is not met because expectations are too high, the individual’s satisfaction level decreases. Marcolin compared pre-use expectations of an IS with postuse perceptions of system performance. She found that when the system performed worse than expected, the user satisfaction was lower than if the system performed as expected. Therefore, like Ginzberg’s results and in accordance with disconfirmation theory, Marcolin found that unrealistically high user expectations were associated with lower levels of satisfaction.

Thus, there is stronger support for disconfirmation theory than for cognitive dissonance theory. Accordingly, we have chosen to use disconfirmation theory as the basis of our study. Supporting this, Yi [49] suggests that the cognitive dissonance effect is difficult to link to the impact of expectations. There may also be a methodological explanation for why Marcolin’s, and Szajna and Scamell’s studies differed in their findings. Marcolin used a field experiment of an IS whereas Szajna and Scamell performed their work in a controlled setting, manipulating user expectations in a laboratory experiment. Szajna and Scamell stated that their study was limited by its lack of an actual business situation.

Disconfirmation theory suggests that it would be in management’s best interests to keep expectations at a realistic level. However, this is easier said than done; little empirical research has directly focused on providing specific ways of managing user expectations. Past research has not examined whether particular expectation categories, when set at an unrealistically high level, have a greater detrimental effect than other categories on user satisfaction. Past research examined expectations generally. If we can determine which expectation categories have more of an effect on IS success than others, then management could focus their efforts on ensuring that users hold realistic expectations of the categories that most influence their level of satisfaction.

## 3. Hypothesis development

## 3.1. The dependent variable: perceived net benefit

Disconfirmation theory suggests that there is a negative relationship between disconfirmed expectations and user satisfaction. User satisfaction is widely used in IS research as an indicator of user perception of the effectiveness of an information system [5,13]. Instead of user satisfaction, we use perceived net benefit as the dependent variable. Seddon [39] suggests that this construct is more inclusive and comprehensive than user satisfaction, defining net benefit as an

idealized comprehensive measure of the sum of all past and expected future benefits, less all past and expected future costs, attributed to the use of an information technology application. Any use of resources (including time) in building, learning to use, and/or using the system is a cost. To measure net benefits, one has to adopt some stakeholder’s point of view about what is valuable and what is not.

Perceived net benefit (PNB) is a perceptual measure of net benefit. PNB and user satisfaction have been argued to be very similar in meaning; Naylor et al. [33] define user satisfaction as the result of an individual evaluating outcomes on a pleasant–unpleasant continuum. Since evaluating outcomes in this way to determine user satisfaction is similar to the matching of benefits and costs, PNB is very similar to user satisfaction. But PNB not only captures user satisfaction but also captures other dimensions, such as IS effectiveness. Although user satisfaction and IS effectiveness are closely related, it is desirable to use PNB as the dependent variable in our study since it adequately captures both user satisfaction and IS effectiveness.

## 3.2. Expectation categories and hypotheses

As Ginzberg and Marcolin did, we first examined the overall impact of having higher expectations than reality. The first hypothesis is that unrealistically high expectations results in relatively lower levels of perceived net benefit and is consistent with predictions of disconfirmation theory. Therefore, we have following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1. With respect to all performance expectation categories combined, users with higher expectations than actual results will report a lower level of perceived net benefit than users whose expectations are either met or exceeded.

Previous research did not investigate the specific effects of the negative disconfirmation of various types of expectation categories on PNB or user satisfaction. In this study, we attempted to do this using nine performance expectations categories.

## 3.2.1. System usefulness

System usefulness, as defined by Davis [11] is ‘‘the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance’’. In terms of expectations, this is the anticipation that using the system will improve job performance, productivity, effectiveness in the job, and quality of work. Benefits that a system produces are considered much more heavily than the costs of the system. The construct itself has often been used as an indicator of user satisfaction [40,41] and was suggested, in user interviews, as being an important criterion that users apply when evaluating IS. Thus, we have following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2. Users with higher expectations of system usefulness than actual results will report a lower level of perceived net benefit than users whose expectations of system usefulness are either met or exceeded.

## 3.2.2. Ease of use

Davis defined ease of use as ‘‘the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free from effort’’. In the context of users expectations, this is the expectation that the system will be user friendly. There has been much research on ease of use (e.g., [12,31]), and according to Seddon and Kiew, it is a factor that affects satisfaction. Marcolin, in her interviews of users, also showed that it was an appropriate expectation category to consider. Therefore, we have following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3. Users with higher expectations of ease of use than actual results will report a lower level of perceived net benefit than users whose expectations of ease of use are either met or exceeded.

## 3.2.3. Compatibility with work

Compatibility with work is the expectation that a new system will fit well with both the way users like to work and the various aspects of their work. Marcolin found that compatibility ranked in the upper half of a list of criteria that users applied to evaluate IS, supporting the potential importance of this variable as a possible influencer of PNB and user satisfaction. Task-technology fit theory [16] also suggests that compatibility of the system with the task is an important determinant of perceived effectiveness. The theory suggests that costs of using technology decrease if the capabilities match the tasks at hand [47]. Support is found consistently in the IS literature for the effect of task-technology fit [21]. Therefore, we have following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 4. Users with higher expectations of compatibility with work than actual results will report a lower level of perceived net benefit than users whose expectations of compatibility with work are either met or exceeded.

## 3.2.4. Information quality

Information quality describes the clarity, accuracy, timeliness, and content of the information system. Seddon suggests that it is an important determinant of IS success and perceived benefits. Therefore, we have following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 5. Users with higher expectations of information usefulness than actual results will report a lower level of perceived net benefit than users whose expectations of information usefulness are either met or exceeded.

## 3.2.5. Knowledge of the system

This construct represents the expectation that users will have a good understanding of the system and become confident users of it. One of the three factors in Baroudi and Orlikowski’s [6] instrument for measuring user information satisfaction (UIS) is user knowledge and involvement. More recently Thompson et al. [45], Venkatesh and Davis [46], and Igbaria et al. [20] have reported links between users Knowledge and IS Use. Therefore, a positive association is expected between perceptions of the knowledge of the system and perceived benefits. Thus, we have following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 6. Users with higher expectations of knowledge of the system than actual results will report a lower level of perceived net benefit than users whose expectations of knowledge of the system are either met or exceeded.

## 3.2.6. Ease of learning

A system that is easier to learn should cost less in terms of time take. The cognitive cost/benefit framework [15] suggests that an individual’s performance is affected by the balance of costs and benefits. Therefore, ease of learning should be associated with perceived net benefits.

Hypothesis 7. Users with higher expectations of ease of learning than actual results will report a lower level of perceived net benefit than users whose expectations of ease of learning are either met or exceeded.

## 3.2.7. Personal benefits

This expectation describes benefits to the user, such as becoming more employable as a result of using the system. The category also describes perceptions of whether the system will be fun and interesting. Individuals evaluate IS subjectively, in just the same way that they evaluate other items they use. Thus, we hypothesize following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 8. Users with higher expectations of personal benefits than actual results will report a lower level of perceived net benefit than users whose expectations of personal benefits are either met or exceeded.

## 3.2.8. Personal sense of accomplishment

This construct represents the expectation that the system will increase the user feelings of self-esteem. The hypothesis is as follows.

Hypothesis 9. Users with higher expectations of personal sense of accomplishments than actual results will report a lower level of perceived net benefit than users whose expectations of personal sense of accom plishments are either met or exceeded.

## 3.2.9. Attitude concerning system use

Attitudes have been linked to intention to use systems and usage in various studies of IS, usually based on the theory of reasoned action [1,2]. Therefore, attitudes toward use may be an important determinant of the perceived benefits.

Hypothesis 10. Users with higher expectations of attitudes concerning system use than actual results will report a lower level of perceived net benefit than users whose expectations of attitudes concerning system use are either met or exceeded.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. The sample

We studied the process in end users by comparing pre-implementation expectations with post-implementation perceptions for the same subject. In order to do this, we needed to collect opinions from individuals of single IS at two points in time. We asked the university librarian at a large university for permission to survey her staff. Since the library was about to upgrade its central cataloguing system, we were able to survey them both before and after the new system was installed. Therefore, 6 months before the library upgraded its old system, we collected Dataset 1 while Dataset 2 was collected approximately 1 year after the new library system had been installed.

To collect Dataset 1, questionnaires asking about expectations of the new system were sent to a randomly selected sample of 112 librarians. This was only onehalf of the subjects in the department, since the university librarian also wanted information on the views of the system then being used. At pre-implementation time, the size of the department was slightly smaller than at the post-implementation study time (224 versus 250). Each questionnaire was accompanied by a cover letter from the university librarian asking the recipient to complete the questionnaire. To increase the response rate, a US\$ 100 lottery prize was offered to all respondents and a reminder letter with a second copy of the questionnairewas sent to all then non-respondents about 1 week before the deadline. Seventy-six questionnaires (68%) were returned by the deadline. Similar steps were used to collect Dataset 2 18 months later, but this time the questionnaire was sent to all 250 librarians in the department. By the deadline, 140 usable responses (56% of 250) had been returned. To measure individual disconfirmation values we needed matched responses from librarians who had filled out both questionnaires. Forty-three respondents completed both questionnaires. We used their responses in the main analysis. Turnover of staff during the 18-month period and the voluntary nature of the study both contributed to reducing the number of matched responses.

We conducted two analyses to test for non-response bias. First, we split the data by work group and tested to see whether the percentages that responded in each group were significantly different to the known percentages in the population. There were no significant differences in either Dataset 1 or 2, so we believe that both datasets were representative of their respective populations. Second, consistent with the procedure suggested by Armstrong and Overton [4], we then tested for statistically significant differences in the responses of late versus early respondents across the measures of the constructs used to test the hypotheses. No significant differences were found between the two groups in either of the datasets.

## 4.2. Analysis

In order to create estimates of the match between expectations and actual use, we calculated the difference between the respondent post-implementation evaluations of the construct measures and their preimplementation expectation responses. Doing this gave us each respondent’s disconfirmation scores for each item for the various expectation categories. Users with disconfirmation scores below zero were classified as negatively disconfirmed (i.e. expectations were higher than actual experience). Users with disconfirmation scores zero or above were classified as nonnegatively disconfirmed (i.e. expectations were either met or exceeded). Using the disconfirmation scores, we compared the PNB scores of users whose expectations were either met or exceeded and the results of users whose expectations were greater than actual. Nonparametric techniques were used to test our hypotheses due to the relatively small sample size. Specifically, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to test whether the post-implementation perceptions of net benefit were statistically different between the two groups. Since all our hypotheses were directional, a one-tailed test was used to test for statistical significance.

## 4.3. Construct measurement

The constructs were measured with items taken from past literature. Each construct corresponds to either one of the nine expectation categories or the dependent variable. A description of the items used to measure the constructs and the sources of the constructs in both of the questionnaires is displayed in Appendix A.

Prior to combining the items, their psychometric properties were investigated. To ensure that adequate construct validity existed and that the data measured distinct constructs, we conducted factor analysis to establish discriminant validity. Fourty-three people filled in both questionnaires. While we could calculate reliabilities on this sample, we could not conduct factor analysis due to its size. Therefore, to be able to test discriminant validity, we used the responses, both pre- and post-implementation of all respondents (sample size of approximately 216). The assumption was that if adequate discriminant validity could be shown in this sample, it would establish confidence that the matched sample $( n = 4 3 )$ also had similar properties, since it was a subset.

## 5. Results

Hypothesis 1 deals with expectations in general. To examine this, we combined all the expectation constructs by finding the means of the construct values. This calculation was performed twice: once for the preimplementation expectations and once with the postimplementation perceptions. Subsequently, we took the difference between the two scores to come up with an overall disconfirmation score for each individual. We used this score to examine the relationship with perceived net benefit (PNB). This method of measuring overall expectations is consistent with Marcolin’s approach. We found that users who had unrealistically high expectations experienced a significantly lower level of PNB than users whose expectations were met or exceeded. The difference was statistically significant (Mann–Whitney $U = - 2 . 7 1$ $P \mathrm { - v a l u e } = 0 . 0 0 3 )$ , as shown in the first row of Table 3. To test the remaining hypotheses, we examined whether respondents who had higher expectations than actual results in the individual expectation categories had statistically significant different perceptions of the net benefit of the system versus those respondents whose expectations were either met or exceeded. The result of this analysis is shown in Table 3. Of the six expectation categories, three of the constructs were found to have significance differences between the two groups. These were: system usefulness, ease of use, and information quality. In all three cases, PNB was lower for the group that had expectations higher than actual, consistent with the predictions of disconfirmation theory. Therefore, the respective hypotheses were supported. We also carried out similar analysis using user satisfaction as the dependent variable (i.e. item PNBH listed in Appendix A) to facilitate comparison with past studies that only examined user satisfaction. The results were consistent.

## 6. Discussion

Hypothesis 1 was supported, substantiating the position that unrealistically high expectations lead to lower levels of perceived effectiveness and satisfaction. This is consistent with most past research. Our results suggest that disconfirmation theory is appropriate to use in an IS setting. System usefulness, ease of use and information quality have consistently been found to be strongly related to IS success and user satisfaction. Our results support this.

## 6.1. Post-hoc analysis

We explored differences in the effects of unrealistically high expectations and conducted some post-hoc analysis to explore the effects of unrealistically low expectations. For example, we concluded that expectations should not be unrealistically high and thus it may be better for managers to initially set user expectations at a very low level than at a realistic level. In order to explore whether this could be a good management practice, we removed all respondents whose expectations were higher than actual from the sample and performed a post-hoc analysis to look at the effects of unrealistically low expectations versus the effects of realistic expectations on PNB. A Mann– Whitney test was used to look at differences between the disconfirmation scores of the two independent samples. The results are shown in Table 4. No statistically significant differences were found between the two groups for any of the categories. However, when we examined the mean scores, we saw three different patterns. Three of the expectations categories seemed to have an upward sloping pattern (i.e. positive disconfirmation scores were higher than the confirmation scores), as illustrated in panel A of Fig. 1. One had scores that were more or less the same (i.e. like panel B of Fig. 1). Two had downward sloping scores, suggesting that the relationship may be more bellshaped, like that in panel C of Fig. 1.

## 6.2. Implications for managers

Apparently, users who hold unrealistically high preimplementation expectations about a system are less likely to be satisfied than those who are less enthusiastic. Therefore, managers should try to control pre-implementation user expectations. This is contrary to one strategy that managers may be tempted to use. In efforts to ‘‘sell’’ a new information system to top management to get support and funds, managers may either deliberately or inadvertently create high expectations.

As suggested in articles on user participation, users should be involved in the development process and thus form realistic expectations. The critical expectation categories, according to our findings, are system usefulness, information quality, and ease of use. Managing these particular expectations types during early stages of the project should have the most positive benefit on the user perceptions of the benefits of actually using the system, once it is implemented.

## 6.3. Implications for theory and limitations

A key difference between our study and prior studies is that we looked at the effects of specific expectation types (as well as expectations in general). However, the relatively small sample size of our study limits the statistical power of our analysis. While the results of looking at negative disconfirmation of expectations have been generally consistent, positive disconfirmation of different types of expectation categories appear to impact perceptions of benefits differently. The lack of consistency in the results makes it difficult to generalize whether keeping initial expectations unrealistically low may be more beneficial than keeping expectations at a realistic level. Past research on positive disconfirmation in non-information systems settings has also found mixed results [3,48].

## 7. Conclusion

Managing expectations is an important consideration for successfully implementing a new information system. Our study has demonstrated the adverse effects that unrealistically high expectations can have on user perceived net benefit of a system. We have also identified several categories of expectations that appear to be more important than others. We suggest that it would be in management’s best interests to develop strategies that ensure that these expectations in particular will be maintained at a realistic level.

Peter B. Seddon is an associate professor in the Department of Information Systems at The University of Melbourne, Australia.
