# Expert versus novice use of the executive support systems: an empirical study

Shin-Yuan Hung<sup>\*</sup>

Department of Information Management, National Chung Cheng University, Chia-Yi 62117, Taiwan, ROC

Received 15 March 2001; received in revised form 26 September 2001; accepted 15 December 2001

## Abstract

Expertise is essential in problem solving, particularly for executives. Much literature indicates that, in solving daily problems, executives may employ both analytical thinking and intuition. To develop skills and apply intuition, company executives require the use of support devices such as executive support systems (ESS), but their value may differ substantially between experts and novices.

Previous research has failed to address the effects of expertise on ESS use. Therefore, this study examines how professionals use them to address business-oriented tasks. The professionals studied were divided into two groups, depending on whether they were experts or novices in the business represented by those tasks. The findings of the experiments show that computer self-efficacy strongly influenced the use of ESS. Moreover, experts felt that the ESS was of greater use when more powerful systems were employed, while the difference for novices between more and less powerful systems was insignificant. Finally, experts rated their user information satisfaction score significantly higher when employing more powerful systems. However, novices rated systems significantly higher only when they cognitively fit the performed task. Implications are discussed. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Expertise; Executive support systems; Executive information systems; Decision support systems; Information systems evaluation

## 1. Introduction

An executive support system (ESS) is a specific decision support system (DSS), which is tailored to meet executive needs: to help top executives discover problems and exploit opportunities. Functions that an ESS typically provides include: on-line status access, drill down, key performance index, exception reporting, trend analysis, multi-dimensional analysis, and other data analysis and communication capabilities. Since the design philosophy of an ESS is that executives are expected to use the system directly without intermediaries, they should be user-friendly and require minimal or no training.

Interest in the feasibility of applying ESS within organizations has increased rapidly over the last decade. Research on the topic has proliferated [2,5,12,14, 15,17,25,26,31,36,40,42,44]. However, currently ESS is not widely applied worldwide, e.g. only 15% of the respondents in Taiwan claimed to be applying ESS [20] and in the United States, in the mid 1990s only 33% used the system [29]. To broaden its use, a fuller understanding is needed of how executives interact with an ESS.

Expertise plays an important role among effects that might influence the interactions between executives and ESS. Much literature indicates that in solving daily problems executives may employ both analytical thinking and intuition [1,11,16,24,27]. Furthermore, expertise is required to develop analytical thinking and effectively apply intuition. It has also been found that expertise is critical in developing effective intuition [6,18,34]. Therefore, ESS may differ substantially in its application between experts and novices. To our knowledge, previous research has not addressed how expertise affects the ESS use. Therefore, this study conducted an experiment to examine how professionals use ESS to address their business-oriented tasks. The professionals were divided into two groups, depending on whether they were experts or novices in the business represented by those tasks.

## 2. Previous research

## 2.1. Executive support systems

An executive information system (EIS) provides executives with easy access to internal and external information that is relevant to their critical success factors, whereas, an executive support system (ESS) in addition provides other support capabilities, including: support for electronic communications, data analysis, and other organizing tools [41]. ESS research emerged in the 1970s. Some important issues are the following.

(1) What is an ESS? Why is it needed? What factors influence its spread and application?

(2) What are the critical components of an ESS? What information technologies can be applied in building an ESS?

(3) What methodologies and tools can be applied to facilitating ESS development? What are the applications of ESS in business?

(4) How can an ESS help the executive solve problems and learn?

As decision making is becoming increasingly complicated and volatile, ESS is beneficial in improving decision-making performance. Previous research has also shown demonstrated that ESS is invaluable to executives; Leidner and Elam [19] surveyed 46 executive users of ESS and found that the more frequent an executive’s use of ESS, the faster and more extensive analysis occurs in decision-making. Vandenbosch and

Higgins [37] conducted a survey of 73 executives. The results indicated that ESS fostered executive learning. However, according to surveys conducted in Taiwan and United States and despite the reported benefits, ESS is not widely used by executives.

## 2.2. The role of expertise in executive decision-making

Expertise is the knowledge and experience people use to make decisions in complex dynamic situations. Expertise has the following characteristics: (1) it is domain specific; (2) it is acquired through stages of development, somewhat akin to the mental development of children; (3) it is a mixture of analysis and intuition. Also, experts employ various thinking strategies and their thinking is more automated [28].

Many studies in cognitive science research have shown the superiority of experts over novices in cognitive functioning, from memory and learning to problem solving and reasoning [3]. For example, chess masters have been found to perceive patterns of play more efficiently and to have a better memory for chess positions. Experts in physics, mathematics, accounting, auditing, insurance judgement, and computer programming reveal similar superior skills [32]. However, decision making research suggests that experts often rely on heuristics to cope with uncertainty, and this in turn leads to systematic biases [21]. Thus, decision support and ESS could be applied to improve performance.

## 2.3. Effect of different levels of expertise

Although much work has been done in studying the effectiveness of the DSS, little is known about the role of expertise in its use. Two exceptions are Mackay et al. [22] and Mackay and Elam [23].

Mackay et al. [22] examined the impact of a specific decision aid on problem solving processes in a semistructured problem. According to their results, among decision-aided users, differences exist in the problem solving processes of task experts versus task novices. By spending less time and pursuing fewer actions in the problem presentation stage, task experts completed the task more quickly than novices. Task experts also have more activities in the idea execution step of the problem. They spent less time in non-problem solving actions. Moreover, in functional and task domains, Mackay and Elam [23] assessed the effects of decision aids on experts and novices. The results indicated that decision aid expertise is required to apply functional expertise successfully.

## 3. Research methodology

This study focuses on assessing how executives interact with ESS to solve problems and on understanding how the expertise of these executives influences the nature of the interaction. A field experiment was conducted to test the hypotheses.

## 3.1. Experimental design

Herein, three major variables including expertise, system function, and task type were studied. Executives were classified as either experts or novices. Two systems that had various functional capabilities were designed: one possessed solely analytical tools, whereas the other had both intuitive and analytical tools. Furthermore, the tasks were divided into two: analysis-inducing task (AIT) and intuition-inducing task (IIT).

## 3.2. Subjects

In order to increase the external validity of the study, we recruited 26 executives from nine mid- to large-sized construction companies in southern Taiwan; they participated voluntarily in the experiment. All were high-level managers. Two of them participated in the pretest. The remaining 24 participated in the actual experiment.

Operationalizing expertise is not straightforward. Several approaches have been proposed, including those selected by people having recognized credentials, rated by supervisors, and subjectively judged by researchers. The latter two approaches are combined here. Twelve of the subjects were classified into the expert group and the other 12 in the novice group, based on the results of their supervisors’ rating and researchers’ review of their work experience. Each subject performed four tasks, resulting in a total of 96 observations. Table 1 displays the profile of the 24 subjects.

As these tasks required in-depth analytical skills, they were classified as AITs (see Appendix A). In task 2, executives were asked to find the flaws of management and in task 4, executives were asked to deal with a crisis caused by competition. Previous studies [7,30,33,43,45] indicated that a DSS can be designed to support both analysis and intuition. Accordingly, we can roughly classify supporting tools of an ESS into analytical and intuitive tools (see Table 3). Analytical tools are primarily used to support quantitative analysis or left-brain activities. Many analytical tools, such as status access, exception reporting, drill down, and trend analysis, are currently popular components of commercial packages.

Intuitive tools are primarily employed to support qualitative reasoning or right-brain activities. Examples of intuitive tools include applying knowledgebased systems to process heuristic rules, analogical reasoning to capture past experience, inductive reasoning to summarize causal relationships, and abductive reasoning to create plausible hypotheses.

Two prototype systems were designed for each task. Thus, eight prototype systems were developed. The development tool was Pilot Software’s LightShip, a popular package featuring user-friendly, multiple data representation formats on the same screen, integration of data from varying sources, and support for rapid prototyping. Analytical Systems provide status access, exception reporting, drill down, and trend analysis tools. Those tools provide relevant information in both tabular and graphical (e.g. bar chart, line chart, or pie chart, etc.) formats. In addition, intuitive systems provide tools to support intuitive decision making, including heuristic rules and past experience. The intuitive tools also provided an explanation mechanism to convince users why the heuristics and cases are retrieved. Executives could easily access needed information and use supporting tools through simple mouse clicks.

## 3.4. Measures

The dependent variables were time to reach the final solution, viewed screens, perceived usefulness, and user information satisfaction. ‘‘Time to reach the final solution’’ was recorded by the experimenter, whereas ‘‘viewed screens’’ was automatically recorded by the system. The other two variables ‘‘perceived usefulness’’ and ‘‘user information satisfaction’’ were determined by asking the executive to fill in a questionnaire. This (see Appendix B) consisted of a 6-item measure of the perceived usefulness, as developed by Davis [9] and a 5-item measurement of user information satisfaction designed by Baroudi and Orlikowski [4]. Moreover, to eliminate the possible influence of executives’ computer literacy, we also measured each of their computer self-efficacy (CSE): Compeau and Higgins’ [8] measure (see Appendix C) was used.

## 3.5. Experimental procedures

All experimental sessions were conducted in the subject’s office and managed by the same experimenter. The procedures were the following.

\- Subjects listened to a standard introductory script and then read a background statement.

\- They were then asked to complete a background questionnaire.

\- Subjects were then trained to use the systems and asked to complete a practice task to learn various functions of the experimental system.

\- They were next randomly assigned four experimental tasks and, when completed, they were then asked to complete a post-test questionnaire.

\- Finally, the experimenter conducted a debriefing session to determine whether the subjects had any questions, comments, or suggestions regarding the experiment.

## 4. Results

To account for differences in the responses due to unique characteristics of the respondents, MANCOVA<sup>1</sup> was used to analyze the data from the experiment. The covariate to be controlled was user’s CSE. Since each of 24 subjects was asked to perform four experimental tasks, there are 12 observations in each of the eight experimental cells. The sample size is acceptable. Table 4 summarizes the results.

## 4.1. Time required to reach final solution

The first analysis examined the amount of time required to reach a final solution. The results could be summarized as follows. First, the covariate, CSE, had a significant effect on the amount of time required to attain the final solution $( F = 5 . 3 6 , p = 0 . 0 2 3 )$ ). That is, executives with lower CSE took significantly longer to reach a final solution than those with higher

CSE (the correlation coefficient between the CSE and the decision time is 0.21). Second, both the main effects of expertise and task type were statistically significant $( F = 8 . 3 3$ $p = 0 . 0 0 9$ and $F = 9 . 3 3$ 4 $p = 0 . 0 0 3$ , respectively).

Thus, we conclude that experts $( \overline { { X } } = 1 1 . 5 9 )$ took less time to reach a final solution than novices $( \overline { { X } } = 1 2 . 1 7 )$ did and executives spent less time on $\mathrm { I I T s } ( \overline { { X } } = 1 0 . 2 0 )$ than on AITs $( \overline { { X } } = 1 3 . 5 9 )$ . The main effect of system functions as well as the interaction effects were statistically insignificant.

## 4.2. Viewed screens

The second analysis using the number of viewed screens as the dependent variable noted that only the interaction effect of expertise and task type was significant at a 0.01 level of significance $( F = 2 . 8 5 $ $p = 0 . 0 9 5 )$ . We further compared the group means and found that experts viewed more screens when they performed an AIT $( \overline { { X } } = 2 0 . 8 6 )$ than an IIT $( \overline { { X } } = 1 6 . 8 2 )$ , whereas, novices viewed more screens when they performed an IIT $( \overline { { X } } = 1 7 . 4 6 )$ than an AIT $( \overline { { X } } = 1 2 . 2 5 )$ . This may be attributed to their distinct task proficiencies. Experts excelled in intuition-inducing problem solving, therefore they did not require a large amount of information when performing an IIT. Conversely, novices did not excel in intuition-inducing problem solving that is they required more screen viewing to obtain as much information.

## 4.3. Perceived usefulness

The third analysis evaluated the users’ perceived usefulness, which is recognized as an indicator for evaluating the success of ESS. Table 4 presents three findings. First, the covariate, CSE, had a significant effect on users’ perceived usefulness $( F = 1 0 . 2 6 ,$ $p = 0 . 0 0 2 )$ . That is, executives with higher CSE levels ranked ESS more useful (the correlation coefficient between the CSE and the perceived usefulness is 0.32). Second, the main effect of the system on users’ perceived usefulness was also significant at a $0 . 0 1$ level of significance $( F = 3 . 2 7 , ~ p = 0 . 0 7 4 )$ Namely, executives felt more useful when they used more powerful systems (i.e. ESS with both analytical and intuitive tools, $\overline { { X } } = 1 . 2 7 )$ than those using less powerful systems (i.e. ESS with only analytical tools, $\overline { { X } } = 1 . 0 0 )$ . Finally, the interaction effect of expertise and system function was also significant at a 0.01 level of significance $( F = 2 . 9 8 , p = 0 . 0 8 8 )$ For experts, more powerful systems $( \overline { { X } } = 1 . 3 1 )$ were considered more useful than less powerful systems $( \overline { { X } } = 0 . 7 7 )$ , whereas, the varying perception between to more powerful systems $( \overline { { X } } = 1 . 2 2 )$ and less powerful systems $( \overline { { X } } = 1 . 2 1 )$ was insignificant for novices.

## 4.4. User information satisfaction

User information satisfaction was examined as an alternative indicator, which could also be employed to evaluate the success of ESS. Table 4 illustrates that the covariate of CSE significantly affects the users information satisfaction with the ESS $( F = 1 1 . 6 0 ,$ $p = 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). Namely, executives with higher CSE levels were more satisfied with the information provided by the systems (the correlation coefficient between the computer self-efficacy and the user information satisfaction is 0.25). Moreover, the interaction effect of tasks and system function was statistically significant $( F = 3 . 9 4 , p = 0 . 0 5 0 )$ . The findings were consistent with prior evidence [10,38,39]. The cognitive-fit theory claimed that the complexity of a task environment could be effectively reduced when the problem-solving aids support the task strategies required to perform that particular task. Thus, it is obvious that analytical-inducing tasks are better supported by systems with only analytical tools $( \overline { { X } } = 0 . 7 7 )$ than those supported by systems with additional intuitive tools $( \bar { \overline { { X } } } = 0 . 6 5 )$ , whereas, IITs are better supported by systems having intuitive support functions $( \overline { { X } } = 0 . 8 6 )$ than those without intuitive tools $( \overline { { X } } = 0 . 5 2 )$

Finally, the three-way interaction effect of expertise, task type, and system function was also statistically significant $( F = 4 . 4 8 , p = 0 . 0 3 7 )$ . Specifically, in both tasks the UIS score was rated much higher when experts employed more powerful systems. However, the novices rated higher only when they employed the systems with tools that cognitively fit the task they performed. Therefore, we conclude that there is a different varying reliance on intuition for problem solving between experts and novices. Experts possess expertise and rely heavily on intuition to solve problems. Therefore, regardless of the task, they will always have greater satisfaction with systems possessing intuitive support functions.

## 5. Conclusions

This work investigated how expertise affects ESS use. Experimental results demonstrate several effects in which expertise contributes to the use of ESS. Due to a lack of empirical studies that explain how expertise determines ESS use among executives, this study attempts to move research to a more advanced stage. Our findings are the following.

(1) CSE strongly influences executive use of ESS. Specifically, it significantly affects the amount of time required to reach a solution, users’ perceived usefulness, and users’ information satisfaction.

(2) Experts require less time to reach a solution than novices. Furthermore, executives use less time on IITs.

(3) Experts view more screens when performing an AIT, whereas novices view more screens when performing an IIT.

(4) Executives feel more useful when they employ more powerful systems. Moreover, experts consider intuitive systems more useful than analytical ones, whereas the difference is insignificant for novices.

(5) As might be expected, analytical-inducing tasks are better supported by analytical systems, and IITs are better supported by intuitive support functions.

(6) In both tasks, the user information satisfaction score rated significantly higher when experts employed more powerful systems. However, novices rated significantly higher only when employing the systems with tools that cognitively fit the performed task.

Implications of the findings are fourfold. First, the CSE of executives must be considered when designing ESS. Although the sample in our experiment was not randomly drawn from the population of all executives, the distribution of 54% with high CSE and 46% with low CSE is acceptable. CSE positively correlated to the decision time, perceived usefulness, and user information satisfaction. Thereby implying that organizations intend to implement ESS should enhance the executives’ CSE.

Second, our findings indicate that the amount of time spent on tasks completion and screens viewed to support different type of tasks are unequal between experts and novices. Third, the effect of expertise should be considered when ESS is designed and implemented to increase system acceptance. For example, an expert executive may be willing to learn and use a difficult system, while a novice executive would be less likely to do so. Finally, consistent with the cognitive-fit theory, executives appreciate systems with functions that cognitively fit the tasks they perform. This is particularly true of novice executives.

Due to the time and resource constraints, some limitations of the study may exist. Only executives in the construction industry were investigated, and the generalizability of the results to those in other industries was not examined due to constraints. The systems used were prototypes that may not be representative of real systems. Finally, this study investigated a subset of the variables found to be important from a search of the literature. Other variables, such as organizational culture and climate, can be examined in the future research.

## Appendix A. Experimental task scenarios

Task 1. Conflicting facts between sales and profits XYZ is a big local construction corporation in Taiwan, whose main products are luxury villas, office buildings and ordinary fair-price housings.
