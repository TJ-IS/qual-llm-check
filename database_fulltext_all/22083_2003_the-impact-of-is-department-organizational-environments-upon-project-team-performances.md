# The impact of IS department organizational environments upon project team performances

James J. Jiang<sup>a</sup>, Gary Klein<sup>b,\*</sup>, Roger Alan Pick<sup>c</sup>

Department of Management Information Systems (IS), University of Central Florida, Orlando, FL 32816-1400, USA <sup>b</sup>College of Business and Administration, The University of Colorado at Colorado Springs, 1420 Austin Bluffs Parkway, P.O. Box 7150, Colorado Springs, CO 80933-7150, USA

<sup>c</sup>Henry W. Bloch School of Business and Public Administration, University of Missouri, Kansas City, 5110 Cherry St., Kansas City, MO 64110-2499, USA

Received 12 January 2000; received in revised form 30 August 2001; accepted 23 December 2001

## Abstract

Projects depend on the effectiveness of teams to be successful. In the past, researchers studied team characteristics in an attempt to find successful combinations of traits that promote team performance. This study examines the relation of the organizational traits of centralization, formalization and maturity to the performance of the team. These organizational influences on the environment are related to overall project team performance, especially the traits of centralization and maturity. The results of the study suggest that organizational structure must be considered when fostering successful projects, particularly those related to planning and authority.

Keywords: Project teams; Centralization; Formalization; Maturity; Effectiveness

## 1. Introduction

The need to use project teams in the design and delivery of information systems (IS) projects has long been recognized. Many firms have broken down functional barriers and are implementing cross-functional IS teams in order to improve responsiveness and quality of the deliverables [38]. Research in the IS area has shown that, team efforts are important in improving the final ‘‘quality’’ of the IS [16]; teams can also respond to project specific goals to allocate resources in the most appropriate fashion [2] and that specific actions taken by project teams can directly impact the success of the system [23]. Still, the literature on software project management suggests that significant cost and schedule overruns often occur even though project team concepts were used [1]. As the importance of IS teams increases, we need to gain an insight into the factors influencing successful IS team performance to further empower them in the achievement of their goals.

In search of factors for successful team performance, researchers have examined the personality characteristics of team members, the relationships and/or interactions among team members, the leadership abilities of project managers and the prescriptions for effective team management [39,41,42]. Other researchers have examined the relationship between IS staff and users [3], but the project team concept is often implied, rather than explicitly examined. More recently, Jones and Harrison [21] examined the relationship between IS project team performance and team cohesion, task priority, user representation and team member involvement. They concluded that the most critical factor of team success is to have management and users provide support and cooperation.

Although it is reasonable to examine the team members, it is also likely that team effectiveness results from the empowerment provided by the orga nizational culture and structure in which the team operates [10]. IS projects are often highly complex and require the coordination and integration of many functional specialties. To manage these multifunctional activities effectively, it is necessary for the team leaders and their personnel to cross organizational lines and deal with resources and staff over whom they may have little or no formal authority. Therefore, a major enabler of IS project teams’ performance is the organizational environment [14,29,36].

This environment includes such features as horizontal and vertical lines of communication and control, resource sharing among projects and project teams, multiple reporting relationships to several sponsors, and dual accountabilities. Such needs may best be met through a centralization that manipulates formal and informal mechanisms to sustain capabilities across the organization [6]. Formal techniques can enhance the shared understanding of work processes among development constituents [35]. Maturity of the IS department is another factor in the development and pursuit of goals [8].

Thus, in recent literature, the possible importance of centralization, formalization and IS maturity is raised. The objective of this study is to examine the impacts of these organizational structure factors on IS project teams’ performance. The results of this study can provide IS project managers with insights on how their organizational settings may affect their project teams and the potential problems (and benefits) that their project teams may face in their organizational settings.

## 2. Organizational factors influencing IS project team performance

There are many studies of and speculations about what organizational characteristics are associated with successful project management [9,24]. One study found that behavioral characteristics are more important than technology in supporting team activities. The fact that organizational culture, resources and rewards have an impact on team performance is readily accepted. And also that fostering any change requires the identification of controllable factors that influence project team performance. The purpose of this study is to investigate the role that certain organizational characteristics may play in determining the effectiveness of IS projects. Three organizational variables have been found to influence the implementation of technology: centralization, formalization, and maturity [13]. We make no claim that these are the only variables worthy of study; we chose them because theory and literature supports their use and there are available and reliable scales to measure them.

Centralization refers to the degree of concentration of the decision-making. Control of resources extends to those making decisions. This dependency relationship explains how units acquire the power to accomplish their objectives in an organization [37,31]. Project teams are responsible for completing a project on-time and within budget, but they are often overlaid on the functional divisions of an organization. If the divisions have autonomy, then the project teams must contend with the functional areas for resources. If, however, the organization has a centralized decision structure, then top management reduces the power of the functional managers over the project team and allows for a more equal footing in the acquisition of needed resources.

The literature has examined this issue only in related areas. Decreased autonomy in decision making has generally led to negative relationships between centralization and innovation [30] and acceptance [33]. Some positive relationships, however, have been reported between centralization and system implementation. From an IS project team perspective, it has been asserted that the implementation of IS generally allows appropriate information to flow to higher managerial levels, thereby facilitating the centralization of decision making [7]. Top management support has been recognized as a critical factor in successful project implementation [34]. In addition, many studies have reported a positive relationship between top management support and interdepartmental cooperation, a scenario more readily found in a centralized structure with concentrated power. Therefore, based on the resource-dependency model and research in related areas, we propose:

H1. Centralization will be positively associated with project team effectiveness.

Formalization refers to the degree of reliance an organization places on formal rules and procedures. Task oriented management refers to the extent to which an organization defines the tasks, standards and procedures associated with more technical jobs [5]. Though this may not be appropriate in all situations, the formalization it provides may increase productivity when the jobs are highly structured or contain well defined steps [32].

Practice and studies in related fields tend to support the contention that formal structures may lead to better project team performance. Structure adds to the quality of systems through increased understanding and improved resource utilization. Many corporations attempt to establish consistent approaches to technology implementation. These vary from developing internal methodologies (e.g. formal system development life cycle methods) to purchasing documentation systems that force IS project members to follow rigidly-defined practice. Most IS research reports positive associations of formalization with system acceptance and implementation. Again from theory and literature in other settings, we hypothesize:

H2. Formalization will be positively associated with project team effectiveness.

IS maturity is an elusive concept [4]. The literature describes many factors associated with better or more mature IS departments. These characteristics include, top management knowledge of information technology [26], top management involvement in IS planning [25], the extent of infusion and diffusion of information technologies, the number of employees in the IS department, and IS performance criteria based on organizational goals rather than cost [27].

Maturity is the result of a history of performance modifications. The Three-Stage Model defines the stages of change that lead to an improvement in performance or a reaction to new conditions [28].

The three stages are: (1) unfreezing, the time of recognizing a need for change; (2) change, the process of making adjustments; and (3) refreezing, setting the changes into the management system. This process continually improves managerial behavior and control as the organization or department matures [40].

The literature supports the importance of maturity in numerous activities. IS planning is improved as the department matures and the goals that are established in mature planning efforts lead to more effective project management. The (evolution/maturing) is important in achieving effectiveness and quality of the system development process and may play a part in the control of resources that can help ensure success. Based on the literature and principles of maturation, we expect:

H3. IS maturity will be positively related to project team effectiveness.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Construct operationalizations

The dependent variable, performance, is not a simple concept. Each organization has its own method of measuring a project team’s performance. There seems to be a general agreement among software engineering managers that certain factors characterize successful teams. For example, Thamhain and Wilemon interviewed more than 500 software engineering professionals and found that more than 90% of them mentioned three measures as the most important criteria: technical success, on-time performance and on-budget/within resources performance. Here, team effectiveness was measured using Ford and McLaughlin’s multiple-item scale [12]. This scale includes these criteria, as shown in Table 1. Centrali zation, formalization, and IS maturity were captured using items developed by Grover and Goslar. We include all these items.

## 3.2. Sample

Five hundred questionnaires were mailed to a random sample of members of the Association of Information Technology Professionals (AITP) in the US. The sample was chosen because these members represent a wide variety of managerial positions and organizational settings. A self-addressed return envelope was enclosed with each survey. Sample members were assured that their responses would be kept confidential. A total of 106 questionnaires were returned—a response rate of 21%, which is typical for a mail survey. A summary of the demographic characteristics of the sample is presented in Table 2. To determine if unusual sample bias existed due to non-response, a comparison was made to other studies sampling the same population. Comparing the sample’s demographic information to previous studies using AITP samples shows great similarities [17–20]. Chi-square tests were conducted to find differences between published studies and the current one. No differences were found in the proportions of age, position, work experience, gender, or organization size.

## 3.3. Construct reliability and validity

In this study, we attempted to use valid and reliable measures. First, items were adapted from previously validated instruments. Second, the reliability and validity of the measures were reassessed with the current sample.

One of the more powerful methods to test construct validity is factor analysis. If all items in the independent variables are factor analyzed and loaded in accordance with the a priori theoretical expectations, then significant aspects of construct validity have been assessed (i.e. the ability of homogeneous items to converge together on a factor and away from others). Principal component analysis was conducted using all of the items for the three independent and one dependent variables. From the analysis, a four-factor solution emerged that accounted for 64% of the variance.

While there is no generally accepted standard on significance of factor loadings, a general cutoff point of 0.40 was chosen, as suggested by Kerlinger. Most of the items chosen had factor loadings <0.60. Centralization, formalization, and team performance loaded into single factors in accordance with a priori expectations as established by the sources of the scales. Two items (the last two) concerning distribution of information technology resources for measuring IS maturity did not load significantly onto any factor. In retrospect, there is face validity to this result. The first five items are concerned with links between top management, IS management, and planning processes; the last two items are concerned with the diffusion of technology. Grover and Goslar also found these items loaded into a separate factor. Therefore, only the first five items were included in further data analysis.

Cronbach alpha was calculated to assess measurement reliability. All were greater than the prescribed 0.70 level, implying an adequate level of internal consistency. For this part of the questionnaire, the Kaiser measure of sampling adequacy [22] indicated the adequacy of sample size (MSA ¼ 0:72). Thus, these items have acceptable reliability and validity for testing the research questions, and the sample is adequate.

This failure to meet an expectation may be due to the relationship between formalization and centralization reducing the impact of formalization in the final model. It may also be due to the presence of formal practices and methodology in IS projects, regardless of the degree of formalization of the organization.

## 5. Discussion

Our goal was to understand the factors that facilitate IS project team performance. It should be noted that contextual factors essentially represent general conditions under which it would be easier and beneficial for an organization to adopt project team management to design and deliver IS projects. For instance, we suggest that a highly centralized and formalized organization, with a mature IS department, will gain more from adopting IS teams concepts for delivering projects. Generally speaking, our results indicate that IS teams are more effective in a centralized, mature environment.

Researchers have observed that the extent of top management involvement in IS planning (IS maturity) and critical decisions (centralization) often influence a higher level of formal coordination of activities between IS units and other division boundaries. Furthermore, the usage of formal IS coordination activities across divisions is associated with several favorable IS outcomes, including effective integration of IS planning activities, advanced IS budget and planning practices and increasing managerial support and funding [15]. Today’s CEOs must be involved in

IS strategic planning to facilitate IS performance as well as in involving IS managers in strategic decision making.

Researchers also have argued that informal mechanisms, such as physical co-location of IS project teams with business units, interdepartmental events between IS units and other units, job rotations and input to performance reviewer from business units, could enhance the level of coordination [11]. A centralized organization often has a significant positive influence on the implementation of these informal mechanisms for coordination.
