# Performance measure of information systems (IS) in evolving computing environments: an empirical investigation

Jaeho Heo $^{*}$ , Ingoo Han

Graduate School of Management, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology, 207-43 Cheongryangri-dong, Dongdaemoon-gu, Seoul 130-012, South Korea

Received 20 September 2000; received in revised form 8 October 2001; accepted 26 December 2001

## Abstract

This paper argues that the contingent evaluation approach can help an organization to evaluate the impact of information systems (IS) on business performance. The structure of IS is one of the most important factors in applying the contingent approach, because it reflects the evolution of the computing environment and is also aligned with business objectives and strategies. We empirically derived four taxonomies of IS structure and tested the relationships between the derived typologies and the prioritized or adopted measures of their performance. Firms with different IS structure were found to consider some measures to be more appropriate than others. The empirical results support the idea that the contingent measurement approach has the potential to be an even more useful framework if it incorporates more contingency factors, such as environmental, organizational contexts, and other characteristics.

## 1. Introduction

Implementation of information technology is a source of increasing cost and concern to management. It is expected that the average Fortune 200 firm will spend 20–40% of its operating budget on IT just to stay competitive [33]. However, researchers have not demonstrated a consistent relationship between information systems (IS) and information technology (IT) investment and organizational performance [7,15,20,41]. Several studies suggest that returns on IS investment, whether they are monetary or not, depend on the system's strategic role [4,13,19,32]. Thus, for evaluating performance, it is important to incorporate appropriate measures that are linked to the system's strategic role, or even the organization's strategy.

Information systems structure is incorporated here as a critical factor affecting the evaluation of IS performance. The goals, objectives, and strategies of organizations are aligned with those of IS through the process of IS strategic planning (ISP) $[35,38]$ . Research $[1,2,16,17,29,30,46]$ suggests that the evolving IS environments are the result of this alignment. Hence, the IS structure has implications for an evolutionary role of IS in an organization, rather than just for its hardware configuration.

This paper discusses how we developed and tested a model that determines which measures are appropriate in an evolving IS environment. The objective was to examine the relationship between IS structure and appropriate measures of IS evaluation that stems from the rationale of the previous researchers. By using the results of empirical tests, we attempted to adopt a contingent measurement approach. Steps are specifically: (1) identifying the characteristics and number of alternative IS structures; and (2) describing and testing the anticipated relationships between IT structure and the appropriate measures of IS performance. The study, through a mail survey of 137 Korean IS managers, developed a taxonomy of IS structure, explored its relationships to IS performance measures, and then found significant relationships.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Importance of the topic

The value of IS has been an issue for a number of years. There is contradictory evidence (e.g. $[7,42,44]$ ) as to whether or not IT expenditure has resulted in the creation of value; the apparent anomaly has been called the “productivity paradox”. Farbey et al. identify two schools of thought on this.

The first is that poor evaluation of IT expenditure suggests that the statistics may be of doubtful quality, adding further confusion; the second is that poor evaluation practices have resulted in incorrect selection and management of projects, resulting in poor returns. In either case, inadequate evaluation gives cause for concern.

According to Jurison [24], empirical studies have presented mixed or even conflicting results. While some divergence can be attributed to a lack of methodological rigor or the ad hoc nature of the evaluation process, even carefully designed studies have produced inconsistent results. He presents three possible explanations.

1. Variables tend to be inconsistently defined or measured, because there is a lack of agreement on what constitutes a dependent variable in this field.

2. There is no inclusion of the effect of intervening time.

3. The complex interactions between technology and organizational users during system implementation make it difficult to compare and develop a consistent theory.

The first problem arises from adoption of conventional input–output accounting methods (e.g. turnover, ROI, NPV, and so on) or surrogate measures (usage and user satisfaction). Effectiveness and innovation is more difficult to quantify in terms of these economic efficiency statistics, as soft and less tangible intellectual assets become more important. The second problem stems from the lack of historic data, but it is being reduced by using public databases like COMPUSTAT in the US. Finally, the fundamental issues represented in “support on attainment of an organizational goal”, remains unsolved. Individual users have different jobs, with different technical skill levels and various and temporary environmental, organizational, and technical situations.

The current evaluation approaches and practices are based on total quality management (TQM), information economics, project management, or cognitive theories $[37]$ . Each method has inherent limitations that evoke the problems, while each also has strength and weakness. To cope with these problems, some researchers have tried to consolidate the approaches by incorporating multi-objective and multi-criteria into their methods. For example, information economics introduced the concepts of value, risk, and human and management factors $[5]$ . Such consolidation makes it possible to include multiple aspects of IS performance and consider time effects by performing a longitudinal study.

Other researchers have suggested multi-dimensional and interrelated measures of IS evaluation, and have presented contingency models for selecting appropriate measures (e.g. $[14,36]$ ). A contingency approach contributes to identification of the complex relationships among variables by analyzing moderate or mediate effects of variables.

It is also important to determine empirically the contingency factors suggested by previous researchers, who have suggested several potential normative factors, such as external environmental variables (industry, competitive environment, culture, etc.) or organizational variables (mission, size, goal, IS maturity, structure, evaluator perspective, etc.). Weil and Olson [48] reviewed MIS contingency research and summarized the ways that environmental or organizational contexts influence IS performance via IS contexts. Studies on information system strategic plan (ISP or ISSP) consistently posit that IS context is linked to organizational strategy or structure through the processes or methodologies of ISP [11,39]. Teo and King [47] suggested the need for a joint evolution of BP–ISP integration. The tighter the integration, the more effectively IS supports business strategies.

The final need is to develop measures that are easy to implement and understand $[26]$ . Myers et al. insisted, along with previously research, that any evaluation system should have a basis of measurement that is “readily understood, simple to implement, easy to administer, and clearly cost effective”. In this light, IS contextual variables are more appropriate than environmental or organizational contextual variables as the contingency factors—they have direct effects on IS performance.

What is needed is a comprehensive contingency framework for assessment of IS performance that considers IS contextual factors. Ballantine et al. also point out a lack of business and IS strategy in small and medium enterprises.

## 2.2. The contingency approaches to IS evaluation

Researchers have introduced the idea of various qualitative or quantitative measures of system performance, such as IS function efficiency, information quality, user satisfaction, work group's decision-making, and strategic or financial impact on organizations [6,12,43,49].

DeLone and McLean created an IS success model and suggested that a systematic combination of individual measures from IS success categories can create a comprehensive measurement instrument. The model is an attempt to reflect the interdependent, process nature of IS success: it is a description of the relationships of the six IS success dimensions. They contend the following.

System quality and information quality singularly and jointly affect both use and user satisfaction. Additionally, the amount of use can affect the degree of user satisfaction—positively or negatively—as well as the reverse being true. Use and user satisfaction are direct antecedents of individual impact; and lastly, this impact on individual performance should eventually have some organizational impact.

This model seems to be the most comprehensive IS assessment model. To verify its validity empirically, Seddon and Kiew [45] tested the model through a structural equation model (SEM) and the results partially supported DeLone and McLean's model. Several researchers also followed the interrelationship model and contingency considerations suggested by DeLone and McLean, and expanded them with slight modifications [8,10,23,28].

Saunders and Jones also suggested a similar idea for the selection of IS success measures. They found that the IS function's impact on strategic direction, the integration of the IS function planning with corporate planning, the quality of information outputs, and the IS function's contribution to organizational financial performance were highly ranked in terms of importance. In contradiction to this, the measures used for these dimensions were found to be weak or surrogates, and the measures of operational efficiency, such as response time and system availability, are more highly valued. The authors suggested that this contradiction may be because IS operational efficiency has been stressed for years, while IS impact on strategic direction is a fairly new dimension and its measures are still being developed.

Myers et al. combined the dimensions of IS success of DeLone and McLean and the contingency framework developed by Saunders and Jones. Fig. 1 summarizes the resulting model. Finally, Ballantine et al. suggested that organizational size is a factor that influences the adoption of a particular evaluation practice. They identified five phenomena of lower IS maturity in SMEs (although they did not include IS maturity explicitly), and then developed propositions on their anticipated effects on the adoption of evaluation practice.

However, previous contingency studies have only suggested those factors IS evaluation that should be included, not how to apply them.

## 3. Research model and hypothesis

The fundamental assumption of our study is that there are some systematic mechanisms to tell why and how firms might adopt a particular set of evaluation measure. The IS structure might provide answer to the question, because it provides implications in the evolution of IS and the role of the IS in providing ease of information sharing. As IS evolves, the ability to identify and capture its intangible benefits will increase and thus the evaluation of IS can adopt more effective measures. This paper adopts the taxonomy of Fiedler et al. (i.e. centralized, decentralized, centralized cooperative, and distributed cooperative computing) to derive the taxonomies of the IS structure in Korea.

There is an even greater need for matching organizational and IT structure to assure the availability of appropriate levels of IT-supported information processing and coordination $[34]$ . To reduce organizational uncertainty, information-handling capabilities and activities are organized in different ways in different IS structures $[9]$ . Therefore, the way to assess the performance of IS emphasizes different factors depending on the alignment of IS with organizational (business) objectives $[27]$ . Saunders and Jones insisted that, as the IS function matures, the measurement focus shifts from operational efficiency and user satisfaction to a more unstructured concern for IS impact on strategic direction.

Thus the research question can be expressed as the following simple and basic hypothesis.

Hypothesis 0. There are relationships between the appropriate IS performance dimensions and IS structural typologies.

Laurinec and Kim re-categorized system quality, information use, and user satisfaction. These operational measures have probably been widely used, so that the measurement instruments are well developed and validated. They thus can be more widely used in relatively immature computing environments [40].

Hypothesis 1. Operational measures (system quality, information use, and user satisfaction) are perceived to be more appropriate than other measures in centralized computing.

The decentralized IS structure is designed to support the decentralized organizational decision-making structure and hence fits organizations composed of the local work-teams with much autonomy $[25]$ . In this type of structure, user groups control their own data, decision-making authority in applications development, maintenance, and procurement. In addition, a local IS can be differentiated from others through requirements for the local market environment, work group member's expertise, etc. Consequently, the focus of evaluation is placed on how well the IS provides the local and specialized information, how satisfied the users are with using IS, and whether the IS can improve the users' job performance.

Hypothesis 2. Individual reaction measures (information quality, user satisfaction, and individual impact) are perceived to be more appropriate in a decentralized computing environment.

Centralized cooperative computing is characterized by a distributed computing process, a high degree of IT-supported communication, and a high degree of data and application shareability. Recent technological developments give room for organizations to pick a good computing environment. Lee and Leifer have stated that functional organizations with production/service types of work-teams (low differentiation and low integration) take a more centralized IS structure. The users of these central IS resources are dispersed through enterprise-wide divisions, which make communication performance a major concern in the management of enterprise IS. In addition, divisional IS groups are often under the control of the central IS groups, and they supervise communication utility and equipment purchasing. Divisional IS groups are located below the business unit level, whereas IS professionals can work closely with each functional unit, controlling applications and data generated by each project team. As a consequence, IS evaluation focuses on system quality (the central IS view), and individual and organizational impact (the divisional IS view). This rationale can be expressed as the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3. Technologic and strategic measures (system quality, individual impact, and organizational impact) are perceived to be more appropriate in a centralized cooperative computing environment.

A distributed cooperative IS structure would have decentralized processing and increased resource-sharing and communication capabilities. Fiedler et al. found that matrix organization tended to have a distributed cooperative IS structure. From many early studies, the matrix structure often experiences management problems, because the dual reporting mechanisms and participant interactions have tended to increase information equivocality. In order to overcome the communication barrier, an interpretive perspective of organizational communication drives the development of enhanced network connectivity. To help organizational members share assumptions and discover causes of conflicts, electronic communications should be utilized $[22]$ . They can facilitate the exchange of opinions and provide rapid feedback, thus increasing information sharing.

Hypothesis 4. Impact-oriented measures (information quality, individual impact, and organizational impact) are perceived to be more appropriate in a distributed cooperative computing environment.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Sample

The research method involved a mail survey of 154 Korean companies. The sample was drawn from the Yearbook of Korean Listed Companies. We set the sample at 500 companies on the basis of size and industry. The questionnaires were sent to the “chief of the MIS department”, whether he or she was an executive, middle-ranked manager, or low-level manager (see Appendix A). Responses were received from 154 firms, yielding response rate of 30.8%, which is significantly higher than the usual mail survey response rate. Seventeen firms were disqualified, because they had missing values, the number of their IS employees was under five, or the percentage of the IS budget to the overall budget was extremely low. The remaining 137 cases were used to test the hypotheses. The sample size is large enough to perform statistical tests. In testing, the unit of analysis is the overall IS of the organization.

The generalizability of a study depends on the representativeness of the respondents. The distribution of the sample, summarized as the cross-section of industries and sizes, is presented in Table 2. The responding companies are generally small or medium in size and represent a wide variety of industries. There is an almost even distribution between the manufacturing sector (manufacturing and construction) and the service sector (financial and business service, retail and wholesale, transportation, and information service/software): 46.7 and 53.3%, respectively.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. IS structural taxonomy

SPSS 8.0 for Windows was used to perform cluster analysis. The first step was to determine the number of clusters. Four groups suggested by Fiedler et al. were included.

The Ward method of agglomerative hierarchical cluster analysis was used to empirically determine the number of groups. By adding one cluster to another, a coefficient of squared Euclidean distance between the two combined cases was calculated and recorded. The value of the coefficient determined whether they could be combined into one cluster or not [3]. The coefficient in this study is large enough to lend support to the four typologies of Fiedler et al.

To supplement the subjectivity of hierarchical cluster analysis, ANOVA and nonhierarchical cluster analysis tests were performed. In each analysis, the observed F-statistic of each group revealed differences at the significance level of 0.01. The combination of the analyses allows for increased confidence in the derived taxonomy. Table 6 shows the variable means related to each of the four IS structure types that the cluster analysis produces. F-values and significance levels, with the test of the equality of variable means across the four groups shown in the last column. As can be seen from Table 7, four clusters have distinctive scores in dispersion of hardware, centralization of IS activities, interdepartmental data sharing.

## 5.2. Test of hypotheses

The results of the ANOVA test are summarized in Table 8. Columns 2–7 show the mean values, and column 8 presents the F-values and significance levels.

Once determined that differences exist among the means, post hoc range tests and pairwise multiple comparisons were applied to determine which of the means differ. Pairwise multiple comparisons test the difference between each pair of means, and yield a column indicating significantly different group means at an alpha level (0.1, 0.05, and 0.01, respectively) as shown by the asterisks. The F-tests indicate that the means of perceived appropriateness of IS success measures differ significantly in each of four groups. This means the results support the central hypothesis. To test whether the findings support each of hypotheses or not, we conducted the Duncan range test. The total sample was divided into high, moderate, and low average scores using a cutoff point based on the standard normal curve for each of the cluster groups. A character corresponding to the average score assigned to a IS measure was placed in parentheses (H, M, and L, respectively).

The F-tests indicate that the group means of these four clusters are significantly different on the prioritization and adoption of IS performance dimensions. The results indicate that there are major differences of prioritized and adopted measures of IS performance between firms in different clusters.

On system quality, the firms in the group of centralized computing (group 1) display the highest mean rating. The Duncan range tests show that the mean value of system quality is significantly higher than those of the other clusters. Firms represented by this cluster also display the highest level of the perceived appropriateness of user satisfaction, while the T-value does not show a significant difference. On both of individual impact and organizational impact, the firms represented by cluster four (distributed cooperative computing) present the highest score. Contrast values resulted from Duncan range tests show significant differences between groups.

## 6. Discussion

Information systems performance measures prioritized and adopted in the firms differ in the four types of IS structure. Two rationales were considered in analyzing these phenomena. The evolution of IS and the role of IS in organizational requirements for information sharing. As IS evolves, the competency to identify and capture the intangible benefit of IS will increase and hence the evaluation of IS can adopt better and more difficult measures.

## 7. Conclusion

Firms with centralized computing (group 1) appear to place more focus on system quality and information quality. The firms with decentralized computing (group 2) emphasize information quality and system quality. In the firms with centralized cooperative computing (group 3), organizational impact and system quality are considered more appropriate than other measures. Finally, the firms with distributed cooperative computing (group 4) consider organizational impact as the most appropriate measure.

Information systems evaluators within an organization need to be able to identify IS structure including IS administration style, hardware configuration, and information/application-sharing across departments. Such assessments can facilitate selection and prioritization of appropriate measures of performance. Then they should develop an instrument consisting of the selected measurement items. At this time, whether the items can be measurable or not in terms of their competence for evaluation and data availability should be considered.

Finally, the measure of IS performance may also need to change in the long-term and the strategic perspective. If an organization is planning to shift from one phase to the next in the IS evolution path, it should consider simultaneously what can be used to measure the IS performance in the next phase.
