# Understanding it adoption decisions in small business: integrating current theories

Cynthia K. Riemenschneider $^{a,*}$ , David A. Harrison $^{b}$ , Peter P. Mykytyn Jr. $^{c}$

$^{a}$ Information Systems Department, University of Arkansas, WCOB 204, Fayetteville, AR 72701, USA

$^{b}$ The Pennsylvania State University, University Park, PA, USA

$^{c}$ Southern Illinois University, Carbondale, IL, USA

Received 24 January 2001; received in revised form 24 October 2001; accepted 30 December 2001

## Abstract

Using a “combine and conquer” strategy with the theory of planned behavior (TPB) and the technology acceptance model (TAM), we applied a series of loosely to tightly integrated models to the IT adoption decisions of small business executives regarding a web site. With application of structural equation modeling (SEM) techniques, we found that there were progressive improvements in fit as the models become more fully integrated. The results indicate that a “Collected” model representing the underlying categories of cognitions from the TPB and the TAM provided a better fit than either the TAM or the TPB alone. © 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Small firms; Technology acceptance model; Theory of planned behavior; IT adoption

## 1. Introduction

During the period 1990–1995, small businesses in the US (defined as firms with fewer than 500 employees) accounted for more than 90% of the nearly 375,000 net new businesses. During that same time these new firms were responsible for almost 77% of the nearly 6.9 million new jobs created [46]. Additionally, small businesses employ 53% of the private work force, contribute 47% of all sales in the US, and are responsible for 51% of the private gross domestic product [49].

However, from an information technology (IT) perspective, small businesses are understudied [15], and they are unique [50]. Cragg and Zinatelli [15] reported that although researchers have examined the nature and degree of IT knowledge as a construct, little research has involved small firms. Similarly, Palvia [40] reported a scarcity of IT-related research dealing with the measurement of value of computing in smaller businesses. May be organizational theories and practices, such as bureaucratic structure and organizational behavior, applicable to large organizations may not be valid in small ones.

In addition, small businesses face unique IT adoption and use problems. Yap $[54]$ stated that small businesses tend to underestimate time and effort to implement IT. Furthermore, Thong et al. suggest that, due to factors such as susceptibility to external forces in competitive environments, smaller businesses take a short-range view of IT implementation.

To gain understanding of how small businesses handle IT adoption problems, our research was intended to integrate two well-established concepts: the theory of planned behavior (TPB) [3] and the technology acceptance model (TAM) [16,17]. That is, we employed a “combine and conquer” strategy to examine this understudied phenomenon. Each theory is well-established in the IT arena [34,47] and the two theories have been utilized in the IT context [1,51].

However, neither theory has been found to provide consistently superior explanations or predictions than the other. Our research, therefore, developed a sequence of models that involved loosely to tightly intertwined combinations of the TPB and the TAM.

Overall, IT research on small businesses tends to lack strong theoretical underpinnings (for example, see $[12,45]$ ). Despite this, simple insight about the nature of firm-level decision-making in organizations involving IT, e.g. a web presence, might provide value. IT adoption decisions in small businesses are typically made by a single executive.

The particular IT used in our investigation is presence on the web, because of its current significance. Rather than study a single type of site, we studied the adoption of any type of web presence.

## 1.1. Varying views of the web

Small business executives may wonder whether web initiatives are focused and fiscally appropriate $[37]$ . They may wonder if the business can attract new customers through the web, whether a web presence will be costly to maintain, if customers will abandon a site due to navigation difficulties, or whether customers' first purchase might be their last.

Many small businesses still have basic problems using PC technologies, including poor software quality, lack of training, and the inability to relate IT investments to business value $[43]$ . On the other hand, web use is increasing in the risk management $[22]$ and the textile and sewn products $[41]$ industries, as well as in advertising and email $[53]$ . It has been estimated that more than 50% of small businesses will be using the web by 2004 $[42]$ . However, although some small businesses have experienced successes involving the web, less than 20% are using it to sell products or services.

Vijayan [52] reports that uncertainty about real business value and the web exists, with an emphasis on attempting to determine how soon the small business may get a return on the investment. McGarvey [35] indicates that many small businesses are concerned about establishing a web-only presence or a combination of web presence to accompany a brick-and-mortar context. The lack of strong profits among dotcom's only exacerbates the problem.

Auger and Gallaugher [5] conducted an exploratory study of small businesses (defined there as 1–300 employees) in a limited number of industries that already had a web presence to conduct sales. Based on the literature, a list of factors that influenced the firm's decision to “go on-line” were found.

## 1.2. Theoretical foundation

MIS researchers have used intention models or behavioral decision theories from social psychology to help explain the use of IT (e.g. [30]). Two theories, Ajzen's TPB and Davis' TAM are among the most popular and well supported. Both theories draw on Fishbein and Ajzen's [21] earlier ‘theory of reasoned action.’

## 1.2.1. Theory of planned behavior

In the TPB, a small business executive's decision or behavioral intention (BI) to pursue a course of action, such as creating a presence on the web, is a function of attitude (A), subjective norm (SN), and perceived behavioral control (PBC). The TPB also theorizes that BI will ultimately result in the action. The actual behavior in our study would be the eventual existence of a web presence. However, the main focus here is to predict an executive's BI, represented by the cognitive process of decision-making. SN is the degree of perceived social pressure that the executive feels to adopt a web presence. PBC is how easy or difficult an executive thinks that adoption will be, involving potential obstacles.

Attitude is assumed to be determined by a sum of cross-products of behavioral beliefs (bb) and evaluations (ev) about the expected positive or negative consequences: $\sum \mathrm{bb}_i\times \mathrm{ev}_i$ . SN is then determined by a sum of cross-products of normative beliefs (nb) about and motivations to comply (mc) with specific social referents (SN) (important persons and groups in one's environment): $\sum \mathrm{nb}_j\times \mathrm{mc}_j$ . PBC is determined by a sum of cross-products of control beliefs (cb) about and perceived power/resources (pp) to deal with potential obstacles:

$\sum cb_{k} \times pp_{k}$ . Further details on these molecular belief structures can be found in Ajzen, as well as several recent applications.

The TPB applies to self-oriented behavior (e.g. exercising or dieting) as well as other-oriented behavior (e.g. donating blood). It can also be applied to strategic decision-making. This social premise of strategic choices underscores the potential influence of SN.

## 1.2.2. Technology acceptance model

In contrast, the TAM was designed specifically to explain computer usage $[18]$ . The TAM presents two cognitions, perceived usefulness (U) and attitude (A), as determinants of intention. Perceived usefulness is defined as the degree to which someone believes that adopting a particular technology will have a performance benefit. For small business executives, an example could be the assumption that a web site would benefit the performance of the firm. Finally, perceived ease-of-use (EOU) refers to the degree to which an individual expects that working with a particular IT will be simple.

## 1.3. Empirical applications of the TPB and TAM

## 1.3.1. Small business research

Both TPB and TAM have been applied in the small business context. Harrison et al. [24] used the TPB in a study of 162 small businesses who were considering the adoption of a form of IT (none of which, however, involved a web site). They found that attitude, PBC, and SN were all significant in predicting a small business executive's decision. Results were consistently supportive, prompting the authors to suggest that the TPB could be used as a general theory of IT adoption in small businesses.

Igbaria et al. [27] studied 203 firms in the manufacturing and engineering industries in New Zealand to analyze factors affecting personal computing acceptance in small firms (in their study defined as between 20 and 100 employees). In contrast to the Davis et al. [18] predictions, perceived EOU was a strong determinant in acceptance, with a larger effect than perceived usefulness. They speculated that the inconsistency might have stemmed from small businesses being driven to accept computer technologies on their EOU.

## 1.3.2. Other IT contexts

Outside their very limited use in small business populations, there is a broader base of empirical support for both TPB and TAM in the IT literature. Liao, Shao, Wang and Chen used the TPB to study adoption decisions in virtual banking by 118 professionals in Hong Kong. They concluded that the TPB only partially explained their intentions to use. Taylor and Todd applied the TAM, the TPB, and a decomposed TPB to predict student usage of a computer resource center. They found that all of the models performed well and were essentially equivalent in predictive ability. Morris and Venkatesh [36] applied TPB to investigate the impact of age on new software usage by workers. They found that younger workers were influenced by attitude while older workers were more influenced by SN and PBC. However, the influence of SN on the older workers did decrease over time.

## 1.3.3. Comparisons and the case for combination

Mathieson performed a head-to-head comparison of TPB and TAM in a decision to use a spreadsheet. He found that both explained intention equally well (in terms of $R^{2}$ -values). This result is consistent with a Davis et al. [18] longitudinal study in which they compared TRA to TAM and found that both theories did a reasonable job of predicting BIs.

He also compared the costs of data collection using each theory, arguing that TAM was clearly easier to use and involved less cost because of the availability of a standard instrument that Davis had developed. Indeed, he concluded that TPB had more context-specific information value, whereas TAM provided context-free information that might be portable across a variety of IT types.

TAM is more narrowly focused on users of IT and on the common elements of the contexts in which they engage in IT. The TAM does not require a separate elicitation of consequences or SN or obstacles for each situation. It has highly reliable and more consistent measurement of usefulness (U) and EOU than is typically present with $\sum bb_{i} \times ev_{i}$ and $\sum cb_{k} \times pp_{k}$ terms. On the other hand, TPB is more broadly targeted.

Given their complementary advantages and disadvantages, a “combine and conquer” strategy in applying these theories could enhance the understanding of IT adoption in small business. For example, Agarwal and Prasad [2] studied initial and continued use of a technological innovation based on theoretical foundations in ‘innovation diffusion theory’ and TAM. In the small business context, we believe the TPB could contribute to TAM by more clearly articulating the role of social referents in executive decision-making. However, within this combination strategy, there are a variety of specific ways that the two theories could be connected or integrated. Here, we propose a sequence of combinations ranging from an “Appended” model (loosely integrated), to “Collected” (tightly integrated), and between them a “Hybrid” type.

## 1.3.4. Appended model

The Appended model connects the two theories in a passive or surface-level way. Its “side-by-side” structure allows TPB and TAM to share attitude and intention. All other constructs and relationships are incorporated separately, without change. Thus, it serves as a superordinate theory, in which both the TPB and TAM are contained or “nested.” This not only extends both theories, but it helps address the lack of predictiveness of attitude by $\sum bb_{i} \times ev_{i}$ recognized by Ajzen in his review and exposition of the TPB. Fig. 1 illustrates this graphically. The dark-shaded constructs and dotted arrows represent determinants of the original TAM; the unshaded constructs and dashed arrows represent determinants of the original TPB. Lightly-shaded constructs and full arrows represent common aspects of both theories (attitude and intention). If the Appended model represents a better fit to the decision mechanisms, then the multiple correlations should improve.

## 1.3.5. Hybrid model

Our Hybrid model is an extension of the Appended one, allowing greater overlap of the TPB and TAM. It includes two additional, “crossover” relationships that recognize the theoretical similarities between determinants in the original theories. The gray, unfilled arrows of the above figure represent these extra relationships. In one, perceived EOU is proposed as an antecedent to PBC. It is intuitive to consider perceived EOU as an antecedent to PBC. In the other crossover relationship, $\sum bb_{i} \times ev_{i}$ composite is proposed as an antecedent to perceived usefulness (U). The original expectancy-value argument about $\sum bb_{i} \times ev_{i}$ being a calculated evaluation or utility of the target behavior fits squarely with the conceptual substance of U.

By including crossover relationships, the Hybrid model allows for greater interconnectedness of the TAM and the TPB. The inclusion of crossover effects has been used previously with improved model predictability. Taylor and Todd [48] tested three variations of the TPB in a consumer setting and found improvements in prediction when they decomposed the belief structures and allowed for crossover effects.

## 1.3.6. Collected model

The Collected model represents a more complete combination at a higher level of abstraction. It involves considering the underlying conceptual structure of both methods. The constructs usefulness (U), attitude (A), and $\sum bb_{i} \times ev_{i}$ all share some element of perceived benefit or anticipated satisfaction in adopting a particular IT. The constructs SN and $\sum nb_{j} \times mc_{j}$ deal with social demands or social (dis)approval associated with the adoption. Perceived EOU, PBC and $\sum cb_{k} \times pp_{k}$ all deal with perceived ability, required effort, or expected difficulty associated with IT adoption.

In the Collected model we gather the original decision constructs into these three more abstract categories, using the original constructs as their indicators or markers. This allowed the explicitly and implicitly similar constructs to be grouped together in broader determinants of intention. This tighter integration is shown in Fig. 2.

## 1.3.7. Predictions

Our purpose was to examine the overall fit of the combined theories to IT adoption decisions made by small business executives. We proposed that combining the theories would provide better explanatory power than using them separately. More importantly, as the theories were more tightly integrated, we believed they would provide a better fit.

## 2. Method

Our investigation used a sample survey methodology. This required that we developed an in-depth (theoretically “grounded”) questionnaire and used it to sample from the US business population.

## 2.1. Identification of sample

The population of US “small” businesses was defined as those organizations having between 20 and 500 employees. In terms of the upper bound, this categorization is consistent with the US ‘small business administration’ (SBA), which defines small businesses based on ‘standard industrial classification’ (SIC) codes, most of which employ 500 as an upper limit. American Business Disk, a commercial database produced by American Business Information, of Omaha, Nebraska, was the source of data on firm contacts. If multiple locations were listed, we selected headquarters addresses, because most decisions about adopting a web site for the firm would be there rather than at a branch office.

## 2.2. Instrumentation

## 2.2.1. Elicitation and pilot studies

Initially, an elicitation study $[4]$ was performed to identify salient consequences, referents, and resources/obstacles pertinent to the TPB. Interview responses were elicited from 26 small business executives in a deliberately varied set of industries throughout the southwest USA. Each executive was either the primary decision maker for IT or an executive who played an important part in making the IT decisions.

A series of open-ended questions was asked about: the advantages/disadvantages (salient consequences within A) of adopting a web presence; the obstacles (salient resources within PBC) which might stop the firm from having a web presence; and the individuals or groups (salient referents within SN) who might approve or disapprove of the firm creating a web presence.

Content analysis was used to identify the most frequent responses. Two different raters individually coded them. An initial test showed that the two raters were in 93% agreement in their identification of the different categories. They later met to resolve rating differences and reach a consensus on the final categories. Table 1 shows the final specifications of perceived consequences, social referents, and obstacles to adopting a web presence with the percentage of respondents from the elicitation study giving that response. Although this information was not used in any hypothesis-testing, it was useful for descriptive purposes. It also supported one of the basic suppositions we had regarding this form of IT: there would be a wide variety of perceptions regarding how adopting a web site could be beneficial or detrimental to a firm.

Based on the context-specific guidelines given by Ajzen and Fishbein and Ajzen, we developed TPB questions for intention (BI), attitude (A), SN, and PBC. Following the same theoretical guidelines, our behavioral belief $(bb_{i})$ , evaluation $(ev_{i})$ , normative belief $(nb_{j})$ , motivation to comply $(mc_{j})$ , control belief $(cb_{k})$ , and perceived power $(pp_{k})$ items were developed to reflect the specific content of the salient cognitions identified in the elicitation study. Items for perceived usefulness (U) and perceived EOU determinants of the TAM were based on previous work by Davis et al. [18] and phrased to reflect the IT of interest. We did not measure actual behavior.

After the questionnaire was finalized, it was piloted with 10 small business executives from a city in the southwestern United States. Several changes were made to the final instrument, based on comments and suggestions from pilot participants; these included the rewording of several questions and the elimination of IT jargon and acronyms.

## 2.3. Main procedures

The final instrument was mailed to 1000 companies that met our criteria for size, randomly selected from the commercial database. A cover letter and a postage-paid return envelope were included with the questionnaire. As an incentive, participants were asked to include a business card with the questionnaire when they returned it if they would like to receive a list of Internet addresses where they could obtain free software on several topics. Although returned surveys were anonymous (to ensure frank and honest responses), the business card was necessary for sending the software. ## 2.3.1. Descriptive statistics

The median age of the executives who returned the survey was 46 years (S.D. = 10.3). The median size of their business was 142.5 employees. The median time the executive had been with his or her firm was 11 years, with a median time in their present position of 5.5 years. Eighty-one percent of those in the sample had an undergraduate or graduate degree. Medians were reported because these variables were positively skewed.

Executives with various job titles participated. Presidents, CEOs, COOs, and vice presidents accounted for 54% of the usable questionnaires, and information systems managers and general managers accounted for another 20%. Of the 156 respondents, 123 were male, 31 were female and 2 left this information blank. The area of expertise or functional background of each of the respondents varied considerably. Management, marketing/promotion sales, MIS/computer science, and finance were the areas cited most often. A wide variety of industries were represented in the sample, including manufacturing, service/sales, government, retail, banking, and medical.

Sixty-four of the 156 sampled executives reported that their business already had a presence on the web. As the TPB and the TAM are models of intentions or decisions about future action, only questionnaires from the 92 executives whose small businesses had not yet adopted a web presence were candidates for our analysis. Five firms were also excluded because they had more than 500 employees, bringing the final sample size to 87. Given our final sample size, the a priori power we had of detecting the effects of proposed determinants on the decision to adopt a web presence were: 23% chance for a small effect size (r = 0.10), 88% chance for a medium effect size (r = 0.30), and a 99% chance for a large effect size (r = 0.50) [14]. Demographic characteristics were compared between the firms that already had a web presence and those that did not. Independent t-tests showed no difference in terms of respondent tenure (with the firm and in the current job), business age, education level, and age. However, companies that already had a web presence tended to be larger (t = 2.97, p < 0.01). In keeping with our opening arguments, this is consistent with findings that smaller firms may not adopt new technology as quickly as larger firms, even when the IT does not require a substantial amount of capital.

## 2.3.2. Individual decision authority

Both the TPB and the TAM are theories about individual decision processes. Therefore, additional data about the respondent's influence over the web presence decision were collected to make sure that this form of IT adoption fit basic theoretical assumptions. We asked “how much does your firm’s decision to have a web presence depend on your personal opinion? …%.” The average reported influence was 69%. If a respondent had answered 0% to this question, their data would have been dropped from the analyses, but none did. Based on the job titles and the decision authority data, the assumptions of the theories were well matched.

## 2.3.3. Measures

The SN measurement was positioned on our instrument immediately after, and in the same formatted “block” of intention items at the bottom of one page of the questionnaire. SN also had an abnormally high correlation with intention $r = 0.74, p < 0.01$ relative to two different meta-analyses of this relationship involving over one hundred investigations [39,44]. From a comparison of sixteen different studies performed over a 5 year period, Ajzen also reported that the range of correlations between intention and SN fell between 0.14 and 0.70. Although this supports our proposals about the importance of social considerations, it also appears that there may be common method variance between intention and SN measures. The correlation between these two variables was inflated because subjects could have taken this as an inadvertent cue that they were responding to questions about the same cognitive content [23].

To assess the construct validity of the rest of the instrument, a factor analysis was performed on the intention (BI), attitude (A), PBC, perceived usefulness (U), and perceived EOU items, which are all formulated by the TPB or TAM as direct inputs into an IT-related decision. Using squared multiple correlations as communalities, oblique rotation, and the parallel analysis criterion for determining factorial complexity $[32]$ , five distinct factors emerged that were consistent with a priori expectations about each of these five constructs (i.e. each item had large, positive loadings only on the construct they were developed to measure). This indicated that the individual items had discriminant validity between the two theories. Table 2 contains the loadings for all items.

## 2.3.5. Analytic approach

Each of the separate and combined models was tested using structural equation modeling (SEM), which is appropriate when paths among multiple independent (exogenous) and dependent (endogenous) variables are specified a priori [28]. When models are contained or “nested” within one another, SEM techniques allow tests for improvement in model fit of the more elaborate model versus the simpler model nested within it. Such is the case for evaluating possible improvements in fit for a series of models that would start with the TAM and the TPB (separately), and move to the Collected model.

More specifically, we applied SEM using maximum likelihood techniques to the covariance matrices of all the TPB and TAM measures. Exogenous variables were allowed to be correlated, which is standard SEM practice [31]. This single indicator procedure involves setting a path of 1 for connecting the latent variable to its sole observed variable (e.g. the latent construct PBC to the total observed score on PBC), and setting the error variance for each indicator equal to its observed variance multiplied by one minus its reliability, i.e. the error variance in each total score was set to the proportion of unreliable variance, and true score variance was set equal to the proportion of reliable variance [29]. This technique helped to minimize the ratio of estimated parameters to independent observations. If we had used item-level instead of score-level indicators, that ratio would have been nearly 1 in our sample, overtaxing the data and violating most rules of thumb regarding the appropriate amount of data needed to estimate complex SEM models (e.g. [10]). It also reduced the dominance of the measurement model over the more important and more relevant structural model [25]. Furthermore, it avoided the serious violations of multivariate normality often exhibited in datasets where each survey or questionnaire item is used as a separate indicator of a latent construct. These violations render many of the standard goodness-of-fit indices (GFIs) to be uninterpretable or even misleading [11]. Finally, Netemeyer et al. [38] showed that this technique produced virtually identical estimates of structural parameters as the multi-indicator approach, while conforming better to the distributional assumptions.

## 2.3.6. Estimation and fit indices

When evaluating the correspondence between a model and data, SEM methodologists strongly recommend tracking a variety of fit indices (e.g. [26,33]). Therefore, we have reported the $\chi^{2}$ lack of fit test (while recognizing its drawbacks), the GFI, the adjusted GFI (AGFI: which penalizes the GFI for lack of parsimony), the normed fit index (NFI: [9]), and the comparative fit index (CFI: [8]), all of these range from zero (complete lack of fit) to one (perfect fit). We also report the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), which has a confidence interval that provides for tests of “adequate” or “close” fit (e.g. [20]). As models provide better fit to the data, the RMSEA approaches zero.

Finally, in each model involving SN, we allowed an error covariance to be estimated between its indicator and the measure of BI. This was compelled by the placement of the SN measure in the same location on our survey as the BI measures. In essence, this statistical method allowed us to adjust for potential common method bias stemming from the physical contiguity of these two measures. It had the effect of statistically reducing the estimated substantive relationship between SN and BI.

## 4. Discussion

We analyzed a series of models that used a “combine and conquer” theoretical approach to help understand a timely and important decision made by members of an understudied population in IT research. We found progressive improvements in fit as the TPB and TAM theories became more integrated, leading to an excellent fit with a Collected model. In that theoretical combination, small business executives adopt this particular form of IT (a web site) because of its anticipated benefits and the social approval associated with doing so. Expected difficulties in acquiring or implementing this type of IT were clearly distinct from other decision-making cognitions, but they did not contribute to the eventual adoption intention.

## 4.1. Theoretical implications

A basic implication from this study is that there is something to be gained by bringing the TAM and the TPB together. First, the Appended model, which essentially combined the TAM and the TPB, performed better than either model individually as indicated by the change in $\chi^{2}$ . However, the Hybrid model offers a richer, but more complex explanation of the ultimate decision, as well as of the decision determinants. Furthermore, the Hybrid model fits better than the Appended model. This finding pushed for a fuller and tighter integration of the TPB and TAM than had been attempted previously. That is, our data showed that the more interwoven Hybrid model improves on (a) how well the TPB and TAM do when they are studied separately, and (b) how well those theories do when they are merely put together in a rudimentary (additive) manner. It sets the stage for the even greater level of abstraction (which also means more comprehensive integration) present in the Collected model.

What is gained by the Collected model is not necessarily richer than the TPB, but it does highlight the broadly defined, conceptual overlap of the TAM and TPB and gives rise to the idea that there are three “fundamental” classes of cognitions or decision parameters when individuals are considering IT adoption: anticipated positive/negative consequences, social approval/disapproval, and perceived ease or difficulty in enacting/implementing the decision. The social component augments the TAM and has been recognized by the latest extension of that theory, TAM2.

In the Collected model, only the paths from anticipated satisfaction $\rightarrow$ BI and social (dis)approval $\rightarrow$ BI were significant, with social (dis)approval $\rightarrow$ BI having the highest structural coefficient. As small business executives consider adopting a web presence, their decision is being made by themselves, but it is also being made for the eventual use of others. We have argued that this characteristic of strategic choices, as opposed to personal choices, would bolster the importance of social expectations in the decision process. Our data fit well with that argument.

## 4.2. Practical and research implications

Reversing the emphasis somewhat can help to explain why executives in small firms fail to adopt what might be seen as clearly helpful and even essential forms of IT. Our findings suggest that this reticence does not stem from expected difficulty or constraints (PBC or EOU) but is because they do not see, or are not aware of, clear anticipated benefits, and they do feel social pressure to bring the IT into their firms.

Further inspection of the normative beliefs can lend some insight into this (lack of) social pressure, by identifying whose approval/disapproval could be being considered by the small business executives.

There were strong correlations between adoption intention and normative belief (nb $_{j}$ ) for nearly every internal and external social group identified as salient to the executives (r = 0.56, 0.51, 0.41, and 0.49 for customers, top management, the internal IT unit, and other employees, respectively; all p < 0.01), except for the small business's competitors (r = 0.01, p > 0.10). In the last case, approval or disapproval is perhaps a confusing idea, in that one might want to do exactly the opposite of what competitors expect.

Anticipated benefit or satisfaction was also an important contributor to the web site adoption decision. Again, more detailed analysis of the behavioral beliefs can provide insight into what small business executives think they can get from having a web presence. Each of the correlation coefficients between the behavioral beliefs (bb $_{i}$ ) and BI was reviewed. The correlation analysis showed that small business executives think of the benefits of a web presence mainly in social (especially customer) contact terms. Separate beliefs that a web presence would enhance the small business' industry image, promote its products or services, improve relations with customers/clients, improve communication with vendors, help keep pace with the competition, generate new business and break down geographical barriers to business transactions were all significantly correlated with executives' adoption intentions (p < 0.05). These findings are consistent with the emphasis that organizations are placing today on customers. In contrast, beliefs about internal matters of security, time, maintenance, and cost were not significantly related to the final adoption intention. This finding regarding cost and maintenance is not consistent with the findings of Auger and Gallaugher for companies that had already created a sales web presence. This supports their claim that the belief systems for previous and potential adopters are not necessarily the same.

Furthermore, regardless of how our models are parameterized, control, effort, or EOU cognitions are seemingly inconsequential in this context of adopting a web site. Indeed, none of the control beliefs $(cb_{k})$ were significantly correlated with intention, indicating that none of the resources (skills, time, financial) by those items was important in final decision-making.

Although resource constraints might be expected to be salient in small businesses, it may be possible that the obstacles to the adoption of a web presence are overshadowed by its instrumentality or potential usefulness to the firm's customers, as well as the social pressure to follow other businesses. In other words, pressures to keep up with the competition and promote service to customers may override any obstacles; firms may believe that it is important to have a web presence and that any obstacles can be overcome. The lack of impact of expected difficulty might also reflect the changing nature of web technology. Inexpensive off-the-shelf software and books are available to guide someone through the development of web sites or the firm may choose to outsource the development fairly cheaply.

## 4.3. Limitations

As with all empirical research, this study has potential limitations on its external and internal validity. First, all of the data were collected using self-report scales from a single executive in each small business. This warrants some caution because common method variance may account for some of the results and it has been cited as one of the stronger criticisms of tests of theories with the structure of TAM or TPB. However, our factor analytic data regarding convergent and discriminant validity do not support the presence of a strong common methods factor. Furthermore, the null effect of any of the difficulty-related constructs (e.g. PBC, EOU) on intention, make it clear that common methods variance was not spuriously inflating all relationships among the self-report measures. Finally, it was not the baseline level, but the pattern of correlations that underlay our most important finding—that the decision models provided better explanatory power as they are combined in more highly integrated ways.

Second, the findings are limited to small businesses in the US that employ between 20 and 500 employees. Even though this range is broader than several other studies, it excludes very small businesses (employing less than 20 people). Many of our sampled small businesses already had a web presence, and those that did had a higher mean number of employees. There may be some distinction between IT decision-making in those firms that employ between 400 and 500 employees and those that employ only 20–100. Furthermore, the fact that all of the businesses were from the US presents a limitation on the external validity of our findings, since small businesses in other countries may not necessarily resemble those in the US.

Third, the lack of discriminant validity for SN was an unexpected limitation of the study. However, by allowing an error covariance to be estimated between the SN indicator and the measure of BI, we were able to adjust for potential bias coming from the physical placement of the measures.

Fourth, we were not able to measure the actual behavior while adopting a web presence. Due to guaranteed anonymity of the participants, we were not able to match responses with the actual companies and go back later to see if the actual company did, in fact, establish a web presence.

Finally, the relatively small sample size is a limitation that limited statistical power. However, the participants differed from past theory-testing research in the TPB and TAM stream in that they involved working executives making real, consequential decisions for their own small businesses. Several facts make the numbers appear valid: the context was meaningful; the chaff rate was low (no surveys were thrown out for carelessness or incomplete sections); and the psychometric qualities of our measures were sound. These all suggest that the participants were thoughtfully engaged in answering our questions. Furthermore, the study was not limited to a specific sector of the economy, as has been the case in prior research (e.g. [19]).

## 5. Conclusion

This study identified salient advantages, disadvantages, social considerations, and obstacles that small business executives see in adopting one form of IT—a web presence—for their firms. Our results indicated that a Collected model that represents the basic categories of cognitions from TPB and TAM, provided a better explanation of this understudied decision. In this Collected model, it appears that the improved social contact (with customers, vendors, etc.) facilitated by the Internet is the driving force behind web site adoption, or that no apparent improvement in such contact underlies the hesitance of small business to go on-line.
