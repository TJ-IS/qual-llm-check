# Diversification and performance of Japanese IT subsidiaries: a resource-based view

Michael R. Wade $^{a,*}$ , Jane I. Gravill $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Schulich School of Business, York University, Toronto, Canada

$^{b}$ Richard Ivey School of Business, University of Western Ontario, London, Canada N6A 3K7

Received 16 June 2001; accepted 1 January 2002

## Abstract

This paper examines the relationship between corporate diversification and performance for Japanese subsidiaries in the information technology (IT) industry. In particular, it reports on our study which drew on the resource-based view of the firm to make the distinction between related and unrelated diversification. Data for more than 5 years were used to test our hypotheses. The results suggest that related subsidiaries out-perform unrelated subsidiaries on a number of dimensions including: performance, survival, and employee productivity growth. The study also found support for resource-based arguments suggesting that related diversification in knowledge-based (tertiary) industries, such as IT, plays a more important factor in firm success than in primary or secondary industries.

## 1. Introduction

Research into the determinants and effects of international corporate diversification has a rich history in management disciplines. Most of this research has been focussed on large multinational firms in North America and Western Europe $[12,51,41]$ . Comparatively little effort has been spent in studying the effects of corporate diversification in economies outside these areas, such as Latin America, Asia, and Eastern Europe $[2,24,33,27]$ . Also, relatively little research has been conducted to isolate the effects of diversification on individual industries $[61]$ . The information technology (IT) industry is one that has been underexplored, despite its size and importance to global trade. This study examined the link between corporate diversification and performance for Japanese IT firms.

The IT sector, comprising the telecommunications and computer industries, is an important area of global trade. In 1997, the global market for IT products and services was US\$ 1.5 trillion, or about 4% of global GDP. Of this amount, telecommunications products and services accounted for approximately US\$ 600 billion and the computer industry accounted for US\$ 900 billion. The IT sector has been growing worldwide at a rate of 18% since 1990 [31].

While the IT sector may be an important component of world trade, it is not well understood. Relatively few studies have considered the global IT industry, fewer still from the Japanese perspective $[26,67]$ . This paper contributes to the understanding of global IT by studying the link between international diversification and performance of Japanese IT subsidiaries.

## 2. Diversification

There are two basic arguments for corporate diversification. First, it erects a ‘buffer of control’ around the firm, providing a cushion against business downturns and other setbacks, particularly in intense and highly competitive environments such as the IT industry $[19,60]$ . Second, it allows for managed and predictable flows of resources between parts of the firm $[5,43,48,20]$ . However, it comes with a price: the more diversified a firm, the more difficult it is to transfer resources among its parts: this, of course, has negative performance consequences.

Much of the early work on diversification focussed on corporate structure. This was based primarily on transaction cost theory, which argues that the minimization of search and coordination costs between partners in a diversified firm allows hierarchical structures to become more efficient than market structures, under certain circumstances. Yet, the evidence to support these arguments in an international setting has been mixed $[40,70]$ .

Some have argued that transaction cost theory does not fully account for variations in performance among diversified firms $[11,56]$ . In order to understand this relationship, researchers began to compare corporate structure with ‘type’ of diversification and examine ways in which patterns of diversification and structure affected corporate financial performance $[58]$ . An important finding was that firms with interrelated business portfolios appeared to out-perform firms with unrelated portfolios, regardless of the type of corporate structure adopted.

## 2.1. Related versus unrelated diversification

The ‘extent’ of diversification was found to be an important factor in the performance of diversified firms, along with, or even apart from, corporate structure. Recent work has focussed on comparing the performance of firms with ‘related’ versus those with ‘unrelated’ diversification $[6,35]$ .

Broadly speaking, related diversification occurs when a parent and its subsidiary operate in congruent areas, while unrelated diversification occurs when a parent and its subsidiary operate in dissimilar areas. A manufacturer that purchases a foreign supplier would be an example of related diversification. A holding company that invests in firms from a variety of different industries would be an example of unrelated diversification. Whether or not a parent and a subsidiary are ‘related’ is essentially an empirical question. Three approaches to operationalizing relatedness have been used in the literature: categorical measures, SIC classification measures, and resource proxies $[18]$ .

Categorical measures are built on typological work $[71]$ and typically involve classification of a firm in terms of one of several characteristic types of diversification. Standard industry classifications (SICs) are the most commonly used determinants in the literature. These systems position a firm on a scale that indicates its relative degree of diversification based on the difference between the parent and subsidiary's SIC code. Proxy systems have been used in situations where categories are hard to apply or where SIC data are not available, such as when diversification occurs within firm boundaries. Such systems allocate a proxy to the underlying resources of each firm, such as R&D or advertising intensity, and compare parent and subsidiary on these measures $[28]$ .

The results of research comparing the effect of related and unrelated diversification on corporate performance have been mixed $[29,53]$ . Some studies found that portfolio similarity had negligible impact on corporate performance, while other factors, such as industry structure and market share, had much greater effects $[44,25]$ . Other studies were unable to find significant differences between firms with related and unrelated business portfolios $[1]$ . A contingency relationship between relatedness (similarity) and performance has also been found. A number of studies have shown that industry structure $[15,34]$ and firm characteristics play important roles in explaining performance, while ‘relatedness’ has little effect net of them. Most of this research has been conducted on the parent company, and very little has studied the effect of related and unrelated diversification on the subsidiary. Overall, results have been divided on the significance of relatedness of the businesses of a firm $[30]$ . No studies were found that looked at the effect of related and unrelated diversification on performance for IT firms, despite the importance of this industry in global trade.

There are also shortcomings of the research on corporate diversification and performance. One is that performance is normally only measured at the level of the parent firm. However, given the relative size difference between parent and subsidiary in many corporate portfolios, any performance impact is more likely to be realized at the level of the subsidiary. This argument is supported by two points: first, wider diversification suggests the presence of fewer competitive advantage-yielding resources that the parent firm can transfer to the subsidiary; second, a given resource will lose more value when transferred from the parent to the subsidiary (or vice versa) in markets that are less similar.

Another problem is that past work has used data in a single time period, often from cross-sectional surveys. Thus, performance or competitive advantage may be measured, but the sustainability of that advantage over time is hard to determine.

## 3. The resource-based view of the firm

Recent conceptual developments in strategic management have focussed on diversification using a resource-based view (RBV) of the firm $[4,68,22]$ . This method views the firm as a bundle of heterogeneous and imperfectly mobile resources. Under the right conditions, the resources allow the firm to achieve sustained competitive advantage. The theory argues that the rationale for diversified organizations ultimately lies in sharing strategic assets and capabilities among units $[37,64,66]$ , rather than simply reducing transactions costs. Related firms can more easily share resources than unrelated firms $[8]$ . In the absence of shared firm-specific strategic assets $[69]$ , a corporation can be expected to perform no better than the sum of its separate businesses $[16,55,65]$ .

The RBV predicts that the performance of related subsidiaries (and, thus, those that are able to share valuable and rare resources) will be superior to that of unrelated subsidiaries. Indeed, several studies have found that a firm's resources serve as the driving force for its diversification strategy [9,52,63,46]. These studies suggest that portfolio relatedness is an important variable in the diversification process. Using the concentric index of diversification [10] as a proxy for relatedness, one study found that narrowly diversified firms received higher rents than widely diversified firms [45].

Other studies found strong support for the link between a firm's base of resources and its diversification strategy in the pharmaceutical and international bearings industries [17]. These results support the resource-based hypothesis that expansion by firms into activities in which they have comparative advantages are most likely to yield rents [50].

A summary of resource-based empirical studies to date show that many support performance advantages for related diversification over unrelated diversification, although the evidence is mixed $[13,36,49,59,62]$ .

The RBV is beginning to be used in IS research $[54]$ . However, most work has focussed on testing relationships between various IT resources and firm performance or competitive advantage $[7,42]$ . No studies were found that focussed on corporate diversification in the IT industry using the resource-based view.

## 4. Hypotheses

The RBV predicts that the possession of resources that are valuable and rare and that can be protected from duplication or sale, may lead a firm to a position of sustained competitive advantage. In the case of diversified firms, those firms that are able to share resources are more likely to succeed than those that are not. Thus, the theory predicts that related diversified firms are likely to perform better over time than those that are unrelated.

Thus,

H1a. The performance of related subsidiaries will be superior to that of unrelated subsidiaries.

H1b. Furthermore, the superior performance of related over unrelated subsidiaries will be sustained over time.

Survival is, perhaps, the ultimate performance measure. In a dynamic and volatile market, like the IT industry, failure is common. The RBV argues that relatedness between parent and subsidiary (resulting in the sharing of valuable and rare resources) protects the subsidiary from loss and ultimately failure in the marketplace.

Thus,

H2. Related subsidiaries are more likely to survive than unrelated subsidiaries.

In order to triangulate among dependent variables, a measure was developed to assess employee productivity in each subsidiary. Resource-based arguments suggest that the sharing of resources between parent and subsidiary help the employees of related subsidiaries to be more productive than those of unrelated subsidiaries.

Thus,

H3. The employee productivity growth of related subsidiaries will be superior to that of unrelated subsidiaries.

Since there is strong practitioner interest, understanding the performance of IT subsidiaries in isolation is valuable. However, from a research perspective, it is also interesting to compare the results from IT subsidiaries to those from other industry categories, such as construction, manufacturing, wholesale and retail trade, services, financial services, and real estate, etc. Conventional wisdom suggests that sustained competitive advantage in complex and/or knowledge-based industries would rely on a higher proportion of intangible and tacit resources and capabilities than industries with a lower degree of complexity. Examples of knowledge-based industries would be the financial services industry, the IT industry, and the service industry. Examples of low complexity industries would be primary or secondary industries, such as fishing, mining, forestry, and construction.

Resource-based theory suggests that relatedness would be a more important factor in tertiary (knowledge-based) industries than in primary or secondary industries. For example, in the fishing industry, the fish and the skills and resources used to catch them may be valuable, but they are not rare. Even if they were, they may be available on factor markets or open to imitation or substitution. Resources needed to succeed in the IT sector, by comparison, may rely on very high level (rare) knowledge, and intangible assets, such as patents, which reduce the extent of imitation. Consequently, related firms in the IT sector could benefit from sharing parent firm resources more than subsidiaries in other sectors.

Thus,

H4. The performance of related IT subsidiaries is superior to that of related subsidiaries from other industries.

In order to test this hypothesis, performance of firms in the IT industry were compared to performance of firms in eight other industry categories.

## 5. Methodology

## 5.1. Data

The main source of data for this study was Kaigan Shinshutsu Kigyou Souran, Kuni-Betsu (Japanese Oversees Investment, by country) published by Toyo Keizei, Inc., a Tokyo-based market research firm. This was supplemented by data from publicly available sources, including: Global Vantage (published by Compustat), the Analysts Guide (published by the Daiwa Institute of Research), and Edgar, an online financial database provided by the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). The Toyo Keizei dataset contains data on more than 28,000 Japanese foreign subsidiaries of approximately 5500 Japanese parent companies. The material reported in this dataset are cross-sectional, but the combination of information from three editions creates a longitudinal profile of Japanese firms' activities. The dataset includes a wide variety of demographic and financial data coded at both the parent and subsidiary levels. It is estimated that the data included in the dataset constitute 41% of total Japanese FDI [38].

Although Toyo Keizei collects data on an annual basis, the data used in this study focused only on the years 1994, 1997, and 1999. The remaining data exists in an unstructured and untranslated form, which is inappropriate for our analyses. To date, this large database has been used by only a few researchers (e.g. [3,39,32]). A number of these studies have looked at the relationship between entry mode and performance [47]. Other studies have focussed on Japanese FDI in certain countries, regions, or industries [14].

IT subsidiaries were separated from non-IT subsidiaries by SIC code. Since the data were coded at the subsidiary level, these breakdowns could be relatively precise. A common drawback with studies using data coded at the parent company level is that all subsidiaries of that parent are included in one SIC code. In the case of large Japanese multinationals, subsidiaries might fall under many different SIC codes. For example, in 1995, Fujitsu's IT revenue as a percentage of total revenue was only 67%, NEC's was 45%, Hitachi's was 19% and Toshiba's was 19%. The SIC codes shown in Table 1 (along with corresponding NAICS codes) were selected, based on their coverage of traditional IT areas. Only, those subsidiaries producing goods or providing services in 'front line' technology areas were chosen for use in our study. A clear distinction was made between subsidiaries using technology, such as consumer electronics firms and auto manufacturers, and companies creating technology or on the leading edge of technology. The former were excluded, while the latter were included.

As Table 1 shows, 67% of the sample used in the study were composed of computer related subsidiaries, 21.5% were composed of general telecommunications products and services, while the remaining 11.5% of the subsidiaries consisted of specific telecommunications segments, such as radio, paging, cellular, and satellite networks.

Subsidiaries less than 2 years old were removed from the sample. Previous studies have shown that subsidiary performance is unstable for the 2 years following inception, and therefore, inappropriate for analysis. Since data are compared at equivalent periods for the two groups, external factors, such as economic, political, and cultural factors, are controlled and valid.

## 5.2. Measures

To establish whether a subsidiary is related or unrelated to the parent, it was first necessary to determine the core business of the parent firm. If the core businesses differed, then the two were considered to be unrelated. A firm's core business was generally determined using an SIC code or similar sub-industry classification; however, a number of studies have noted shortcomings of using exclusively SIC-based measures to determine the extent of diversification activities. Here, the SIC code was considered along with a measure of diversification experience to determine whether a parent and subsidiary were related or not. Consideration of only the parent's SIC code might under-appreciate the extent of the parent firm's experience in international markets. For example, a parent may operate in one industry, but invest extensively oversees in another. So, areas in which the parent firm has considerable experience in foreign markets should also be considered part of the parent firm's core business (even if it is in different from the parent's SIC code) [21]. By this expanded measure, fewer subsidiary firms were considered to be unrelated to the parent.

Thus, the rules for “relatedness” were:

\- Subsidiaries that have the same three digit SIC code as the parent are related.

\- Subsidiaries with SIC codes in areas in which the parent has significant international investment experience are also related.

\- Subsidiaries with a different three-digit SIC code from the parent and operating in areas in which the parent has limited international investment experience are unrelated.

Performance in the dataset was given on a three point scale: loss, breakeven, and gain. The use of subjective, perceptual measures of performance is well supported in the literature $[23]$ . Since there were only three levels of performance data in the dataset, analysis of means was considered to be an inappropriate tool. Instead, performance was considered a categorical rather than a continuous variable and a Chi-square statistic was utilized. This is consistent with other authors' conclusions about this dataset. Performance measures exist for all years in the dataset.

Survival has been used by some researchers as a proxy for performance, although other researchers have noted that it is a rough guide at best $[72,57]$ . Survival is a measure of whether the Japanese subsidiary remained an active concern during the reporting period: in this case, between 1994 and 1999. The most common reason for a subsidiary not surviving was that it failed and was closed down. However, alternative explanations do exist, e.g. the subsidiary could have been sold or otherwise disassociated with its Japanese parent, but still survived. A Chi-square test was used to determine whether related subsidiaries survived more often than unrelated subsidiaries.

Employee productivity growth was determined as subsidiary sales growth per employee between the years 1997 and 1999. There was insufficient data to make any determination of employee productivity growth in the years prior to 1997. The function used to measure employee productivity growth is shown by Eq. (1). ## 5.3. Descriptive statistics

Table 2 shows descriptive statistics for the selected IT subsidiaries. No significant differences were found between related and unrelated subsidiaries in size, neither along individual dimensions such as number of employees, subsidiary sales, or capital invested in the subsidiary (using t-tests), nor along an aggregate measure of subsidiary size incorporating all individual dimensions and years (using a MANOVA test). No significant differences were found between the two groups by geographic location or sub-industry classification. The fact that no significant differences existed among the two groups along these dimensions was important to minimize alternative explanations. The two groups were found to differ, however, in age, when grouped in blocks. Related subsidiaries tended to be proportionately younger than unrelated subsidiaries in the dataset.

## 6. Results

Hypothesis 1a (H1a) stated that subsidiaries related to the parent would out-perform those that were unrelated. Hypothesis 1b (H1b) proposed that performance advantages for related subsidiaries were sustained over time. Analysis of the data confirmed superior performance for related subsidiaries for the years 1994, 1997 and 1999 (see Table 3).

Hypothesis 2 (H2) stated that related subsidiaries were more likely to survive than unrelated subsidiaries. The data supported this hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3 (H3) stated that growth in employee productivity over time would be higher in related subsidiaries than in unrelated ones. This hypothesis was also supported by the data. A large absolute difference in employee productivity growth between the two groups (effect size) was found. The mean value for related subsidiaries was 39%, compared with 9% for unrelated subsidiaries. An ANOVA test was performed on the data with subsidiary age and size entered as covariates: results of this test showed a significant differences between the two groups.

Analysis showed that there was a significant performance difference (in 1999) between related and unrelated subsidiaries in two industries. These were the IT and the finance, insurance, and real estate industry (see Table 4). In both cases, related subsidiaries out-performed unrelated subsidiaries. This result supports H4.

Related subsidiaries were found to survive significantly more often than unrelated subsidiaries in 6 of the 10 industries. In no case did unrelated subsidiaries survive significantly more than related subsidiaries. This did not directly support H4. However, the results do support the RBV, because the industries that showed significantly better survival rates for related versus unrelated subsidiaries were those in secondary and tertiary industries. The industries without significant differences between the two groups were either primary industries or industries of relatively low complexity.

No significant differences in employee productivity were found between related and unrelated subsidiaries in any of the industries.

In sum, the data supported three hypotheses, and partially supported the fourth. Subsidiaries that were related to the parent by either sub-industry category (based on SIC code) or by investment experience, were found to perform better over 5 years between 1994 and 1999 and survive longer, than unrelated subsidiaries. Employee productivity growth between the two groups also differed significantly. Related subsidiaries in the IT and finance, insurance, and real estate industries out-performed unrelated subsidiaries, while no difference was found between the two groups in other industries. Relatedness was found to be more important in the performance and survival of firms in tertiary industries, than firms in primary or secondary industries.

## 7. Conclusions and discussion

## 7.1. Implications for researchers

This paper adds to the growing, but contradictory, literature on the effect of (related and unrelated) diversification on corporate performance. Our study suggests that the contradictory evidence may be due to differences in industry; possibly that knowledge-based (tertiary) industries, such as IT, may be more strongly affected by relatedness than primary industries.

In addition, this study makes a number of contributions. One is that performance is measured at the subsidiary rather than the parent company level. Measurement at the subsidiary level is, perhaps, more appropriate. The subsidiary is more likely to be influenced by the parent, than the parent by the subsidiary. As a consequence, relatedness may play a more prominent role in the success or failure of the subsidiary than the parent. Most studies have regarded measurement at the level of the subsidiary as preferable, but data collection difficulties and complexity precluded its use.

Another contribution is to further our understanding of corporate diversification in an international context. There is a paucity of studies that look at diversification across borders, and fewer still from a Japanese perspective. Complexities of language, culture and environment, as well as large costs conspire to keep such research to a minimum. A third contribution stems from the study's focus on the IT industry, which is one of the larger, more prominent and influential industries in the world. Yet our understanding of it is under-developed. The results of this study suggest that diversification relatedness is more important in IT than it is in other industries.

A fourth contribution is that the research supports the use of the resource-based view of the firm as a lens through which to study the relationship between diversification and firm performance.

## 7.2. Implications for managers

This study carries a number of implications for managers. Perhaps the most important is that of diversification relatedness, particularly in the IT industry. The study suggests that IT managers who are considering a diversification strategy are likely to enjoy performance benefits if they have related rather than unrelated investments. The former tended to perform better and survive longer than the latter in the IT industry. Additionally, employee productivity in related subsidiaries appeared to be superior. Conversely, relatedness appeared to be less important in industries that are less knowledge-based.

## 7.3. Limitations

This study contained limitations common to all research using archival data. While the use of a three-level dependent variable to measure performance is unconventional, it has, however, been used in other studies. In order to strengthen the results, alternative dependent measures were used to triangulate the results of the main performance variable. An additional limitation concerns the study's sample. The Kaigan Shinshutsu Kigyou Souran, Kuni-Betsu bases its firm list on the Tokyo Stock Exchange, thus, the sample was constructed only on large, public firms. A third limitation concerns the determination of diversification using SIC codes, which has been criticized in the literature. In order to offset this, our study used an additional measure of diversification based on investment experience. However, better measures of diversification may be preferable. A fourth limitation is in the definition of the IT industry. It is more of an art than a science to decide what is IT and what is not. Here, the IT industry was defined to include the computer and telecommunications industries, and thus, the results are only externally valid for them. A fifth limitation is that the study focused on only one country (Japan), and thus, its generalizability is limited to that context. Finally, the data used in the study covered the time period from 1994 to 1999, and thus, was not able to draw conclusions from recent periods of high IT sector volatility.

## 7.4. Summary

Our study looked at the relationship between corporate diversification and performance at the subsidiary level for a set of Japanese firms in the IT industry investing overseas. It made a distinction between subsidiaries that are similar or related to the parent and those that are dissimilar or unrelated. Results are largely in line with resource-based thinking. The performance of related subsidiaries in the IT sector were found to be superior to that of unrelated subsidiaries over a 6-year-period. Further, related subsidiaries were found to survive more frequently and fail less often.

The study also found that diversification relatedness was a more important factor in the performance of IT subsidiaries than in most other industries. This suggests that the complexity of the IT industry causes it to rely more on knowledge and resource sharing between parent and subsidiary than in other industries. This finding carries implications for managers in IT firms: they should carefully consider diversification efforts.
