# DSS implementation in the UK retail organisations: a GIS perspective

Syed Nasirin $^{a,*}$ , David F. Birks $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Computing, Thames Valley University, Slough SL1 1YG, UK $^{b}$ University of Bath, School of Management, Bath BA2 7AY, UK

Received 12 November 2000; received in revised form 8 August 2001; accepted 24 November 2001

## Abstract

Geographical information systems (GIS) are becoming more prevalent in both day-to-day and strategic decision-making by retailers. Given the array of internal and external databases they use and integrate, and the human and organisational development needed, a most apposite description of how a GIS may be introduced for retailing decision-making is “system implementation”. There is little published work on GIS implementation as applied to retailing. Retailers wishing to understand the critical success factors of implementation cannot assume that studies from other industries can provide simple generalisations. This article presents exploratory case studies developed using a grounded theory approach, reflecting GIS implementation experiences of British retailers. Three major retailers who have already developed and utilised their GIS to varying degrees are used in a case study.

## 1. Introduction

Geographical information systems (GIS) have rapidly developed as input to decision support systems (DSS) employed by retailers; they have become vital to support decisions made by retailers for both operational day-to-day such as sales promotion activities and long-term strategic decision-making (e.g. the location of new stores). GIS have developed to support an array of marketing mix decisions based upon an awareness of where customers reside in relation to the store and an understanding of their purchasing behaviour. Tonks [5] argued that the relative advantage of a GIS lay in its ability to locate new customers through its discriminatory power. In other words, the GIS is capable of merging numerous retailers' internally-generated databases with databases produced by 'external' sources (e.g. geodemographic databases at a national level). The use of these 'external' databases allows a far more powerful analysis of existing internally-generated databases. The system is also able to produce more effective presentations of the database analyses [40,41]. Moreover, Sleight [33] has pointed out that, "The ability of geodemographics to link to research data is undoubtedly one of the strengths of the technique". He further supported his point by terming GIS “a common thread” to link to various marketing mix activities together. These overlaying features thus create a variety of analytical models.

The availability of geodemographic databases at a national level, for example, in the UK, the National Readership Survey (NRS), is also a major factor in changing retailers' desires to utilise GIS. Databases are also becoming more portable (from mainframe to CD-ROM) at relatively lower price. Such data would be vastly more expensive if retailers sought to generate it themselves. As competition faced by retailers becomes more intense, as signified by the publication of league tables showing market share and profitability, the need has grown for retailers to understand their existing and potential customers. The need to analyse and interpret markets using creative and accessible technology has grown. This helps to explain why retailers are moving their focus towards GIS technology. Besides fundamental store location decisions, GIS has been employed by retailers in all sorts of marketing mix decisions, such as direct mailing and sales forecasting $[25]$ . In short, GIS are established as giving competitive advantage, enhancing organisational planning, and decision-making in a wide array of functions. There are two key advantages of using GIS:

1. magnificent discriminatory power, i.e. the ability to describe and track individual consumer behaviour in great detail, and

2. the ability to represent characteristics of customer attitudes and behaviour geographically and visually.

There is no doubt that the use of GIS has increased in the retail sector. In understanding the phenomena of GIS adoption there, it appears that the primary focus has been on its applications and benefits, with limited emphasis upon the issues around its implementation $[21]$ , which is a vital issue for retailers who wish to utilise its well-publicised applications and benefits. A GIS cannot just be ‘installed’ like a piece of software; the retailer should be aware of many issues to achieve success. The implementation of a GIS has many similarities to the implementation of DSS, many of which have never reached the benefits claimed when the system was purchased. This begs the question of what happened in the period from conception to full employment of the system $[3,6,13,16,22]$ . The reasons why studies dealing specifically with GIS implementation are significant may be epitomised as follows:

1. Despite the popular and increasing significance of GIS technology in retail organisations, little effort has been made to identify the successful GIS implementation process or individual factors of success. Most research efforts are not found in retailing but in other more developed areas, e.g. local governments. It appears that the primary focus has been on the system applications with lesser concern on implementation. Some of the “received wisdom” about issues leading to successful GIS implementation needs to be critically evaluated. Managers accountable for GIS implementation cannot presume that the significant factors of success (and failure) are equally significant in retailing [34,39].

2. There is little research on GIS implementation in retailing in the United Kingdom. The insights from other geographical settings may be generalisable but, given the specific competitive nature of the UK retail market and the nature and quality of databases available in the UK, these extraneous factors be important.

The purpose of this paper is to describe the characteristics of successful (and failed) GIS implementation processes as experienced by UK retailers. This article presents the results of exploratory case studies developed through the utilisation of a grounded theory approach. Three major retailers were studied. The cases reflect the experiences of market leaders and retailers that struggle to emulate their success.

## 2. The present state of GIS implementation research

GIS implementation has only been studied within the past 15 years. Most studies have focused on applications, such as site decisions and spatial analysis $[12,15,24,28]$ , and determining appropriate promotional media, e.g. door-to-door distribution of leaflets through postcode clustering $[4,31]$ . Another area of development has focused on the benefits of the system, e.g. doing things that are hard to quantify in cash terms but seem better because efficiency is likely to be improved [14,20], or analysing competitive threats. The relative youth of this field limits the amount of implementation studies that can be found (although it has been applied in various sectors within retailing).

## 3. Theoretical framework

The core of our theoretical framework (Fig. 1) is guided by the systems development life cycle (SDLC) [7–9]. This is used to identify and describe the interrelated activities that occur in the implementation process. The traditional SDLC arose out of a need in the 1960s to provide structure to the implementation of large software systems. However, this life cycle presents the process design in a somewhat static “pipeline” manner. In reality, the true design process is iterative, but the SDLC helps by setting boundaries for our study [37]. There are two key environments: external and internal. The external environment refers to forces outside an organisation that may shape a GIS (e.g. competition, pace of GIS development and availability of independent data sets). The internal environment refers to organisational issues that may shape the implementation. All these strategies can be, and are, used to implement GIS. The framework was based upon common themes found in GIS implementation in local governments. The rationale for selecting them was that this area has been much more developed and represents a stable environment. Examining studies into this context helps to draw parallels and aids in understanding the significant issues [11]. Three time stages of GIS implementation activities (prior to the implementation, during, and after) were utilised to establish a tentative structure for the retailer case studies.

## 3.1. Choice among the emerging themes

Based on our review of local authority GIS implementation, we concluded that there are enormous numbers of successful implementation factors. The relative significance of each factor could not, however, be readily generalised and built into a significant focus of investigation in the chosen case studies. These factors were subject to many location and organisational factors. In summary, the initial review of GIS implementation in local authorities revealed that more attention should be given to organisational aspects of the implementation.

These findings concur with Campbell's [10] study on successful GIS implementation projects in the USA and Kraemer et al.'s [18] study on GIS in general. However, if a decision is made to choose one or more of these issues for further investigation, a major drawback lies in being able to capture the entire “map” of the process. The implementation process consists of a set of concepts for organising data. The “map” of GIS implementation can only be captured by not leaving out any of these driving forces. Key to the implementation process framework is a set of principles that provide a foundation for setting aims and monitoring progress.

## 4. Research design

The research design was driven by its exploratory nature. Conducting case studies allowed basic frameworks to evolve to a point of being able to “map” the implementation process. Through contrasting processes in the three chosen case organisations, issues to pursue and decision-makers to question became apparent. By adopting a grounded theory approach, this evolution of issues and decision-makers allowed a “grasp” rather than “measurement” of the informants. It helped us appreciate the wider organisational forces and the unanticipated influences of the phenomena, leading to the formation of grounded theories $[23,44]$ .

## 4.1. Sampling decisions

The basis for selecting the informants was based on Glaser and Strauss's [1] concept of theoretical sampling [29]. With a focus of producing a “GIS implementation process map”, the selected sample was not pre-specified but occurred inductively, as driven by the conceptual demands of the study. Three organisations were selected to supply the needed data, reflecting various approaches to GIS implementation. Table 1 summarises their profiles. The unit of analysis for this study was a series of organisational activities taking place over time: the entire GIS implementation process.

## 4.2.1. Stage 1

It quickly became apparent that only major retailers were employing GIS. Of these, it proved extremely difficult to gain access to explore how they went through the process. The process started through a promising meeting with two representatives from one of the leading GIS vendors in the UK market; they had many contacts with leading retailers. A series of follow-up calls were made through letters and telephone calls but unfortunately no response was received during a period of around 3 months. We decided not to continue waiting and to develop other means of approaching the informants.

## 4.2.2. Stage 2

Fortunately, a GIS user conference organised by the Environmental Systems Research Institute (ESRI) was held in London. The organisers gave us the opportunity to present the benefits of this study to attendees. After identifying organisations and decision-makers from the conference list, a focused brief was developed and presented. This led to a follow-up on-site meeting with a GIS expert in North Tyneside Council (NTC), Newcastle, United Kingdom. Although not retailers, NTC could provide a pilot case study, which was helpful in developing a basic framework, in finding issues to explore, and in deciding how the issues could be tackled with different decision-makers. Overall, it provided valuable lesson in interviewing and analysing data, and in helping to develop a strong credibility for the case development.

## 4.2.3. Stage 3

Following this, issues required for the interview were developed, focusing on GIS implementation process in UK local authorities. A set of key topics was sent to NTC, giving the GIS expert time to prepare for the meeting. This opportunity allowed a test of knowledge and methods of eliciting data, which had been pre-specified, to some extent. Although this organisation may be considered “infant” in GIS implementation (in comparison to other local authorities), the data supplied was more than sufficient in terms of understanding the development of inter-relationship of issues in GIS implementation. At the end of the session in NTC, reference was made to another GIS expert in Gloucestershire County Council (GCC), which was then approached through the same process as NTC. A speedy response was received from the IS manager and a new set of topics was sent to Gloucester to let the IS manager prepare for the meeting. Many new insights were shared with the IS manager in the meeting.

These pilot studies in local authorities were relatively unconstrained by internal or external validity. It was a matter of testing the topics, developing questions, building up interviewing skills, and developing empathy and credibility with GIS experts in retail organisations.

## 4.2.4. Stage 4

However, it was still proving difficult to gain access to any retail organisations that had coped with GIS implementation. Fortunately, access was gained to a membership list of individuals belonging to Intergraph, one of the leading GIS vendors. The names of individuals who were members were sent a brief introductory letter on the research topic, hoping to persuade them to agree to an initial meeting. As a result, three major retailers come forward, expressing their willingness to co-operate with the research. Given the commercial sensitivity of their processes and their relative success, we have labelled them Firms A, B, and C. The first in-depth interview and collection of in-company archive material occurred in that order. Prior to the interviews, a set of topics was sent to the managers, allowing them time to consider and reflect, rather than having to respond in an ad hoc fashion. Most of the subsequent interviews were on a one to one basis except with Firm C where, on one occasion, a group interview was performed. The approach to conducting interviews and the topics were restructured and rephrased as the case studies progressed. The questions were open-ended and each “revelation” was incorporated into an issue to be probed in the next interview. Choices of informants and interaction were also driven by the intention to expose any similarities and dissimilarities, providing the opportunity for comparing and understanding key relationships in the setting. Miles and Huberman [26] have termed this conceptual-driven sequential sampling.

## 4.3. Case data analyses and verification

The data were analysed across the three sites to detect similarities and differences. Within Firm A, the iterative approach to analysing data was more open-ended than for the others. The focus was on the development of core and sub-themes through the content analysis. The data was categorised. Once all the data were analysed, the core and sub-themes were reorganised. These themes became key candidates for a set of common concepts that linked with a number of related sub-concepts. These preliminary sets of themes and sub-themes guided the second and third case studies, allowing the process of data analysis to be more definite.

Following the “constant comparative analysis method”, Firms B and C data were contrasted with those of Firm A. This analysis employed Miles and Huberman’s method for across-site contrast and pattern clustering that involved matrix displays. The same process that was employed by Firm A was followed for Firms B and C. The data was first grouped into the initial concepts and sub-concepts generated by Firm A. It was found that the preliminary concepts generated by Firm A, however, did not necessarily conform to the results emerging from Firms B and C. Evaluating this non-conformity led to significant clarification, in that an emerging theoretical framework forced reconsideration of some of Firm A’s core and sub-concepts.

The data was validated by interviewing other respondents using the same information gathered in earlier interviews at the same organisation. Key aspects of previous interviews were reviewed by asking the same questions of different people. For example, in the case of Firm A, the first interview was conducted with the GIS project manager and his data were transcribed, forming a “draft” that served as a framework for the next interview. The resultant framework was empirically valid, because it could account for the unique data of each site and could help to generalise patterns across them. It also corroborated the interpretations made by asking several respondents exposed to the same experiences in the same department $[17,32,35]$ . Emerging concepts were checked by examining them across participants and with triangulation of methods (interviews and observation) which further served to strengthen the emerging themes, sub-themes, and concepts. This analysis forced the confrontation of emerging explanation with possible alternatives.

## 5. Cross-case findings

Several core and sub-themes emerged from the cross-case analyses that employed the SDLC as a basic conceptual framework.

## 5.1. Forces to GIS implementation

The analysis started by determining the internal factors that acted as “forces” towards GIS implementation. The five key forces were:

1. An ever-increasing sophistication of site research models (one of the major strategic uses of GIS). There was evidence of prolonged frustration at the time taken and the limitations of “conventional” or PC-based GIS approaches.

2. The desire to use graphical representations of site research models to empower decision-makers. The non-graphical outcomes of traditional site research models, the inability to represent the results on a map, were seen as a major limitation.

3. Crudely managed internal and external data sets.

4. An increasing amount, complexity, and competing values of both internal and external data sets.

5. Continuous internal pressure to lead or maintain present organisational position in the market by senior management and investors.

There were also some external forces that shaped GIS implementation. Collectively, they combined to pressure senior management to lead or maintain their present organisational market position. The three key external forces were:

1. The intensification of competition within the industry—with associated expectations,

2. An awareness that their close competitors were increasing their investment in GIS, and

3. An awareness of the world-wide retailers' attention to GIS, particularly in Europe and North America, linked with a mushrooming of GIS data and GIS vendors (this facilitated the evolution of the technology).

## 5.2. Implementation planning

Most implementation planning tasks are initiated and developed by a site research departmental head or the GIS project manager $[30]$ . GIS implementation was initiated by such managers in response to the aforementioned forces and to realise opportunities in employing the system. Through effective implementation planning, they contended that the scope of GIS benefits could be broadened and could continue for a long time with less organisational trauma. They tried to arrange the implementation tasks in a logical sequence and to schedule resources for each activity. In strengthening their plans, Firms A and C research departmental heads developed a dialogue with GIS specialists from non-competing organisations. In contrast, Firm B proceeded without having any help from others (a “trial and error” approach). Overall, implementation plans were conducted jointly by three parties: site research senior managers, system developers, and users.

## 5.3. Senior management awareness and support

Senior management awareness of the benefits of GIS was one of the leading forces in ensuring smoothness of the system implementation. At Firm A and Firm C, a large number of senior management believed in the benefits of GIS prior to the implementation and thus the idea was well received. At Firm A, Site Research Department (SRD) senior managers were responsive and supportive. The support was seen and understood by the users throughout the development of models and in the upgrading of the system (e.g. of hardware).

The department continuously employed GIS in developing the site research models through continuous communications between senior managers and users (regular departmental meetings, informal discussions, electronic mail announcements, etc.). There were also a few assigned GIS specialists who helped users with their day-to-day queries about GIS.

Firm C's Store Information Department (SID), director was seen as the advocate of GIS implementation. He had portrayed his “faith” in the system by leading the purchase of the network-based GIS. His level of support increased as the project started to develop. However, at Firm B, the level of support provided by senior management was marginal, except in provision of funds. This was considered the major reason why the system did not realise its full potential.

## 5.4. GIS champions

A GIS champion is someone who is committed to the idea of implementing it within the organisation.

They tirelessly pursue the goal of GIS implementation by selling the idea to senior management, co-workers, and anyone willing to listen. At Firm A, GIS implementation was headed by a group of senior managers, system developers, and users. They worked together, tirelessly selling the idea to other potential users and keeping the concept alive by confronting any implementation threats. Once the system started to develop, they continued to persuade other senior managers and users to commit to the idea of implementing GIS in the entire organisation. At Firm A, site research departmental heads led the project as GIS champions by working with their subordinates, i.e. they led the purchase of the system. GIS champions drove the adoption of the system by holding a series of presentations and meetings and, as the system started to develop, more users were “sold” on the ability and benefits of the system. They acted as an interface vigorously supporting the implementation initiative. Attention was focused at the joint effort with senior managers, system developers, and users. However, at Firm B, there was only one senior manager who could be considered a GIS champion. As a result, he was unable to perform all the tasks required and was also unable to counter the threats posed during the implementation process.

## 5.5. User needs analysis

User needs analysis serves to educate senior managers and system developers about the necessary structure of the system and its use $[43]$ . In this study, the analysis process along with implementation planning helped to educate potential users about the concept of GIS and what they could realistically expect it to do for them. At Firm A, the analysis was categorised into two key themes: user and data needs. Discussions were held with the users and observations were made. At Firm C, the experience gained by senior managers and system developers while using the old system was employed to improve the new network-based system. The departmental head ascertained continuous formal and informal support and training, and discussions were held with users in an attempt to improve the system. As the GIS started to develop, senior managers and system developers held further discussions with users on a regular though informal basis. However, at Firm B, an incomplete analysis led to the misidentification of data usage and no post-need analyses were held.

## 5.6. Applications development and database management

Detailed application design should focus on the design of user applications. They should be designed to include pull-down menus and graphical user interfaces (GUIs) that will meet all the needs. For example, at Firm A, careful attention was paid to user feedback in developing these applications. A standard application design procedure was developed. Its menus presented a set of clear choices, in a consistent manner. A significant part of the development effort was a post-implementation evaluation, because, as users become more sophisticated, they frequently demanded more from GIS and deficiencies were discovered. Users reported problems and requests for new features to system developers who were responsible for managing the maintenance effort.

Again at Firm A, system development tasks were based on SRD accumulated experience and knowledge. A joint team had been set-up to deal with these problems. At Firm C, a system and applications development strategy was also based on SID's experience of its previous PC-based GIS. Application prototypes were developed prior to the actual implementation. Further joint application design (JAD) tasks were organised by SID in developing the applications in which internal and external data were maintained on a regular basis. In addition, at Firm C, an experienced consultant with knowledge of Firm A's GIS applications was brought into the organisation to help with the basic set-up of applications. System prototypes were created, not just to evaluate the “technicalities” of GIS, but also to accommodate users' expectations and requirements. However, at Firm B, implementing GIS applications generated specific and general specifications for them. Although GIS had been recognised as a tool to strengthen sales forecasting and site research models, hardware was not upgraded (there was no plan to upgrade the system).

In terms of database management, there were also several issues faced in managing the databases, e.g. conversion and maintenance. At Firm A, careful attention was given prior to managing the data sets. Its design tasks were based on senior managers and user's feedback. The data was digitised as soon as the system was installed. At Firm C, the data was merged internally by the ISD staff and then maintained and updated on a regular basis. Relevant external GIS data sets were acquired to strengthen the internal data sets as the process started to develop. However, at Firm B, multiple sources of data sets were one of the key factors leading to limited applications of the system. Most of the data sets were in various data formats and this caused severe data problems.

## 5.7. User awareness and involvement

The changing nature of the organisational context suggests a need for appropriate approaches to facilitate better communication. Although formal communication and documentation can be valuable, it was found that this might not be workable, because of the informal nature of the system implementation practices. Although participative design was seen as improving the communication between parties, it was noted that senior manager–user involvement was not feasible due to the organisational norms. One way to overcome these could be to support the mechanisms to improve communications with intermediaries, such as GIS champions. In Firm A, participative design was employed to deal with the continuous changing conditions.

Excellent communications throughout the SDLC was essential. The success of the eventual GIS implementation rested upon the ability of the group to communicate meaningfully. As the system started to develop, users began to hold primary responsibility for operationalising the system, e.g. in maintaining and updating the data. Users were told how they would fit into the process and what would be expected of them, and various attempts were made to facilitate users' involvement (e.g. via regular meetings). At Firm C, senior managers were expected to provide regular feedback to users about the development stage of the system. There were some sceptical users, and it has been stated by Firm C's GIS project manager that the advantage of having “sceptics” was in having to “think” thoroughly throughout the process before making any commitments. However, at Firm B, although users were familiar with the system, user involvement was self-initiated. The payback of the system was realised only by a small number of users.

## 5.8. User resistance to change

Understanding resistance to change was a significant factor in GIS implementation. In all cases, it was strong during the initial stage of implementation, basically due to lack of familiarity of the users in operating the system. GIS implementation difficulties were at their peak during the turnover period (the stage when the old system was about to be terminated and replaced). An extreme level of enthusiasm displayed by the champion in Firm A helped to reduce the strong level of resistance amongst other users, while in Firm C, user resistance to change was slowly overcome as GIS started to develop through support of senior managers. Once user confidence was gained, they overcame resistance themselves. Some was reduced by providing users an opportunity to experiment with the system, e.g. in developing application prototypes.

## 5.9. User training

Training strategies were mostly determined by senior managers. There were off-site training courses organised by key vendors. As implementation proceeded, scheduled and non-scheduled training courses were conducted. The emphasis in earlier stages of implementation was on building user awareness (familiarisation) through regular meetings. Informal day-to-day training was given by senior to junior site researchers, and monthly training was conducted on a regular basis. Proper compilation of training materials was developed. The training included specifics on:

1. the underlying concepts of GIS and site research decisions, and

2. functional components of GIS and how the system worked. However, at Firm B, GIS training was structured by users on a self-initiated basis. Firm B relied solely on its key vendors for training arrangements of in-house and off-site training courses.

## 6. The emerging theory of GIS implementation process

The likelihood of a GIS succeeding and having the desired organisational influence depends strongly on an understanding of how to manage GIS implementation. A generalised approach is proposed, based on the SDLC. A framework of GIS implementation process is presented in Fig. 3, based on the cross-case analyses. It incorporates the emerging framework of GIS implementation based on the seven key concepts that emerged from the analyses.

Effective implementation depends very much on the behaviour of senior managers and users and success increases with their willingness and motivation. A key individual should be identified in the department/organisation to champion the project and be involved in its design and development. Although there are many GIS conversion strategies available, one of the most frequent strategies followed was a phased introduction model. This allows systematic implementation tasks to be conducted by retailers.

User expectations and requirements should establish a dialogue between senior managers, system developers, and users. These analyses are best characterised as a learning process that takes place continuously during the implementation. Possible fear and doubt about the nature of the system may also make it hard for users to make an objective assessment. If implementation is viewed unfavourably, resistance may develop and continue even after implementation. Users suggestions should be elicited and considered for applicability and they should be given reasons for refusal if their idea is not implemented [19].

In line with Prerau's [2] work on attempts to minimise users' resistance, senior managers should establish courses to explain the potential of GIS, e.g. its ability and benefits. Finally, all the methods for designing and developing the application as well as the system should be documented for straightforward future reference.

There is a need for senior managers to cultivate on-going management support for GIS applications in general. A definite allocation of time is necessary for user learning through training.

## 7. Concluding remarks

It is now recognised that GIS implementation is an organisational process $[27,36,38,42]$ . Our findings were based on the experiences of a UK retailing “market leader” and two “followers” who struggled to keep up. The findings suggest that senior managers in retail organisations should clarify the context of implementation to decision-makers that will be supported by the system and those who will operate it. Having clarified these issues, senior managers can effectively plan the strategies and facilitate the action to enact the implementation.

The outcomes of our study have strengthened the contention that it is valuable to view GIS implementation as a process. This effort was an attempt to derive a better understanding of GIS implementation practices in the retailing industry. A successful GIS implementation process involves a highly organised series of process management. It tends to be evolutionary and iterative in nature.
