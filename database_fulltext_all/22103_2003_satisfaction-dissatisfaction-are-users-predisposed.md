# Satisfaction/dissatisfaction: are users predisposed?

Jon Woodroof $^{a,*}$ , William Burg $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Accounting and Business Law, University of Tennessee, 623 Stokely Management Center, Knoxville, TN 37996-0560, USA $^{b}$ Department of Accounting and Information Systems, University of Alabama, Birmingham, AL, USA

Received 19 June 2000; received in revised form 8 August 2001; accepted 23 December 2001

## Abstract

User satisfaction (US) is probably the most studied construct in information systems (IS) research. Despite such attention, reported results regarding the relationship between US and IS success have been inconclusive and sometimes contradictory. Is it possible that something else is occurring in this relationship that has not been modeled in past studies? Perhaps user dispositional traits are confounding the results. Users may be predisposed to be either satisfied or dissatisfied in a way that has nothing to do with the system itself. This paper investigates the impact that user predispositions have on how users perceive IS. The results of the investigation indicate that there is significant relationship between user predispositions and US.

## 1. Introduction

Job satisfaction is one of the measures of the quality of life in organizations and is considered worth understanding and improving, even if the consequences do not relate to job performance. Initially, in job satisfaction research, it was commonly and strongly thought that a happy worker is a productive worker. As early as 1973, Lawler stated that this theme was losing credibility, and that the study of job satisfaction, though not diminishing, was indeed shifting [25]. Researchers continued studying job satisfaction because they were interested in finding its causes. This reason for studying job satisfaction is likely to be an increasingly prominent one as we begin to worry more about the effect that work in organizations has on people and as our humanitarian concerns for the kinds of psychological experiences people have during their lives increase. Because it is generally thought that people have a right to be satisfied with their jobs and to pursue satisfaction [14], there is a legitimate commitment to studying and understanding job satisfaction.

This same argument can be made for continued research on a user's satisfaction (US) with an information system (IS). Although it can be argued that an understanding of US is needed in order to continue to design better IS and improve the systems development process, US research is important for other reasons. For example, all else being equal, organizations would rather have their employees satisfied than unsatisfied with the IS they use; it seems reasonable that satisfied users would be more likely to feel empowered, engaged, and fulfilled than would unsatisfied ones.

Thus, much work has been performed on the construct of US over the past several decades. In fact, it has probably been one of the most studied constructs in IS research. What is more, as new technologies constantly alter the ways users view systems and access data, the US construct continues to be of interest. For example, Cole [4] showed that an interactive information retrieval system, designed to inform the user while the user is on-line with the system, can increase US. Van Birgelen et al. [39] found that US with an IS is central to establishing decision maker trust, as well as promoting the use of the information.

## 2. Problem

Despite the staggering amount research attention and resources that the US construct has received over the past 25 years, reported results have been inconclusive and often contradictory. In fact, there is currently no agreement on a conceptual definition for US, no consensus on how best to measure it, and no clearly articulated theory relating it to IS success $[24,29,42]$ .

How does one determine if an IS is successful? Conventional systems analysis approaches to developing application software continue to have difficulty ensuring that the software has high US [10]. It can be extrapolated from earlier estimates that well over US\$ 1 trillion has been invested in IS technology in the service sector alone [34]. Has this investment increased end user productivity? Does the IS aid managers in making better decisions? Does the IS contribute to overall organizational effectiveness? Methods for making such evaluations have been the subject of much debate [16,20,28].

What are IS managers to do when required to justify very large expenditures? IS success involves so many complicating intermediary factors that measuring it directly becomes difficult, if not impossible. One surrogate of IS success that has been used is IS effectiveness. However, this also involves many complicating intermediary factors.

According to Kim [23], the surrogates developed for measuring IS effectiveness fall into three categories: US [3,22], level of system usage [37,38], and information value regarding user decision performance [12]. Cyert and March [5] proposed the concept of US as a surrogate of IS success. Since then, US has been considered one of the most important determinants [9] and the most widely used measure of IS success [7]. However, the same problem occurs: like its predecessor (IS effectiveness) and its predecessor's predecessor (IS success), US cannot be directly observed.

Thus, US must be further operationalized. System accuracy, system timeliness, and volume of output have been used to measure the US construct. The measures of perceived usefulness, ease-of-use, task importance, and quality of output have been used to investigate the relationship between US and computer usage in the workplace [6]. Other ways include content (accuracy and relevance), presentation (format and mode), and service quality. Assuming that researchers have properly performed this thrice removed measure, what can be concluded about the relationship between the constructs of US and IS success? Several researchers have questioned this strained linkage, and numerous studies examining the relationship between US and IS success (system effectiveness) have reported conflicting results. In fact, over recent years, research on the relationship between US and IS success has generated more controversy than agreement.

According to Jarvenpaa, a successful and important stream of research is one that:

\- defines the boundary for the research to be conducted;

\- documents the parameters and assumptions;

\- is purposeful, self-correcting, cumulative, and replicable;

\- minimizes conflicting and inconsistent results [21].

Why has there been no successful stream of research in the area of US and IS success? Possibly something else is happening to affect this relationship, involving a factor that has not been modeled in the past.

## 3. Purpose of the paper

In order to breathe new life into this important area, researchers must become more creative in studying the US construct. Other possible factors that may be confounding the results must be considered. Perhaps researchers studying US have not actually been measuring what they thought they were measuring. Weak construct validity, as well as other methodological problems, may have contributed to the lack of progress and the mixed results on US [43].

This paper explores a previously unexplored path to determine whether a new relationship could be confounding studies on the relationship between US and IS success. We posit that user predispositions may be the culprit, and we investigate whether users may be predisposed to be either satisfied or dissatisfied in a way that has nothing to do with the system itself or the users' intrinsic and extrinsic motivations in using the system.

Though many researchers have investigated the various dimensions of US [2,8,13,15,17–19,27,30,31,33,35,43], there has been no research investigating the potential linkage between US and a user's general predisposition to be either satisfied or dissatisfied.

## 4. Negative affectivity

Watson and Clark [40], in reviewing a wide variety of research studies on personality, discovered consistent relationships among various measures of negative emotions. They suggested that these scales were actually measuring a more global construct they called negative affectivity (NA). NA has several distinguishing characteristics:

\- Individuals having high NA tend to focus on the negative side of themselves, others, and the world in general, dwelling upon mistakes and failures.

\- Individuals having high NA are less satisfied with themselves and with life in general.

\- Individuals having high NA tend to report more negative emotions across time.

\- Individuals having high NA tend to report more negative emotions regardless of the situation and stress level.

\- NA emphasizes how individuals feel about their world, rather than how they handle themselves in the world.

Levin and Stokes [26] took the work done by Watson and Clark and applied it to their research in organizational behavior. They investigated the role of NA as a dispositional determinant of job satisfaction. They posited that, because high levels of NA had been found to be associated with a type of cognitive bias through which people approach and interpret their life experiences, this same affective tendency might influence how people experience and evaluate their jobs. Specifically, the purpose of their study was to “examine the ability of NA, as conceptualized by Watson and Clark, to account for variance in job satisfaction above and beyond that accounted for by task/job characteristics.” Their study showed that NA was significantly associated with task/job satisfaction, indicating that an individual’s satisfaction with his or her job may be impacted by temperament and overall disposition to experience negative affect.

Because this relationship has been found in organizational behavior research, it would seem reasonable that it might be found in assessing US with an IS. Perhaps users bring their predispositions with them when they evaluate a system. Perhaps people who are optimistic about life in general tend to see the “good” in an IS and be satisfied. Others may be more apt to focus on the weaknesses of the IS, and thus, be dissatisfied.

## 5. Research design

The users (subjects) in this investigation were 43 senior college students. Because they have both predispositions and the ability to express impressions, they should not be any less qualified to be subjects than non-student subjects. The following research question was investigated.

Is there a relationship between a user's predispositions and his or her satisfaction with an information system?

To address this, the following procedures were followed. First, subjects used a software application over the course of a semester. The software was a web application that enabled subjects to: (1) view the course syllabus and schedule; (2) view the class roster; (3) view and download class notes; (4) check grades; (5) send email to the instructor; (6) view project requirements; (7) download project templates; (8) register attendance and (9) take exams.

At the end of the semester, subjects filled out three questionnaires: (1) the NA instrument (Appendix A), a 21-item questionnaire (developed and validated by Levin and Stokes) designed to assess subjects' predispositions; (2) the US instrument (Appendix B), an

11-item questionnaire (developed and validated by Woodroof [41]) designed to assess subjects' satisfaction with an IS and (3) a questionnaire to gather various demographic information (gender, computer experience, grade expectations, etc.).

Because the specification of the context within which US is to be examined was critical to the investigation, the US dimension and attributes were explicated. For this investigation, the dimension of US under consideration is that with the system interface. The important attributes of an interface (from prior research) are as follows.

(a) Input/output attributes: readability of characters, color schemes used, layout of fields and text, and message feedback.

(b) User friendliness attributes: response time, time to learn, rate of errors and ease of recovery, and retention over time [36].

(c) Information quality attributes: content, accuracy, and timeliness [32].

The 11-item US questionnaire consisted of four items suggested by Zmud et al., four items suggested by Shneiderman, and three items suggested by Pearson. These all related to the system interface. Both US and NA instruments required subjects to record their responses using a five-point Likert scale. On the US instrument, the five-point Likert scale used one for low US and five for high US. On the NA instrument, the five-point Likert scale used minus two for low NA and plus two for high NA.

We begin with the following null hypothesis:

$H_{0}$ : p = 0; where p is the population correlation coefficient.

The question to be addressed is, “is the observed sample correlation coefficient r (-0.194) sufficiently large in absolute terms to indicate that the population correlation coefficient value p is significantly different from 0?” If p is zero in a bivariate normal distribution, then the two variables are independent.

The major assumption is that the data pairs come from a bivariate normal distribution. One way to check for bivariate normality is to test for the normality of the distributions of NA and US separately. This can be done using the Lilliefors test, a procedure for comparing the empirical distribution function of the standardized sample values against the standard normal distribution function (SNDF). Using an electronic spreadsheet, the Lilliefors test was performed and the graphs in Figs. 2 and 3 were generated.

The white line in each figure plots the SNDF. The solid black lines on either side of the SNDF represent Lilliefors bounds for a sample size of 43. The broken lines represent the standardized sample data.

Because the plot of the standardized sample values is within the Lilliefors curve bounds for both the NA and the US responses, the data can be reasonably assumed to have come from a normal distribution. Having made this determination, a two-sample t-test assuming unequal variances was conducted on the data set. The results of this test are shown in Table 2. Because the test statistic (41.5) is greater than t-critical (2.64), $H_{0}$ is rejected at P = 0.005 (P-value is much smaller than 0.0001). The negative value of the sample correlation indicates that lower levels of US are associated with higher levels of NA.

## 7. Conclusions

The IS success construct cannot be readily measured objectively. Because of the lack of objective measures of IS success, US has emerged as a convenient surrogate. However, it is a subjective surrogate of IS success, measuring users' perceptions of the IS they use $[1,11]$ . Because US has been the most widely used measure of IS success, the majority of this research is vulnerable to factors that can influence how users perceive their IS. This paper analyzed one such factor, user predispositions, as measured by a user's NA.

In the job satisfaction literature, NA has been shown to be a useful construct, and studies have concluded that it influences a worker's perception of satisfaction with the job. Thus, studying a similar relationship between NA and US has intuitive appeal. In the study, the experimental method identified IS users with NA traits, and these traits were found to predispose them to report less satisfaction with the IS that they evaluated. Echoing the results found in job satisfaction research, users may be predisposed to be either satisfied or dissatisfied with an IS in a way that has nothing to do with the system itself.

These findings may indicate that the way users of IS are tested for satisfaction should be changed. Their predispositions could be captured and used as a covariate in the model of US. In this way, users would be tested for their satisfaction with only the items related to the IS itself.

These findings may also help those responsible for IS to better manage the responses of users. If managers know the predispositions of their users, then they may be able to partition the dissatisfaction associated with the system from the dissatisfaction associated with predispositions, thus, enabling them to better manage user dissatisfaction.

## Appendix A. Negative affectivity instrument

1. After an embarrassing experience, I worry about it for days.

2. I know that things will continually improve in my life.

3. I feel that I have a great deal to be proud of.

4. I often feel restless and jittery for no apparent reason.

5. Things rarely work out the way I want them to.

6. I am not as well liked as most people.

7. Every day seems exciting, new, and different.

8. My feelings are more easily hurt than most other people.

9. I can easily concentrate on things for as long as I like.

10. Whenever someone criticizes me, I think about it for days.

11. I am hopeful and optimistic about the future.

12. When things go wrong, I blame myself.

13. I rarely lose sleep over worrying about something.

14. I am a person of worth, at least as good as other people.

15. I always expect the worst to happen.

16. I am more content and happy than most other people.

17. Happy endings only occur in the movies and in fairytales.

18. I am not as self-confident as most other people.

19. When I meet people for the first time I am tense and uptight.

20. If I could live my life over, I would do many things differently.

21. The future seems rather bleak and unpromising.

## Appendix B. User satisfaction instrument

1. The font and size of characters used make the screen more readable.

2. The color schemes used in the application are pleasing.

3. The layout of fields and text on the screen is thoughtful.

4. The message feedback is helpful.

5. The system is accurate.

6. I get the information I need in time.

7. The information content meets my needs.

8. The response time is good.

9. The time required to learn the application is reasonable.

10. It is easy to recover from the errors I make when using the application.

11. I will be able to use this application again in about 1 month without having to be reminded of how to use it.
