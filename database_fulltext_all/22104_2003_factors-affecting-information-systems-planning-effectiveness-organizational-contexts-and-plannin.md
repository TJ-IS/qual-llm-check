# Factors affecting information systems planning effectiveness: organizational contexts and planning systems dimensions

Eric T.G. Wang $^{*}$ , Jeffrey C.F. Tai

Department of Information Management, School of Management, National Central University, Chung-Li, Taiwan 32054, ROC
Received 19 January 2001; received in revised form 12 October 2001; accepted 27 December 2001

## Abstract

Information systems (IS) planning is recognized as one of the dominated managerial issues of MIS. Based on prior studies in strategic and IS planning, this study integrates three domains to investigate the effects of organizational contexts and planning system dimensions on the effectiveness of IS planning from a contingency perspective. The model is supported by the empirical data, showing the importance of many contextual factors and planning system dimensions to attaining greater effectiveness of IS planning. In particular, the results demonstrate the pivotal role of an organization's improved planning capability in mediating the effects of organization contexts and planning system dimensions on IS planning effectiveness. The implications and limitations of this study are also discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Information technology (IT) is transforming the nature of competition and has inspired many new commercial ventures. Applications of IT have allowed firms to gain operational efficiency, create strategic value by providing new and better services, achieve tighter coordination and cooperation with their partners, and even rearrange the value processes within their industries. Since information systems (IS) have virtually spread across all business activities in every industry, they are broader in scope and more effective than ever before. As a result, the need of IS planning has been recognized as a dominant managerial issue and thus of academic interest $[7,35,40]$ .

The IS planning has been described as a managerial process for integrating IS considerations into corporate planning process, linking IS applications to business goals, and determining information requirements necessary for meeting an organization's short as well as long term goals. Various frameworks and methodologies have been developed for assisting firms in conducting better IS planning, such as IBM's business systems planning [24], strategy set transformation [31], critical success factors [66], business information characterization [29], portfolio management [46], information engineering [45], and end/means analysis [82]. These approaches are primarily functional, being used to decompose business information requirements into IS plans. Other research attempts to identify the potential use of IS in phases of strategy conception, development, selection, and implementation. Examples include customer resource life cycle [25], value chain analysis [58], and strategic use of IS in competition [9,47]. These all emphasize formal planning with a strong tendency to consider the planning process as choices of known techniques or methodologies [38]. However, as indicated by Mintzberg [51], formal planning is simply the effort to formalize parts of decision-making activities through decomposition, articulation, and rationalization, and the ultimate outputs of planning or the intended plans quite often consist of only part of the realized strategic plans. Through field studies, Earl [12] also concluded that most IS strategic planning is a continuous interaction between formal methods and informal behavior. In order to gain a fuller understanding of the factors that can affect IS planning, the process dimensions should be included in analyzing IS planning [68].

Several studies have examined IS planning processes in terms of their key characteristics; e.g. Lederer and Sethi [39] reported that organization and implementation are important dimensions of IS planning. Das et al. [10] broadly distinguished IS planning dimensions into content and process variables, and suggested that formality, scope, participation, influence, and coordination were important dimensions of IS planning. Yasai-Ardekani and Haug [83] conceptualized the design dimension of planning as formality, size of planning effort, process sophistication, planning horizon, and the extent of involvement of the CEO and top management. Segars and Grover [69] identified comprehensiveness, formalization, focus, flow, participation, and consistency as its six process dimensions, and suggested that the internal alignment of these dimensions should be positively associated with planning effectiveness. In general, these process dimensions reflect the underlying managerial values, beliefs, and experiences that a firm holds regarding IS planning.

Raghunathan and Raghunathan [64], on the other hand, adapted the planning system success model from the strategic management literature, and attempted to conceptualize and empirically validate the IS planning system success construct. Lederer and Salmela [37] used an input-process-output model as a basis to formulate a theory. Premkumar and King [60] represented it in terms of “means” and “ends” dimensions, i.e. quality of IS planning process and planning effectiveness, respectively, and examined the impacts of organizational characteristics on planning effectiveness from a contingency perspective.

Thus, the effectiveness of IS planning depends on the nature of IS function, the organizational characteristics, and the planning process itself $[61]$ . Although prior studies have identified a variety of contextual factors and dimensions, little connection has been made between them. Much of the process-oriented research has recommended using integration or implementation mechanisms while ignoring the possible contingent effects of contextual factors. This may lead the planning system to be less adaptable to different organizational contexts and hence overly deterministic $[67]$ .

The main purpose of this study is to extend prior research by assessing empirically the effects of both the content and process dimensions of IS planning on its effectiveness. It also examines the effects of organizational contexts on these dimensions and the effectiveness of planning.

## 2. Conceptual foundation and research variables

## 2.1. Conceptual framework

A conceptual framework of the determinants of planning systems effectiveness, similar to the model of Venkatraman and Ramanujaman [80], is used as the basis. It has three conceptual domains, as depicted in Fig. 1.

![](/api/attachments/9K9AJJB4/fulltext/images/7521d9eb9614b691e790c0e728faffbdbbfc7f3024f126f086e727ca592cda20.jpg)  
Fig. 1. Conceptual model.

The first domain deals with the organizational contexts of planning. Organizational factors, such as centralization, formalization, and future role of IS, are included in this $[32,63,75]$ . The second domain consists of two categories: organizational coalignment and environmental assessment. Organizational coalignment reflects the internal alignment among the four organizational factors having strong influence on an organization's IS planning processes and effectiveness, whereas environmental assessment captures the orientation of the planning system. Following Raghunathan and Raghunathan $[64]$ , the third domain contains two distinct aspects of IS planning effectiveness: the improvements in IS planning capability and the extent to which the planning system objectives are fulfilled.

Overall, this study holds that organizational contexts can affect the effectiveness of IS planning indirectly through the mediating effects of the planning system's dimensions, while these dimensions can influence the improvements in planning capability directly and the fulfillment of planning objectives indirectly. Consistent with prior studies [79], the improvements in planning capability can affect the fulfillment of planning objectives.

## 2.2. Research variables

## 2.2.1. Organizational contexts

Organizational structure is an important factor affecting MIS success and strategic IS applications $[13]$ . Since formalization and centralization are fundamental elements in organizations $[14,84]$ , they must be included. Another factor is the future role of IS. This variable is adapted from the “strategic grid” research for capturing the potential impact of the future role of IS $[53]$ .

## 2.2.2. Planning system dimensions

Organizational coalignment and environmental assessment are investigated in our study. Four process-oriented variables: resources committed to planning, acceptance of planning, implementation mechanisms, and integration mechanisms have been demonstrated to facilitate IS planning processes and are critical to effective planning $[74]$ . This conceptualization is similar to the views of megastrategy $[50]$ or strategy as a pattern or stream of major and minor decisions $[19,49]$ .

“Resources committed to planning” has been a focus of attention in characterizing IS planning processes $[18]$ . “Acceptance of planning” is also important in planning implementation $[2,73]$ . These two factors should reflect the organizational supports to IS planning activities, and are similar to the “favorable organizational context of planning” suggested by Steiner $[72]$ .

“Integration mechanisms” are the methods used to integrate business goals and plans into IS plans $[36]$ . Possible integration mechanisms include the participation of IS managers in strategic business planning and active interaction between IS and business planners. “Implementation mechanisms” are the efforts to overcome problems, such as management control systems for review and feedback, resource mobilization for implementing the plans, and top management and user involvement in monitoring implementation $[11,43]$ . By adopting the view of “fit as covariation” $[77]$ , organizational coalignment is the internal consistency of these four factors.

Environmental assessment comprises of the organization's assessments of its internal and external environments. “Assessment of internal environment” includes a comprehensive analysis of strengths and weaknesses, past performance, reasons for past failure, and feasibility of the intended plan [1,22]. “Assessment of external environment” involves analysis of external events and trends in the economic, political, social, regulatory, and information technological environments. To some extent, these factors represent the content domain of a planning system and are consistent with the comprehensiveness dimension of strategic IS planning proposed by Segar et al. [70].

## 2.2.3. IS planning system effectiveness

The domain of IS planning systems effectiveness has two parts: “improvements in planning capability” and “fulfillment of planning objectives”. These two constructs adopt the “improvement judgment” and “goal-centered judgment” perspectives, respectively. The planning processes can be considered either as logical incrementalism $[62]$ or deliberate and emergent strategies $[52]$ .

“Improvements in planning capability” is used in this study to identify the generic capabilities required for every formal administrative system, including dealing with uncertainty and effective management control $[3,44]$ . “Fulfillment of planning objectives” is a multifaceted concept $[30]$ that is conceptualized with reference to the goals pursued by the organization $[71]$ , including enhancing management development $[33]$ , predicting future trends $[16]$ , improving decision-making, and avoiding problem areas $[65]$ . “Improvements in planning capability” may thus be considered as the “means” to achieve higher key planning objectives. As a result, “fulfillment of planning objectives” represents the “ends” perspective and is a function of planning capability $[41]$ .

## 3. Hypotheses and research model

Our study tried to integrate the organizational contexts, planning systems dimensions, and planning effectiveness into a contingency model.

## 3.1. Organizational contexts and planning system dimensions

Prior studies have indicated that IS plays different roles in organizations, and different planning approaches are required to support the different roles $[4]$ . Attention must be paid to analyzing the capabilities of the IS and relevant procedures and mechanisms must be developed to facilitate planning and reduce resistance, etc. $[48,59]$ . The recognition of the future importance of IS will also motivate an organization to analyze its internal as well as external environments more comprehensively for identifying its future opportunities and potential threats. Thus, it is hypothesized that:

H1. The importance of future role of IS is positively related to organizational coalignment.

H2. The importance of future role of IS is positively related to the extent of environmental assessment.

Research in IS planning has been predominantly subscribed to the rational approach of planning and formal procedures and methodologies have been implemented by practitioners to structure and conduct IS planning activities $[81]$ . In addition, a planning system is as an integral part of management activities and is the foundation from which other organizational activities are derived $[42]$ . Accordingly, broader organizational contexts contribute to organize and formalize IS planning. For example, formalization and centralization can systemize the provision of required resources as well as the procedures. Organization structure can also serve as the “attention directing devices” that “screen out some parts of reality and magnify other parts” [55]. Consequently, this study assumes that both formalization and centralization should be positively related to organizational coalignment as well as environmental assessment.

H3. Formalization is positively related to organizational coalignment.

H4. Formalization is positively related to environmental assessment.

H5. Centralization is positively related to organizational coalignment.

H6. Centralization is positively related to environmental assessment.

## 3.2. The relationship between planning system dimensions

The process dimensions of a planning system have been assumed to be the fundamental architecture of the planning process. When fitted with the content dimensions, the process dimensions should have positive effect on achieving favorable performance outcomes. Thus, users and the planners of IS are likely to be more devoted to identifying new opportunities and providing useful inputs during planning [26]. Adequate use of integration and implementation mechanisms can also provide the planners with more sufficient review and feedback information. In other words, the organizational coalignment of a planning system will have positive influences on the organization's assessments of its internal as well as external environments.

H7. Organizational coalignment is positively related to the extent of environmental assessment.

## 3.3. Organizational contexts and planning system effectiveness

In a highly formalized organization, the information collection and dissemination procedures and the analytical methodologies are codified in structural forms, which can then be beneficial to continuous improvements in planning capability. The extent of centralization, however, is likely to affect planning capability adversely, since a high degree of centralization tends to prevent lower-position managers from providing relevant input information. Consequently, the needed skills and knowledge may be less utilized in planning when the organization is more centralized, hindering the improvements in planning capability.

H8. Formalization is positively related to improvements in IS planning capability.

H9. Centralization is negatively related to improvements in IS planning capability.

## 3.4. Planning system dimensions and planning system effectiveness

A central theme of past commentary is that the process characteristics of a planning system should be structured or internally aligned $[15,34,78]$ . Since our four process factors are derived from the “best performing” characteristics of prior studies, the gestalt of their configuration should shape a distinct profile that can produce synergic effects on the operation of the whole system $[76]$ . Consequently, this study holds that the internal alignment of the four process factors identified will have positive influences on IS planning capability.

H10. Organizational coalignment is positively related to improvements in planning capability.

Further, the nature of planning is deemed a rational-adaptation. With more comprehensive assessments of the internal and external environments, an organization can identify tools, methodologies, and mechanisms useful for developing effective IS plans, thereby making it more adaptive. It is then natural to expect that the extent of environmental assessment will positively affect the capability of IS planning.

H11. The extent of environmental assessment is positively related to improvements in planning capability.

Planning capability is conceptualized here in terms of the ability of a planning system to foster control as well as creativity. Flexibility and management control are critical for a planning system to adapt to new opportunities while preventing the planning process from going out of control. Thus, an effective IS planning system should not only have build-in flexibility, but also be able to maintain the process in control. Certainly, having a planning system with greater capabilities in anticipating environmental changes and maintaining management control should provide the organization with a better chance of attaining its IS planning objectives.

H12. Improvements in planning capability is positively related to fulfillment of planning objectives.

## 4. Methods

## 4.1. Survey procedures

A mail survey was used for collecting the data. A draft questionnaire, based on measures identified in the literature, was translated into Chinese by a bilingual research associate and refined and verified for its translation accuracy by two MIS professors. The Chinese version then was examined by two IS senior managers for content and face validity, resulting in some minor modifications of the wordings of some survey items. The final instrument was mailed to the CIO's of 1000 firms randomly selected from the directories of the 1997 Common Wealth 1000 largest firms in Taiwan. Due to the need for a relatively large sample size and to keep the research costs down, this study relied upon the data collected from CIO's. Although the use of single informant may result in method variances as well as informant biases, the CIO presumably is the most knowledgeable informant about the issue [23].

## 4.2. Measurement

Multi-item scales were used for measuring the research variables using a five-point Likert-type scale (see Appendix A for the scale items).

## 4.2.1. Future role of IS

This construct is related to the “strategic grid” classificatory scheme; it represents the extent to which firms critically depend on the IS function for their future operations. Although the guidelines for operationalizing the strategic grid have suggested both categorical and continuous-scale items, it is difficult to aggregate categorical items into a single scale. Consequently, this study adopted the continuous scale developed by Raghunathan and Raghunathan $[64]$ and Premkumar and King $[60]$ .

## 4.2.2. Formalization and centralization

The operationalizations of formalization and centralization were based on Hage and Aiken's [20] multi-item scales, which have been widely used and validated in prior studies. Formalization assesses the extent to which rules, procedures and activities are written in the organization, while centralization assesses the extent to which the decision-making authority is centralized at the topmost management level.

## 4.2.3. Organizational coalignment

Organizational coalignment was operationalized as a higher order latent factor represented by the internal consistency among the four process-oriented variables: “resources committed to planning”, “acceptance of planning”, “integration mechanisms”, and “implementation mechanisms”. Since these four variables are critical to successful planning process and implementation as suggested by various studies, the “fit as covariation” approach was used to operationalize the internal alignment of these focal process variables.

The operationalizations of these four variables were also based on Venkatraman and Ramanujaman $[80]$ and Premkumar and King $[60]$ . “Resource committed to planning” measures the extent to which the inputs are provided from organizational members across levels and functions and financial resources are committed to the planning process. “Acceptance of planning” measures the degree of acceptance of organization members regarding IS planning, such as accepting the outputs of the planning exercise and the participation of line managers in the IS planning process. “Integration mechanisms” measure the integration between business planning and IS planning, such as the use of joint task forces, involving business management in IS functions, and the extent to which IS managers participate in strategic business planning. “Implementation mechanisms” assess the extent to which management control systems are used, resources are mobilized for implementation, and top management and user are involved in monitoring the implementation of IS plans.

## 4.2.4. Environmental assessment

Environmental assessment represents the analysis of external events and trends in the economic, political, social, regulatory, and technological sectors, and the analysis of internal aspects of organization's strength and weakness, past performance, and the reasons for past failure. Our operationalizations of the internal and external facets of environmental assessment were adapted from the instrument developed by Venkatraman and Ramanujaman [80].

## 4.2.5. Improvements in planning capability and fulfillment of planning objectives

In our study, planning system effectiveness was approached from two angles: improvements in planning capability and fulfillment of planning objectives. This allows an examination of both process and outcome benefits of planning and leads to a more comprehensive evaluation of planning systems effects. Improvements in planning capability were conceptualized in terms of the ability of the system to foster control as well as creativity, and therefore, were operationalized as the improvement in planning capabilities both in dealing with uncertainty and maintaining management control. On the other hand, a “goal centered” approach was adopted for assessing the effectiveness of the planning system in terms of the extent of fulfillment of key planning objectives; these include better integration of business goals and strategies with IS plans, greater exploitation for IS opportunities for strategic advantage, and better planning control of hardware and software resources. The scales used for measuring these two constructs were from in several studies related to planning systems success, such as Venkatraman and Ramanujaman $[80]$ , Premkumar and King $[60]$ , and Raghunathan and

Raghunathan [64]. Although the latter's empirical result showed that the scale for measuring “improvements in planning capability” could be regarded as unidimensional, a close inspection revealed that the scale items can really be broadly distinguished into two clear dimensions: uncertainty handling and management control. Consequently, our study measured the construct with a two-dimensional scale.

## 5.2. Measurement validation

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was carried out to assess the underlying factor structures of the measurement items. Because the total number of items was large, they were divided into four heterogeneous groups for performing factor analysis with a varimax rotation $[27]$ . The threshold employed for judging the significance of factor loadings was 0.50 $[21,28]$ . Subjective methods used to determine the number of latent variables included latent root criterion, Scree test, and percentage of variance explained. After purification, clear factor structures were obtained, as shown in Tables 4–7. Because all measurement items loaded on a single factor unambiguously, each of the measures showed sufficiently convergent validity and is thus undimensional.

## 5.3. Analysis of structural model

The structural equation modeling approach was applied to test the proposed hypotheses. The scale items of each measure were aggregated into a composite scale by averaging the items. The research model was then constructed, as depicted in Fig. 3.

As the model specified, environmental assessment and improvements in planning capability were multidimensional constructs, each with two indicators. Organizational coalignment, by treating “fit as covariance”, represents the internal alignment of the four facilitating process factors of IS planning. Since the other constructs were single dimensional, their measurement errors were estimated as the product of $(1 - \alpha)$ and the variance of composite score.

The result of the structural equation model estimation is shown in Table 9. For overall model fit, even though the Chi-square value was statistically significant $(\chi^{2}(\text{d.f.}:42)=65.36, P=0.012)$ , the other indexes appeared to indicate a very good fit (e.g. CFI = 0.98, IFI = 0.98). Other than those parameters constrained to unity for model identification, the estimates of measurement sub-model also indicated that the regression coefficients were highly significant $(P<0.001)$ . Since the model showed a reasonable good fit with the data, and thus was unlikely to be severely unspecified, the estimates could then be used for testing the hypotheses. As Table 9 shows, only one of the 12 failed to be significant $(P>0.10, \text{one-tailed test})$ . Besides, the explained variances $(R^{2})$ of the four endogenous variables were all relatively high $(R^{2}>0.40)$ , showing that the endogenous variables were well explained by the factors proposed in the study.

## 6. Discussion

As shown in Table 10, the relationship between centralization and organizational coalignment is not significantly different from zero (P > 0.1), and thus,

H5 is not supported. However, centralization does appear to have a negative effect on improvements in planning capability (H9) and a positive, though marginal, effect on environmental assessment (H4). These results suggest that overly centralized structure can weaken an organization's IS planning capability, but such structure may be beneficial to the organization for obtaining more comprehensive environmental assessment during planning. Further, as hypothesized, the postulated positive influences of formalization on organizational coalignment (H3), environmental assessment (H4), and improvements in planning capability (H8) are all supported by the data. These results, thus, lend strong support to the profound influences of formalization on the various dimensions of an organization's IS planning system. As for future role of IS, the results show that the importance of the future role of IS tends to motivate an organization to pay consistent attention not only to all the organizational factors that can facilitate IS planning (H1), but to environmental assessment as well (H2). In addition to these direct effects, the future role of IS can also generate positive influence on an organization's improvements in planning capability as well as fulfillment of planning objectives through the mediating effects of organizational coalignment and environmental assessment.

The results further show that environmental assessment can positively affect an organization's improvements in planning capability (H11), which in turn can exert very positive influences on its fulfillment of planning objectives (H12). Therefore, as the organization analyzes its environments more comprehensively, it tends to have its planning objectives better fulfilled through improved planning capability.

Organizational coalignment appears to be very influential to the success of IS planning. Though lack of any direct effect, organizational coalignment can be (indirectly) beneficial to the organization's fulfillment of planning objectives, mediated by better environmental assessment and improved planning capability (H7, H10, H11, H12). It is no wonder that recent studies have exerted much effort in identifying those organizational factors that, when internally aligned, can lead to higher IS planning effectiveness.

## 7. Implications and conclusion

Although a theory of IS planning is currently lacking, our study synthesized and deduced a contingency model that is generally supported by empirical data. Three major managerial implications can be derived from the study.

First, a planning system's capability plays a pivotal role in achieving planning objectives. However, most intended strategies are not realized due to application difficulties and environmental changes that are not under the control of the organization: thus, only a small portion of planned strategies are realized. Since an IS planning system's capability is critical to the transformation and the realization of IS plans, it is important for IS planners to deem planning as a device for organizational learning.

Second, both the content and process dimensions of a planning system are important to the attainment of IS planning effectiveness. Our study showed the substantive relationships between the content and process dimensions of IS planning. Since the effectiveness of planning relies on the organization's accommodation, organizational coalignment should be a logical antecedent to the usefulness of any planning tools. Our empirical results also demonstrated that both the organizational contexts and the various dimensions of the planning system can directly or indirectly contribute to the improvements of the organization's IS planning capability. These results are supportive to the notion that planning is a rational-adaptation process.

Third, the impacts of organizational contexts on IS planning can inform planners about the contingent relationships between the planning system and its contextual factors. However, the influence of centralization on IS planning is somewhat ambiguous. The empirical results show that centralization has positive effect on environmental assessment and negative effect on the planning system's capability, but no significant effect on organizational coalignment. Thus, centralization may require the planners to pay more attention to the organization's internal as well as external environments, but overly centralized structure may also harm the planning system's capability.

Even though the empirical results are largely supportive to the postulated model, this study nonetheless has several limitations. First, since the data was collected from a single informant, possible biases cannot be eliminated. Second, although some process variables from prior research were incorporated, the substantive causal relationships could not really be established by cross-sectional data. Longitudinal or process-oriented studies based on a sufficiently broad analytical approach, such as the contextualism on organizational change proposed by Pettigrew $[56,57]$ , should be able to provide additional insights into the issues analyzed here. Third, the facilitating factors of IS planning in this study, especially those internally aligned factors, are hardly exhaustive, even though they have been identified as critical in the extant literature. Fourth, this study used aggregated data to perform the structural analysis for assessing the research hypotheses. Since some information could be lost because of data aggregation, the reliability and validity of the results might be undermined. Finally, because the data was collected in Taiwan, the distribution of the scale of the firms surveyed may be quite distinct from that of other countries. It is, thus, possible that the results obtained in this study cannot be generalized.
