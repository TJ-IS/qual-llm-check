# Decision making performance of interacting groups: an experimental investigation of the effects of task type and communication mode<sup>\$</sup>

\* Uday S. Murthy, David S. Kerr

Mays College of Business, Department of Accounting, 4353 TAMU, Texas A&M University, College Station, TX 77843-4353, USA Received 28 June 2001; accepted 10 February 2002

## Abstract

Research on the effectiveness of computer-mediated communication (CMC) has begun to explore the interaction between task type and communication mode. This study investigates the interaction between communication process goals and communication modes using teams with a shared history performing two tasks: idea-generation, requiring the conveyance of information, and problem-solving, requiring convergence on the best solution. Results reveal a significant interaction between communication mode and communication process goals. When the goal of the communication process was conveyance of information, CMC teams and face-to-face teams performed equally well. However, when the communication process goal involved convergence, face-to-face communication resulted in better performance than CMC. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Group decision making; Computer-mediated communication; Media synchronicity theory; Teams; Group interaction; Dispersed organizations

## 1. Introduction

Many decisions in organizations are performed in group settings rather than by individuals acting alone. Information technology is increasingly being used to support collaborative work in a variety of organizational settings. Group support systems (GSS) and other forms of computer-mediated communication (CMC) allow members of a collaborative team to interact simultaneously and anonymously to generate ideas, make decisions and solve problems [5]. CMC allows interacting groups to overcome many of the problems inherent in face-to-face communication, such as dominant individuals, the fact that there can be only one person speaking at any time and the lack of a real-time record of comments made in a meeting. A number of studies have explored the effects of CMC on various aspects of group decision-making behavior [1,13,14,19,24]. Our study extended prior work by simultaneously considering the effects of task type and communication mode, using real-world business tasks and Web-based CMC of the kind commonly found in present-day businesses.

Considerable research in the information systems literature has established that CMC is superior to faceto-face communication, especially in the context of ‘‘brainstorming’’(idea generation) tasks [4,10,21,22]. The superior performance of computer-mediated groups in such situations has been largely attributed to parallel communication, which allows participants to convey ideas concurrently without having to wait their turn (as in face-to-face communication). Most prior CMC brainstorming research employed generic tasks (e.g. uses of an extra thumb), where subjects could contribute ideas without having to recall any specialized knowledge [11,12]. There have been suggestions in the literature that task is one of the more important moderators explaining the effects of using GSS [8] and that the task could account for as much as 50% of the variance in group performance [16]. Recent CMC research has begun to explore the interaction between task type and communication technology, with mixed results [18,20].

The purpose of the current study is to further investigate the interaction between task type and communication mode using tasks that are more representative of real-world business settings where participants must recall specialized knowledge, discuss and debate alternative points of view and reach agreement on the best solution. The goal of such problem-solving directed collaborative work is convergence, i.e. determination of and agreement on the key alternative(s) that would best solve the problem at hand. Such work is quite different from idea generation, where the goal is the conveyance of ideas among participants with no need to discuss or debate the merits of those ideas.

It is unclear whether CMC would result in superior performance relative to face-to-face communication when convergence is the goal because of the increased need for synchronicity in the communication medium [7]. Proposed by Dennis and Valacich media synchronicity theory suggests that CMC may be very effective for certain types of communication processes but less effective or actually detrimental in other processes. Specifically, the theory posits that, for communication processes involving only the conveyance of information, CMC is preferred, while for communication processes involving convergence, face-to-face communication is preferred.

Our study experimentally investigated the interaction between communication process goals and communication modes using an idea-generation case that requires conveyance of information and a problem-solving case that requires convergence processes. The cases involve identifying internal controls that could be designed into an online sales order processing system (idea generation) and determining which specific control procedure would prevent the recurrence of each of a number of errors (problem solving). Unlike the majority of prior CMC research where participants were formed into ad hoc groups for the purpose of the study, participants in our study had a shared history with multiple episodes of working together in teams. In addition, participants were required to recall specialized domain knowledge on both cases. Idea-generation and problem-solving proficiency were compared between teams meeting face-to-face and CMC teams. The study also responded to calls for research using Web-based CMC systems that are more representative of the types of environments prevalent in present-day organizations [9].

## 2. Theory and development of hypotheses

Many decisions in organizations are made by teams whose members share their expertise and present alternative points of view. We are now in an era of the ‘‘knowledge worker,’’ where the majority of jobs in virtually all organizations needs information. Moreover, the knowledge needed to compete effectively in the market place is often dispersed throughout the firm. As the need for professionals to collaborate, regardless of time and space boundaries, intensifies, organizations are increasingly deploying group support systems, such as Lotus Notes and similar technologies, that permit both asynchronous and synchronous communication regardless of participant location (e.g., SiteScape Forums, IRC chat, AOL Instant Messenger, Microsoft NetMeeting). The three distinguishing features of CMC, parallel communication, group memory and anonymity, mitigate some of the process losses in face-to-face communication, such as production blocking and evaluation apprehension.

Media synchronicity theory, as proposed by Dennis and Valacich, distinguishes between two communication processes, conveyance and convergence. Media synchronicity is the extent to which the communication medium allows individuals to work together on the same activity at the same time; i.e. have a shared focus. Conveyance is the exchange of information.

This is often a divergent process, since participants do not need to focus on the same information at the same time nor do they necessarily need to agree on the meaning of the information being conveyed. Convergence is the development of shared meaning of information, i.e. participants work toward understanding each other’s views and reaching agreement on them. Media synchronicity theory posits that conveyance is performed best when associated with low media synchronicity, while convergence is performed best when the medium allows high synchronicity.

The goal of convergent processes is to reach agreement on the meaning of information, whereas the goal of conveyance processes is to share information. Since the goal of convergent processes is to reach agreement, immediate feedback is more important than for conveyance of information. Face-to-face communication is associated with a higher degree of immediate feedback.

Parallel communication, a feature of CMC, allows team members to share information simultaneously. Parallelism can be beneficial when conveying information, since each team member can input information independently and concurrently with other members. However, parallelism can be a hindrance when convergence is the goal, since it is difficult to get all team members focused on a specific comment or idea when they interact from remote locations, each empowered to contribute information that the others see. CMC is associated with high levels of parallelism, while face-to-face communication has low parallelism. As indicated by Dennis and Valacich other media characteristics may impact the communication process, e.g. reprocessability of information permits team members to review and look at messages previously shared with the team; rehearsability allows a team member to review and edit a message prior to sending it. CMC is generally associated with a higher degree of reprocessability and rehearsability than face-to-face communication.

Per media synchronicity theory, for tasks requiring only the conveyance of information, CMC teams have the potential to outperform face-to-face teams because of a relatively low need for member interdependence and coordination (i.e. low feedback requirements and high parallelism), resulting in a good match between the requirements of the communication process and CMC. On the other hand, when convergence is the goal, the performance of face-to-face teams should improve relative to that of CMC teams, because of their increased need for member interdependence (high feedback requirements and low parallelism), resulting in a poorer fit between the communication process requirements and CMC. Thus, the key question being investigated here is whether the use of a medium providing high synchronicity (face-to-face communication) leads to better performance than a medium providing low synchronicity (CMC) as the need for member interdependence in the communication process increases (i.e. when the communication process requires the conveyance of information as well as convergence).

This suggests the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. As the need for synchronicity in the communication process increases, the relative performance of face-to-face teams will improve vis-a-vis CMC teams.

This hypothesis predicts an interaction between the communication process goal (conveyance versus convergence) and communication mode (face-to-face versus CMC), the key prediction of media synchronicity theory.

In addition, it is also of interest to assess team members’ satisfaction with and preferences for CMC versus face-to-face communication under differing communication process goals. The media synchronicity hypothesis addresses the performance of teams in both communication modes. However, the hypothesis does not (directly) address the issue of team member satisfaction with the communication mode as a function of the communication process. Thus, it is unclear whether participants’ satisfaction levels will mirror the expectations about performance.

With few exceptions, prior research has found that members of teams interacting face-to-face are more satisfied with their team’s performance than members of CMC teams [6,23]. However, the focus of prior studies has been on ad hoc groups formed solely for the purpose of the experiment. In contrast, our study utilizes teams with a shared history of several episodes of working together. Although the results of studies based on ad hoc groups cannot be generalized to teams with a shared history, we expect a similar relationship to exist between satisfaction and communication mode. Teams with a shared history are more likely to possess cohesive and efficient working relationships. This leads to the hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2. Participants in face-to-face teams wil be more satisfied with the team experience than participants in CMC teams under both conveyance and convergence communication process goals.

## 3. Method

In our study, we compare the performance of CMC and face-to-face meetings for creative (idea generation) and intellective (problem solving) cases.

## 3.1. Subjects and design

An experiment was performed using 76 students enrolled in a masters degree program in accounting as subjects. The students were taking an advanced accounting information systems (IS) course and were formed into teams of four people at the start of the semester. Prior to the experiment, subjects had worked together to discuss and complete several course projects. Thus, the teams were ‘‘established,’’ with a shared history of working together. Subjects were required to participate in the experiment as part of the course requirement and they were told that a portion of their grade would be based on their performance in the experimental task. Teams were assigned to experimental conditions randomly. Seventy-six subjects in 19 teams of four worked in CMC and face-to-face discussion environments. All teams completed two cases, an idea-generation case, requiring conveyance of information and a problem-solving case, requiring both conveyance and convergence. Half of the teams completed the idea-generation case while meeting face-to-face and the problem-solving case using the CMC-based system. The other teams used the CMCbased system to complete the idea-generation case and met face-to-face to complete the problem-solving case. The order of the two cases was counter-balanced, as was the interaction mode. Since all teams interacted in both communication modes (face-to-face and CMC), any differences in team history would be present in each interaction mode and should therefore not confound the results.

## 3.2. Experimental cases

For the idea-generation case, participants were given a written narrative description of a company in the process of developing an on-line sales order processing system. They were instructed to assume that their team had been hired as consultants to assist the company in the development of the system. Each team was to discuss the system to develop a list of specific

(1) general control procedures to be implemented in the company’s IS department and

(2) application input control procedures for the company’s sales order processing system.

Half of the teams completed this case in the CMC mode and half completed the case face-to-face. In the problem-solving case, subjects were given a written narrative description of a company that had been experiencing problems with its on-line computer system for processing sales transactions. The problems involved 10 specific errors and irregularities. Each team was required to discuss the system and, for each problem, identify the most appropriate specific general or application control procedure that could be implemented to prevent the error or irregularity from recurring. Teams that completed the idea generation case by interacting face-to-face completed the problem-solving case using the CMC-based system and vice versa.

Since the purpose of this study was to examine the differential effects of CMC on problem solving and idea generation in teams, the choice of tasks was crucial to the study’s internal validity. The benefits and usefulness of CMC are a function of the complexity of the task to be performed. Although CMC and GSS can be used in a wide variety of group judgment tasks, their use is probably more appropriate for relatively complex, unstructured tasks. In the tasks here, the focus is primarily on internal control design issues, a non-trivial, relatively unstructured area that has received little attention in prior research and one in which management, consulting and auditing team members are likely to interact.

As Connolly et al. suggest, laboratory experimentation with student subjects is a useful step in exploring various effects of CMC on collaborative problem solving. To reduce the chance that the experimental task might be too complex for them to complete and to ensure that subjects had enough specialized knowledge of the experimental task, subjects were limited to masters-degree program students enrolled in an advanced IS course. Internal control topics had been covered in class and subjects had been tested on these topics. The experimental task focused on evaluation of the operations and design of internal control over sales-order processing, an area with which these sub jects were familiar. Unlike tasks used in prior electronic brainstorming studies, the task was more representative of real-world tasks and required participants to recall specialized knowledge (i.e. relevant internal controls for the computerized sales-order processing functions) in order to suggest improvements in the design of the processing system.

Both cases were completed by all subjects in a single session. Each team in the face-to-face discussion mode was seated at a table in a separate conference room. One member in each team was selected to serve as the team’s scribe to record the team’s solution. This person was instructed to write down ideas that arose during discussion on a large notepad for all team members to see. Subjects were not permitted to use any reference material. Each face-to-face team was monitored by a facilitator from a control room via closed-circuit television.

Teams in the CMC discussion mode used the WebBoard system developed by Duke Engineering and O’Reilly and Associates Inc. This system supports same time/same place discussion, same time/different place discussion and different time/different place discussion. Due to the lack of voting/ranking tools or support for process structuring (beyond threadeddiscussions), WebBoard represents a CMC environment rather than a GSS. All subjects had prior experience using the system. Each subject received a written explanation of the software, and assistance was available for any participant needing help. There were two features that subjects used, a ‘‘chat’’ tool that all team members used to communicate and a ‘‘topic posting’’ tool used by a designated team member to collate relevant ideas from team members. The reason for requiring a team member to collate relevant ideas in both meeting types was to allow team members to view all relevant controls and increase the realism of the task. Each team was given 30 minutes to complete each case.

## 3.3. Dependent measures

The primary dependent measure was the number of relevant internal controls correctly identified by each team. The solutions to each case were given to an independent grader who received a grading key for each case. These keys were prepared prior to the start of the experiment and described the scoring scheme to be used in assessing team solutions. The grader could not tell whether a list was created by a CMC team or a face-to-face team. Teams received points only when their answers matched acceptable answers on the grading key. Only one grader was used.

Each participant completed two debriefing questionnaires; one immediately after each case. These contained questions specific to the CMC-based system used and the tasks employed. Fourteen statements were presented in each questionnaire to measure various aspects of participants’ satisfaction levels.

To discern the underlying constructs in the 14-item questionnaire and to assess construct validity, a principal components factor analysis with varimax rotation was performed. The results are shown in Table 1.

Four item groupings were formed based on the factor loadings using a cut-off of 0.60. These groupings were labeled: (1) satisfaction with performance; (2) comfort with communication mode; (3) Satisfaction with other members’ participation; and (4) autonomy preference. Questions that dealt with subjects satisfaction with their performance during the session were grouped as the first of these factors. The second included questions about subjects degree of comfort or contentment with the mode of communication (CMC or face-to-face). The third covered questions relating to the perceived degree of participation, attentiveness of members and possible domination of the session by individuals. The final one focused on whether subjects felt they could have done better on their own or needed more time. All but one of the questions loaded on these four factors.

Reliability of the questionnaire was assessed by calculating Cronbach’s alpha for each of the four factors. The resulting Cronbach’s alpha scores were 0.83 for satisfaction with performance, 0.79 for comfort with communication mode, 0.61 for satisfaction with other members’ participation and 0.36 for autonomy preference. Nunnally [15] suggests that alpha levels of 0.80 and above are appropriate for affirming reliability. Consequently, it was decided to retain the first two factors but to drop the third and fourth ones.

## 4. Results and discussion

Hypothesis 1 predicted an interaction between communication mode and the need for synchronicity in the process, with the performance of face-to-face teams improving relative to that of CMC teams as the need for synchronicity increases. Results reveal a disordinal (crossover) interaction, the pattern of which is consistent with the hypothesized effects of CMC (Fig. 1). SPSS revealed three outliers in the data, i.e. instances in which a team’s performance differed from the mean performance of all teams by more than two standard deviations. To determine the extent to which results were driven by these outliers, analyses were performed with and without the outliers. There were no meaningful differences in results. Accordingly, the results of tests of Hypothesis 1 reported below exclude outliers.

Consistent with the predictions of media synchronicity theory, teams performed better when communicating face-to-face in the problem-solving case and when communicating via the CMC-based system in the idea-generation case. For the idea-generation case,

ANOVA results for satisfaction with performance reveal a statistically significant interaction between communication mode and communication process goal. Accordingly, the simple effect of communication mode at each level of process goal was examined. For the conveyance (idea-generation) case, results reveal that participants who interact face-to-face are significantly more satisfied with their team’s performance than are participants who interact using the CMCbased system. This is consistent with Hypothesis 2. However, there was no significant difference in the convergence (problem-solving) case. The reason for this is not clear. Analysis of the comfort with communication mode construct indicates greater comfort levels with face-to-face interaction than with CMC interaction. This result holds across both communication process goals. Overall, these results are consistent with Hypothesis 2. Subjects were generally more satisfied when they met face-to-face than when they met via the CMC-based system. This is consistent with the majority of prior research. However, caution should be exercised before concluding that subjects interacting via CMC will always be less satisfied than those interacting face-to-face. In a longitudinal study of users’ reactions to a CMC-based system, Chidambaram [3] found improvements over time in the levels of satisfaction with repeated use of the system.

## 4.2. Team members’ preferences

After having completed both cases, participants were asked to indicate which communication mode (face-to-face or CMC) they would prefer to use if they were required to complete another project requiring team discussion, which of the two communication modes allowed them to express their thoughts and ideas more freely, and in which of the two communication modes they felt that members of their team paid more attention to their comments. Results revealed that participants strongly preferred face-toface to CMC interaction. Seventy-four percent of the participants indicated they would prefer to interact face-to-face and only 4% indicated they would prefer the CMC mode and 22% expressed no preference. Seventy-four percent (8%) felt they were able to express their thoughts and ideas more freely in the face-to-face condition (CMC condition), while 18% felt there was no difference in their ability to express thoughts and ideas. Finally, 40% (9%) of the participants indicated their team members paid more attention to their comments in the face-to-face (CMC) setting, while 51% indicated there was no difference.

## 5. Summary and conclusion

Based on media synchronicity theory, it was hypothesized that CMC would be more appropriate when the goal of the communication process is solely the conveyance of ideas than when the goal is to converge or reach consensus. Idea-generation and problem-solving performance was compared for teams meeting face-to-face and via CMC. The results revealed a significant disordinal interaction between communication process goal and communication mode. The performance of face-to-face teams was found to improve relative to that of CMC teams as the need for synchronicity in the communication process increased. Subjects performed significantly better when communicating face-to-face for the problem-solving case, while there was no statistically significant difference in performance between faceto-face teams and CMC teams for the idea-generation case. Consistent with prior research, subjects were generally more satisfied when they interacted face-toface than when they interacted via CMC. Specifically, subjects who worked in face-to-face teams were more satisfied with their team’s performance and communication mode than when they worked in the CMC environment. Regarding subjects’ preferences, face-to-face interaction was preferred to CMC.

It should be noted that all participants in this study were students. While it would have been desirable to use business professionals, they are very difficult to acquire and use as subjects, given their busy schedules. Remus [17] and Briggs et al. [2] provide empirical support for the use of business graduate-student subjects as surrogates for business professionals.

The findings suggest that when the group task requires participants to converge on a solution, interaction should occur face-to-face. However, when the goal of group interaction is limited to generating ideas, then CMC can be employed. It should be noted that CMC permits ‘‘any time, any place’’ meetings and can therefore represent a significant advantage over face-to-face meetings, at least from a cost-benefit perspective.
