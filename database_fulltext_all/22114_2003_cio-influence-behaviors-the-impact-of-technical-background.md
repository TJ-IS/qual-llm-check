# CIO influence behaviors: the impact of technical background

Harvey G. Enns<sup>a,\*</sup>, Sid L. Huff<sup>b</sup>, Brian R. Golden<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>MIS, OM and Decision Sciences Department, School of Business Administration, University of Dayton, Dayton, OH, USA

<sup>b</sup>Ericsson Professor Information Systems, School of Information Management, Faculty of Commerce and

Administration, Victoria University of Wellington, Wellington, New Zealand

<sup>c</sup>Rotman School of Management, University of Toronto, Toronto, Ont., Canada

Received 4 June 2001; accepted 1 April 2002

## Abstract

In their roles as senior managers, Chief Information Officers (CIOs) are often responsible for the initiation and implementation of information systems (ISs) that are vital to the success, and even survival of the firm. In so doing, CIOs must exercise influence successfully in order to attain these objectives. This study reports a two-phase investigation of CIO influence behaviors, with particular emphasis on the relationship between the CIO’s technical background and his or her use of influence. In the first phase, interviews were conducted with CIOs and their peers to assist in the development of testable hypotheses. Phase two involved the analysis of 69 matched pair surveys and tests of the relationships between CIOs’ technical backgrounds and seven CIO influence behaviors, as posited by socialization theory. The second phase’s results were not supportive of socialization theory. A modified version of Holland’s theory of vocational choice suggests a plausible explanation for the results. The findings challenge the popular assumption that CIOs with greater technical backgrounds are unable to successfully influence other top executives.

## 1. Introduction

The potential for the Chief Information Officer (CIO)<sup>1</sup> to play a critical role in the strategy and change processes of firms is arguably greater today than ever before. Among other things, CIOs are often responsible for the initiation and implementation of information systems (ISs) that are vital to the success, and even survival of the firm. In so doing, they are frequently required to attempt to influence others in the organization. For instance, CIOs spend much of their time attempting to convince other top managers to commit to strategic IS initiatives [40], share in a vision for IS [18], and allocate resources to IS projects [44].

It is important to understand, therefore, the ways in which CIOs achieve success (or failure) as they attempt to accomplish such goals. Some researchers have suggested that CIOs experience relatively low rates of success in accomplishing the goals described earlier [1,10]. In contrast, other researchers have demonstrated that some CIOs are effective at influencing others in the organization [18].

This inconsistency in the literature may result, in part, from researchers’ failure to systematically account for individual characteristics of the CIOs under investigation. For instance, a limited body of research and anecdotal evidence has shown that CIOs with exclusively technical backgrounds tend to focus on technical issues [1,18] and are reluctant to get involved in non-technical issues [51]. CIOs have also been shown to be socially isolated [13] and averse to engaging in social networking [8]. Such behaviors are likely to negatively impact the CIO’s ability to use his or her influence successfully [23,43], and this may have significant consequences for the firm. As suggested by Fiegener and Coakley [22], ‘‘A CIO’s hesitance to exert influence may increase the risks that the agenda for IS will be shaped by other, less knowledgeable sources.’’ Similarly, Ross and Feeny [54] have suggested that weakness in the leadership of the IS function may dilute the organization’s ability to make informed and effective strategic and operational decisions.

To date, there have been no systematic studies that confirm or non-confirm this stereotypical view of the technically sophisticated, interpersonally unskilled CIO. The purpose of this research is to examine CIO influence behaviors, with an emphasis on the differences among CIOs resulting from their technical backgrounds. Specifically, the present study addresses the following question:

Do CIOs with ‘‘greater’’ technical backgrounds differ from CIOs with ‘‘lesser’’ technical backgrounds with respect to the influence behaviors they use when attempting to influence peer executives?

We frame our investigation, hypotheses, and analysis around socialization theory [55,60]. When examining past research on this topic, few studies of influence in the organizational theory literature have systematically examined how top executives influence their peers (i.e. executives operating at the same organizational level). Most of what is known about this topic is based on studies of CEO upward influence (e.g. when attempting to influence members of a board of directors) [62], or inferred from case study anecdotes [11,38]. These studies revealed that top executives exhibit at least some of the influence behaviors identified by other researchers (see [68] and Table 1 for a summary). Nonetheless, no dominant patterns of influence behavior emerge from these accounts. Note also that researchers who conduct research on influence behaviors do not focus their attention on forms of influence, which may involve lying, suppression of information, corruption, or the like [67].<sup>2</sup>

IS research in the area of influence behaviors has been especially sparse. A few studies have indirectly examined the influence behaviors of CIOs. For example, Fiegener and Coakley suggested that less effective CIOs used a narrower range of influence tactics [22]. CIOs’ technical background have also often created barriers to effective influence [50,63]. For instance, the technical background and subsequent technical focus of the CIO contributes to resistance to new IS initiatives in firms [63]. Finally, early descriptive work by Kotter [37] provided examples of ineffective IS managers’ influence behaviors. It should also be noted that the literature has discussed only a few specific influence behaviors at the CIO’s disposal. For example, coalition tactics were used to convince executives of the potential strategic impact of IS [40], to achieve a shared vision of IS’s role in the organization [18], and to create a positive impression of the IS department [22]. Rational persuasion has also been used by CIOs to identify new uses of IT, and create a positive view of IS [22]. However, with one exception [4], no systematic studies on this topic have been published.

## 2. Theory development

There is broad agreement in the literature that managers with technical backgrounds have a personal orientation towards certain influence behaviors, which has been affected by the socialization process they experienced when they developed their technical knowledge and skills [14,63]. Socialization theory provides a perspective on what happens when these technically focused managers make the transition into senior management ranks.

## 2.1. Socialization theory

Socialization, broadly speaking, is the process by which an individual learns and adopts the norms, value systems, and behavior patterns of a group [56]. For example, new organizational members learn how to act in their different surroundings by observation of and interaction with long-term members. Socialization is also formally accomplished via orientation programs, for example. Of particular importance are behaviors and norms that are learned through early organizational experiences, such as a first job or university experience, since they lead to relatively stable patterns of behavior [46,55]. Thus, socialization theorists contend that a newly promoted manager will continue to engage in behaviors that were acceptable and reinforced in the manager’s prior work environment. This is consistent with the concepts of manifest and latent roles [29]. Manifest roles focus on the way group norms produce prescribed similarities in the behavior of those in equivalent roles. Latent roles are relatively stable and are acquired via different degrees of commitment to professional skills, reference group orientations, and loyalty to the organization.

The concept of latent roles suggests that people playing different manifest roles may be performing similar latent roles and conversely, that those performing the same manifest role may be playing different latent roles. The concept of latent role may then aid in accounting for some of the differences (in behavior or belief) among those in the same manifest role . . . ([29], p. 286, emphasis added).

The concept of latent roles is important in our research since latent roles may impact the behavior of CIOs who have greater technical backgrounds. Despite the fact that they function in the manifest role of CIO, with expectations that they behave in prescribed ways, CIOs may continue to act out their latent role, and behave more like their technologist colleagues [28].

Socialization theory also suggests that the work and educational experiences of individuals has a bearing on their use of influence behaviors [47,55,60]. It has been argued that individuals with greater technical backgrounds, in contrast to those who have limited technical backgrounds, tend to have different experiences that affect their use of influence behaviors. Technically oriented people, according to advocates of this perspective, tend to deal with objects and things, are more task focused, and are less relationship centered [30,39]. In addition, the socialization of technically trained individuals differs from that of non-technically trained managers [47,56]. For instance, technically oriented individuals are often focused on contributions to their profession, as opposed to their organizations [28].

Further evidence in support of this notion comes from Schein [55], who argued that learned behaviors can remain the same over different contexts, depending on the degree of centrality of the individual’s social self. Similarly, Van Maanen [60] suggested that change in behavior can occur, but occupational identities and associated behaviors are not easily discarded due to their centrality to a person’s self-image. In a study of scientists and engineers, Miller and Wager [47] found that their organizational experiences reinforced rather than changed their role orientations, a result of socialization from their formal education experiences.

This socialization process impacts the behaviors considered acceptable within technically oriented organizational communities [28,61]. Professional socialization is sufficiently enduring that, e.g. physicians who assume management positions continue to think and act like non-management professionals (e.g. with respect to resource allocation decisions) [17,28]. There is general agreement in the literature that managers coming from technical backgrounds will have a personal orientation towards influence that is affected by the socialization process in their field [14,63]. The socialization view indicates that technologically oriented individuals will continue to behave in the ways they are accustomed to, even when working in new environments. However, no systematic tests of this theoretical perspective have yet been conducted in the CIO/peer context.

## 2.2.1. CIOs’ technical background

‘‘Technical background’’ may be conceptualized in a variety of ways, normally centering on the length and type of a CIO’s education and work experience [47]. A CIO’s technical background has an impact on his or her subsequent behaviors [55,60]. In particular, we argue below that technical background will impact the nature and variety of influence behaviors the individual employs. Gaines, e.g. suggests that technical professionals tend to rely heavily on a single influence behavior, rational persuasion [26]. Other researchers [18] view technical background as a factor, which partially determines the CIO’s particular influence behaviors—rational persuasion, consultation, coalition, etc. Furthermore, some CIOs seek training to help them learn how better to influence others in the organization [41,59], while others seem to possess these abilities when they assume the CIO role [22]. Thus, the literature is equivocal with respect to how technical background impacts the choice and use of influence behaviors by CIOs.

## 2.2.2. CIOs’ influence behaviors

CIOs can be expected to engage in a variety of influence behaviors. However, since there is very little previous research on CIO influence specifically, prior research on influence behaviors in all managerial roles was examined. Kipnis et al. [35] examined the ‘‘influence tactics’’—the behaviors applied by an ‘‘agent’’ of an influence attempt to gain something from the ‘‘target’’. That research demonstrated that influence behaviors vary due to the goal of an influence attempt [57] and the direction of influence (i.e. upward, lateral or downward) [64]. Prior research also notes that individuals often use mixed strategies or a combination of influence tactics [20]. Finally, this research provides a taxonomy of influence behaviors (Table 1) useful for an initial investigation of CIO influence behavior. Also, a recent paper by Enns et al. [4] applied Yukl’s [68] influence behavior taxonomy to examine the barriers and facilitators to the successful use of influence by CIOs.

Further discussion of the specific influence behaviors studied follow in the hypotheses section. In short, these influence behaviors can be divided into three main categories, as suggested by Kipnis and Schmidt [36] and used by others [15,20]. ‘‘Soft’’ behaviors include consultation, personal appeals, and ingratiation, ‘‘hard’’ behaviors include pressure, coalition and exchange, and rational persuasion is treated as a separate category.

## 3. Phase one

There have been no previously reported studies of which influence behaviors CIOs are most likely to use and which factors impact influence behavior variety. To address these issues, we conducted an exploratory qualitative examination as a first step in our research. This approach was used to assist in the development of the hypotheses. Finally, since this study is based on a theoretical model developed outside of the IS context, it was important to develop confidence that the model and concepts might be appropriate for a study of senior IS managers.

## 3.1. Method

## 3.1.1. Participants and procedure

Focused interviews were used to collect data for phase one (Appendix A). An introductory letter was sent to the CIOs of 11 organizations to solicit their participation; seven CIOs agreed to participate. Two of the interview sites were in the financial services industry, two were in natural resources, two were in food processing and one was in technology leasing. All the interviews were conducted in the province of Ontario, Canada and the interviews were conducted with both the senior executive in charge of IS and one other member of the top management team (TMT). A total of 14 interviews were completed in seven companies, all but two of which were conducted face-toface. The interviews lasted from 30 to 60 min and, given participant agreement, were taped and transcribed, and later verified with the interviewee. The CIOs were asked to describe the influence behaviors they used, and the peer executives were asked to comment on the influence behaviors that they observed their CIOs exhibiting. They were asked to comment specifically on an actual, recent situation in which their CIO presented a strategic initiative for organizational approval. Following Mintzberg et al. [49], ‘‘. . . strategic simply means important, in terms of the actions taken, the resources committed . . .’ (p. 246).

## 3.1.2. Analysis

Verbatim transcripts were created based on the interview data. The transcripts were reviewed and verified by the executives that had participated in the interviews and only minor changes (e.g. spelling, sentence structure) were made. Data analysis in qualitative research entails the review and organization of the information contained in the interview transcripts. This is done in order to better understand the phenomenon under investigation and to present the findings in a clear manner [6].

The interview data were thus organized based on the data display technique advocated by Miles and Huberman [45]. The interview protocol, which reflected the concepts of interest, was used as a framework to create the categories in the display and to group related data. For example, an influence behavior category was created to capture data about the types of influence behaviors the CIOs used. Once this was done, multiple techniques were used in the analysis. These techniques included: notation of patterns and themes and counting the frequency with which various items occurred [45]. For instance, rational persuasion was mentioned as an influence behavior in six of the seven cases.

## 3.1.3. Preliminary results

The employment background of the CIOs ranged from a primarily technical work history (i.e. programmer, operations research analyst, chemical engineer), to a significant background in non-technical areas (e.g. strategic planning, general management). Three of the seven CIOs interviewed had extensive employment backgrounds in non-technical areas and the remaining four had predominantly technical backgrounds in IT and non-IT areas. The interview data suggested that the more influential CIOs, as reported by the peer, had a significant employment background in non-technical functions. The less influential had a predominantly technical background, in IS and non-IS positions. This appeared to be consistent with socialization theory.

Most of the influence tactics identified by Yukl [68] were revealed by at least one of the informants. The most common influence behaviors exhibited in this situation included rational persuasion; coalition building; and consultation. A number of technically centered CIOs used rational persuasion combined with persistence (a pressure tactic) exclusively, and continued to use this tactic combination even in the face of resistance. Some of the peer executives mentioned that an over-reliance on rational persuasion was not effective when making proposals about strategic projects.

According to the top executive peers in the sample, some of the CIOs interviewed in phase one were more ‘‘influence aware’’ than others. The more influence aware CIOs were better able to vary their influence behavior to suit the situation. The interview data suggested that the more influential CIOs had significantly greater education and work background in non-IS related functions. This also appeared consistent with socialization theory [55].

Overall then, phase one results indicated that the central influence behaviors described in Yukl’s prior work were manifested in CIO behaviors. Phase one also suggested that a CIO’s technical background was an important consideration with respect to CIO influence behaviors, and that socialization theory might be a reasonable explanation for the variation in the use of influence behaviors by CIOs. However, phase one did not provide sufficient evidence to allow strong conclusions to be made. For instance, the sample size was small. Therefore, a phase two was initiated.

## 4. Phase two

## 4.1. Hypotheses development

Hypotheses were developed as a result of examination of socialization theory, the results of phase one, and influence behavior literature. As mentioned earlier, these influence behaviors can be divided into three main categories: rational persuasion, ‘‘soft’’ behaviors, and ‘‘hard’’ behaviors [15,20,36]. The subsections that follow develop hypotheses about CIO technical background and influence behavior.

## 4.1.1. Rational persuasion and ‘‘soft’’ behaviors

The interviews in phase one suggested that CIOs will vary in terms of the influence behaviors they exhibit. All of the CIOs interviewed employed rational persuasion. However, the CIOs with greater technical backgrounds used rational persuasion almost exclusively, while the other CIOs demonstrated greater variation in the influence behaviors they exhibited. This is consistent with socialization theory, which indicates that the prior socialization experiences of

CIOs with greater technical background predispose them to the use of rational persuasion, as opposed to other influence behaviors, in their attempts to influence their executive peers. Thus, suggesting the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1. CIOs with greater technical backgrounds will use rational persuasion more than CIOs with lesser technical backgrounds.

As indicated earlier, CIOs are often thought to lack the interpersonal skills to successfully achieve objectives in their organizations. Due to their technical backgrounds, some CIOs tend to focus on technical skills [1,18], avoid non-technical issues [51] or are hesitant to engage in social networking [8]. Thus, CIOs with greater technical backgrounds may be reluctant to engage in ‘‘soft’’ influence tactics such as consultation, personal appeal, or ingratiation [36]. This view is consistent with socialization theory arguments that suggest that behaviors adopted in one social context are maintained in other contexts. High tech managers tend to be more interested in both tangible evidence and objects than in people and relationships in the early stages of their careers [30]. So, personal appeal and ingratiation behaviors are weakened by a high tech executive’s natural preference for using concrete evidence rather than such soft influence behaviors when convincing others [39].

Hypothesis 2. CIOs with greater technical backgrounds will use consultation less than CIOs with lesser technical backgrounds.

Hypothesis 3. CIOs with greater technical backgrounds will use personal appeals less than CIOs with lesser technical backgrounds.

Hypothesis 4. CIOs with greater technical backgrounds will use ingratiation less than CIOs with lesser technical backgrounds.

## 4.1.2. ‘‘Hard’’ behaviors

Socialization theory suggests that CIOs with technical backgrounds are reluctant to use ‘‘soft’’ behaviors, requiring them to find other ways to influence organization members. Exchange, which includes an exchange of favors or promises of a share in future benefits, is a likely alternative, especially when used in a manipulative way [20]. The world of the technical specialist is less ambiguous than that of general managers [48] and therefore exchange behavior, which is often tied to tangible outcomes, is more consistent with technical specialists’ experiences. Previous studies have indicated that some technically oriented managers use this behavior [10,37]. Furthermore, there is evidence to suggest that more effective CIOs recognize when certain influence behaviors are not working and try different ones [22]. Therefore, CIOs with lesser technical backgrounds, the group observed to be more ‘‘influence aware’’ in phase one of the study, are expected to be more hesitant to engage in exchange tactics. On the other hand, CIOs with greater technical backgrounds would continue to use the exchange even though it might not be effective, which is consistent with the socialization expectations. Thus, the following hypothesis can be drawn.

Hypothesis 5. CIOs with greater technical backgrounds will use exchange more than CIOs with lesser technical backgrounds.

Coalition building is used when the agent leverages relationships with others to compel a target to go along with a proposal. For instance, getting the target’s superior to influence the target about a proposal. Coalition involves interactions with a variety of people. Specifically, coalitions involve the development of informal networks, and they are relationship oriented. If technically oriented managers are reluctant to engage in interpersonal influence, due to their focus on ‘‘things’’ [19], they would be especially averse to the extensive interpersonal interactions necessary for coalition building. Furthermore, some authors have noted that effective CIOs nurture potential coalitions through ongoing relationships with other executives [40]. For instance, Earl and Feeny [18] found that more effective CIOs took every opportunity to engage in informal networking to build relationships with other executives in order to accomplish tasks in the organization. Less effective CIOs were not as likely to engage in this activity.

Hypothesis 6. CIOs with greater technical backgrounds will use coalitions less than CIOs with lesser technical backgrounds.

Finally, phase one interviews suggested that some CIOs used persistence (a type of pressure). This tactic was used primarily by CIOs with greater technical backgrounds. Thus, persistence and other forms of pressure are expected to be more prevalent among CIOs with primarily technical backgrounds. This prediction is consistent with socialization theory. This suggests the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 7. CIOs with greater technical backgrounds will use pressure more than CIOs with lesser technical backgrounds.

## 4.2. Method

A survey instrument was developed drawing on the previous work of Yukl and others. Then a large-scale mail survey of CIOs working in US and Canadian companies was conducted in order to test the hypotheses posed earlier.

## 4.2.1. Participants and procedure

The population for the survey consisted of companies with greater than US\$ 200 million in revenue. Companies smaller than this are less likely to have top executives (i.e. CIOs) heading their ISs function. Responses were solicited with respect to a specific IS project that the CIO had recently attempted to promote. Data were gathered from a CIO and also from a managerial peer at every participating organization. This was consistent with the use of informant pairs in phase one and with the focus on examining lateral influence.

CIO contact names and company data were obtained from the Directory of Top Computer Executives database from Applied Computer Research (ACR) in Phoenix, AZ. ACR maintains a database of top IS executives in Fortune 1000 manufacturing and Fortune 1000 service firms, including the CIOs of significant divisions within these firms. A sampling frame of 2500 CIOs, which represented only the most senior IS executives, was derived from this database. This database has been used previously by other IS researchers studying CIOs [2,58].

There were a number of criteria required to qualify a CIO and peer for the study. The project must have happened during the last 18 months (so as to minimize memory loss or distortion [32]). Also, the project must have been strategic. Strategic projects were defined as those that were so important that they required approval from top management [53]. Both the CIO and the peer had to agree that the project that they were going to respond to on their surveys was strategic in their organization. Third, the CIO must have worked with the peer for at least 6 months, to insure the peer would have had an opportunity of observing the CIO’s influence behaviors [65,66].

## 4.2.2. Measures

CIO technical background was measured via four survey items about the technical component of the individual’s work experience and formal education [47]. See Appendix B for sample questions. Respondents rated their previous education, overall work history, and detailed work history (i.e. last 5 years and the first job held out of college) on a scale (1 ¼ non-technical, 7 ¼ highly technical). The mean score of these ratings was used to produce an overall CIO technical score. Influence behaviors were measured using the 1997 version of the Influence Behavior Questionnaire (IBQ), which measures each influence behavior with four items [65]. See Appendix B for sample questions.<sup>3</sup> Furthermore, while the interviews indicated that the use of combinations of influence behaviors did occur in practice, in the main study we limited our attention to the impact of single influence behaviors, since it is difficult to assess the effectiveness of each influence behavior used in combinations in a questionnaire design [20].

The survey also included measures of control variables, including organization size (revenues, number of employees) and executive demographics (age, organizational tenure, job tenure). See Appendix B for sample questions. The hypotheses tests (see later sections) were conducted using ANCOVA and MAN-COVA, to determine whether the control variables influenced the results; they did not.

To assess content validity, the scales were pre-tested in two steps. The first step involved testing the survey item wording for clarity and understanding using five

Ph.D. students and five business school faculty members. Subsequently, some of the survey items were modified to provide greater clarity, and some were discarded because the original survey appeared too long for a senior executive sample.

The survey instrument was then pre-tested again with a small group of CIOs and their peer executives to fine-tune the wording in light of the senior executive subject context. In addition to completing the survey, the pre-test respondents were also asked for other relevant information, such as the time it took to complete the survey. Eight complete responses were received, including three matched pairs. Brief telephone conversations were held with those who completed the survey to determine whether any questions or instructions were unclear. The telephone interviews indicated that a number of items required re-wording to better reflect executive/peer relationships, not superior/subordinate relationships. These changes were made in consultation with the primary author of the original IBQ.

## 4.3. Analyses

## 4.3.1. Reliability

The CIO influence behavior constructs were measured with the peer executive responses to mitigate self-report bias. The CIO technical background construct was measured with the CIO responses. Selfreport bias was not considered a significant issue for this construct since it captures factual data [27]. Reliability analysis, using Cronbach alpha scores, was conducted. As illustrated in Table 4, most of the Cronbach alphas were close to or greater than 0.70, which is acceptable [52]. The scores for consultation and exchange were below 0.60. However, we included these measures to test the full range of the hypotheses; we draw attention to this again in the discussion of limitations.

## 4.3.2. Validity and descriptive statistics

Convergent and discriminant validity were assessed in the following way. The matrix of loadings and cross-loadings, created from confirmatory factor analysis output, was reviewed to determine whether items loaded highly on the constructs they were intended to measure. PLS-Graph [9] was used for this purpose. The loadings ranged from 0.534 to 0.884, which conform to similar loadings found in other published IS research [5]. In all cases, the items loaded most highly on the constructs they were intended to measure. There was some concern about high cross-loadings (i.e. greater than 0.5). For example, question 21 associated with ingratiation had a primary loading of 0.827, but a cross-loading of 0.521. We, thus, followed the recommendations of Barclay et al. [5] and only included items which cross-loaded if their primary loading was theoretically justifiable and if their elimination would have had a negative impact on the reliability of the construct. In virtually all of these cases, the loadings on the intended variable of interest were considerably higher than the cross-loadings. Thus, it was decided to include all but one of the items. This decision increased the reliability of personal appeal from 0.758 to 0.787 (Table 4). MANOVA was used to test Hypotheses 2–4, the relationship between CIOs’ technical backgrounds and their ‘‘soft’’ behaviors. MANOVA was also used to test Hypotheses 5–7, the relationship between CIOs’ technical backgrounds and their ‘‘hard’’ behaviors. Table 6 presents a summary of the ANOVA and MANOVA findings.

Taken together, the results provide no support for either stereotypical accounts of CIOs or socialization theory as applied to CIOs. Also, this lack of support for socialization theory cannot be attributed to small sample size. Post hoc power analysis [12] suggested the effect size between the technical background of CIOs and the individual influence behaviors is quite small (i.e. less than 0.20 in all seven cases). In the case of rational persuasion, for instance, a sample size of over 1500 would be required to detect the effect of CIO technical background on this single dimension.

Finally, the same analysis was repeated, this time using the CIOs’ responses to the influence behavior questions. The results were the same as reported above (i.e. none of the hypotheses were supported).

## 5. Discussion

Given the ‘‘taken-for-grantedness’’ of the stereotypical CIO, who is both interpersonally and politically unsophisticated, we believe our results raise the need to reflect on myth versus reality. Still, we recognize that the lack of support for socialization theory requires much caution in our interpretations. A plausible explanation for the results of the hypotheses tests is that, as CIOs with a primarily technical background advance up the organizational hierarchy during their careers, they tend to learn how to successfully influence others. As one CIO mentioned in the phase one interviews, ‘‘Top executives do not rise to the top without having some ability to influence others.’’ Follow-up interviews with some of the phase two participants also confirmed this explanation. They also suggested that IT managers who do not have the influence skills to operate in top management ranks are not chosen for the CIO role.

This explanation is consistent with Hill and Collins-Eaglin’s modified theory of vocational choice (MTVC) [30]. MTVC suggests that it is reasonable to expect that some managers with technical backgrounds can adapt their ‘‘vocational consciousness’’ to new job requirements. Thus, Hill and Collins-Eaglin [30], invoking Holland’s [31] theory of vocational choice, suggested that job and career choice will mirror the individual’s personality orientation. Holland’s theory categorizes individuals into one of six archetypes: realistic (focused on things); investigative (task-oriented); artistic (creative); social (extroverted); enterprising (enjoys persuasion) and conventional (prefers order). This theory suggests that the farther apart two archetypes are, the greater the psychological dissimilarity between them. Hill and Collins-Eaglin [30] created a ‘‘modified’’ version of Holland’s theory, which divides Holland’s environments into two domains. Hill and Collins-Eaglin [30] proposed that technical specialists occupy the first domain, and that they are primarily interested in ideas and things. Managers tend to inhabit the second domain and they are more interested in people and organizations. The technical manager spans these domains, and

. . . is an example of one of the few occupations, which must theoretically incorporate some degree of inconsistency or polarity into their interest and personality profile . . . ([30], p.182).

As the technical manager moves into higher positions within organizations the manager is confronted with a world that is progressively more fragmented, requires extensive interaction with others, and involves exercising power [48]. Typically, in the management progression process, there is a point at which the means to success shifts away from managing subordinates to influencing other subunits in the organization [42]. This shift is called ‘‘breakpoint leadership,’’ and involves influencing the firm’s strategic direction, exercising influence across intraorganizational boundaries, and selling new directions to senior management. In contrasting supervision and breakpoint leadership, McCall pointed out:

Supervision requires knowing how professionals think and how to stimulate them to use their talents; breakpoint leadership requires knowing how the organization works, and how to use power and influence to get things done. One type of leadership produces technical innovation; the other makes that innovation an organizational reality ([42], p. 20, emphasis added).

Hill and Collins-Eaglin [30] demonstrated that some technical managers can make this transition and continue to be comfortable with this integrative vocational consciousness. These authors noted that:

The intriguing aspect of the present data is the possibility that organizational integration does not occur through designed structural mechan isms alone, but also through the career system which allocates to key roles people whose vocational consciousness has an integrated quality and which enables them to manage the conflict between bureaucratic and professional ideologies [p. 188, emphasis added].

This line of argument suggests that, as CIOs with a primarily technical background move up in an organizational hierarchy, they can learn how to act as other executives in this organizational environment do, consistent with MTVC [30]. Thus, it is possible for managers with technical backgrounds to adapt their ‘‘vocational consciousness’’ to new job requirements and cultures [30]. A well-developed ability to adapt to new cultures (e.g. participative, authoritarian etc.) is consistent with ideas regarding the high degree of political competence top managers possess [24]. Thus, IT managers with greater technical backgrounds, and with the influence and political skills to operate in top management ranks, are chosen for the CIO role because they have an ‘‘integrated vocational consciousness’’ [30]. The modified vocational choice explanation is consistent with other studies of executive work, which suggests that executive positions require general interpersonal and conceptual skills more than narrow technical skills [34]. This, in turn, weakens top executives’ relationships with their previous occupational subculture [25]. Likewise, our examination of CIOs does not support socialization theory. Future researchers are encouraged to utilize the MTVC perspective to determine whether it can help explain the CIO influence behavior phenomena.

In terms of managerial relevance, the results run counter to the stereotypical view of the interpersonally unsophisticated CIO. The results of phase two of the study suggest that there are no significant differences between the CIO groups—‘‘greater’’ technical and ‘‘lesser’’ technical—with respect to their use of influence behaviors. This has implications for the selection of CIOs. If interpersonal skills are an important selection criterion (and effective influence behaviors are clearly a manifestation of interpersonal skill), then an examination of a CIO’s training and work history, particularly if it has been highly technically focused, is not sufficient. For instance, some organizations purposefully choose CIOs with little or no technical background [7]. This research suggests that an IS professional with a highly technical background should not necessarily be precluded from consideration for a CIO position.

The results from this research also have implications for the training and development of CIOs. For example, recent articles have provided accounts of CIOs attending seminars on how to influence their colleagues and other top executives with respect to IT initiatives [41,59]. This research suggests that either CIOs already have these skills, or that these can be and are being learned.

One of the strengths of this research is that the informants and respondents in both phases were practitioners from 49 industries. This diversity makes possible relatively strong generalizability of the findings to CIOs in general. This research also made use of two informants per company. Using peer assessments of CIO influence behavior substantially lessened the risk of self-report bias in the data.

Of course, the limitations must also be recognized. A relatively small sample was used in the phase two analyses. Nonetheless, given the top executive context and the requirement for two top executives to complete a survey at every site, the response rate was acceptable. (Researchers working with top executive samples are advised to try to obtain industry sponsorship for their research, e.g. via the Society for Information Management in the case of CIOs, to increase response rates.) Additionally, there was a heavy reliance on subjects’ recollections of past events. Memory loss can distort response accuracy [33]. However, the informants were not asked to respond to questions that could potentially cast them in an unattractive light. This tends to mitigate self-serving biases that may impact retrospective accounts [27].

Since the response rate was relatively low, special attention was given to the possibility of a non-response bias. The potential non-response bias was analyzed by comparing the demographics of the last 16% CIO and peer responders (i.e. ‘‘slow’’ responders, who required four to five reminders), with the 21% CIOs and peers who returned the survey without a reminder (i.e. ‘‘quick’’ responders), similar to the procedure advocated by Armstrong and Overton [3]. Unpaired t-tests revealed that there was no difference between the groups on demographic variables for ‘‘slow’’ versus ‘‘quick’’ responses from CIOs. Similar results were obtained for the unpaired t-tests of the peers’ demographic variables. The only exception was membership in the TMT, which demonstrated a significant difference (e.g. 69% of ‘‘quick’’ peers were part of the TMT and 100% of the ‘‘slow’’ peers were part of the TMT). Thus, taken together, the results for the CIOs and the peers suggest that non-response bias is not a serious concern.

Phase one informants suggested that technical background was an important determinant of CIO influence behavior for at least some of the influence behaviors. Nonetheless, the results from a larger sample size used to test this assertion indicated otherwise. One possible explanation for this inconsistency was the small sample size used in phase one. Also, three CIOs in phase one reported two levels below the CEO or were temporary members of the TMT. As a result, they may not have been as similar to the other four CIOs as was initially assumed, making general propositions about CIO influence behavior, relative to their technical background, dubious. Future research designs should carefully compare respondents on reporting relationships to ensure sample similarity, as was done in phase two.

The study was also confined to initial influence attempts and the survey methodology did not allow for analysis of the impact of influence behavior combinations [20]. Studies about CIO influence behaviors focused on follow-up attempts or utilizing a robust qualitative approach may lead to different results.

Prior accounts of CIO influence behavior have been largely descriptive [18,22]. One of the results of this descriptive focus is that, while antecedents to influence behavior are of interest to influence researchers in general [67], the antecedents of CIO influence behavior have been under-researched. Although this study has begun to address this gap in the literature, it is advisable that researchers should attempt to replicate this study and consider other relevant variables. Researchers have suggested that there are other determinants of influence behavior use [67]. The agent’s traits (e.g. power motivation, self-confidence) and skills (e.g. effective use of an influence behavior) have an impact on the choice of an influence behavior. For instance, CIOs with greater technical backgrounds and a high power motivation may not use consultation because they may want to control all aspects of the projects they initiate and are reluctant to share ownership of their ideas with their peers. Additionally, situational variables such as the uniqueness of the proposal and organizational/environmental constraints on acceptable behavior shape influence behavior choice [67]. By contrast, the CIOs in this study were not directly asked the details of why the influence behavior was deployed in a specific situation. Future research on CIO influence behavior antecedents should take these considerations into account.

Finally, the reliability scores for consultation and exchange were below 0.60. The changes made to the original IBQ to accommodate the top executive environment require additional work. Therefore, more effort should be expended to improve the measures associated with these constructs in order to increase their reliabilities.

## 6. Conclusions

This study set out to understand the ways in which CIOs use personal influence to achieve successful acceptance and implementation of strategic IS projects. The research question focused on the relationship between the CIO’s technical background and his or her use of influence behaviors with peer executives.

In summary, the results did not support the socialization perspective. The technical background of the CIO does not appear to impact influence behaviors in top executive, peer contexts. There is only a non-significant difference between ‘‘lesser tech’’ and ‘‘greater tech’’ CIOs with respect to the influence behaviors they exhibit. The alternative explanation is that top executives (including CIOs) do not rise to the top without having the ability to utilize influence behaviors successfully. CIOs with ‘‘greater’’ technical backgrounds may be able to make appropriate adjustments to their influence behaviors in top executive environments or, as the data indicate, there may be sufficient variation in interpersonal skills. Thus, it appears that only highly interpersonally skilled individuals—regardless of technical background—advance to the CIO position.

This research extends the work of Kipnis et al. [35] and others whose primary focus has not been on top executive peer influence behavior. This is also the first (relatively) large sample study which has put the conventional wisdom about CIOs to a systematic empirical test. Our results provide evidence that the stereotype of technically oriented CIOs as being interpersonally less adept than their peers has little empirical support [13,51]. At a minimum, the results highlight the importance of more research on this practically important, and theoretically interesting question. Our results also suggest the importance of knowing what we do not know, but may believe, and how erroneous beliefs (e.g. of CIO proclivities and style) may lead to dysfunctional organizational practices.
