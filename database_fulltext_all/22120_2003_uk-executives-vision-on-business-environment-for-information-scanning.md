# UK executives’ vision on business environment for information scanning A cross industry study

Xianzhong M. Xu<sup>a,\*</sup>, G. Roland Kaye<sup>b</sup>, Yanqing Duan<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>University of Portsmouth Business School, Portsmouth, PO4 8JF, UK <sup>b</sup>Open University Business School, Milton Keynes, MK7 6AA, UK <sup>c</sup>University of Luton Business School, Luton, LU1 3JU, UK

Accepted 20 May 2002

## Abstract

Business environment scanning contributes a great deal to strategic intelligence gathering. However, earlier studies treat the business environment as a single entity. This study based on a multi-industry survey examined how UK executives perceive the stability of the business environment in which their companies operate, and what environmental sectors are perceived more strategically important than others for information scanning. The effort reveals that UK executives perceive the immediate-task environment information more strategically important than far-general environment information, but the perception of far environment information differs widely from industry to industry. A corporate ‘‘radar’’ system for business environment scanning is proposed to offer practical guidance. Our conclusion is that industry-specific environment sectors should be identified for strategic information scanning.

Keywords: Environmental scanning; Strategic information; Information processing; Business environment; UK survey

## 1. Research context

The business environment is viewed as a source of information that continually creates signals and messages that organisations should consider important [2]. In particular, the external dimension of the business environment has been emphasised with respect to strategic uncertainty and strategic information scanning. This is evident from the definition of the business environment as ‘‘the relevant physical and social factors outside the boundary of an organisation that are taken into consideration during organisational decision-making’’ [4]. Research into environment scanning [15] also highlights the outside view of a company’s boundary, and recognises that strategic thinking begins with a study of the external environment. It is suggested that environmental scanning needs to identify emerging situations, hazards and opportunities that may affect a company’s operation and strategic direction [1]. Scanning invokes a process that causes the company to expand decision-making to include the perspectives of outsiders, e.g. present and prospective competitors, customers, regulators, stakeholders, and the perspectives of economic condition, political climate, technology development, social and cultural changes. It is estimated that the emerging business will need access to external environmental information on a previously unparalleled scale [17].

Unstable environments create strategic uncertainty for executives. Individuals in decision units with a dynamic–complex environment experience the greatest uncertainty in decision-making [5]. As complexity and rate of change in the environment increase, the amount of uncertainty perceived by senior managers also increases. This is supported by empirical evidence [14] that strategic uncertainty caused by business environment turbulence leads to increased demand for strategic information. Executives in high-performing companies tend to scan environmental information more frequently and more broadly in response to strategic uncertainty than their counterparts in low-performing companies.

However, research into the business environment remains at the level of classifying it into sectors. It has not yet been become available as a means to examine the difference between the environment sectors with respect to strategic information scanning and industry variances. A common classification of the business environment is the internal versus external dimension, i.e. factors that fall within the boundary of the organisation constitute the internal environment, while factors beyond the boundary form the external environment. The environment is also conceptualised as having several sectors that exist in two layers. The one closest to the organisation is the task environment, with sectors that have direct transactions with the organisation, such as competitors, suppliers, and customers. The outer layer represents the general environment and refers to sectors that affect organisations indirectly, such as the economic, legal, social, and demographic ones [3]. However, the business environment has been treated as a single entity, and the sectorial difference has not been made explicit to enable more effective and selective information scanning.

Furthermore, earlier studies on business environments seem to be more descriptive than explorative. The description, as an enabler for organisational change, has been well discussed, e.g. [7], but determining how an organisation can successfully navigate the uncertainty has been generally ignored. It has been argued that managers have to learn how to use variety reducers to filter out the complexity of operational and environmental variables and to capture only information of relevance to them and the organisation. An organisation should have an adaptive subsystem to detect environmental variation [10]. Senior managers must find a scanning mechanism that yields adequate information on external events without information pollution.

Because the business environment becomes more diverse, dynamic and complex, and the information intensive feature varies from industry to industry, e.g. the food industry in many countries is relatively lowtechnology-based, whereas the electronics industry is high-technology driven. Thus, there is a need to identify environment sectors that have the greatest impact.

## 2. Methodology

This study was initiated to test how UK executives perceive the importance of the environment sectors for strategic information scanning in different industries. Due to its nature, a case study approach was judged inappropriate: any findings would be limited by the individual who was interviewed and the industry viewpoint may be unique. A survey method was viewed as appropriate. Therefore, a questionnaire was developed and it was exposed to a pilot survey to ensure content validity of all items used and to gain constructive criticism.

## 2.1. Sampling

The sample population was drawn from the Financial Analysis Made Easy (FAME; UK company database) database by identifying industry activities as computers, electronics, food, chemicals, and transport. This ensured that a wide spread of activities were sampled. Another criteria for selecting the sample was that the current number of employee was more than 100. The sample, therefore, excludes small entrepreneurial firms. This resulted in a potential sample frame of 1645 companies. Duplicated companies and directors were removed and 1518 questionnaires were sent to the relevant managing directors.

Posting the questionnaire was conducted in two stages, resulting in a total of 242 returned questionnaires. Amongst these, the valid responses were only 155, representing a relatively low response rate (10.2%).

## 2.2. Explanation of the response rate

Even though the response rate of the survey appears low, it is a valid and useful sample, because:

(a) The sample is fairly distributed among the five industries.

Apart from the companies in the transport industry (9%), companies are normally distributed in the other four industries, ranging from 20% (chemical; food industry) to 26% (electronics industry).

(b) The sample fairly represents each of the industry as shown in Table 1.

The average rate of samples representing the industry is 9%, with the lowest 6.2% in the food industry and the highest 11.8% from the chemical industry. This demonstrates a normal distribution. The sample selected is not biased as far as the industry is concerned.

(c) The sample non-responses are caused by the nature of this research.

A non-response analysis was conducted to determine why there were 87 unusable questionnaires. The questionnaire asked the respondents to give reasons if they decided not to complete the questionnaire. Six major reasons are identified as follows: company policy of not participating in surveys (23%); not capable or not in a position to answer the questions (17%); work pressure and no time to answer questionnaires (14%); business is ceased trading or addressee has left the company (13%); survey topic is not relevant (9%) and survey topic is sensitive (1%). It would have been surprising if a high response rate was received for this type of research, since the questionnaire requires executives’ time and knowledge. It is natural that people, not capable or not in a position to answer the questions, will not return the questionnaire and this implies that the completed questionnaires are more likely answered by executives themselves. A total of 14% non-responses are due to work and pressure. Many non-responses result from companies that ceased trading, etc. Finally, 23% of the companies adopted a policy against external surveys; this is similar to a survey by King and Sabherwal [11] who report that among the 195 non-responses, 17.9% were due to ‘‘company policy against response to survey’’.

## 2.3. The questionnaire

The finding is a part of a large scaled survey of UK executives’ perception on strategic information as well as their behaviour and pattern in acquiring strategic information. One of the main dimensions of the questionnaire is, therefore, to examine how UK executives perceive the importance of the business environment for strategic information scanning and whether the perceived importance of environment sectors is different because of industry differences. Two main questions are related to this test:

(a) How do you feel the business environment in which your company operates?

The answers are measured on a 5-point Likert scale with 5 meaning very unstable<sup>1</sup> and 1 meaning very stable.

(b) What kinds of information do you think are strategically important for you to receive?

The choices given are related to the immediate and far environment including: customer and key markets; market share/industry structure;

competitors/competition; suppliers/distributors; economic climate; political/legislation condition; technology innovation; and social/cultural conditions. An open space is provided to allow respondents to add other information not included in the list. The data is categorical in nature, thus, descriptive statistics, such as frequency, can be used in analysis.

This paper reports only the results from these two questions, rather than the whole questionnaire.

## 2.4. The measurement

The environmental stability is measured subjectively with measure based on Meznar and Nigh’s [13] work. They argue that a perceptual measure is appropriate since it is manager’s perception of their environment that is expected to affect a firm’s public affairs activities. They also reported that such measures of social and political uncertainty were significantly correlated to objective measures. Another argument is given in [6], that an information advantage based on environmental opportunities and problems depends on the management’s perception of signals missed by other organisations. The business environment sectors here are classified according to their relative immediacy to decision makers [18]:

\- Immediate environment sectors including the sectors that are common in a specific marketplace or industry, e.g. market size, trends, and concentration of the industry.

\- Far environment sectors include those sectors that are common to companies in all industries and may have indirect impact on operations.

UK executives’ perceptions are presented in Table 2.

By ranking the mean scores, executives from the computers and food industry tend to perceive their environment more unstable than those from other industries. ## 3.3. The perceived strategic importance of environment sectors by industries

As a further step, the study examined whether the perceived importance of the environment sectors is different because of industry difference. The result for each industry is shown in Fig. 1.

Within the computers industry, over 73% of respondents perceive immediate environment sectors as being strategically important, while only 48.6% perceive far environment sectors as strategically important. The difference is significant $\left( P = 0 . 0 4 \right)$ The supplier sector, which is assumed to be part of the immediate environment, was not perceived as important as other immediate environment sectors. The T-test shows an improved significance level $\left( P = 0 . 0 2 \right)$ if supplier sector is removed from the immediate environment.

In the food industry, over 85 of the executives perceive the immediate environment information to be of strategic importance and 75% of the executives indicated the importance of information about the far environment. A T-test shows the difference between the perceived importance of immediate and far environment information. The finding suggests that all the environment sectors are strategically important, but customer sector, market sector and competitor sector are more important than other sectors for information scanning.

The perception of executives from the chemical industry is similar to that of the food industry. The primary environment sector for information scanning is customers, market condition, and competitors; the supplier sector was relatively less important. The far environment has been seen to be strategically important for scanning, in the economic, technology, and legislation sectors.

Over 50% of the executives from the food and chemical industries rate social and cultural sector as being strategically important. This is significantly higher than the response rate from the computer industry (25.7%) and electronics industry (41.2%). This suggests that these two traditional industry executives believe that economic condition, legislation, and social/cultural changes are more likely to affect their company’s strategic direction. The higher perception on the legislation sector may be attributed to the fact that chemical and food products probably are more tightly regulated than computer and electronics products in the UK.

A different picture is generated from the transport industry: the immediate environment is not perceived as more important than the far environment. Even removing supplier from the immediate, the statistical test shows an even closer mean score between the importance of the immediate environment sector and that of the far.

## 4. Discussion

This study reveals that customers, competitors, and market sectors have been perceived most strategically important by UK executives from all industries. This partially confirms the finding of Daft et al. [4]. This suggests that the immediate environment sectors should be treated as the primary environment sectors for strategic information scanning. However, the supplier sector has not been seen as strategically important by UK executives. This is contrary to the assertion [9] that the focus of scanning should be on marketrelated sectors of the external environment, with information on customers, competitors, and suppliers being important. In general, the far environment sectors have been seen less important than the immediate ones for information scanning. For example, the sociocultural sector was perceived as less important. Auster and Choo [2] reached a similar finding. The sociocultural sector was found to have minor strategic uncertainty and impact on manufacturing firms in the study by Daft et al. [4] in the US. As far as the regulatory sector is concerned, the importance of this information varies across industries. This finding is in line with work by Daft et al. [4]. However, it is contrary to the report by Auster and Choo [2].

The result suggests that the perceived importance of immediate environment sectors does not differ widely from industry to industry. UK executives focus on scanning for strategic information on customers, competitors, and the market/industry sectors. CEOs are more concerned with the immediate environmental factors that affect their business directly. However, the strategic significance of the far environment sectors perceived differs widely across industries.

## 5. Developing a corporate radar system for strategic information scanning

Business organisations should develop a radar-type system (or function) to continuously, but selectively, detect significant signals from environment sectors. Past studies [8] suggest that most scanning activities do not look into the strategic dimensions and that very few organisations had adopted a systematical and structured approach. The environment sectors for scanning are not differentiated in terms of strategic significance that is industry-specific. Within the available scanning systems, most are rudimentary and can be classified as ‘‘irregular’’, ‘‘primitive’’ or ‘‘ad hoc’’ models. These models are mainly reactive in their orientation and merely used to enhance understanding of a specific event. A corporate radar system for strategic information scanning is illustrated in Fig. 2.

![](/api/attachments/YCEN5APQ/fulltext/images/20acd8d5985b3a2c2d208f267a64613e71a8839d54ea8665ad86839356fdbe25.jpg)  
Fig. 2. A corporate radar system.

The system is based on the findings of this study, but also draws ideas from Liu [12]. The sensing function acts as an alarm for managers. It also provides: signal intensity—the degree of strategic importance of signals released from the variables, and signal strength— the clarity of the message.

As an example, in the computer industry, customer, competitor, market/industry, and technology sectors have more strategic importance than other variables, showing that they have a high intensity signal. The signal strength of each variable needs to be assessed, i.e. is the message explicit and regular or is it tacit and irregular?

This suggests a need for the following:

\- A sensor-centred scanning mechanism—to continuously scan the most important environment sectors where the strength of the signal is strong. A computer-based IS that incorporates intelligent data scanning facilities could effectively support this function.

\- A filter-centred scanning mechanism—to scan the environmental sectors for information regularly arriving at the organisation. This information has to be selected from a potentially large mass of data, and filtered for relevance. Because those environment sectors are less important for deriving strategic information, the filter function is vital to eliminate information overload. Computer-based data filtering functions can be applied to facilitate the process.

\- A probe-centred scanning mechanism—to detect weak signal that may have strategic impact. Information from those environment sectors is less structured and explicit and thus, not easily collected. Much of executives’ information is ‘‘soft’’: opinions, predictions, hearsay, ideas, rumours, and gossips [16]. The vague signal captured needs to be amplified to assess its potential impact on the strategic direction of the organisation.

\- A discard mechanism is needed to ignore a weak signal from the less important environmental sectors.

It can be argued that in each environment sector, the signal can be strong or weak: either can be important. It is argued by Stoffels [15] that ‘‘the strength of signals is related to the uncertainty of environment, i.e. weaker signals are associated with the remote environment, and strong signals with the task environment. The environment scanning effort is much required in the remote environment as the visibility of the future diminishes with increasing turbulence, and predictability deteriorates accordingly.’’

## 6. Conclusion

UK executives do not fix their scanning on a particular immediate environment sector. The level of perceived strategic importance of the immediate environment sectors is not different from industry to industry. A continuous monitoring and scanning mechanism for market, competitor, and customer information should be established to help the executives. The significance of technological, economic and regulatory sectors differs from industry to industry, the scanning mechanism should be industry-specific, and be selective to detect only strategic important signals.

A number of limitations of the study are recognised. Firstly, the study is based on only five UK industries; the results generated from this study may not be applicable to other industries or the same industries in other countries. Secondly, the sample selected is medium to large sized companies, the results may not be applicable to very large or small and entrepreneurship enterprises. Thirdly, the environmental scanning mechanism (the corporate radar system) is proposed but non-proven. Last but not the least, this study is descriptive rather than explorative, i.e. it does not address the ‘‘why’’ question. Executives’ vision and knowledge in identifying environment sectors for scanning, in noticing, interpreting significant information have not been addressed. Future study is needed to develop the corporate radar system and to address the implementation issues.
