# An empirical study of smart card technology

Gregory E. Truman<sup>a,\*</sup>, Kent Sandoe<sup>b</sup>, Tasha Rifkin<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Information Systems, Babson College Math/Science Division, 231 Forest Street, 02457-0310 Babson Park, MA, USA

<sup>b</sup>Management Information Systems, California State University, Chico, CA, USA

<sup>c</sup>UBS Paine Webber, Jersey City, NJ, USA

Received 19 March 2001; accepted 1 April 2002

## Abstract

Smart card technology remains novel for consumers and merchants, in spite of several attempts by financial institutions and other interested parties to deploy the technology on wide scale bases. After Citibank, Chase, VISA and MasterCard launched a smart card trial in New York city, the trial was deemed a failure and was subsequently cancelled. Drawing from innovation and critical mass theories, and using merchant and consumer data, we provide explanations for the trial’s failure. Despite the fact that the technology’s relative advantages were significantly related to consumers’ and merchants’ acceptance, we found consumers and merchants disposed against acceptance of this smart card technology. We found no evidence for critical mass effects as a predictor of either consumers’ or merchants’ acceptance. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Smart card; Electronic payment systems; Innovation theory; Critical mass theory

## 1. Introduction

As an electronic payment system, smart card technology presumably offers numerous advantages, including shorter transaction time and greater convenience for consumers, lower cash handling cost and fewer transaction errors for merchants, and greater earned float and less assumed risk for financial institutions. While these and other advantages exist, the technology remains novel for most consumers and merchants. In addition, the financial service and technology industries have invested substantial resources for developing smart card technology and for devising trial implementations. While many involved parties remain optimistic that smart card technology will eventually become commonplace, most trials suggest that attaining wide scale use will be a formidable challenge [1–4]. This challenge is borne out in trials conducted in Canada, Europe and Asia [2,3,5], and in the United States where some argue that even greater formidability may exist [1]. In light of these presumed advantages, substantive investments, and trial outcomes, there is vested interest in more fully understanding the issues preventing wide scale acceptance.

Among these issues, consumer and merchant acceptance were identified by Clemons, et al. [4] as key to attaining wide scale use and eventual success of electronic payment systems. Much of the empirical research on consumer and merchant acceptance has occurred in at least one of three research contexts, each reducing the contextual authenticity in which the general public’s acceptance would be expected to occur. One research context includes communities<sup>1</sup> of merchants and consumers that are typically defined according to organization membership or affiliation, such as universities, medical complexes and the military. In these contexts, there exists a tendency for consumers to transact with the community of merchants for various reasons such as price discounts and lack of alternatives. Communities offer a useful research context in some circumstances, such as when resolving technical issues. However, this context may not be appropriate for studying consumers’ and merchants’ acceptance, particularly when the smart card offers the only means to transact.

The second context involves special use smart cards that may be used for a narrow set of merchant sectors. For example, public transportation and voice communication services may be purchased with a card sometimes referred to as the ‘metro card’ and ‘calling card’, respectively. While special use cards offer various advantages for purchasing the respective service, an average consumer buys many different products and services. Thus, the average consumer would require many different smart cards. The general consensus holds that a situation will eventually evolve such that one card can purchase different products and services across a broad set of merchant sectors. We refer to this kind of smart card technology as general use, which has been deployed and studied much less extensively than the special use kind.

The third research context relates to the fact that a majority of smart card trials has been conducted outside the United States. While there are exceptions like the 1996 Olympics and New York city trials, the American trials suggest that the technology has been unenthusiastically received by the American public [1,7]. In contrast, some European and Canadian trials have met with marginal success in that smart cards appear to be modestly popular among consumers or merchants [2,5]. These foreign developments create intrigue and motivation to study why the US trials are comparatively less successful.

In summary, we know little about whether smart card technology may be widely accepted by American merchants and consumers in a context that neither involves special use technology, nor is confined to a community affiliated by organization membership. Other than the work on the New York city trial by Van Hove [1], who admittedly draws conclusions from limited and inconclusive secondary data, we know of no empirical studies that has examined smart card technology in these contexts. Thus, our research objective is to study some issues that may affect general use smart card technology acceptance by American merchants and consumers in a context where the technology is deployed outside a community of affiliates.

## 2. Research context

From October 1997 through 1998, MasterCard, Visa, Citibank and Chase sponsored a trial implementation of general use smart card technology in New York City’s Upper-West Side area. The technology consisted of a smart card and smart card reader (SCR) used by consumers and merchants, respectively, and the trial area was defined by geographic considerations rather than community affiliation. Because the research context is set in a trial implementation, the respondents had already adopted the technology as a condition to participating. Consequently, we use measures related to continued acceptance for our dependent measures. In our theoretical discussion and subsequent analyses, and according to sponsors’ intentions, we assume that this smart card technology is an alternative payment method to cash in small purchase occasions.

## 2.1. Overview

We begin with a discussion of innovation and critical mass theories, which we identify as useful for explaining why smart card technology may or may not be widely accepted. We interweave the theoretical discussion with the development of specific propositions related to American consumers’ and merchants acceptance of the technology. Next, we present the research methodology that was used in this study, and follow with the results. We close with a discussion of our findings, some limitations of the study and several conclusions about smart card technology.

## 3. Theoretical background and proposition development

Smart card technology is novel to most consumers and merchants therefore it satisfies a common definition of an innovation—any idea, practice or object that is perceived as new by the adopter [8]. Thus, we believe that it is proper to treat smart card technology as an innovation, despite the fact that various forms have existed for some time albeit on limited trial bases. Moreover, we assume that the technology’s perceived benefits will influence consumers’ and merchants’ attitudes and use behaviors. Because these definitions and assumptions are shared by Rogers’ innovation theory, we use innovation theory to formulate propositions that relate the technology’s benefits to its acceptance.

## 3.1. Innovation theory

One tenet of Rogers’ innovation theory is that an innovation’s characteristics are related to adoption and use behaviors among potential adopters, and may assist in predicting whether an innovation gains wide scale acceptance.<sup>2</sup> Rogers [8] identifies five innovation characteristics, including relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability. Many from various disciplines have drawn from Rogers’ work in order to examine the relationship between these innovation characteristics and adoption and use behaviors. In conducting a meta-analysis on the cumulative research findings, Tornatzky and Klein [9] concluded that relative advantage, complexity and compatibility are consistently related to adoption and use behaviors.

Rogers defines relative advantage as the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being better than, superior to, or advantageous over the idea that precedes it. Complexity is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as relatively difficult to understand and use. Compatibility is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as consistent with the existing values, past experiences, and needs of potential adopters. To summarize, high relative advantage, low complexity and high compatibility are theoretically related to an inclination to adopt and use an innovation. Based on Tornatzky and Klein’s work, and similar to other recent studies [10,11], we develop several theoretical propositions on the relationship between the technology’s innovative characteristics and its acceptance by potential adopters—consumers and merchants in this instance. We alternately adopt consumer and merchant perspectives.

## 3.1.1. Consumer perspective

Consumers’ card use involved three distinct procedures—obtainment, transaction and replenishment. Card obtainment occurred once, either by consumers filling out and submitting an application or by sponsors selecting and enrolling consumers. Consumer transaction involved interaction with a merchant. Depending on SCR placement, either the merchant or consumer would swipe the card through the SCR after verifying the transaction amount. The SCR would deduct the amount from the consumer’s card, and add it into the merchant’s SCR memory. No password or signature was required. Replenishment involved transferring funds from a demand deposit account to the card. A consumer could use either a reloading station or a bank teller, both of which were located at the sponsoring banks’ branch locations within the trial area. We structure development of our theoretical propositions on these three procedures, while stressing the latter two since they occur with greater frequency.

Innovation theory would suggest that favorable perceptions of smart card technology’s relative advantages over cash should be associated with greater consumer acceptance of the technology. Cash handling may be cumbersome, because it may require multiple retrievals, must be counted, and usually requires some change return. However, when using a smart card, a consumer retrieves the card, swipes it through the SCR device, and confirms the amount. Presenting several advantages during a transaction, the smart card requires handing over one item instead of numerous bills and coins, entails no counting of money, and reduces the chance of error. Collectively, these advantages should increase the ease and reduce the time to transact in frequently occurring small purchase occasions, yielding overall greater convenience to the consumer. To the extent that a consumer perceives greater convenience, then that consumer should be inclined to accept the technology. We offer the following proposition.

Proposition C1. Consumer perceptions of smart card technology’s favorable impact on convenience (relative advantage) should be positively associated with acceptance of smart card technology.

Innovation theory would suggest that favorable perceptions of smart card technology’s complexity should be associated with greater consumer acceptance of the technology. One potential source of complexity may lie in the replenishment procedure. Similar to cash replenishment through ATM withdrawls, a consumer must regularly replenish the card’s stored value. Thus, while there is no difference between the card and cash in terms of replenishment procedure requirements, the reloading stations were much less numerous and more difficult to locate than common ATMs. To the extent that consumers viewed card replenishment as comparatively problematic, they may perceive added complexity. Thus, we argue that where consumer perceptions are adversely impacted by problematic replenishment procedures, their acceptance will be negatively impacted. We offer the following proposition.

Proposition C2. Consumer perceptions of the difficulty associated with replenishing the smart card (complexity) should be negatively associated with acceptance of smart card technology.

Innovation theory would suggest that favorable perceptions of smart card technology’s compatibility should be associated with greater consumer acceptance of the technology. While sponsors expressly promoted the smart card as a substitute for cash, we believe that consumers’ perceptions regarding compatibility may be affected by their prior experiences involving credit or debit cards. For example smart cards are nearly identical in size and shape to credit and debit cards, and the transaction procedures are similar. Due to these similarities, we argue that consumers who use credit or debit cards will be more inclined to perceive smart cards as a compatible payment method, consequently they will be more inclined to accept smart card technology. We offer the following proposition.

Proposition C3. Consumer use of credit and debit cards (compatibility) should be positively associated with acceptance of smart card technology.

## 3.1.2. Merchant perspective

Merchants’ use of the SCR also involved three distinct procedures—installation, transaction and reconciliation. Assisted by the sponsors, merchants had to install and setup the SCR device before they could accept smart cards. The transaction procedure called for either the merchant or consumer to swipe the card through the SCR, and the consumer would invariably confirm the amount. Finally, reconciliation involved transferring the stored value from the SCR device to the merchant’s bank account. Reconciliation was an additional procedure, typically performed at closing when cash was counted for deposit purposes. As we develop our propositions, we refer to all three procedures while stressing the latter two since they occur with greater frequency.

Innovation theory would suggest that favorable perceptions of smart card technology’s relative advantages over cash should be associated with greater merchant acceptance of the technology. The smart card potentially streamlines the transaction procedure by eliminating the need for cash handling, which should lead to at least two advantages. First, there exists potential for reducing average transaction time because cash counting by consumer and merchant is eliminated. Second, less cash handling renders less error potential when counting and accounting for cash during transaction or reconciliation procedures, respectively. Shorter average transaction time and reduced error occurrence may lead to overall cost savings, such as effecting decreased staffing requirements for example. To summarize, we argue that merchants should realize transaction time reduction, error reduction, and some overall cost benefit yielding several relative advantages over cash. To the extent that merchants realize these effects, then they should be inclined to accept smart technology. We submit the following proposition.

Proposition M1. Merchant perceptions of smart card technology’s favorable impact on transaction time, error reduction and costs (relative advantage) should be positively associated with acceptance of smart card technology.

Innovation theory would suggest that favorable perceptions of smart card technology’s complexity should be associated with greater merchant acceptance of the technology. From initial one-time installation to ongoing transaction and reconciliation, all procedures involve operation of the SCR device. Thus, we assume that merchants’ experiences with the SCR will largely determine their perceptions of the technology’s complexity. To the extent that SCR procedures proceed easily and reliably, then the technology should not be viewed as overly complex. Under these conditions, merchants should be more inclined to accept the technology. We submit the following proposition.

Proposition M2. Merchant perceptions of smart card technology’s easy and reliable operation (complexity) should be positively associated with acceptance of smart card technology.

Innovation theory would suggest that favorable perceptions of smart card technology’s compatibility should be associated with greater merchant acceptance of the technology. We take the position that the smart card is ostensibly similar to debit and credit cards in transaction procedure, even though debit cards require PIN codes, credit cards require signature verification and both require a telecommunication link. Thus, merchants experience with credit and debit cards may arguably affect their perceptions of how compatible smart card technology is to existing transaction procedure. To the extent that smart card technology is perceived as compatible to existing business practices, merchants should be more inclined to accept the technology. Accordingly, we submit the following proposition.

Proposition M3. Merchant use of credit and debit cards (compatibility) should be positively associated with acceptance of smart card technology.

We find that the adoption of some technological innovations cannot be fully explained by innovation theory. This is particularly true for technologies where their associated benefit or value is dependent on the number of others who use the same technology. Information technologies that fit this characterization include spreadsheets [12,13], electronic data interchange (EDI) [14], object-oriented programming languages [15], and various interactive media such as email and computer conferencing [16]. Under circumstances where the inherent benefit imparted to an individual is dependent on collective action, some have argued for an expanded theoretical view that includes consideration for potential critical mass effects [14,15].<sup>4</sup>

## 3.2. Critical mass theory

Critical mass theory inclusively refers to various literature streams that speak to the same phenomenon—the notion that an individual’s benefit derived (or denied) from using an innovation is dependent on the size of a participating community. For this study, one relevant aspect of critical mass theory is the notion of network externalities (or effects). While network externalities may be derived through various sources, Fichman and Kemerer [15] and Katz and Shapiro [17] identify the number of potential adopters as one source. Sharing this conceptual view, Shurmer [13] bifurcates network externalities into direct and indirect parts. He argues that direct network externalities derive benefits that are ‘‘. . . generated from the growing number of users adopting the product’’ (p. 232). Many have argued that various communication media are susceptible to these direct network externalities, where the benefit derived from using the communication medium is directly proportional to the number of adopters. The telephone is a common example of a technology that is prone to direct network externalities, while email, document exchange (fax) and videoconferencing are more contemporary examples.

In contrast to direct network externalities, Shurmer also identifies ‘‘. . . indirect network externalities (that) are derived from features extrinsic to the product, which increase with the number of adopters, and which add value to the product’’ (p. 232). These indirect effects may occur where complementary products are involved. Examples include credit cards and credit card reader devices, VHS tapes and VHS recordingplaying machines, DVD discs and DVD drives, and high definition television sets (HDTV) and HDTV broadcasting among many others. Using the last example, we offer that the benefit in using an HDTV set is proportional to the number of HDTV broadcasters. Moreover, this relationship is interdependent in that the benefit in broadcasting HDTV signals is proportional to the number of households having HDTV sets. In many cases where complementary products are prone to indirect network externalities, two interdependent constituencies are involved.

We offer that smart card technology fits this characterization, because the consumer and merchant constituencies are interdependent with respect to obtaining benefit from two complementary products—the smart card and SCR. Each group obtains benefit when members of the other group increase, because the potential utility of the card or SCR is increased. In short, while we do not preclude the possibility of direct network externalities, we argue that any inherent benefits derive largely through indirect network externalities. In sum, we take the position that smart card technology may be prone to critical mass effects of the nature specified as indirect network externalities in Shurmer [13]. Thus, for consumers (merchants) the indirect network externalities—and attending critical mass effects, play out largely in terms of perceptions related to wide scale merchant (consumer) acceptance.

Proposition C4. Consumer satisfaction with the number of merchants that accept smart card technology should be positively associated with consumer acceptance of smart card technology.

Proposition M4. Merchant satisfaction with the number of consumers that use smart card technology should be positively associated with merchant acceptance of smart card technology.

## 4. Research methods

We describe the research setting and procedures.

## 4.1. Research setting

The trial area was defined by two postal zip code areas in Manhattan’s upper-west side neighborhood, among the most densely populated areas in the United States. The sponsors believed that high population density would provide for a large number of potential merchant and consumer adopters, thereby improving the likelihood of attaining critical mass [18]. Sponsors also knew that the consumer population was affluent [19], and they believed it consisted of ‘‘early technology adopters’’ [20]. Identified from the sponsors’ demand deposit account databases, approximately 50,000 consumers and 600 merchants who resided in the trial area were invited to participate in the trial [21].

Sponsors invested tens of millions of US\$ in developing and promoting this smart card technology [22]. As part of the promotion effort, subsidies were provided to both the consumers and merchants [19,21,23]. Merchants received the SCR at no cost, which yielded a value between US\$ 300 and 600. Consumers were provided the card with US\$ 25 stored value and a reader device for free, and could reload the card at reloading stations. These stations were reconfigured ATMs in most cases, and all were located within the trial area. No transaction fees was incurred by either group during the trial.

## 4.2. Research procedures

These data were collected as part of an earlier study, which had alternative research objectives. The research procedures were designed for this earlier study.

## 4.2.1. Pilot survey

Based on a review of industry-trade publications, separate surveys were created for consumers and merchants. While the surveys’ format, length and scales were generally similar, wording modifications were necessary on some items due to the perspective differences. Three merchants participated in the pilot survey. Minor change to the wording of several items and the addition of one item were warranted. No change to any scale was necessary. Although additional piloting of the survey may have been beneficial, we decided against further pilot tests since the suggested changes were relatively minor, and we believed that the literature review was sufficient for establishing content validity. Only the merchant survey was tested, because sponsors disallowed branch access from the research team as a means to identify participating consumers. Identifying participating consumers through arbitrary selection of pedestrians in the trial area was wholly unsuccessful.

## 4.2.2. Sampling

Because the trial area was small and densely populated and merchants were identified by a window logo, a team of research representatives canvassed the area in order to establish contact with merchants.<sup>5</sup> After assessing willingness to participate, the representative would interview the merchant and record the answers. The interview normally lasted about 10 minutes. About 80% of merchants agreed to participate, and most nonparticipating merchants declined because the on-duty person felt uninformed about the technology. We obtained 96 merchant responses. A list of merchant types is provided in Appendix A.

Due to the difficulties associated with identifying consumers during the pilot, the sponsors allowed access to branch sites by one research team member. We established random contact with consumers as they visited reloading stations at five select branches. Similar to data collection procedures involving the merchants, the researcher interviewed the consumer after requesting permission. About 40% of consumers agreed to talk with the researcher, and about 50% of these consumers used the smart card, therefore about 20% of consumers participated in the survey. We obtained 72 consumer responses.

## 4.2.3. Measurement items

The measurement items as they appeared on the survey are shown in Appendix B. All measures use a five-point Likert scale unless indicated otherwise. For consumers’ perceptions concerning relative advantage, C-Adv measured consumers’ judgment about the convenience of using the smart card over cash in small purchase occasions. C-Complex measured the ease in finding reloading stations. The complexity item is reverse-coded therefore higher values mean less complexity. Consumers’ perception of compatibility was measured with two binomial scales that recorded whether a credit card or debit card had been used in the past week. Each item was coded as ‘1’ if the consumer had used the respective card, otherwise it was coded ‘0’. C-Compatible is computed as the sum of these two items. C-Mass asked respondents to indicate their general satisfaction with the number of merchants that accepted the smart card. Finally, C-Accept measured consumers’ willingness to invest in equipment for add-on capabilities, which we use as a surrogate for acceptance.

For merchants, relative advantage was measured through three items. M-Adv1, M-Adv2 and M-Adv3 asked about merchants’ perceptions concerning the impact on the cost, error and time associated with processing transactions, respectively. Complexity was measured with two items. M-Complex1 captured how easy it was to install SCR devices, and M-Complex2 measured how reliably the SCR devices operated. The complexity measures are reverse coded therefore higher values mean less complexity. M-Compatible asked merchants to indicate all accepted payment methods besides cash and smart cards. Among these options, credit cards and debit cards were included. Merchants were coded as ‘0’ if they accepted neither, ‘1’ if they accepted either, and ‘2’ if they accepted both. As a measure for critical mass effects, M-Mass asked merchants to indicate what percentage of consumer purchases US\$ <10 was being made with the smart card. Rather than having the merchant specify a percent figure, the scale was nominalized to four response items in order to ease the task of responding. The response items included <10, 10–25, 25–50 and >50%. M-Accept asked whether merchants would be willing to buy the necessary SCR device, which we use as a surrogate for merchant acceptance.

## 6. Discussion

The main finding shows that relative advantage was a significant predictor of consumers’ and merchants acceptance. Provided that consumers and merchants were not accepting of this technology, and that consumers and merchants were neutral towards and disagreeing with statements concerning the technology’s relative advantages, respectively, these results offer a possible explanation behind the trial’s failure. While relative advantages are important to gain consumers and merchants’ acceptance, this smart card technology was not favorably rated on the relative advantages that actually resulted. This is consistent with other studies that showed that merchants believed cash-handling costs actually increased after adopting the technology, and that only a small percentage of consumers felt that the smart cards provided any time savings [1]. As a consequence, consumers and merchants may have been disinclined to accept this smart card technology. It may be beneficial for sponsors to critically evaluate the relative advantages provided by this smart card technology vis-a\`-vis cash.

Notwithstanding the fact that critical mass effects played no role in predicting acceptance for either group, neither groups’ responses suggests that wide scale use occurred. C-Mass was 2.94, indicating that consumers were largely neutral in their response to the number of merchants taking the smart card. A frequency distribution on M-Mass indicated that 68% of merchants had <10% of transactions under US\$ 10 committed with the smart card, and 88% had <25% committed with the smart card. These findings are consistent with the anecdotal evidence that suggests a failed attempt to reach critical mass [1].

One explanation for why the trial failed to reach critical mass relates to Granovetter’s work on thresholds of collective behavior. Granovetter [25] argues that individuals will vary in terms of a theoretical threshold that determines a minimum required number of participants before an individual will join the collective action. Applied in this context, Granovetter’s model would suggest that consumers (merchants) will vary in terms of the number of required participating merchants (consumers) before they are willing to use the smart card technology. While some use occurred, suggesting that those with low thresholds joined in, evidently usage did not reach levels where a majority of consumers’ and merchants’ thresholds were met. Thus, critical mass effects did not set in and ultimately were not attained. Markus [6] cites situations where a critical mass of adopters is not reached, and the innovation enters a period of subsequent decline and cessation—the precise outcome that occurred.

## 6.1. Trial declared failure

In the end, merchants found that a small percentage of transactions occurred with smart cards. A large supermarket that had approximately 50,000 weekly transactions processed only seven to ten with smart cards on average [19]. More generally, the overall dollar volume of smart card purchases was US\$ <2 million dollars in the first year [26]. A general consensus emerged among the sponsors that it would be extremely difficult to attain wide scale use. After the media declared the trial a failure, the sponsors announced that the trial would be discontinued. Shortly afterwards, merchants’ and consumers’ use dropped off rapidly. These were disappointing results, provided that sponsors had publicized this trial as the first test of general use smart card technology in the United States [26], and had held high expectations for attaining critical mass [27].

This trial represents a situation where an innovation is acquired, but its widespread use does not follow. Fichman and Kemerer [28] refer to this phenomenon as an assimilation gap. They identify knowledge barriers and critical mass effects as predisposing an innovation to an assimilation gap.<sup>7</sup> Unlike technologies such as CASE, relational databases, fourth generation and object-oriented programming languages [28–31], we argue that high knowledge barriers do not apply here. The data showed that merchants and consumers judged the smart card technology as simple to use, consequently high knowledge barriers should not have impeded the card’s wide scale use. Critical mass effects may offer an explanation for the assimilation gap however, despite the lack of empirical support in our data.

Drawing on anecdotal sources, we found several reasons behind the sponsors’ failed attempt to reach critical mass. First, the limited geographic area had adverse consequences for consumers [26]. Although consumers lived within the trial area, many traveled outside to work or school on a regular basis [20,22]. A sponsor representative commented that ‘‘. . . some consumers were unable to use the cards in areas where they may work . . ..’’ Thus, consumers were outside the trial area for a large part of each day, thereby unable to transact with the card. Second, while the cards were of the general use kind in that they were accepted by several hundred merchants, some key merchant sectors such as transportation (taxis and subways), communication (pay phones), and retail (vending machines, kiosks, newspaper stands) were not included in the trial. This may have contributed to overall low consumer use, because some frequent and common purchases could not be made with the card. Similar results occurred in the Atlanta trial [26,32]. The sponsors conceded that future trials, where card use is constrained by either geographic area or merchant sector, are unlikely to reach critical mass [33,23].

Third, idiosyncratic technical problems involving the reconciliation procedure led to some merchant dissatisfaction [21]. The SCR device proved unreliable during reconciliation for some, which caused some merchants to remove the device altogether [22]. Moreover, sponsors were not generally responsive to these technical problems during the trial’s early period, and some problems occurred due to inadequate training in proper procedure [21]. Whether technical or trainingrelated, problems involving critical payment systems are necessary to prevent because a few instances will likely prompt rapid merchant withdrawal. Fourth, substantial behavior modification must occur for consumers to regularly substitute smart cards for cash in small purchase occasions [3]. At the end, sponsors realized unexpected psychological resistance and entrenched behavior patterns, which will require time to overcome [21]. Education and advertising programs may be instrumental for effecting behavior modification.

## 6.2. Future of smart card technology

In surveying the future of smart card technology, we offer that sponsors’ efforts may be usefully directed to several aspects of technical, product and marketing strategy development. First, concerning technical development, the sponsors reportedly used this trial to learn, as noted by one sponsor representative who indicated that ‘‘. . . our expectations were really modest. We were using this as a learning process to . . . make sure that we understood the whole process of introducing smart cards.’’ One learning experience involved the successful integration of the sponsors’ back-end systems [33].<sup>8</sup> This was a notable accomplishment, despite some other technical shortcomings such as those related to merchants’ reconciliation procedure. We may expect that future trials will be partly initiated and executed for promoting further technical advancement [22]. Under circumstances involving trial implementations of mission-critical systems, any sponsor is advised to have organizational support systems in place in order to rapidly respond to and rectify problems that do occur.

Second, product development may be beneficial to rethink the concept. Before the trial began, the sponsors shared the common assumption that consumers view cash as inconvenient. However, there are indications that consumers may be more inclined to use smart card technology as a substitute for coins rather than paper bills [3], which may be explained by the weight, bulkiness and clumsiness of coins relative to their value [26]. One sponsor representative commented that, ‘‘. . .useful consumer applications . . . might include laundry machines and newspapers, which would eliminate the need for exact or small amounts of change.’’ In short, the sponsors learned that cash is not uniformly perceived as inconvenient [32], and that smart card technology’s perceived convenience may vary according to comparison to paper bills or metal coins. Consequently, some product development efforts may usefully focus on ways to promote smart card use as a substitute specifically for coins.

Besides several general use debit and credit cards, an average consumer often has numerous special use cards as well. For instance, an average consumer may carry charge cards for department stores and gas station chains, enrollment cards for video checkout and health care plan, and identification cards for driving privilege and workplace premise access. The accumulation of general and special use cards may cause some consumers to experience ‘‘card-overload’’, leading to disuse or loss. As a means to avoid card-overload, some have suggested that smart card capabilities be built into a multi-purpose card that acts as a credit, debit and smart card [34,32]. The multi-purpose card concept could conceivably be extended to include special use cards as well. Consequently, product development efforts may usefully explore ways to fold the smart card capabilities into the multi-purpose card concept.

Finally, marketing strategy development may be needed in order to differentiate smart cards from credit and debit cards. It was reported that some consumers would not participate in the trial, because they did not understand how smart cards were different from credit and debit cards [23]. If merchants and consumers do not perceive substantive practical or financial differences between smart cards and debit or credit cards, they will be disinclined to use smart cards in lieu of cards that are more widely accepted. For example, one difference involves the relatively high transaction cost overhead that causes debit and credit cards to be prohibitively expensive in small purchase occasions [3,4]. In raising these practical and financial differences to consumers’ and merchants’ awareness, sponsors may usefully develop and implement advertising programs as one marketing strategy option. Another option may involve subsidies in order to promote use among early adopters until a critical mass is reached [3,12]. While various subsidies were used in this trial, they evidently were not sufficient to counteract the forces working against critical mass attainment.

## 6.3. Limitations

The data were collected after the initial adoption decision therefore they are retrospective. This may introduce measurement error, because respondents assessment of the innovation may be affected by their use of it. In addition, we study only one innovation here. Tornatzky and Klein [9] suggest that single innovation studies are not sufficiently robust to generalize to the population of innovations. However, our objective was not to test innovation theory, but to advance our understanding of smart card technology.

Another potential limitation relates to the sampling method. The surveys were completed in face-to-face interviews, which may have created reluctance on behalf of consumers and merchants to convey adverse or critical information in a face-to-face encounter [35]. Thus, merchants and consumers who declined to participate may have been unfavorably predisposed toward the technology. To the extent any selection bias occurred, our data would yield favorable results compared to the population of trial participants.

In addition, we have single-item measures for some of the theoretical constructs. Single-item measures lead to less reliable and valid measurement of the underlying construct, because respondents may interpret the same measurement item differently [36]. Multi-item measures offer one means to mitigate this measurement bias. In this paper, we retrofit the theoretical constructs onto existing measures and data set, yielding one measure for some theoretical constructs. While admittedly contrary to ideal research practice, we feel that our research effort nonetheless holds merit because the findings inform about an important management objective concerning smart card technology.

## 7. Conclusions

We conclude that the relative advantages associated with this smart card technology were instrumental in gaining consumers’ and merchants’ acceptance of it, and that poor to neutral evaluation of the technology’s relative advantages vis-a\`-vis cash may have contributed to the trial’s failure. While the research design prevents us from conclusively showing a causal relationship between relative advantage and acceptance, our results are consistent with one.

While we cannot form any conclusion concerning the role of critical mass effects, trial sponsors obtained consensus in their belief that reaching a critical mass of consumers and merchants will be extremely difficult, albeit essential for attaining wide scale use. We offer that sponsors’ efforts may be usefully directed to several aspects of technical, product and marketing strategy development as alternative means toward attaining critical mass.
