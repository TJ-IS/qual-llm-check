# An assessment of the relationship between ISD leadership characteristics and IS innovation adoption in organizations

Srinarayan Sharma<sup>a,\*</sup>, Arun Rai<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Decision and Information Sciences Department, School of Business Administration, Oakland University, Rochester, MI 48309, USA

<sup>b</sup>eCommerce Institute, College of Business Administration, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303, USA

Received 30 March 2001; received in revised form 15 January 2002; accepted 23 April 2002

## Abstract

This study examines the relationship between IS innovation adoption and leadership characteristics in information systems departments (ISDs); computer-aided software engineering (CASE) is the specific innovation considered here, with positional power and job tenure of the ISD leader the prime leadership characteristics. A sample of top IS executives in US organizations was the object of the survey. Discriminant analysis was used to analyze the collected data. Contrary to the commonly held belief in the innovation diffusion literature, we found that both positional power and job tenure of the ISD leader are negatively related to organizational adoption of the IS innovation.

Keywords: Organizational innovation diffusion; IS innovation; ISD leadership; CASE; Individual characteristics

## 1. Introduction

Computer-aided software engineering (CASE) is a software technology that brings an engineering discipline to the systems development, maintenance, and project management processes, through the use of automated tools and techniques. It has been viewed by both researchers and practitioners as a potential means to cut development and maintenance cost, ensure quality, reduce development time, and increase productivity of IS [15,20,27]. However, few organizations use CASE on a regular basis in spite of the dismal state of software development in many organizations [52]. According to a recent study [53], only 16.2% of software development projects are completed on-time and within budget. A majority (52%) of these projects cost 190% over their initial budget estimate, are completed 220% over original time estimate, and incorporate only 61% of originally specified features and functions.

Low adoption of CASE is troublesome and the importance of looking into factors leading to CASE adoption can hardly be overemphasized. Identification of factors that characterize adopter organizations has also been one of the major issues in innovation diffusion literature [14,47,60]. Identification of such factors should lead to their proactive management in organizations and facilitate tool adoption within the organization.

Many variables have been identified in past studies of innovation. In a widely cited work, Kwon and Zmud [30] classified these variables in five broad categories:

individual, structural, technological, task-related, and environmental factors. While many past studies have examined the last four of these, few have investigated individual factors in the context of organizational diffusion of IT innovations. Leonard-Barton and Deschamps [35] also noted that research on innovations within organizations has paid little attention to individual variables during implementation. Successful implementation of an innovation depends on initiatives of individuals, particularly those in the leadership positions.

In this paper, we focus on CASE adoption and its relationship with selected individual factors. In particular, we examine the relationship between CASE adoption and characteristics of information systems departments (ISDs) leadership in organizations. Specifically, the leadership characteristics considered are job tenure of IS leaders and their positional power in the organizational hierarchy. We are unaware of previous studies in the areas that have examined such leadership characteristics.

A long-standing criticism of diffusion research has been the inconsistency of its findings [13,37,40]. According to Iivari, theoretical and methodological weaknesses of CASE research may have been a major cause of these contradictory findings in the past. A majority of systems development research is normative in nature and based on anecdotal evidence [63]. Small sample sizes have further limited the generalizability of the findings [42]. We drew on IS implementation and innovation studies and collected data from a large-scale randomized national survey to examine the relationship between CASE adoption and characteristics of ISD leadership.

## 2. Research model and hypotheses

Although there are numerous studies on IS implementation using innovation diffusion theory in the IS literature (e.g. [5,7,18,19,32,33,43,45]), three are widely cited: Rogers [48], Kwon and Zmud [30], and Tornatzky and Fleischer [60]. We started with Kwon and Zmud’s IS implementation framework, and integrated this with the other two to derive a theoretical framework for our study.

Kwon and Zmud’s model integrates literature on IS implementation and innovation diffusion and provides a cogent framework, which was subsequently refined by Cooper and Zmud [7]. According to this framework, IT implementation is an organizational effort directed toward diffusing appropriate information technology within a user community. Organizational diffusion is a multi-stage process starting with initiation and progressing through adoption, adaptation, acceptance, routinization, and infusion. The four postadoption stages indicate an increasing level of assimilation of the innovation in the organization.

An innovation is initiated when an organization recognizes either a need for change, usually triggered by a problem or opportunity (need-pull) or when internal or external forces promote a new technology that promises to enhance organizational performance (technology-push) [64]. Initiation involves scanning, gathering and evaluating information regarding problems, opportunities, and technology capabilities, and finding a match between problems/opportunities and technological solution. Under a rational model of innovation progression, initiation leads to adoption if a proper fit is found between organizational needs and technological capabilities. Adoption involves rational and political negotiations to get organizational backing and a decision to invest resources necessary to accommodate the change-effort associated with the introduction and use of technology to solve organizational problems or to take advantage of new opportunities.

Many variables have been identified which influence different stages of this model. Kwon and Zmud classify these in five broad categories: individual, structural, technological, task-related factors, and environmental factors. Rogers has shown organizational innovativeness to be related to such independent variables as individual (leader) characteristics, internal organizational structural characteristics (such as centralization, complexity, formalization, interconnectedness, organizational slack, size), and external organizational characteristics (e.g. system openness). Tornatzky and Fleischer put forward three elements of an organization’s context by which it adopts and implements technological innovations: organizational, technological, and environmental context.

In any organizational innovation diffusion process, there are many decision makers playing a variety of roles, such as idea generator, process/product champion, and management. Process or product champion and gatekeepers have received much attention in the literature. Although the roles of champion are well articulated, little attention has been given to their characteristics, though their roles and characteristics are exceedingly important. Duchesneau and Dutton [11] suggest that many ‘‘individual difference’’ variables may actually operate as proxy variables, i.e. measurable quantities that represent more fundamental processes. In this paper, we focus on selected individual characteristics of IS leadership and their relationship with CASE adoption.

Past research has shown that organizational position and role of the individuals taking leadership in the diffusion process may influence organization’s innovative behavior [3]. This is consistent with the findings that people who allocate organizational resources influence innovation adoption [21,50,62]. Here, we examine two specific variables that may relate to the ability of the ISD leader to allocate resources for innovation: job tenure of the ISD leader and positional power held by the ISD leader within the organization.

## 2.1. Positional power of ISD leader

An organization is a system of individuals who work together to achieve common goals through a hierarchy of ranks and a division of labor [49]. ‘‘Organizations are created to handle large-scale routine tasks through a pattern of regularized human relationships’’ [47]. Their efficiency as a means of orchestrating human endeavors is, in part, a result of stability, which stems from the relatively high degree of structure that is imposed on communication patterns. Among other things, a predictable organizational structure is obtained through prescribed roles and authority structure.

‘‘Organizational tasks are distributed among the various positions as roles or duties. A role is a set of activities to be performed by an individual occupying a given position. Positions are the ‘‘boxes’’ on an organizational chart. Individuals may come and go in an organization, but the positions continue, as do the roles expected of individuals filling the positions’ [48]. In formal organizations positions do not have equal authority; they are organized in a hierarchical authority structure that specifies who is responsible to whom, and who can give orders to whom.

Executives in high-level positions have more influence in the allocation of resources necessary to promote innovation [41]. Unfortunately, top leaders are poorly positioned to identify operational-level problems or to suggest relevant innovation to meet these needs [36]. On the other hand, the lower level executives in IS organizations are closely involved with the development of different IS. They are more familiar with the inherent problems associated with their development and maintenance. Since CASE helps solve many of these problems, the executives at lower level are more likely to adopt CASE than those at the top. Hence, we hypothesize, in the null form.

H1<sub>0</sub>. CASE adoption by the ISD is not related to the positional power of the ISD leader.

## 2.2. Job tenure of ISD leader

Job tenure of an organizational member is generally related to institutional legitimacy. An organizational member who has been a member of the organization for a longer period of time has increased functional and/or political knowledge. If that organizational member is in a position of leadership, he or she has more say in allocating resources to projects than a person who has been with an organization for less time.

CASE tools are relatively costly, and associated personnel, training, and infrastructure costs are still higher [26]. For CASE adoption to occur, adequate resources need to be allocated. Consistent with resource-based organizational theory, an ISD leader with longer job tenure is more likely to allocate resources towards CASE adoption.

On the other hand, organizational decision-making theory has shown that, among successful managers, those who are older and have longer tenure take fewer risks and are less innovative than those who are younger and have shorter tenure. In the innovation diffusion literature, job tenure has not been found to be related to innovation [9,29,34].

Hence, we hypothesize in the null form.

$\mathbf { H } 2 _ { \mathbf { 0 } } .$ CASE adoption by the ISD is not related to the job tenure of the ISD leader.

## 2.3. Control variables

## 2.3.1. Size

Past studies on the relationship between organizational size and adoption behavior has shown equivocal results. Many studies have found that larger organizations are more innovative due to their flexibility to absorb more risk (the so-called Schumpeter hypothesis) [12,38, 46,61]. Others have found size unrelated to IT adoption [2]. A few studies have shown that the increased flexibility of smaller firms facilitates innovativeness [17]. We include organizational size in our model as a control variable to eliminate its confounding effect.

## 2.3.2. Industry membership

Past research has also shown that industry membership influences innovative behavior [65]. Bretschneider and Wittmer [4] provide evidence of difference in the rate of adoption of microcomputer technology across industry sectors. Hence, we include industry membership as another control variable.

## 3. Methodology

Consistent with the majority of the past work on innovation diffusion [59], a survey methodology was used to collect data. An initial version of the survey instrument was based on the theoretically grounded operationalization of various constructs advocated by Churchill [6] and Nunnally [39]. This version was subsequently refined by pretesting the survey with eight academics having significant experience in the use of CASE to ensure content coverage of the constructs and clarity of the questions asked. Their suggestions were incorporated in the survey instrument. The instrument was further pilot tested with the heads of IS departments of four different organizations located in four cities in the Midwest. The multiple phases of instrument development and testing resulted in refinement and restructuring of the survey instrument, as well as in establishing the initial content validity [8,28].

## 3.1. Data collection

The sample was selected from the ‘‘Directory of Top Computer Executives’’ maintained by Applied Computer Research Inc., Phoenix, Arizona. This database was selected because it is one of the most diversified database about IS organizations and their IS executives in the US and it includes a variety of firms, from the very small to the very large. Indeed it claims to keep a record of top IS executives of all organizations in the US.

Since resources were limited, a single-informant strategy was adopted. Huber and Power [25] argue that if only one informant per organization is to be targeted, that person most knowledgeable about the focus of the research interest should be selected. Since our research focuses on positional power of the IS leader, his or her job tenure, and CASE adoption decision for the IS organization, the head of the IS department was considered to be the most suitable subject.

The study questionnaire was mailed to a random sample of 1582 top IS executives from a list of approximately 34,000 executives in about 15,000 firms. About 3 weeks later, a follow-up mailing was sent to executives who did not respond. Of the 1582 questionnaires, 22 were returned because the respondents were no longer employed at the respective organizations. Of the remaining 1560 questionnaires, 360 were returned, for a response rate of 23.1%. Of these, 10 were not usable. Thus, the final sample for this study was 350.

## 3.2. Sample profile

Respondent were typically IT directors or MIS managers and had been with the organization on average for 7.6 years (see Table 1). A little more than half of the organizations they represented were in the manufacturing sector, while the service sector accounted for about a little above one-third (see Table 2). The rest were in the government sector. The median annual sales revenue of the organizations in the sample was US\$ 155 million. These organizations employed a median of 14.5 employees and had 11.5 active projects, of which 35% was development related and 60% was maintenance/enhancement related. Furthermore, median value for the duration of an ISD leader’s employment in these organizations was 6 years. Finally, the median value for the time elapsed (when the data were collected) since these organizations had experimented with CASE was 6 years. Out of these 350 organizations, 46 were using CASE at the time of the survey, 59 had considered using it at one point in time, but did not use it, and 245 had not even considered using it.

## 3.4. Dependent variable

The dependent variable adoption decision was operationalized as a dichotomous variable (0: no, 1: yes). The respondents were given a definition of CASE adoption decision (see Appendix A) and asked to indicate if their organization had a made a decision to adopt CASE.

## 3.5. Control variables

Consistent with past measures, organizational size was measured by the number of people in the information system department, as CASE is usually adopted by the IS department [44]. The respondents were asked to provide the number of people in their ISD (see Table 5 for the distribution of organizations by ISD size). They were also asked to provide their type of industry. To simplify analysis, these types were consolidated in manufacturing, service, and government sectors.

## 4. Data analysis and results

Discriminant analysis was used to test the hypothesized relationships. The major purpose of this is to predict group membership on the basis of a number of predictor variables. It involves deriving the best linear combination of predictor variables to maximize differences among groups. The objective is to maximize between-group variance relative to withingroup variance. If multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) shows that group membership produces significant differences on a combination of variables, then using discriminant function analysis that combination of variables can be used to discriminate among groups [31,57]. The MANOVA showed that the combined predictor variables significantly differentiate ‘‘adopters’’ and ‘‘non-adopters’’ (Wilks’ l ¼ 0:91, $F ( 4 , 3 2 7 ) = 8 . 1 3 , P = 0 . 0 0 )$ . Since MANOVA shows significant multivariate effects, the nature of the relationships between the independent and dependent variables was further investigated using discriminant analysis.

To interpret the relative predictive power of each independent variable, the discriminant loadings are considered relatively more valid than standardized coefficients [22]. While there are no rigid rules about the goodness of these values, the general guidelines are that values above 0.25–0.30 are satisfactory and acceptable. ## 5. Discussion

Three variables—ISD size, job tenure of the ISD leader and positional power of the ISD leader— emerged as important variables in the discriminant analysis between adopters and non-adopters of CASE.

## 5.1. Control variables

An examination of the mean value for ISD size reveals that adopters had significantly larger ISD size (121 employees) than non-adopters (52). It reinforces the findings in innovation and IT diffusion literature of the importance of organizational size. Larger ISDs have more resources and better infrastructure to facilitate adoption of CASE technology. The larger ISD may also have more slack resource to expend. The larger organizations are also better able to absorb more risks.

Contrary to the findings of Bretschneider and Wittmer [4] and Zmud [64,65], the industry sector, was not found to be more likely to adopt CASE. Aggregation of the industry sectors into manufacturing, government, and service categories may have contributed to this result.

## 5.2. Positional power of the ISD leader and IT adoption

Hypothesis $\mathrm { H } 1 _ { 0 }$ was rejected. Positional power of the ISD leader was found significant in differentiating adopter organizations from non-adopters. An examination of Table 7 shows that the innovativeness seems to be associated more with the proximity of the ISD leader with the systems work than to the top of the organizational hierarchy, and hence, their ability to find and allocate resources. Past research has shown that IS leaders must have high levels of IT knowledge to facilitate its assimilation. Unfortunately, the closer the IS leaders are to the top of the organizational hierarchy, the less knowledge they have about operational IT issues. They are less familiar with the problems associated with IS development and maintenance. Even though CASE helps solve many of these problems, they do not adopt CASE. On the other hand, the IS leaders farther from the top are more familiar with operational IT issues and the problems associated with developing and maintaining IS, thus, they adopt CASE.

Research in the IS literature has consistently shown that for organizational innovation to be successful it must have top management support. This study shows that if the top management role is examined at a lower level of granularity, CASE does not really have top management backing. That is probably why CASE has ‘‘failed’’ as a software innovation [24].

## 5.3. Job tenure of the ISD leader and IT adoption

Hypothesis $\mathrm { H } 2 _ { 0 }$ was also rejected. Job tenure of the ISD leader was found significant in discriminating between adopters and non-adopters. ISD leaders in adopter organizations had shorter job tenure (4.7 years) than those in non-adopter organizations (8.0 years). The positive influence of the institutional legitimacy associated with the longer job tenure seems to be outweighed by its negative association with innovativeness. Taylor et al. [58] have shown that managers view lengthening job tenure as a violation of the relational employment contract held with their corporate employers and experience decreased organizational commitment. Executives with longer job tenure may have more psychological commitment to the organizational status quo [1,54,55] and organizational values [51]. As a result, they resist change and are less inclined to engage in innovative activities. Also, the longer one stays in an organization, the more one loses freshness of ideas and the more likely that the mindset becomes that of the organization. That is probably why the reengineering literature recommends that organizations should start their BPR initiative with ‘‘new blood’’ at the helm [10,23].

## 6. Limitations

This study had many limitations; among others, it used as follows.

 A single-respondent sample.

Though this practice is common in IS research, it may produce bias, because of the respondent’s lack of knowledge, leading to guessing or random selection of a score.

 Cross-sectional data that has obvious limitations in explaining causality.

 The respondent’s job title as their position in the organization.

One can argue that titles vary from organization to another. Thus, MIS director of one cannot necessarily be compared with another’s. We contend that while this may be true, we were more concerned with the position of the IS leader in the hierarchy. Organizations typically have chief executives, MIS director, MIS manager, and IS administrator in their organizational hierarchy in that order, so if the hierarchy does not significantly deviate from that order, the findings should still be valid.

 We focused only on the individual variables in this study, at the cost of excluding structural, technological, task-related, and environmental variables. Inclusion of these variables could yield different results.

 One can argue that CASE came into being in a period when IS executives saw IT as helping only with the mundane operational issues such as payroll processing and considered CASE an inevitable expense.

Possibly they did not realize the strategic importance of IS and did not throw their support behind it. It is also possible that once they thought that CASE was not delivering on its promise, they withdrew support. Alternatively, lack of initial support could have led to failure.

 The study used a fairly large random sample, but non-adopters constituted about 87% of them and may have skewed the result. Though the study used CASE, a type 1b IS innovation [56], its findings may not be applicable to other innovations such as database management systems, etc.

## 7. Conclusions

It is imperative that IS theories are robust enough to account for a range of phenomena under their scope. IS research has a tendency toward jumping on the latest bandwagon at the cost of circumventing critical examination of past innovations. Such practice has led to weak theories that explain very little variance. Innovation theories should be applied to both past and present innovations, and to both failed and successful innovations. Such application will lead to more robust theories.

This study has examined a so-called ‘‘failed’’ innovation, CASE using innovation diffusion theory. While many past studies showed that CASE adoption was significantly related to top management support, the data in these studies were collected by asking respondents perceptual questions in survey questionnaires and did not correlate the actual respondent job titles, a more objective measure, with their response on the top management construct.

Here, we have extended the line of inquiry on management support by examining two variables related to the characteristics of the leader of the IS function: positional power of the IS leader and his/her job tenure. We have found that CASE did not garner top management support in our organizations. We found that positional power is inversely related to CASE adoption. We also found that shorter tenure of the ISD leader is associated with higher adoption of CASE.
