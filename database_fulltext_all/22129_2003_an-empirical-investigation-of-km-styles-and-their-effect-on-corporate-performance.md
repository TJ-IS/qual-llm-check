# An empirical investigation of KM styles and their effect on corporate performance

\* Byounggu Choi, Heeseok Lee

Graduate School of Management, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology, 207-43 Cheongryangri-dong, Dongdaemoon-gu, Seoul 130-012, South Korea

Received 25 November 2000; received in revised form 15 January 2002; accepted 21 May 2002

## Abstract

For years, companies have strived to manage knowledge more effectively, the primary motivation being improved business performance. However, not all knowledge management (KM) styles may be equally effective. This paper analyses how various KM styles affect performance. Fifty-four firms were selected as the sample base for an empirical test. The test results are presented here. It was found that KM methods can be categorized into four styles: dynamic, system-, human-oriented, and passive. The emphasis of the dynamic style is on both (i) knowledge reusability through information technologies and (ii) knowledge sharing through informal discussions among employees. This dynamic style results in higher performance. Human- and systemoriented styles do not show any difference in terms of corporate performance; the passive style is less effective. This outcome is in line with previous observations that both tacit and explicit knowledge are important in capitalizing on corporate knowledge. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: KM; KM style; Corporate performance

## 1. Introduction

Recently, many companies have shown an interest in implementing knowledge management (KM) methods [76], but most companies are nervous about initiating their own KM programs. However, KM methods vary depending upon knowledge types and organizational core competence [6,22], but the primary motivation for any company must be improved business performance. Managing knowledge effectively is not a trivial task; few companies are capable of adopting KM methods to improve organizational performance and not all methods are equally effective. Managers should align these methods with their corporate culture.

Management focus is required for developing an advantage over competitors. KM methods can be categorized according to two dimensions of management focus; the first focuses on explicit knowledge and, thus, emphasizes the capability to help create, store, share, and use explicitly documented knowledge, while the second focuses on tacit knowledge and emphasizes knowledge sharing by interpersonal interaction [27,77]. Past studies about these methods can be classified into two camps. Some suggest that companies should pursue either explicit or tacit knowledge predominantly [70]. In contrast, other studies propose that companies manage explicit and tacit knowledge simultaneously [3,32,52].

Many knowledge managers have faced difficulties in employing KM methods because it is still unclear how they improve corporate performance. What catapults a company from good to truly effective? Past studies fail to address the link between KM methods and organizational performance.

This paper addresses KM methods from both explicitand tacit-oriented perspectives. These are investigated empirically and categorized into four styles: dynamic, system-, human-oriented, and passive. The relationship between KM styles and corporate performance is analyzed to capitalize on corporate knowledge.

## 2. Past studies

Nevis et al. studied seven organizational learning orientations and 10 facilitating factors, and attempted to understand knowledge acquisition from both product and process perspectives. They described two directions for enhancing learning capabilities of an organizational unit: to embrace the existing learning style and improve its effectiveness, or to change the learning orientation. Through case studies of various industries, they suggested that each organizational unit be balanced between the two directions.

Bierly and Chakrabarti investigated 21 US pharmaceutical firms from 1977 to 1991 empirically and categorized them into explorers, exploiters, loners, and innovators. Innovators acquire knowledge through exploration and exploitation, while loners are ineffective learners. It was found that innovators and explorers tend to be more profitable.

Jordan and Jones explored the key dimensions of organizational knowledge and developed a framework for assessing a company’s KM methods. Their framework deals with tacit- and explicit-oriented methods. The tacit-oriented method attempts to acquire internal and opportunistic knowledge and share it informally; the other attempts to acquire external and focused knowledge and share it formally. They felt that the right balance between explicit- and tacit-oriented style should be struck in order to utilize innovative ideas with better results.

Hansen et al. investigated management consulting firms and divided their KM methods into codification and personalization. Codification acquires and shares knowledge by reusing it explicitly. In contrast, personalization relies on experts’ interactions. They propose that companies should pursue either method in isolation to utilize corporate knowledge effectively (for example, M consulting firm puts 80% of KM efforts in one method and 20% in the other).

Zack analyzed the knowledge gap between what the firm must know and what it actually knows about managing knowledge. KM is categorized into aggressive and conservative styles. Firms that exploit internal knowledge exhibit a conservative style, while aggressive firms explore knowledge without regard to organizational boundaries. He concluded that the former class of firms tends to outperform the latter.

Swan et al. criticized the IT-driven KM by using two case studies. Cognitive and community styles were presented. The first emphasizes linear information flows and knowledge codifying through IT. The latter emphasizes dialogue and knowledge sharing through social networks, including occupational groups and teams. They felt that the community style was better.

Table 1 compares these studies in terms of KM processes, KM category, research method, industry applications, corporate performance, and the KM suggested. This suggests that the KM problem is very fuzzy. However, it is noted that KM methods are likely to be categorized on the basis of explicit- and tacit-oriented points of view. For example, the codification method of Hansen et al. and the explicit-oriented method by Jordan and Jones are based on explicit knowledge, whereas the personalization method by Hansen et al. and the community approach by Swan et al. are based on tacit knowledge. System-oriented knowledge reusability can sharpen codification, while human-oriented interactions can enhance personalization.

Thus, KM may be categorized into two styles: one attempts to strike a balance between the explicit- and tacit-oriented methods, while the other focuses on one of them, e.g. the balanced method by Nevis et al. or Jordan and Jones, the innovator by Bierly and Chakrabarti, and the aggressive method by Zack seek the right balance. In contrast, the 80–20 split rule by Hansen et al. and the community by Swan et al. stress the usage of one particular method.

However, most studies fail to address the empirical relationship between KM styles and corporate performance. Some studies focus on a particular industry sector such as the consulting or pharmaceutical companies. In contrast, this paper explores the relationship between KM styles and corporate performance. Furthermore, it considers a variety of industries.

## 3. KM styles

Polanyi [57] originally categorized knowledge into two types: tacit and explicit. The latter is easily formalized and documented [16,28]. It is what can be captured and shared through information technology. Tacit knowledge resides in the human mind, behavior, and perception, and thus, it is difficult to be formalized and communicated [54,62]. It evolves from peoples interactions, and requires skills and practices [43,59].

From explicit- and tacit-oriented perspectives, we categorize KM methods into four styles: dynamic, system-, human-oriented, and passive (see Fig. 1). The explicit-oriented level considers the degree of codifying and storage of organizational knowledge needed for a person to access and use it easily. The tacit-oriented level corresponds to the acquisition and sharing of organizational knowledge through personal interaction.

Companies of a passive style show little interest in KM. It is not managed in a systematic manner; organizational structure, culture, or IT is not used for managing knowledge. Firms in this type do not truly exploit knowledge. In the current turbulent business environment, this style reduces the effectiveness of companies quickly.

Companies of a system-oriented style put more emphasis on codifying and reusing knowledge. They increase codifiability through advanced ITs [61,63], and thus, decrease the complexity of accessing and using knowledge [26,47]. Faster response to the customer and lower cost per knowledge transaction are possible [24]. Knowledge is managed and shared in a formal fashion, such as a prioriprocedures, codes, and a working manual [21]. Economies of scale and organizational effectiveness can be obtained by reusing codified knowledge [42,53,56]. Group and standard training programs can enhance management capability. This can eliminate the need for further communication and coordination among organizational members [19].

The emphasis of the human-oriented style is on acquiring and sharing tacit knowledge and interpersonal experience. Knowledge originates from informal social networks; standard procedures may be ignored to discover better ways of operating. Human dimensions are critical for effective KM [38]. Thus, meaningful knowledge may not be simply retrieved from the database or repository [45,72]: it is shared informally, because intimacy among organizational members is important. Communication and trust are critical success factors [29,36]. Organizations tend to seek radical learning abilities and prefer mentoring or storytelling [71]. Strategic communities are archetypal organizational arrangements in this style [17,67,68].

Companies of the dynamic style emphasize both explicit- and tacit-oriented methods, and are thus, integrative and aggressive. They manage tacit and explicit knowledge in a dynamic fashion; they are similar to a communication-intensive organization, as proposed by Blackler [5]. They depend on encultured knowledge and IS are introduced to support group work among individuals separated in time and place [65]. Companies of this dynamic style exploit proven knowledge and explore new potentials [44,58].

## 4. Research objectives and method

## 4.1. Objectives

Two objectives of our empirical investigation were: (i) to validate the four KM styles and (ii) to find any difference in corporate performance depending on these four styles. Most previous studies suggest that corporate performance differs due to different KM styles, and this suggestion may be found in the strategic management field [12,34].

Effective KM may need both exploration and exploitation. Firms that adopt both tend to outperform their competitors. Similarly, integrating explicit- with tacit-oriented methods leads to more innovative outcomes [4]. Therefore, this paper assumes that the dynamic style would result in a better performance, i.e. it sets up the hypothesis that corporate performance depends on KM style.

## 4.2. Sample

Sample size is determined on the basis of statistical power analysis, which depends on three factors: effect size, alpha, sample size (e.g. [25]). Typically, the effect size can be estimated from previous empirical results. However, due to lack of empirical studies in KM, it may be determined through a pilot test and a review of the literature [46,48,75]. The effect size value adopted here is 0.45. We consider two alpha values; 0.05 and 0.1. An adequate power level is usually 0.8. 60 samples are needed when the effect size value is 0.45 and the alpha value is 0.05. 48 samples are required for an effect size of 0.45 and an alpha of 0.1. This is calculated by SPSS SamplePower1.2.

Based on these factors, for a response rate of 63% in the pilot test, 100 firms were randomly selected. Samples were restricted to the listed companies in order to include major companies in Korea. The Annual Corporation Reports by Maeil Business Newspaper Co. [40] was the source for sampling, because it analyzes all listed companies on the Korea Stock Exchange.

Questionnaires were administered to a total of 1290 middle managers, with from 5 to 15 middle managers in each firm. Middle managers were selected because they are found to play key roles in KM and top managers are often sensitive to showing their roles in organizational success [18]; also line managers are generally incapable of understanding the characteristics of the overall organization. Both interviews and mail were used.

## 4.3. Measures

Research constructs were operationalized based on pretest and related studies. For the questionnaires, a multiple-items method was used and each item was based on a six point Likert scale from ‘very low’ to ‘very high’; such a scale avoids a midpoint, in order to eliminate the neutral default option [1]. All variables are perceptual ones.

Explicit- and tacit-oriented degrees were used to categorize KM methods. They were adopted from relevant literature and evaluated by pilot testing. The explicit-oriented degree measured knowledge codification, knowledge acquisition and sharing in codified forms, and documentation. The tacit-oriented degree measured the level of knowledge acquisition from experts and co-workers, face-to-face help by experts, informal dialogues for knowledge sharing, and knowledge acquisition through one-on-one mentoring.

Corporate performance was measured from a nonfinancial perspective. This study adopted a measure developed and validated by Deshpande et al. [14] and Drew [15]. It consisted of output items such as overall success, market share, growth rate, profitability, innovativeness, and business size compared with key competitors. Such measures present a ‘‘balanced scorecard’’ and are effective for comparing business units and industries.

All operational definitions of measures and their related literature are summarized in Table 2. Further details can be found in Appendix A.

## 4.4. Analysis procedure

Reliability and validity tests were performed and then cluster analyses were carried out to categorize the firms. A major issue of the clustering techniques is in determining the number of clusters, which can be determined in three steps:

1. Use Ward’s hierarchical technique using the squared Euclidean distance and an agglomeration schedule [25].

2. Perform K-means non-hierarchical analysis as a validity check.

3. Validate the result using an analysis of variance (ANOVA).

After determining the number of clusters, we tested the hypothesis. The sample was analyzed using of SPSS for Windows, Version 10.0.

## 5.2. Reliability and validity test

Table 4 summarizes the number of items and the results of the reliability and validity tests for the KM variables. The internal consistency measures (Cronbach’s alpha) were obtained in order to assess the reliability of the measurement instruments. Factor analysis checks used discriminant validity [33]. Because multi-item constructs measure each variable, factor analysis with varimax was employed to check unidimensionality among the items, and those with factor loading values lower than 0.5 were eliminated. The item-to-total correlation, between each item and the sum of the remaining items, was used for convergent validity. Items whose item-to-total correlation score was lower than 0.4 were eliminated. One item relating to the explicit-oriented side and one to the tacit-oriented side had item-to-total correlations of less than 0.4 and, thus, were eliminated from further analysis. Corporate performance, the dependent variable, consists of six items; the sixth (business size) was eliminated from further analysis.

## 5.3. Interrater reliability and agreement analysis

Because there were replies from multiple respondents, it was necessary to assess interrater reliability and a measure of their agreement [10,73]. Interrater reliability, an index of consistency, presents prepositional consistency of variance among raters [35,39]. In contrast, interrater agreement represents interchangeability among raters, the extent to which raters make the same ratings [30].

The interrater reliability was assessed by the interclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Because different raters were used to rate the companies, their ratings were averaged, and ICC(1, k) can be computed via one-way ANOVA [64].

James et al. [31] developed indexes for measuring within-group agreement for a set of raters with a single item $( r _ { \mathrm { w g ( 1 ) } } )$ or multiple items $( r _ { \mathrm { w g } ( J ) } )$ . Because we need to evaluate multiple targets, $r _ { \mathrm { w g } ( J ) }$ was computed for each target and then these $r _ { \mathrm { w g } ( J ) }$ values were averaged for all targets. Table 5 summarizes the results of interrater reliability and agreement.

A number of studies suggest that ICC ranges from 0. 512 to 0.991, and $r _ { \mathrm { w g } ( J ) }$ ranges from 0.69 to 0.96 [8].

## 5.4. KM styles

Cluster analysis was performed to derive KM styles for explicit- and tacit-oriented degrees. The decision on the number of clusters was guided by an agglomeration schedule, which displayed the squared Euclidean distances between each case or group of cases. The agglomeration coefficient shows rather large increases from four to three clusters $( 5 . 4 7 - 3 . 8 5 =$ 1:62), three to two clusters $( 8 . 2 6 - 5 . 4 7 = 2 . 7 9 )$ and two to one cluster $( 2 1 . 9 8 - 8 . 2 6 = 1 3 . 7 2 )$ . To help identify large relative increases in the cluster homogeneity, the percentage change in the clustering coefficient was calculated (see Table 6). Based on the percentage change in agglomeration coefficients, the appropriate number of clusters was found to be four.

## 6. Costs of KM styles

The cost of KM may be very high [66]. Therefore, understanding of the costs for each KM style is important [50].

Whether taking a passive, system-, human-oriented, or dynamic style, the costs of KM will remain high. However, it is difficult to find the value of KM because KM is not directly related to its costs. To obtain a clearer view, we therefore, attempted to synthesize the costs of KM styles from two major perspectives: IT and human resource. These two are seen as the most important enablers for KM initiatives. In addition, most studies have considered them as the key anchors for selecting appropriate KM methods [13].

The system-oriented style invests heavily in IT to search for specialized knowledge and communicate among members, using technologies such as groupware, communication systems, and repositories. But the costs of developing and using KM systems (KMSs) are high. In addition, maintaining knowledge may be costly; without the expenditure of time and efforts to keep knowledge current, KMS would fail to yield any advantage [23]. In contrast, this style makes relatively little investment in human resource management activities; because standard training is usually adopted, training cost is low. For example, an insurance company invested in a data warehouse, a data-mining tool, and a database to acquire customer knowledge via database marketing. In addition, the Lotus Notesbased KMS called IBPS was developed. All documents can be processed and searched through this system. ‘‘Finding a flaw in a gem’’ is designed for a quick knowledge update. The company has invested US\$ 1 million in developing KMSs. However, this company has a small community and little support for training employees.

The main purpose of IT for companies of the human-oriented type is to facilitate exchange of tacit knowledge. However, it recruits people with extraordinary care; these firms hire people who can use their analytic and creative skills on unique and complex business problems. Moreover, they usually have oneto-one mentoring and informal training programs. The community of practice plays an important role in knowledge sharing and creating. For example, another company began its KM initiative by organizational learning; the main objective was rapid learning and sharing. It divided KM teams into three learning teams: knowledge operation, autonomous learning, and job-site knowledge. ‘‘Knowledge mileage incentive,’’ ‘‘knowledge master,’’ and ‘‘enhancing team vitalization program’’ are designed for consolidating learning organizations. Among US\$ 1.3 million for human resource management, almost 30% has been invested in supporting KM activities. In contrast, its current KMS supports repository functions only. The company invested about US\$ 500,000 in building KMSs.

Because firms of the passive type show little interest in KM, both costs for IT and human resource are low. For example, another company, a Korean underwear manufacturing company, initiated KM to cut waste out of its operations. However, this company has no community of practice. The company has not really spent money for KM activities. It could not hire welleducated people, because of their financial circumstances and the fact that many of their competent people left due to lack of organizational support. Also knowledge-oriented tools like Notes, Intranet, and even desktop computing and communications have not been fully supported. The company invested about US\$ 60,000 for implementing KMSs; the system has almost never been used.

The dynamic style puts emphasis on managing both tacit and explicit knowledge. Such firms invest heavily in IT and human resources. For example, one of Korea’s leading retailing companies began KM with a focus on both human resources and IT. Ten communities of practice were organized and each was divided into four or five subgroups. Their training programs cover 17 basic and 15 special subjects. ‘‘Knowledge award’’ and ‘‘the Guinness book within the company’’ are peculiar characteristics. The company has invested US\$ 3.6 million to support KM activities like communities of practice. Furthermore, the KMS called HKIS was built for knowledge storing and sharing. This system includes 25 knowledge categories, such as job manuals, internal reports, and best practices. To acquire knowledge about customers, the company built a voice of customer (VOC) and customer relationship management (CRM) system. The company spent about US\$ 1 million in building KMSs. For effective knowledge search and exchange, the electronic document management system (EDMS) was also built.

## 7. Implications

Our result has both theoretical and practical implications. First, it validates that KM should consider two factors: human and system. The system-centric approach alone placed too much emphasis on codified knowledge, and, thus, is insufficient for managing tacit knowledge, while the human-centric approach put too little emphasis on structured knowledge to make a long-term difference to organizations. Most dynamic business processes require a firm-specific blend of both explicit and tacit knowledge. The mixture of the system- and human-oriented style results in better corporate performance.

This is the first study to explore the relationship between KM styles and industry types. Many studies have investigated internal factors, such as task types, product characteristics, or knowledge characteristics for developing KM styles [2,11]. And still, external factors are to be investigated: the industry type can be considered a firm’s external factor. Our result implies that both the firm’s external and internal factors come into play in formulating management styles.

Effective KM requires a symbiosis between explicit and tacit knowledge. This symbiosis should streamline the interplay between information technology and human resource management processes [20]; technology may just add to information overload. A manager of the KM team in H company, Korea’s leading petrochemical company, confessed, ‘‘we focus on implementing a knowledge management system to store knowledge because our most important knowledge is explicit like mechanical and instrumental drawings, technical data, and the operating manual. However, we put little emphasis on reward, evaluation or the knowledge sharing mechanism. Therefore, our system has not been used prevalently, and, thus, much potential to leverage expertise and foster organizational learning remains unrealized. Our people usually rely on a network of their human relationships. It is imperative to elevate this system from mediocrity to sustained excellence with the relevant human resource management activities.’’

Lastly, while the dynamic style can lead to higher corporate performance, it may require higher costs, e.g. dynamic costs are likely to be influenced by the size and complexity of the social network. The costs of maintaining linkages usually increase exponentially as it increases in size [49]. In addition, managers should see if the benefits of a KMS outweigh the costs of its implementation.

## 8. Conclusion

This paper categorizes KM methods into dynamic, system-, human-oriented, and passive styles. Empirical tests suggest that organizational performance differs between them. The dynamic style integrating explicit- with tacit-oriented methods is found to result in better performance. There are several unresolved issues that may be considered from this effort:

1. Our sample was obtained from the Annual Corporation Reports by Maeil Business Newspaper Co. The firms are relatively large and profitable; small or venture firms are not included. This may introduce a bias that makes these four KM styles appear to perform above average. Including small or venture companies might lead to more robust results.

2. Managing for creativity is important to increase the range of a company’s knowledge, but managing knowledge effectively may not necessarily create innovation [69] and current studies fail to explain how KM styles can help explore new possibilities.

3. Ensuring successful KM requires close attention to the costs. More effort should be devoted to this.
