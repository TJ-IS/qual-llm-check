# Strategic information and strategic decision making: the EIS/CEO interface in smaller manufacturing companies

Bruce A. Walters<sup>a</sup>, James J. Jiang<sup>b</sup>, Gary Klein<sup>c,\*</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management and Marketing, College of Administration and Business, Louisiana Tech University, P.O. Box 10318, Ruston, LA 71272, USA

<sup>b</sup>Department of Management Information Systems, University of Central Florida, P.O. Box 161400, Orlando, FL 32816-1400, USA <sup>c</sup>College of Business and Administration, University of Colorado, P.O. Box 7150, Colorado Springs, CO 80933-7150, USA

Received 3 May 2001; received in revised form 23 January 2002; accepted 25 May 2002

## Abstract

Executive information systems (EIS) often focus on providing external data to top management for environmental scanning. However, for many executives, the need for internal information may be just as strong, in order to provide an accurate appraisal of the firm’s ability to capitalize on opportunities. Yet much of the environmental scanning research to date focuses on the external environment. To place a framework on this issue, categories for both internal and external data are derived from the management literature. A survey of CEOs for small manufacturing firms indicates that internal information is just as important in analysis of new situations as is the external information. Additionally, the CEOs place no particular order on access to the information. EIS should be designed to allow for access to all categories of information in an order selected by the executive and provide important internal information for making strategic decisions. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Executive information systems; Environmental scanning

## 1. Introduction

An executive information system (EIS) provides executives with easy access to internal and external information relevant to their critical success factors [22]. However, the literature contains only a few studies of EIS development practices and these are broad in scope [35,36]. As a consequence, there are many aspects of developing an EIS that have not been the subject of rigorous research. What makes EIS development particularly interesting and challenging, however, is the unique tasks faced by chief executive officers (CEOs) that affect the effort. They perform highly unstructured work that is often difficult for them to describe with sufficient precision to determine their information requirements [20]. In fact, many of the studies consistently identify providing timely, accurate, and relevant information as the most critical problem [14,25]. A logical start in solving such problems is to examine how CEOs scan their internal and external environments when gathering information with which to make strategic decisions [29,40].

Environmental scanning is an essential activity undertaken by top executives in order to be effective in steering the organization in a changing environment [7]. Potentially important information is increasingly abundant, however, executive time is scarce. CEOs need accurate, timely information in order to make effective decisions. Attention is often focused on key subsets of the available data, while some potentially important data sources are ignored [12]. Additionally, strategic decisions in many firms are often influenced by design characteristics of the firms’ scanning systems [39]. Thus, EIS design is crucial to certain strategic decisions.

This heightens the role of the EIS in providing the appropriate information, in the right form, for top management to use in information search [21]. Over thirty years ago, Aguilar [1] noted that environmental changes were making it increasingly important for management to foresee and understand their effect. Twenty years ago, Rockart and Treacy noted that ‘‘. . . today’s volatile competitive conditions heighten the desire among top executives for ever more timely information and analysis’’ [26]. The current competitive environment may be even more volatile and unpredictable due to increased globalization, mergers and acquisitions, and an explosion in technology applications and new business practices. Yet budget constraints often preclude useful access to information from every segment of the environment. Moreover, CEOs’ cognitive limits require some prioritization of information [30]. Thus, to be effective in delivering information to strategic decision makers, the EIS developer needs to know which categories of information executives consider important and are likely to use.

When executives gather information for making strategic decisions, is the internal environment as salient and useful as the external one? In attempting to answer this question, both internal and external elements are included in an investigation of the degree to which executives use information choices. Although the importance of internal information might appear to be intuitive, much of the previous research on environmental scanning has emphasized the external environment [9]. These studies were primarily descriptive efforts to classify and/or order sectors of the external environment. A firm’s internal circumstances also produce important and changeable data that compete for executive time and attention, yet this aspect of executive scanning has been relatively ignored [3,8]. These arguments suggest that executives must scan both the external environments and the internal characteristics of their firms.

In our study, top executives of smaller manufacturing companies indicated the degree to which they use particular categories of data when gathering information for making strategic decisions. Not only did they indicate the importance they attach to the different categories of information, but also the order in which they tend to consult them.

## 2. Background

Executives are sophisticated information seekers constrained by bounded rationality [4,6]. Because the magnitude of the scanning task is beyond any individual’s information processing capability, top executives must set priorities. Efforts to monitor the external environment alone can take as much as one quarter of top executives’ time. This leads them to pay selective attention to key criteria in making strategic decisions. CEOs are likely to be selective in the scanning task; better information increases the sense of controllability and the focus of information use is likely to shift, depending on the competitive environment in which the firm exists [28,33,34].

Ideally, an EIS should be able to:

(1) extract, filter, compress, and track critical data;

(2) provide online status access, trend analysis, exception reporting, and ‘‘drill-down’’;

(3) access and integrate a broad range of internal and external data; and

(4) be user-friendly and require minimal or no training [17].

Three broad guidelines for developing a successful EIS are often recommended in the information systems (IS) literature.

(1) It must meet the senior executive’s information needs.

(2) Its designers must have business skills in order to understand CEOs’ information needs.

(3) It must be so easy to use that it might be considered to be intuitive.

The EIS technology itself is not of concern to CEOs. Several studies have examined the methods used in determining EIS information requirements [37];

unfortunately, there are few studies that have examined the specific content of information used by executives for strategic decision making.

## 2.1. The external and internal environment

The external environment consists of sectors containing important information that may impact the firm. Scanning content studies have generally examined which sectors are attended to by managers (e.g. [16]). Smeltzer et al. [31] found that over 75% of owner/managers of small firms without planning departments considered the most important environmental segments to be either economic conditions or the marketplace. Daft, Sormunen, and Parks examined the scanning practices of CEOs in single-business manufacturing companies. They found sectors of the external environment to be ordered, by decreasing managerial perceptions of strategic uncertainty, from: customer, economic, competitor, technological, regula tory, to socio-cultural.

Among the external environmental sectors represented by these efforts, six have emerged as prominent and consistently salient to top executives. They include the market, technological, competitive, political/legal, economic, and the socio-cultural environments. Brief descriptions of each sector are given in Table 1. Daft et al. [7] noted that executives could overcome time and information processing limitations by focusing their scanning efforts on specific sectors.

Although external scanning is critical for strategic success, it is not sufficient for high organizational performance. Effective adaptation also requires that a CEO adjust the internal aspects of the organization to match the demands of the external environment, as demonstrated by early techniques such as SWOT analysis [19]. Effective adaptation requires that a CEO adjust the internal aspects of the organization to match the demands of the external environment. The CEO must produce an ‘‘objective appraisal of resources’’ internal to the firm [11]. Moreover, Porter noted that management should assign the value chain activities that occur inside the firm to categories that best represent their contribution to the firm’s competitive advantage.

Following Porter’s suggestions, and for sake of parsimony, six characteristics of a firm’s internal environment (i.e. value chain) are included: the market research function, product research and development, basic engineering, financial management, cost controls, and operational efficiency [23,24]. These internal dimensions are basic to competitive advantage, and yet may receive different emphases, depending on the business-level strategy pursued.

## 2.2. Hypotheses

Investigation of both external and internal sectors together may be illuminating with regard to how executives perceive the internal/external ‘‘mix’’ when gathering information. Information emanating from within the firm may sometimes be more important to top executives than external information. They both change over time, they both must be understood, and they both compete for the CEO’s limited time and attention. Porter has persuasively argued that organizational success requires continual and aggressive attention to the distinctive competencies provided by a firm’s internal resources. This leads to our first hypothesis.

H1. When gathering information for making strategic decisions, top executives of smaller manufacturing companies place at least as much importance on internal characteristics of the firm as they do the external environment.

In addition to the importance executives place on various types of information, we are also interested in the order in which they might consult information sectors when their situation changes. The knowledge of which sectors are more likely to be consulted earlier in the search process may affect EIS design [15], particularly in today’s industries. March and Simon [18] proposed that variables perceived to be under control will be considered first, and that only after ascertaining that a solution cannot be achieved with these variables will attention shift to others.

In smaller firms, internal elements of the organization may be perceived as controllable. Indeed, top managers are often urged to ‘‘adapt’’ their firm to the environment. This is reminiscent of the ‘‘resourcebased’’ versus the ‘‘industrial organization’’ view of firm performance [2]. In the former, firm resources are believed to be the primary determinant of competitive advantage and performance; in the latter the external environment is given prominence with regard to the strategies that firms select.

The question of which set of environmental sectors—external or internal—should be considered first appears equivocal. Thus, we propose that both internal and external elements will be given attention both early and later in the search process. Stated another way, external and internal sectors will be roughly equally distributed throughout the search process.

H2. When top executives of smaller manufacturing companies gather information for making strategic decisions, both internal and external environmental elements will be represented throughout the search process. There is not a significant relationship between the internal/external distinction and order of search.

## 2.3. Sample and procedures

Manufacturing firms were selected from those in a large southwestern state they were selected from the Directory of Manufacturers and met the following criteria.

(1) It must be an independent business rather than a subsidiary, a division of another firm, or a unit of a conglomerate. This ensures that the executive’s scanning behaviors are not influenced by a parent firm [5].

(2) It must be a single-business firm [27], indicated by operation in only one or two four-digit SICs considered to be in the same industry. Thus responses focus only on the environment of the primary business.

Top executives were sent a packet containing a letter requesting participation, along with three questionnaires. Packets were sent to the CEOs of 273 autonomous, non-diversified manufacturing firms. Usable responses were received from the top executives of 116 firms. In addition, 12 firms returned questionnaires from one additional top executive, and eight firms returned questionnaires from two additional executives. Our primary interest was on the scanning habits of CEOs, as they are likely the primary strategy-makers in firms of this size. Our evidence indicated this to be the case; of the executives returning only one questionnaire, 27 stated that they were the only executive qualified to provide relevant information. Moreover, for those firms returning more than one questionnaire, there would be difficulty in discerning the degree to which the additional executives participated in overall decision making. ## 2.4. Constructs

The variables representing environmental scanning were adapted from external environmental sectors used by Daft et al. and Aguilar, as well as internal sectors of the value chain believed to be critical to competitive advantage. Specifically, subjects were asked to rate the importance of six sectors of the internal environment and six sectors of the external environment when gathering information for making strategic decisions for their firms, on a 1 (very important) to 7 (not at all important) Likert scale. Additionally, they were asked to rank-order the 12 sectors to reflect the order in which they would be consulted when placed in a new and different competitive situation.

In order to ensure that internal and external dimensions are distinct, we surveyed 170 senior business policy students, and asked them to classify the 12 sectors as internal or external. All six internal and external sectors were included in a listing in the survey, intermingled and without any cues as to their ‘‘external’’ or ‘‘internal’’ classification. Subjects were asked to classify the segment on a scale from 1(clearly external) to 5 (clearly internal). The scale was recoded for analysis to be negative for external and positive for internal. The subjects distinguished the external from internal sectors with overwhelming and consistent success. Specifically, we conducted independent sample (i.e. matched-pair) comparisons between each of the six external sectors and each of the six internal sectors, resulting in 36 pair-wise comparisons. We controlled for collective experiment-wise error rate using the Bonferroni procedure. As reflected in Table 2, the differences between means of every one of the internal sectors and every one of the external sectors were all statistically significant at $\alpha = 0 . 0 1$ . Then, a pilot test of the questionnaire was conducted using three CEOs who were not included in the final sample. The intent was to check the clarity and appropriateness. These tests resulted in minor clarifications.

Yet respondents indicated they would first explore the market environment (an external sector). The socio-cultural environment was rated last in terms of both importance and sequence.

It appears that a firm’s internal characteristics are perceived to be at least as important for gathering information as the external environment (H1). Indeed, the top two sectors in terms of importance are internal. A one-tailed t-test conducted between the overall mean of the internal items (2.87) and the overall mean of the external items (3.60) indicates that the internal mean is greater than or equal to the external mean at a 0.05 confidence level. Also, it can be seen that both internal and external sectors appear to be fairly well represented throughout the search process (H2), if we use the order rankings as a proxy for order of search. A runs test indicates that a random pattern exists at the 0.05 confidence level. These findings support the notion that both the internal and external environment are consulted throughout the environmental scanning process.

## 4. Discussion

Our goal was to describe the importance of the interface between the EIS and the CEO. Some of our findings seem important regarding the content of environmental sectors receiving attention from CEOs. Such scanning is the first step in the chain of activities leading to environmental adaptation. These activities are crucial to the decision-making processes of executives and are supported by EIS, whose design guidelines have been broad in scope and focused on external data. However, data internal to the firm certainly have value.

The importance of internal data stresses the need to incorporate all dimensions of data in the design of an

EIS. The development of the internal categories according to the value chain concept allows a well-accepted and validated framework to be applied to the navigators associated with an EIS. These complement the use of the external categories that could also serve as primary links to sources. It becomes imperative to provide the links to the internal sources identified. Customer databases have indicators of market to be mined. Product and manufacturing capabilities may be stored in knowledge bases. Accounting and production data serve to provide a picture of the core capabilities of the firm. Each category has its own implications in terms of information EIS ability and access to stored data (or data that should be stored). Access to the external factors has implications regarding the collection or purchase of external data along with standard and novel search technology [13,32].

An EIS must at least must interface with external and internal database systems, store aggregates for rapid access, efficiently index and retrieve information, consolidate various sources and formats of data, and sort screen data according to user defined criteria. Other important data handling features may include the ease of addition and modification of data and to maintain its integrity and timeliness. Screen management techniques must also be included as an EIS can produce up to a million screens of information [10]. Aids in learning and exploration should be common in order to promote a productive and revealing session [38]. Order of access and devices used to keep track of the exploration may be a very personal trait and should perhaps be individualized for each executive.
