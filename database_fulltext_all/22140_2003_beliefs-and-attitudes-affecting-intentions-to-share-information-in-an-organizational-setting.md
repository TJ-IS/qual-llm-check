# Beliefs and attitudes affecting intentions to share information in an organizational setting<sup>\$</sup>

Keith E. Kolekofski Jr., Alan R. Heminger<sup>\*</sup>

Air Force Institute of Technology, AFIT/ENV Blg. 640, 2950 P Street, WPAFB, OH 45433-7765, USA

Received 2 February 2001; accepted 3 January 2002

## Abstract

Information resource management (IRM) asserts that information is a resource that managers should use wisely to improve their organization’s operations and to ensure that their company is competitive in today’s marketplace. One IRM principle is sharing information in ways that enhance efficiency and effectiveness. Yet, there are many examples where information is not readily shared. Workers’ beliefs and attitudes towards sharing information may help explain this shortcoming. In this study, we propose a model that defines the influences on one’s intent to share information, based upon the theory of reasoned action (TRA). We then test our model by surveying the workers in a unit of a large governmental organization that often fails to reap the benefits, despite proclamations of support for IRM principles. Our study explored the employees’ beliefs and attitudes about sharing organizational information and suggests that the role that attitude plays may be more complicated than first considered. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information sharing; Information ownership; Information stewardship; Organizational information; Theory of reasoned action (TRA)

## 1. Information resource management

Today’s operating environment requires information systems that ‘‘align (themselves with) . . . the needs of the enterprise . . . (and are) both more responsive to competitive and strategic pressures and more cost-effective in delivering solutions to the business’’ [16]. These demands, matched with the ceaseless advancements in technology, have spawned a proliferation of information systems (IS). Computers have altered our view of data and (the resultant) information, which is no longer considered merely a cost of doing business; today, it is seen as a resource that provides strategic advantages for the organization that uses it well.

Richard Nolan, in his article, ‘‘Computer Data Bases: The Future is Now,’’ was one of the first to examine information’s importance as an organizational resource. Management, he said, ‘‘should begin to think of data as a basic resource. It should accept this idea as a natural consequence of functional specialization of the general management function’’ [12]. His approach provided the foundation of a new discipline called information resource management (IRM); its philosophy advocates applying sound management principles to information. IRM proponents proclaim its benefits, which include:

 The value of data is optimized when data (are) . . . managed to be shared by many applications and knowledge workers.

 Processes are managed to maximize value-adding activities and eliminate no-value-adding activities.

 Technology is exploited to achieve the value of both data and process for enabling just-in-time informating (sic) [6].

 IRM benefits the cost-effective operation and development of information systems [11].

Information is a resource that should be aligned with the business’s objectives in order to benefit the organization; this includes sharing information among those who can make profitable use of it. Yet, employees seldom share information. Why does this happen? Several researchers contend that an employee’s information ownership attitude (i.e. the tendency to treat information as a personally owned resource vice a corporate resource) may limit his or her desire to use information to benefit the organization. As a desired alternative, researchers have proposed an information stewardship attitude [5,15,18]. It is therefore important to explore employees’ beliefs and attitudes to see whether there are significant factors that can be controlled in the workplace in order to maximize this impact.

## 2. Beliefs and attitudes

Fishbein and Ajzen, in their theory of reasoned action (TRA) [8] proposed a relationship that links beliefs, attitude, intentions, and behaviors. Beliefs influence one’s overall attitude about an object. This, in turn, guides the individual’s intentions, which influence behaviors regarding the subject. This conceptual framework is shown in Fig. 1.

TRA provides the structure for our examination of how employees use an organization’s information. We surveyed employees of a large governmental organization to learn more about the possible determinants of intentions to share information. Our examination explored the first three items in the TRA model. We did not explore actual behaviors.

Many beliefs have been examined as possible determinants of an individual’s attitude about information or its sharing. These constructs include beliefs about information (e.g. type, amount, and value), interpersonal relationships (e.g. acceptance of others, selfinterest, and reciprocity), organizational factors (e.g. organizational norms and social aspects), and task relevancy. It is hypothesized that one’s attitude will tend toward ownership or stewardship of the organization’s information.

## 2.1. Beliefs about information

Constant et al. [3] discovered a link between the type of information and an individual’s attitude about sharing it. One of their experimental findings was that ‘‘the relationship between perceptions of organizational ownership and attitudes about sharing differ(s) with the type of information.’’ Employees are more likely to share personal knowledge (e.g. computer expertise) than tangible information (e.g. a computer program).

## 2.2. Beliefs regarding interpersonal relationships

In addition to factors involving the information itself, interpersonal relationships often influence information sharing. Weenig and Midden examined two dimensions of acceptance of others—the availability and strength of ties between the sender and receiver of information. While diffusion of information relied on only the availability of ties, adoption decisions required stronger bonds [17]. Friendships and personal contacts heavily influence communication between individuals; when these exist, the likelihood of information sharing is increased.

Self-interest and reciprocity also play roles in information sharing. Both factors exert a negative influence on sharing (assuming negative reciprocity). These factors could also induce fear of retribution and blame if the shared information is perceived to be bad. Therefore, the perceived quality of information may be an important factor in assessing self-interest and reciprocity. However, mediators, such as prosocial attitudes and positive organizational norms (i.e. policies, procedures, and culture) may mitigate or overcome this negative impact.

## 2.3. Beliefs regarding organizational factors

The organization’s norms also influence the sharing process. Even if negative reciprocity hinders an exchange of information, sharing may still occur if the provider believes that the information benefits the organization. Brief and Motowidlo identified organizational commitment, citizenship, and whistle-blowing as prosocial organizational behaviors. One aspect of citizenship, the belief most closely associated with sharing information, is performing activities for the sake of the organization rather than for the benefit of the individual [2]. O’Reilly and Chatman further describe this prosocial transformation with respect to psychological attachment to the individual’s organization. Their research examined the underlying dimensions of organizational commitment and their impact on prosocial behavior and turnover [14].

## 2.4. Beliefs regarding task relevancy

Zmud, in his work on the dimensionality of information, derived four constructs. One of these—relevancy (synonymous with task dependency)—determines whether or not the requested information is related to the task. The components are: accurate (accurate and believable), factual (factual and true), quantity (complete, effective, material, and sufficient), and reliable/ timely (current, reliable, timely, and valid) [19].

Our initial model, presented in Fig. 2, suggests that beliefs about information, interpersonal relationships, organizational factors, and task relevancy determine either an ownership or stewardship attitude. This will subsequently influence the likelihood of sharing information.

## 3. Methodology

We developed a survey instrument to measure the proposed constructs—examining the underlying beliefs that may contribute to an attitude towards sharing information (Appendix A). Characteristics of information ownership and stewardship were used to build the 20 scales. These characteristics include: type, amount, and value of information; acceptance of others, self-interest, and reciprocity; organizational norms and prosocial transformations; and task relevancy. Two questions were written to measure each belief using a seven-point Likert scale; approximately half of the questions were reverse scored. In addition, two questions captured the individual’s self-reported attitude regarding information ownership and stewardship; only binary responses were allowed for these. The attitude questions were prefaced with definitions of each term. The definition of an information owner was gleaned from Weldon’s research; the definition of information steward is a paraphrase of English’s work. A small amount of demographic information was asked; space for written comments was also provided.

Because the survey instrument was not established in previous research, the questionnaires were pretested. Nine graduate students and one employee completed the pretest. Students were used to avoid exhausting the limited sample frame. After competing the survey, the pretest participants were interviewed in an effort to bolster the instrument’s content validity. To minimize measurement error, participants were asked to reveal problems with the instrument’s readability and to ferret out confusing or ambiguous questions. Each instrument version included suggestions made by the participants up to that point.

## 3.1. Units of analysis

Because the individual’s decision to share or not share the organization’s information is of interest, this study’s unit of analysis is the individual.

## 3.2. Sample population

The target population was the faculty and support staff of the Air Force Institute of Technology (AFIT). AFIT is comprised of four schools which serve the United States Air Force. There are two graduate schools, one for engineering and one for management, a school that provides shorts courses and distance learning, and a school dedicated to the broad needs of the Air Force civil engineers. In addition, there is a unit within AFIT that places and monitors Air Force officers at civilian institutions, as well as a unit that collects and stores all graduate education records for all Air Force officers. Across these various units there are many types of information collected, stored, processed, and disseminated. Although the unit of analysis was the individual employee, every attempt was made to present a cross-section of the organization itself. Due to a constantly fluctuating workforce, the total number of subjects in the sample population is not precisely known; it was about $5 1 0 \pm 1 5$ at the time of the study.

## 3.3. Sample

The sample was selected by choosing every other name from a list of faculty and support staff e-mail addresses. The survey was e-mailed to 249 addresses.

## 4. Results and analysis

We received responses from participants representing all 13 of AFIT’s functional areas; five individuals did not identify their functional area. Eighty-five usable responses were received—a response rate of 34%. Because of personnel turnover and difficulties keeping e-mail lists current, it is not known how many of the email addresses were invalid. The response rate may have been higher if outdated addresses were excluded.

## 4.1. Construct validity

Initial efforts to confirm construct validity between the paired questions (i.e. nine beliefs plus selfreported attitude) failed. Although 9 of the 10 calculations were positive at the 0.05 level of significance, only three cleared the 0.60 level of acceptability.

A factor analysis was then applied, using SAS, in an effort to determine the number of latent variables— grouping the original list of ten pairings. Based on the analysis, three primary factors were identified. A factor loading of 0.39 was used to determine the factors that loaded with significant strength; six items were eliminated from consideration.

## 4.5. Factor B—instrumentality

Items that loaded in Factor B contained the following beliefs:

 Share with respect to size;

 Share with respect to amount;

 Symbol of power;

 Value affects sharing;

 Whom information benefits;

 Question information requests.

This multidimensional factor contains elements regarding the context of the information itself and the implications of sharing it. In other words, the characteristics describe the instrumentality of sharing information. The dimensions that reflect physical measures of the information (e.g. size, amount, and value) support research by Epstein and King [7], O’Reilly [13], and Laughlin et al. [10]. The components that address the impact of sharing (e.g. power and benefit) support the research by Kelly and Thibaut [9], Weenig and Midden, and Constant et al.

## 4.6. Factor C—value for feelings

Two items consistently loaded on the third factor. These items shared the following element:

 Sharing with regard to treatment by the requester.

This return-in-kind attitude reflects the impact human nature has on sharing. How the requester treated the information holder in the past, whether with kindness or with disdain, may determine whether the information holder is willing to share information. The operationalization of the value for feelings factor was reciprocity. This factor strongly supports previous work by Constant et al.

## 5. Discussion

We used the TRA model to explore the beliefs and attitudes that influence intentions to share information in an organizational environment. Our model, based on the literature, suggested that a number of beliefs influenced a single ownership versus stewardship attitude. Our study indicated that our original model was incomplete and suggest that these beliefs influence two additional attitudes: instrumentality and value for feelings. The combination of these three attitudes, in turn, influences intention to share information (see Fig. 3).

To the extent that it accurately captures the factors that influence information sharing in organizations, our model has ramifications for managers who wish to encourage widespread sharing of information. Our research suggests that managers should consider worker attitudes toward the ownership versus stewardship of organizational information. However, they should also address the instrumentality of sharing and the interpersonal feelings of those engaged in potential information-sharing relationships. Instrumentality includes the size and amount of information requested, what the information represents in terms of power, the value it represents, and who will benefit from the sharing. All of these influences may impact the likelihood that information will be appropriately shared in the organization.

## 6. Conclusions

Information is a crucial resource in today’s organizational environment. Because it is not often widely shared and used, information is not being utilized as fully as it could be as an organizational resource. Therefore, wise managers will strive to encourage information sharing. However, to achieve successful information sharing requires addressing the factors that can impact it.

This study provides evidence for three factors that managers should address when striving to encourage wider sharing of organizational information. We evaluated a model of information sharing, making use of

the TRA model, and found that, in addition to stewardship/ownership attitudes, managers should also consider the instrumentality of sharing information as well as interpersonal feelings between the information holder and those with whom the information could be usefully shared.
