# The concept of contingency beyond ‘‘It depends’’: illustrations from IS research stream

Narayan S. Umanath

College of Business Administration, University of Cincinnati, 333 Lindner Hall, Cincinnati, OH 45221-0211, USA

Received 1 March 2001; received in revised form 1 February 2002; accepted 1 July 2002

## Abstract

With the advent of the widely heralded ‘‘new economy’’, organizations are undergoing profound changes. Interestingly, information systems/information technology (IS/IT) has emerged as the vehicle for launching new organizational forms so that market forces can be effectively managed to yield favorable business solutions. A critical issue for managers to tackle in this environment is the core idea of fit among organizational domains in order to optimize performance. The role of academics in this endeavor is to clarify the notion of fit conceptually as well as methodologically.

This paper synthesizes alternative concepts of fit starting from congruence and contingency, and attempts to clarify the enduring concept of contingency beyond the common sense notion of ‘‘It depends’’. Then it demonstrates the enriching value of the principles advocated in this paper by incorporating these principles in a selected set of prior empirical work in IS. Finally, a methodological enhancement to contingency analysis is proposed—an alternate solution to the systems approach to fit. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Contingency research in IS; Forms of fit; Contingency principle; Congruence; Contingency and fit; Systems approach to fit

## 1. Motivation

Today organizations are experiencing significant changes. The very nature of the business model is changing as new frames of reference and new stakeholders begin to emerge. As a consequence, corporate management is wrestling with new organizational forms (e.g. virtual and networked organizations) to manage the market forces for achieving favorable business solutions. As managers grapple with organizational design issues for inter-organizational contexts in the decision-making environment of contemporary business practice, information systems (IS) and information technology (IT) have emerged as the infrastructure catalyzing boundary spanning business processes within and across organizations through electronically mediated work arrangements.

A critical issue for managers and organization designers in this regard is the core idea of fit in the organizational domains. The contribution of academic research in this regard is the investigation and formulation of precise conceptual and methodological meaning of fit. This paper synthesizes alternative concepts of fit starting from congruence and contingency, and attempts to clarify the enduring concept of contingency beyond the common sense notion of ‘‘It depends’’.

The contingency perspective is a principle for organizing knowledge in a given area of study. It enables a researcher to relax assumptions underlying theoretical propositions and/or systematically introduce factors to explain or predict expected phenomena. Empirical work in information systems (IS) is replete with contingency analyses (For a review, see [28]). Yet, many IS researchers tend to treat the concept rather loosely, i.e. using the common sense notion of ‘‘It depends’’ instead of a precise functional form(s) relevant to a specific research model. Weill and Olson point out that the field of MIS can benefit from the experience of organizational researchers in contingency analysis. Organizational design researchers have generated a stream of research examining the context–structure–performance contingency relationships, providing a base to study the contingency concepts as a research paradigm.

The basic theme in structural contingency research is that organizational context and organizational structure must somehow fit together in order for an organization to perform well ([4]). However, it is argued that there are several perspectives of the concept of fit [7]. Traditional approaches assessed fit on the basis of bivariate relationships, as if context and structure variables covaried in independent pairs. In contrast, advocates of the ‘systems approach’ to structural contingency [15,25] contend that the perspectives that consider how single contextual factors affect single structural characteristics and how these pairs of contextual and structural factors interact to explain performance are reductionist in nature.

IS research has benefited from work done in organizational design research on the conceptual and methodological aspects of contingency and fit in very specific ways:

(1) understanding ’contingency’ as a form of fit;

(2) theorizing the functional form of the relationship(s) (e.g. fit as congruence, fit as mediation, fit as moderation, fit as gestalt);

(3) selecting the appropriate functional form for the relationship(s) conceptualized; and

(4) selecting the appropriate analytical procedure for the functional form formulated.

The purpose of this paper is to discuss contingency principle in terms of various forms of fit and demonstrate how IS research has progressed by exploiting this powerful research paradigm.

## 2. Background

Two major concepts underlying the phenomenon of fit are congruence and contingency. The term fit and its linguistic substitutes (e.g. match, co-alignment, consistent with, conform, appropriate for) have often been used to mean congruence, contingency, or both, thus confounding the distinction between the two. Congruency propositions hypothesize simple, unconditional association among the variables in a model, while contingency connotes conditional association of two or more independent variables with a dependent outcome. For instance, ‘‘higher the operational uncertainty in a supply channel transaction, higher will be the electronic integration between the channel partners’’ is a congruence proposition. A contingency hypotheses, on the other hand will be of the form ‘‘as product complexity increases, increase in electronic information transfer in the supply channel will lead to better channel performance.’’ Methodologically, to be congruent, the design variables must covary. Contingency hypotheses entail explicit, a priori statements of various system states where the integrity of the system is maintained but in a markedly different condition. In terms of scientific goals of a theory, congruence research is an attempt to understand the laws of relationship among the research variables, while contingency research aims at predicting system states where the integrity of the system (i.e. units, laws of relationship, and boundaries) is preserved.

In a nutshell, congruence is a criterion-free specification of fit, while contingency, entails criterionspecific anchoring of fit. Also, the specification of the precise functional form is essential for fully specifying fit. Theoretical conceptualization and computational procedure for testing are different issues; but the correspondence between a particular concept and a testing scheme is often isomorphic and therefore must be carefully selected [26]. This idea is explained further with examples in the Section 3.

## 3.1. Fit as congruence

Examining fit as how variables from two domains relate to each other in independent pairs is referred to as the selection approach. That the variables in each pair jointly affect performance is simply taken for granted in this approach and therefore is not an explicit part of the research question. For example, higher the customer trust in an internet channel, higher will be the use of electronic banking by the customer tested as an assessment of fit simply takes for granted that that this relationship will lead to improvement in a bank’s performance. This description of fit is consistent with the definition of congruence. The typical testing scheme associated with the selection approach is the assessment of simple correlation between each pair of context–structure variables. The main limitation, as it were, of this approach is the absence of explicit links to a criterion variable like performance. Consideration of variables from the two domains in isolated pairs and extrapolating the findings to inferences associating the root domains is also problematic. This problem, however, can be resolved by using a more general version of the selection approach consistent with the definition of congruence. Here, as opposed to treatment of variables in independent pairs, sets of the two domains’ variables are related, essentially depicting a congruent pattern in a multiattribute configuration [24]. A detailed discussion follows in Section 3.4.

## 3.2. Fit as contingency

The criterion-specific view of fit specifies existence of intervening effect(s) (e.g. context, strategy) in the relationship between an antecedent (e.g. structure) and its consequence or criterion variable (e.g. performance). Contrary to the criterion-free selection approach, this perspective is anchored with respect to a particular criterion variable. The criterion-specific view of fit can be specified in terms of two distinct functional forms—mediation and moderation.

## 3.2.1. Mediational model

Fit as mediation portrays a transitive effect and is expressed by the functional form $Z = f ( X )$ and $Y = f ( Z )$ indicating the necessity for the presence $o f Z f o r$ transmitting the effect of X on Y. A pictorial representation of contingency as a mediation effect is shown in Fig. 1. The implication in this example is that bigger the organization size, higher will be the IS maturity of the organization and higher IS maturity of the organization leads to better IS performance. Here, IS maturity is said to be the mediating effect, because one is not proposing that the size of the organization directly influences the IS performance in the organization, but that organization size has an effect on its IS maturity and IS maturity of the organization in turn influences performance. Test of fit as mediation is usually carried out using path analysis procedures [16]. Venkatraman points out that the functional form of fit, viewed as indirect effect, lacks precision, especially when more than two independent variables are incorporated. In addition, statistical corroboration of transitive effect is possible only via an approximation of t statistic, even though sophisticated path-analytic tools (e.g. LISREL, EQS) are capable of analyzing a set of regression equations simultaneously.

## 3.2.2. Multiplicative model of moderation effect

Fit as moderation espouses the idea of explaining/ predicting variation in a criterion variable (e.g. performance) in terms of co-variation between predictor (e.g. context) and moderator (e.g. structure) variables. Fig. 1 includes a pictorial representation of contingency as a moderation effect. The implication here is that an emerging effect due to interaction between, say, organization size and IS maturity influences IS success. The assumption here is that organization size and IS maturity are independent of each other, i.e. irrespective of organization size, the IS maturity of an organization can be higher or lower. Only because the two variables are independent, the interaction produces a third new effect not present in either organization size or IS maturity individually. A popular analytical form for the moderation effect is the multiplicative model. Computationally, the multiplicative model assesses fit using procedures exploring statistical interaction, e.g. subgroup analysis (t-test, Chi square test), multiple regression analysis, and analysis of variance (e.g. [19]).

The traditional objection to the multiplicative model is the alleged problem of multicollinearity [13]. That is, in the preceding example, if the assumption of independence (orthogonality) between organization size and IS maturity is incorrect and indeed they are correlated, then there is a contamination of the so-called new effect imputed on the interaction between the two. However, for interval level measurement of independent variables, multicollinearity can be all but nullified by appropriately shifting the origin of the independent variables; and the significance of the interaction term will be unaffected. With ratio scales, however, such a transformation to eliminate multicollinearity is inappropriate, since the transformation alters the meaning of the measures [21]. The true limitation of the multiplicative model emerges when the theory dictates joint assessment of the main and interaction effects, because the arbitrary scale of origin for interval level measurements renders the standard (beta) coefficients meaningless in a regression model that includes interaction terms. Interestingly, this limitation is not present for ratio level scales, i.e. with ratio level measurements, joint assessment of main and interaction effects is not a problem.

Schoonhoven [20] points out inadequacies in the implementation of the interaction approach to fit. Lack of clarity of theoretical propositions, she argues (1) obscures the fact that contingency arguments produce interactive hypotheses, and (2) obscures the functional form of the interaction effect(s). She further asserts that non-linear effects could be buried in the proposed interaction effect(s) and that symmetrical property implicit in contingency hypotheses suggest non-monotonic effects. A compelling set of evidence also exists for the ability of multiplicative term(s) in linear models to approximate various nonlinear and non-compensatory functional forms [2,10]. Thus, given the interactive implications of contingency propositions, linear form of operationalization is reasonably robust despite Schoonhoven’s concerns. The remedies prescribed by Schoonhoven are also empirically demonstrated in the work of Kim and Umanath [14].

## 3.2.3. Deviation score approach for moderation effect

An alternative to the classical interaction effect (multiplicative model of fit as moderation) is the deviation score approach. This method is demonstrated in [3] through the examination of the impact of deviations in structural properties from an ideal context–structure configuration. Residuals from the regression of variable X on variable Z are used to reflect fit (or rather misfit) with the ideal, which subsequently can be related to the criterion variable, Y. An empirical example exemplifying this approach is presented in detail in Section 3.3.2. This method does not suffer from multicollinearity problems. More importantly, comparison of the magnitudes of the beta weights of all the effects is possible irrespective of the measurement scale used. Thus, this analytical procedure is especially suitable when theory dictates joint assessment of main effects and contingency predictions. The principal limitation of the deviation score approach is the establishment of the validity of the socalled ideal model to measure deviation scores; the use of regression residuals as deviation scores arbitrarily assumes that the least square line from the research data is the ideal profile. In addition, confounding of error variance with measurement errors in the residual analysis procedure tends to exaggerate the lack of fit measured as deviation from the normative profile.

The deviation score analysis may be appropriate to test fit if the underlying theory is conceptualized as deviation or when the theory dictates joint assessment of main and contingency effects. However, contingency propositions, in general, are more attuned to the multiplicative model [11]. The choice is not a matter of operational convenience, but an issue of theoretical justification.

Both mediation (contingency as transitive effect) and moderation (contingency as co-variation effect) perspectives are of value, although, with different theoretical connotations. What is critical with respect to choice of perspective is appropriate theoretical justification. Both the mediation and moderation models of fit discussed earlier are also limited due to the pairwise consideration of variables for fit. Examining pairs of representative variables from the theoretical domains (e.g. context and structure), and their impact on a criterion variable (e.g. performance) is acceptable, as long as the inferences made are confined to the pairs of variables studied. Studying several such pairs in isolation and extrapolating the findings to inferences associating the root domains (e.g. context and structure) is a leap of faith that is problematic on grounds that the resulting conclusions ignore the multivariate/holistic aspects of the relationships.

## 3.3. Related empirical work in IS

The set of papers discussed in this section have all made significant contribution to IS research. These articles are used here to merely demonstrate methodological enhancements that can sometimes add clarity/precision to the understanding of the phenomenon investigated. By no means does this section cover all IS research that employ some form of the contingency approach.

## 3.3.1. Model clarification and enhancement

Early IS researchers often examined fit on the basis of selection approach. Ein-Dor and Segev [5], for instance, examined the association between organization structure and IS structure. Hypotheses of the form, the more centralized the organization structure, (a) the more centralized is the IS structure, (b) the higher is the level of IS integration, and (c) the higher is the rank of the head of IS, etc. were tested using simple correlation analysis procedure. These hypotheses indicate a congruence effect implied by the various bivariate relationships. Theoretical rationale for the choice of this functional form for the fit would have strengthened the hypotheses. The authors also propose a negative correlation between Organization Size and IS Structure because of the expected negative relationship between Organization Size and its Structure. This implies contingency effect due to Organization Structure in the relationship between Organization Size and IS Structure; and the rationale offered by the authors tends to support a transitive effect implying a functional form of mediation. Then, simple correlation analysis is insufficient and richer analytical methods based on the mediation model (see Section 3.2.1) would have captured the phenomenon more completely.

Raymond [18] extended the work of Ein-Dor and Segev by introducing IS sophistication as an intervention in the relationship between variables of the Organizational context and IS success domains. However, the functional form of this contingency effect depicted in the research model is not fully captured by the set of hypotheses proposed and is also inconsistent with the analytical procedure employed to test the research model. The author ascribes a mediating role to IS sophistication and also refers to it as a moderating variable elsewhere in the paper. This may just be an inadvertent use of the terms mediation and moderation synonymously. Since the organizational context variables are portrayed as affecting the IS success variables directly as well as transitively via IS sophistication as the intervention, the effect modeled is partial mediation (see Section 3.2.1). The assessment of simple bivariate congruence between pairs of variables via correlation analysis across the three domains will not capture the full import of this mediation effect. The post hoc analysis where the author evaluates partial correlations, captures the mediating effect of IS sophistication in an indirect way. A more direct assessment is possible via pathanalytic techniques.

Tait and Vessey [22] study the impact of four variables representing user system, technical system and development process on user involvement as well as system success. The study also aims at examining the mediating role of User involvement in the relationships between each of the four independent variables and System success. This clarifies User involvement as the contingency variable as depicted in the research model. The rationale for the mediation effects hypothesized is not quite clear in the theory development. For instance, System complexity is predicted to have a negative effect on System success and a positive effect on User involvement. Also, User involvement is expected to have a positive influence on System success. These three independent effects are theoretically supported. However, the positive transitive effect of System complexity on System success via User involvement cannot be automatically inferred. While path analysis is an appropriate technique to investigate fit as mediation, use of two independent regression models for the path analysis violates the simultaneity implied in the relationships by the contingency propositions. In addition, while the coefficient of a transitive effect can be computed as the product of the appropriate path coefficients, the significance of the effect cannot be precisely determined. A standard statistic to test the significance of the product of the path coefficients is not available. This may have prevented the authors from reporting specific statistical tests for the contingency effects. Venkatraman observes that Sobel’s t statistic provides an approximation to evaluate the significance of the product of path coefficients.

## 3.3.2. Illustration of more advanced models

Nidumolu [17] presents a near perfect execution of the mediation model (contingency as a transitive effect). His research model derives theoretical support from structural contingency and risk-based software engineering perspectives and leads to a multipath mediation model (p. 194)—a precise expression of the functional from of fit as mediation. His research model includes a transitive effect hypotheses along with an exploratory examination of several other transitive effects. The path analysis procedure simultaneously examines three regression equations. The only issue of clarity ensues from how the tool used (EQS) tests the significance of the transitive (indirect) effect.

Kim [12] also developed a set of intervention hypotheses relating three Task characteristics and Group size with two Coordination modes while User information satisfaction served as the dependent variable. He used the deviation score approach (see Section 3.2.3) to assess fit as moderation. The procedure involves computation of residuals by regressing each of Impersonal and Personal coordination modes on the contingent variables Task predictability, Problem analyzability, Task interdependence and Group size in bivariate pairs. Then, each pair and its residual are collectively regressed on the criterion variable, User information satisfaction. While the introduction of this analytical technique to IS research is in itself a contribution, the author bases the choice of this functional form over the multiplicative model by simply showing symptoms for multicollinearity. Since multicollinearity in a multiplicative model is a manageable problem (see Section 3.2.2), rejecting this functional form in favor of residual analysis for multicollinearity reasons is debatable. As argued in Section 3.2.3, the procedures are not interchangeable and the choice must be theory-driven. Kim does not offer theoretical rationale for the choice of deviation score approach as the appropriate analytical procedure to assess the moderation effects theorized. Also, the use of the least square line from the research data itself to serve as the normative profile to compute deviation scores is questionable.

The multiplicative model for fit as moderation is a powerful vehicle to investigate contingency effects when theory suggests that co-variation between antecedent variable and intervening variable affects the criterion variable. The multiplicative model to study the moderating effects of several organizational context variables on the relationship between user involvement and perceived usefulness of IS is used in [6]. Since this part of their study is purely exploratory, it is not guided by specific hypotheses. Thus, it is difficult to evaluate this work on the basis of issues raised in Sections 1 and 3.2.2.

Kim and Umanath [14] used the multiplicative model to study IS subunit task context as an intervention on the relationship between IS subunit structure and its effectiveness. The hypotheses derived using organizational information processing theory [1,23] led to contingency propositions. Since organi zational information processing theory is about how the fit between information processing needs (operationalized as the context of the subunit) and information processing capability (operationalized as the structure of the subunit) affects subunit effective ness/performance, the focus of the theory is exclusively on the co-variation between context and structure variables. Thus, there is no theoretical expectation to jointly assess main effects and contingency effects. Therefore, the multiplicative functional form is highly suitable for investigating the contingency relationships proposed. A single multiple regression equation is used to express the complete research model. Thus, possible compensatory effects are automatically accounted for. The authors also systemati cally handle multicollinearity and the potential specification errors (e.g. non-linear effects, assessment of non-monotonicity). While the study exemplifies proper execution of the multiplicative model to examine fit as moderation, pair-wise consideration of the domain variables limits the study from making broader inferences about the root domains, i.e. IS subunit context and IS subunit structure. In short, richness of analysis is sacrificed via aggregation of the individual dimensions in order to accommodate to the constraint inherent in the analytical procedure used. A holistic assessment of the four IS structure variables and the three task dimensions without any reduction is, in this case, viable using a systems approach (e.g. the pattern analysis procedure, Section 3.4.1).

The functional forms and their analytical expressions discussed so far are still incapable of characterizing holistic patterns of interdependencies since they assess fit via pairwise consideration of domain variables. While organization design research has made some headway in multivariate assessment of fit, contingency research in IS has not yet treaded this frontier. To that extent the systems approach described in the next section is an incremental contribution to contingency research in IS.

## 3.4. Systems approach to fit

In this section, structural contingency (organizational context–structure–performance relationship) is used to illustrate the systems approach to fit. Schoonhoven observes that since information required and information yielded are abstractions in the information processing model, they can not be directly measured (for a description of the Information Processing model, please see [8]). Van de Ven and Drazin postulate that these two key constructs viz., information required and information yielded, are the latent constructs embodying the many manifest features of organization context and structure. Therefore, we use organization (subunit) context and structure as surrogates for information required and information yielded, respectively.

Galbraith questioned the viability of a single ideal structural design for a given context. Even facing similar contingencies, organizations have at their disposal not just a single answer, but a feasible set of solutions to choose from, i.e different, but equally effective structure patterns. Miller argues that dynamic interaction among variables of environment, organizational structure and strategy generates organizational configurations or adaptive patterns that are holistic in nature [15]. Such holistic patterns of interdependencies are characterized by the systems approach.

## 3.4.1. Pattern analysis method

The pattern analysis technique (e.g. [27]) is an analytical procedure to operationalize the holistic view of the systems approach. Fit or co-alignment in this perspective is the degree of adherence to an externally specified multidimensional normative profile of an ideal type; deviation from this normative profile in hyperspace implies weakness in co-alignment. The proposition tested by this approach is that the lack of alignment of a business unit’s structural components to specific requirements dictated by its context will adversely affect its performance. Thus, the nucleus of this methodology is the specification of a hypothetical ideal profile over a pre-selected set of variables for each level of the contingent factor (e.g. context). The procedure then compares the structural pattern of sampled organizations (focal units) with the ideal profile over the same set of variables. This is done by computing the Euclidean distance from the focal units to the ideal profile. The longer the pattern distance of the focal unit from its ‘‘ideal profile’’ in the hyperspace of the set of structural dimensions, the poorer is its expected performance. The test for the performance impact is provided by the correlation between performance and the pattern distance. Significant (negative) correlation implies significant performance degradation with respect to the ideal profile, suggesting rejection of the focal unit.

The pattern analysis procedure can be viewed as the multivariate version of the deviation score approach presented in Section 3.2.3. The major problem in this technique is the validity of the definition of the normative model (ideal profile). An ‘empirically ideal’ type derived as a subset of the sample being analyzed is a weak substitute for a theoretical definition which, of course, is rather difficult to specify. Sample-specific generation of ideal profiles can also amount to self-fulfilling prophecies due to possible tautological relationship resulting from such a procedure. Pattern analysis solution, in essence, is only as good as the normative (ideal) profile formulated.

Also, while the pattern analysis procedure overcomes the limitations of pairwise analysis and treats fit as a ‘gestalt’, it still lacks prescriptive power in that it does not generate an optimal structural configuration for adoption; it only evaluates a target configuration supplied from an external source (e.g. a manager).

## 4. An alternate solution to the systems approach

A critical premise of the systems approach is that the organization designer faces two basic, sometimes, conflicting choices. They are:

(1) Select an organization pattern of structure that ‘fits’ the set of contingencies facing the firm. This is labeled as the matching imperative.

(2) Maintain internal consistency in the design. This is called the internal consistency imperative.

Potential conflict between these two goals implies trade-off decisions for the management. This paper proposes two variations of an organization design model and corresponding analytical tests, one where the match across two domains (e.g. context and structure) is maximized, and the other where the internal consistency of the two domains are independently maximized. The former (matching imperative) may trade-off some internal consistency in order to maximize match, while the latter (internal consistency imperative) achieves maximal internal consistency in the two domains at the expense of possible loss of some match across the two domains. Any target configuration that is not significantly distant from both internal consistency and matching configurations is construed an optimal solution.

## 4.1. Latent constructs

A related concept that arises out of the multivariate treatment of the research variables is the idea of latent constructs emerging from the tight interdependence (matching or internal consistency) among the observed variables. Latent constructs may be defined as unobservable, theoretical abstractions existing at a higher plane than the constituent observable functional variables, and embody the essence of the functional variables. Since the latent constructs can be derived through a variety of aggregation procedures, it is imperative that theory should guide the choice of aggregation procedure; otherwise, the appropriate interrelationship among the observed variables sought by the researcher will not be correctly captured. The matching and internal consistency imperatives are theoretically distinct, though complementary conditions. The former entails maximizing co-variation across two domains (e.g. context and structure), while the latter seeks to maximize variance shared among elements within each of these two domains. The priority in the matching imperative is to seek maximum coupling between sets of variables in the two domains even at the expense of cohesion (internal consistency) within each set. On the other hand, the internal consistency imperative seeks to maximize cohesion among dimensions within each of the two domains; here, coupling or match across the two sets is a secondary concern. While the consequence can sometimes be mutual conflict, high coupling among variables of two domains may also to a certain extent promote cohesion (internal consistency) within each domain and vice versa. In short, the forces underlying the two imperatives may be in resonance and in conflict to different degrees simultaneously.

## 4.2. Analytical solution

Canonical correlation analysis is one method for deriving the matching configuration. The canonical variates derived may then be regressed against a criterion variable (e.g. performance). The canonical model selects linear functions that have maximum covariances between domains. It identifies a linear composite of the set of variables of one domain and a linear composite of the set of variables of the other domain such that the correlation among the composites is the highest attainable ([9]). This fulfills the condition of the matching imperative. In the regression analysis that follows the target of interest is the regression model itself. Does the model capture the phenomenon of interest? If so, how well? That is the critical question in this solution.

Likewise, the internal consistency configuration can be derived using principal components/factor analysis; the resulting independent factors of the two domains and the interaction thereof can be regressed against performance. The factor model selects linear functions of variables of a domain that have maximum variances. It identifies the dimensions that are latent in a set of variables comprising the theoretical domain. The domains (e.g. context, structure) are evaluated separately. Thus, the procedure precisely corresponds with the internal consistency imperative. In the regression analysis that follows in this case the target of interest is the ‘interaction’ term(s) of the latent factors of the domains modeled.

The analytical procedure involves the following steps:

 Generate two specific configurations (boundary solutions):

(1) maximize cohesion in each of the two domains—internal consistency;

(2) maximize coupling across the two domains— external match.

 Assess internal consistency of the matching configuration.

 Assess degree of match observed in the internal consistency configuration.

 Assess impact of internal consistency configuration on performance.

 Assess impact of matching configuration on performance.

## 4.3. An explanation of the solution proposed

An organization’s business activities can be such that its IS subunit’s structure needs to nurture a high level of coupling with its external context, be it within or outside the organization’s boundaries. Then, the matching configuration derived here is the optimal solution since it seeks to maximize external match between the IS subunit context and its structure in a firm. In fact, this solution is rendered optimal because a higher level of coupling is by definition impossible.

Likewise, the internal consistency configuration is the optimal solution when the design focus is on cohesion within the IS structural domain as well as within the IS subunit’s context. While structural cohesion within a subunit can be managed/controlled, is it possible to control cohesion in the context of an IS subunit? The answer is a qualified ‘Yes’, because in some organizations the IS subunit’s context may be dominated by factor and conditions internal to the firm. If cohesion in such contextual factors is expected to significantly enhance the IS subunit’s performance, a firm may choose to manage the contextual factors. This is probable especially where the IS function is strategic to the firm. Once again, a configuration more cohesive than the one derived here is by definition impossible.

In some situations enforcement of matching imperative may also induce significant level of internal consistency within the participating domains and in other situations enforcement of matching imperative may limit internal consistency within the participating domains. Likewise, enforcement of internal consistency may either yield high degree of match across the domains or retard match across the domains. These conditions have not been investigated in this paper. Note that the independence between matching and internal consistency imperatives (Section 4) is a premise underlying the systems approach. It is possible that the two imperatives are overlapping; further one could dominate the other depending on conditions beyond the two domains (e.g. strategy variables).

What if a firm wants a context–structure configuration for its IS or any other subunit that maximizes external match as well as internal consistency over the domains as theorized by the systems approach? This and other prior research does not precisely identify a configuration that meets this specification. In fact, even the existence of a target configuration meeting this systems solution cannot be guaranteed.

How would one develop the configuration (variable composition and their relative weights) that closely adheres to the normative (ideal) specifications? Miller’s definition of ‘gestalt’ as a commonly recognizable adaptive pattern or configuration may serve as a useful guide to the management in locating target configuration(s) that as closely resemble(s) the empirically derived configurations as possible. The target configuration chosen may then be evaluated by performing the pattern analysis test described earlier (see Section 3.4.1) using the internal consistency and matching configurations as the ideal profiles to compare against. If the correlations between performance and the computed pattern distance of the target configuration with respect to the matching configuration as well as with respect to internal consistency configuration are statistically insignificant, one can conclude that the target configuration is optimal. Significant (negative) correlations imply significant performance degradation with respect to the twin ideal profiles, suggesting rejection of the target configuration.

A significant contribution of this methodology is that it is at least partially prescriptive. It certainly characterizes holistic pattern of interdependencies simultaneously subject to multiple contingencies. Also, a procedure to derive theoretically formulated boundary configurations is offered; in fact, these two configurations (matching and internal consistency) taken together are more legitimate as ideal profiles than arbitrarily chosen ideal profiles for evaluating target solutions. One limitation of this work is that the matching and internal consistency configurations derived here may not be easily interpretable as practical designs by themselves. Also, the analytical solution presented addresses holistic treatment of only two domains of variables. An extension of the solution to multiple domains is a target for future research.

## 5. Summary and conclusions

This paper seeks to clarify how the concept of contingency extends beyond the common sense notion of ‘‘It depends’’. Building on congruence and contingency as the basic expressions of fit, the research teases out richer models of fit and articulates analysis techniques to implement and test the proposed models culminating in a solution to enhance the systems approach to assessing fit.

The incremental contribution of this paper is that it proposes a method to formulate boundary instances of holistic configurations of organization design that can be used as the benchmarks (ideal types) to evaluate current or planned organization designs. In addition to being of value in the design of new forms of IS deployment in inter-organizational contexts, this research also contributes to the reference discipline of organizational management.

From a practicing manager’s perspective, techniques to examine fit at different levels of sophistication well grounded in conceptual rationale are presented with empirical examples. The manager is alerted to the fact that latent constructs of holistic patterns maximizing internal consistency and external match within the organizational domains (e.g. environment, structure, strategy) entail trade-offs. The manager is provided an opportunity to design several holistic configurations that can be equally effective and actually measure the efficacy of their designs in terms of the benchmarks. That the benchmarks emerge from data and can reveal insights otherwise not discernable by strict theoretical means enhances credibility of the solution with the practicing managers.
