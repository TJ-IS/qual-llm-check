# Measures of perceived end-user computing skills

Gholamreza Torkzadeh<sup>a,\*</sup>, Jungwoo Lee<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of MIS, College of Business, University of Nevada, Maryland Parkway, Box 456034, Las Vegas, NV 4505, USA <sup>b</sup>Graduate School of Information, Yonsei University, Seoul, South Korea

Received 1 August 2000; received in revised form 1 February 2002; accepted 1 July 2002

## Abstract

Effective use of information technology (IT) is considered a major determinant of economic growth, competitive advantage, productivity, and even personal competency. End-user computing (EUC) skills influence how well this IT is used by the individuals within organizations. This article reports on the development of an instrument for measuring perceived end-user computing skills. Using a survey of 282 respondents, we conducted a factor analysis and propose a 12-item instrument that measures end-user computing knowledge and ability. Reliability and validity of the instrument is presented. The instrument’s theoretical and practical applications are discussed. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Published by Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: End-user computing skills; Measurement; Knowledge; Ability

## 1. Introduction

In today’s end-user computing (EUC) environment, the bottom line is not how good information systems (IS) are, but rather how well they are used (e.g. whether they empower users to service customers); how well these systems are used is a function of EUC skills, which empower individuals to utilize information technology (IT) and perform a variety of functions in an organizational context. MIS researchers point out that adequate understanding of EUC tools and motivation to use them on the part of end-users will lead to the realization of the full potential of EUC [4,7,13,21,25,26,28,30,32,34,35,37].

The interest in measures of EUC skills has been shaped by the organizational perception of the enduser role in the innovation process. Cooper and Zmud [12] describe a typical pattern of technology innovation that includes six stages: initiation, adoption, adaptation, acceptance, routine use, and infusion. The EUC skills construct is a central concept for the realization of each stage in this model, especially for the infusion stage. System professionals are typically responsible for guiding this process through the acceptance stage. Once the system is accepted, system analysts usually move on to another project. Users are primarily responsible for making full use of the technology in their work [10]. As end-users assume greater responsibility for information systems, it will become increasingly important to develop measures appropriate for the end-user computing skills.

A reliable measure of EUC skills may have several advantages: (1) unlike measures of affect (e.g. user satisfaction), skills construct is less likely to be subject to attitude bias or unstable expectations; (2) measures of skills might provide appropriate indicators for evaluating each stage of the innovation process; (3) it is difficult to imagine how IS impact, user satisfaction, or user involvement can be realized without some degree of end-user computing skills; and (4) although satisfaction is of interest to the immediate user group and system developers, how EUC skills will influence the outcome of innovation process may be of greater interest to executive management.

Despite these advantages, literature in several domains (MIS, innovation theory, impact of IT) has shown little interest in developing measures of EUC skills. A recent study of IS research lists more than 30 MIS topics for the last two decades [9]. While this list suggests significant research in the EUC domain, it indicates little effort directed toward the development of EUC skill measures. Of those studies that examine computer user skills many are designed to measure specific software skills, or professional skills [6,24,27] instead of examining the general proficiency of the end-user. This lack of an instrument is partly due to the lack of widely accepted theoretical definitions of EUC skills and partly due to problems in trying to develop omnibus measures.

This paper reports on the development of an instrument for measuring the end-user computing skills construct. The focus is on operationalizing skills in terms of user perceptions.

## 2. The end-user computing skills construct

EUC refers to direct interaction with application software by managerial, professional, and operating level personnel in user departments [16]. This study uses this definition of end-user and includes individuals who may develop their own applications or use codes written by others. Thus, end-users are identified in terms of their interaction with rather than development of application software.

The EUC skills construct is defined in terms of user’s knowledge and ability to utilize computer hardware, software, and procedures to design, develop, and maintain specific applications for task-related activities. This definition includes skills related to the analysis of information requirements, evaluation of application features, and the ability to improve or modify input and output forms/screens. EUC skills may also include knowledge of high level programming languages.

EUC skills may improve the quality of design decisions and resultant applications, increase user involvement and user satisfaction, improve utilization, and increase user commitment and acceptance of the resultant application. EUC skills help to prevent costly system features that are unacceptable to users [29] or, through end-user tools, to customize an applications more easily in response to individual differences or preferences for output format or content.

Here the EUC skills construct is viewed solely in terms of the end-user rather than being a construct that provides relative expertise via a systems analyst. In other words, end-users see themselves as product designers, managers, financial analysts, and so on and not as systems analysts or programmers. The EUC skill is a potentially measurable construct that may help our understanding of the factors that lead to success.

Understanding EUC skills can enhance our ability to predict or explain IS acceptance and utility. It could help our understanding of what people believe, how people feel, and how they might behave (e.g. a user’s intention voluntarily to use a system, to get involved in an application development process, or to use the system directly versus indirectly through others). It could influence systems success directly (e.g. ease of implementation, increased usage, or more satisfaction) or indirectly (e.g. increased involvement in the continuous improvement process, or increased innovation).

The end-user computing skills is an important theoretical construct, because of its potential for helping us discover both forward and backward links in a causal chain (i.e. a network of cause and effect relationships) that are important to the MIS research community [18,33]. In this ‘‘system to value chain’’, the end-user computing skills construct is potentially both a dependent and independent variable; it is treated as a dependent variable when focus of the research are upstream activities or factors that create successful information systems (e.g. user training, or top management support) as an independent or mediating variable when focus is on downstream factors (e.g. system usage, or user satisfaction).

## 3. Methods

Because there are no widely accepted definitions of the EUC construct, an exploratory study was used to determine skill items from a review of literature and proposals by practitioners. To ensure that a comprehensive list of end-user skill items were included, five practitioners were consulted. Those who assisted us in this study were experienced managers or supervisors in the retail, manufacturing, and communications industries.

To assess whether the items were measures of phenomenon desired and to verify that important aspects of EUC skills were not omitted, comments from practitioners were compared with questions included in the survey. Comments from practitioners enabled us to verify that respondents understood the items. This facilitated instrument refinement and improved the focus. Practitioners felt that the list of items was complete.

We generated 24 end-user skill items to measure technical ability, business knowledge, educational background, and computing experience. Items were not sorted and sub-headings were not used. A global measure of perceived overall computer ability was included to serve as a criterion. A single-item measure of confidence was added to assess the extent of users’ perceived confidence in accepting new computer applications. The 26 items are presented in the Appendix A. The questionnaire used a five-point Likert-type scale, where 1: not at all; 2: a little; 3: moderately; 4: much; and 5: a great deal. The purpose of the survey was described, and respondents were asked to answer demographic questions about the nature of their business, their positions within the organization, their levels of formal education, years of experience with the business, and years of experience with computer systems. The instructions asked the users to circle, for each question, the response that best described their ratings.

To make the results generalizable, we gathered data from a variety of firms. The MIS managers of 22 US firms in northwest OH and southeast MI were contacted and participation of their computer users with the study was requested. Once a firm agreed to cooperate, research assistants directly contacted their end-users, described objectives of the project, and left Likert-type questionnaires for them to complete. The completed questionnaires were either directly mailed by the respondents or were collected by research assistants who contacted users about 2 weeks later. Over half the firms contacted agreed to participate in the project, and over 75% of users completed questionnaires.

## 3.1. Sample characteristics

A sample of 282 usable end-user responses was obtained from a variety of industries and management levels with considerable experience. The industries represented in the sample were manufacturing and processing (26.9%), finance, banking and insurance (12.1%), educational institutions (15%), health services and hospitals (15.3%), wholesale and retail (13.9%), transportation, communication and utilities (5.5%), government agencies (2%), and others (9.3%). The respondents identified themselves as professional employees (48.3%), middle level managers (19.5%), first level managers (11.6%), top-level managers (2.4%), and others (18.3%).

The respondents had on average of 9.3 years of experience ðS:D: ¼ 6:8Þ in their field, and had on average 8.0 ðS:D: ¼ 5:7Þ years of experience with computer systems. Most (73.7%) respondents had college or university degrees in: information systems (22%), accounting and finance (20%), medicine (17%), computer science (12%), engineering (11%), management (11), marketing (8%), and others. Some respondents majored in more than one field. Nearly 80% of respondents were middle level managers, supervisors, or professionals (e.g. market researchers, accountants, engineers).

## 3.2. Survey method

The construct validity of each item was examined to ensure that the items measured the perceived EUC skills. This was established by showing that the instrument was an appropriate operational definition of the construct, it purported to measure. Two methods of construct validation cited by Kerlinger [23] and utilized in MIS research [15,17,22] were used here. The first, correlation between total scores and item scores, assumes that the total score is valid, and the extent to which the item correlates positively with the total score is indicative of construct validity for the item. In this study, each item score was subtracted from the total score in order to avoid a spurious part–whole correlation [11]; the result was a corrected item–total which was then correlated with the item score.

The second approach is factor analysis, which examines the underlying structure or components of the instrument. This method helped to identify factorially pure items that would facilitate the testing of more specific hypotheses [36], and to identify the components that make up the total measure [5]. Bartlett’s test of sphericity had a significance level at 0.0001 suggesting that the intercorrelation matrix contains enough common variance to make factor analysis worth pursuing. The ratio of sample size to number of skill items (12:1) was above the minimum (10:1) ratio suggested for factor analysis by Kerlinger. The items being factor analyzed were selected because they were closely related to each other (i.e. all items were thought to be measures of the same construct); these items may have relatively large common variance and error variance compared to their unique variance.

A measure of criterion-related validity was also examined to identify items that may not be closely related to the end-user computing skills. The global item measuring perceived overall computer ability was used as a criterion scale. To the extent that each item was correlated with this, the scale provided a measure of criterion-related validity. Items should indicate a favorable or unfavorable attitude toward the object in question. If the item is ambiguous or appears to indicate a neutral attitude, it should be eliminated [19]. Often the investigator makes this decision. In this study, a measure of criterion-related validity was examined to identify items that did not indicate favorable or unfavorable attitudes.

The domain sampling model provides a rationale for excluding items before factor analysis. The key assumption is that all items, if they belong to the domain of the concept, have an equal amount of explanatory power. If all the items in a measure are drawn from the domain of a single construct, responses to those items should be highly intercorrelated. The corrected item–total correlation provides a measure of this [8]. Churchill suggests the need to purify the measures at an early stage of research. He contends that: when factor analysis is done before purification, there seems to be a tendency to produce many more dimensions than can be conceptually identified. This is partly due to the ‘‘garbage items’’ that do not have the common core but do produce additional dimensions and confound the interpretation of the factor analysis.

## 4. Data analysis and results

Items were excluded if their correlation with the corrected item–total was <0.5 or if their correlation with the criterion scale was <0.6; there are no accepted standards for this cutoff. The correlations with the corrected item–total and the criterion item were significant at $P < 0 . 0 0 1$ and similar to those used by others in MIS research. Thus, the cutoff was considered high enough to ensure that the retained items were adequate measures of the end-user computing skills. Thus, the number of items was reduced to 16 before conducting factor analysis.

Factor analysis was used to identify the underlying factors or components that comprise the EUC skills construct. Items that were not factorially pure were deleted to form a modified instrument that would facilitate the testing of more specific hypotheses. The use of imprecise and ambiguous terms to label the factors was avoided [1].

Using the sample of 282 responses, the data was examined using principal components analysis as the extraction technique and the varimax method of rotation. Several items had multiple loadings of >0.4 on other (non-primary) factors. Items with many multiple loadings may be good measures of overall end-user computing skills, but including them in the scale blurs the distinction between factors.

To improve the distinction between factors, items which had factor loadings greater than 0.3 on other factors were deleted from the instrument. Three more items were deleted because (1) they had lowest correlations with criterion, (2) they had lowest factor loadings, (3) the alpha level would remain about the same if these three items were deleted, and (4) descriptions of these items were similar to other items that had higher factor loadings and correlations with the criterion. These deletions resulted in a 12-item scale for measuring end-user computing skills. One factor with Eigen value ¼ 7:8 emerged as explaining 65% of the variance. Each of the 12 items had a factor loading >0.70.

The descriptions and loadings for the 12 items are presented in Table 1; grouped by their highest factor loading. Each of the 12 items had a corrected item– total correlation (a measure of internal consistency) >0.64 and a correlation with the criterion measure of >0.56. The correlation for each of the 12 items was positive and significant $( P = 0 . 0 1$ or lower). This 12- item instrument had a reliability (Cronbach’s alpha) of 0.95 and a criterion-related validity of 0.80. In summary, based on the analysis and results, it is suggested that these 12 items provide a reliable and valid instrument for measuring EUC skills. This instrument can be used with confidence by researchers and practitioners alike. However, efforts to provide additional evidence of the instrument’s validity, internal consistency, and stability are encouraged.

## 5. Discussion

Ideally one would like to evaluate the EUC skills construct based on its degree of influence on productivity or quality of work and decisions. Most researchers prefer behaviorally based measures to those measures of affect for many situations. However, a ‘‘decision analysis’’ approach, where actual skills are measured, is generally not feasible unless end-user skills are tied to a specific application and closely monitored. Perceived end-user skills are as critical as actual skills in affecting the users’ attitude toward involvement in the design and development of an application, use of IS, or efforts to modify an application and custom tailor it for a specific task. An application’s utility in decision making, the end-user computing skills, and other individual and organizational factors influence the quality of the information product. Separating the impact of each factor is not always feasible.

The MIS research domain has significantly benefited from studies that employ users’ perception to operationalize behavioral constructs, such as involvement [3,20] or attitudinal constructs such as satisfaction [2,14,31]. In an end-user computing environment where users have continual access to a system and may enhance their skills by both doing and learning, the perceptual measures may be appropriate, especially where the quality of output is perceived.

In this study, items all tap an individual’s perceptions of his or her own skills rather than measuring the skills themselves. Thus a word of caution is appropriate: perceptions do not always correspond to reality; respondents may misrepresent their skills, either deliberately or because they make inaccurate estimates. The items also include questions that measure diverse skills including knowledge and use of high level programming languages, such as SQL. While this reflects the diversity among end-user computing population, it may only be partly appropriate for very low skilled end-users.

Understanding the end-user computing skills construct is important in evaluating the success of enduser computing phenomenon. Although much research has focused on user involvement in the development of applications and the impact of this involvement on user satisfaction and system usage, little research has been done on the relationships between EUC skills and user involvement, user satisfaction, or system usage. The 12-item skill instrument may be used to assess end-user computing skills across departments or for a variety of situations.

## 5.1. Exploring the instrument’s application

This instrument could be used to examine the outcome of training programs designed to improve employee skills or to predict the outcome of application development efforts. Although there may be reasons for additional questions to evaluate specific aspects of end-user computing skills, this set of 12 items are general in nature, relate to end-user knowledge and ability and can be used for variety of situations. This provides a common framework for comparative analysis.

The sample data gathered for this study was taken from a variety of industries and management levels. This cross industry, cross-position feature of the data makes it appropriate for the development of tentative standards. Percentile scores for the 12-item instrument are shown in Table 3, with 60 being the highest score. These statistics may be useful in more precisely evaluating end-user computing skills across departments or positions.

This 12-item end-user computing skills instrument may also be used by MIS researchers. The relationship between user involvement and user satisfaction is complex and may be influenced by other variables. The end-user computing skills construct is an important variable for explaining or predicting the outcome of user involvement in application development situations.

EUC skills are expected to influence user’s confidence in accepting new computer applications. This in turn may be expected to influence user’s desire to be involved, actual level of involvement, and the resultant benefits; e.g. user satisfaction with the system. Although the relationships between these variables are not surprising, the level of these associations or the relative influence of each variable in a framework has not been studied.

To explore the relationships between end-user computing skills, perceived involvement, desired involvement, and user satisfaction, we considered the correlations between them (see Table 4). For this analysis, the proposed multiple-item instrument measured end-user skills. Other variables were measured through singleitem global scales previously used in MIS research. These items were: perceived involvement, ‘‘Overall, how much do you actually get involved in the development of application software?’’; desired involvement, ‘‘Overall, how much do you want to get involved in the development of application software?’’; and satisfaction, ‘‘Overall, how much are you satisfied with your computer systems?’’ A single-item was also used to measure user’s perceived confidence: ‘‘The extent of your confidence in accepting new computer applications?’’ All measures used a five-point scale where 1: not at all, and 5: a great deal.

## 6. Conclusions

One limitation common to many research efforts has been weaknesses in the reliability and validity measures. Reliable instruments are critical for comparative and cumulative research findings. As the need for more focused research increases and evaluating information systems become more acute, researchers and practitioners require more reliable instruments. This paper presents progress toward the development of a standard measure of EUC skills. The proposed instrument is short, easy to use, and appropriate for both practical and research purposes. It appears to have adequate reliability and validity. This instrument can play an important role in research that connects upstream and downstream success constructs from beliefs, to attitudes, to behavior, to the social and economic impacts of IT.
