# An assessment of EDI use and other channel communications on trading behavior and trading partner knowledge

Makoto Nakayama<sup>\*</sup>

School of Computer Science, Telecommunications and Information Systems, DePaul University, 243 South Wabash, Chicago, IL 60604, USA

Received 25 November 2001; received in revised form 28 June 2002; accepted 20 July 2002

## Abstract

Effective communication between trading partners is vital for operational efficiency and better trading relations. Such communication takes place at different managerial levels, using a variety of means, including Electronic Data Interchange (EDI). Interestingly, past studies report both positive and negative effects of EDI use in trading relations. Yet, missing from these studies is an analysis of the relationship between EDI use, the communication outside EDI, and their impact on trading relations.

This study examines retailers and suppliers separately. It analyzes EDI use, trading partner communication frequency between top management and between operational management levels. The focus is on learning how such communications affect partner knowledge, cooperation, conflict, and joint decision making

Survey data from 99 retailers and 72 suppliers found that more partner knowledge by top management resulted in greater EDI use. For retailers, EDI use enhances partner knowledge of operational personnel but it reduces cooperation. For suppliers, in contrast, EDI use does not enhance partner knowledge but it does reduce conflict.

Keywords: Electronic Data Interchange (EDI); Channel communication media; Interorganizational relationship (IOR) bond; Path analysis; Partial least square (PLS)

## 1. Introduction

As information becomes a critical resource in managing marketing channel operations, fair exchange of that information becomes of central concern to channel managers [6,40,41]. Towards that end, numerous reports say that information technology (IT) is a key enabler for successful channel operations and trading relationships (e.g. [12–14,45,50]).

Nevertheless, reports from both the industry and academic press suggest that using Electronic Data

Interchange (EDI) is a double-edged sword. On one hand, EDI facilitates a well-coordinated distribution as well as a better understanding of trading partners operations. On the other hand, channel conflict inevitably occurs due to the uneven distribution of EDI benefits between trading partners.

Yet, EDI is only one of the several communication media that partners use for exchanging trading information. Others include face-to-face meetings, the telephone, e-mail, and informal business gatherings. Because EDI information only covers common trading events in a predefined, formatted manner, other vital trading information must use other means to communicate. For example, unusual operational difficulties and events in the channel are shared through informal conferences, phone calls, emails, and memos. Thus, to gain a greater understanding of how EDI use impacts the whole behavior pattern between trading partners, this study analyzed communications between retailers and suppliers. It investigated how channel communication activities and EDI use impact each other. Do they affect interorganizational relationship (IOR) bonds, such as conflict and cooperation? Furthermore, are there significant differences between retailers and suppliers?

Today EDI is certainly not the only interorganizational IT used. However, unlike the technologically mature EDI, others, such as extranets, supply chain management, and shared database systems are still relatively immature innovations. Therefore, problematic implementation and experimentation in establishing efficient communication patterns are common there, whereas EDI users rely on well-established patterns.

Furthermore, because EDI is a structured medium in terms of communication content and exchange practice, this study can isolate and focus on the impact of structured information exchanges on other communication activities and the IOR bond. In addition, this study can compare and contrast the pattern of communications for retailers versus suppliers.

## 2. Theoretical Background

While some EDI studies find a positive impact of EDI use on the IOR bond, others note a negative impact from EDI use. Yet, marketing studies find that the level of communication determines the nature of the IOR bond (e.g. [3,36]). Because EDI is a structured communication medium (i.e. the elements of the message are predefined), it is unlikely that the volume of EDI impacts the IOR. Therefore, does the level of EDI use depend on the way that firms communicate using other media?

## 2.1. Positive impact of EDI use on IOR

One group of EDI studies found a positive impact on the IOR bond due to EDI use. These studies reasoned that EDI supplies more information to facilitate mutual understanding between EDI users.

Based on field interviews, Stern and Kaufmann [46] conjectured that EDI provides higher satisfaction in the bargaining situation, because it makes available more complete and accurate information. They also noted that setting up an EDI-link requires sustained and frequent contacts between firms at various departmental and organizational levels. Indeed, in another empirical study, Bensaou [5] found that the level of cooperation in buyer–supplier relations was positively related to the scope of EDI use. Using transaction cost economics (TCE), he argued that EDI use increased cooperation because it reduced task uncertainty between the firms. Mackay [30] reported similar findings from surveys conducted with Australian auto suppliers. Using a cross-sectional survey with retailing companies in the US, Vijayasarathy and Robey [49] found that more EDI use leads to higher channel cooperation through its effect on information exchange intensity and channel formalization. Finally, Stump and Sriram [48] reported that the percentage of transactions using IT increases the overall closeness of buyer–supplier relationships. It should be noted that in none of these studies were the responses of retailers and suppliers isolated and analyzed.

## 2.2. Negative IOR impact of EDI

In contrast, some IS studies document the downside risks for IORs from EDI use. Hart and Saunders [22] report that EDI-based information exchange increases a firm’s vulnerability because firms share sensitive operational information with trading partners. Moreover, the adoption of EDI systems creates risks, such as transaction specific investment, information asymmetries, and loss of resource controls [16]. For example, Clemons and Row [15] describe how grocery retailers lose bargaining power because EDI causes an information asymmetry with suppliers. Since retailing is event-driven, retailers depend on promotional funds from manufactures. If EDI gives manufactures detailed store level retail records, the bargaining power of retailers for promotional funds erodes with such arrangement as ‘‘pay for performance.’’ Similarly, other studies [4,26] show that many retailers are reluctant to share detailed sales information with their suppliers, because it gives suppliers more power in their bargaining negotiations. Mackay reported that only about one-third of his survey respondents believed that there was an improvement in trading relations, while the majority indicated no improvement.

Many of the responding auto dealers reported that car manufacturers had ‘‘forced’’ them to adopt EDI, and that EDI benefits went only to the manufacturers.

These studies give two important insights. First, the information shared via EDI can have negative repercussions. Second, the benefits and risks of EDI use are not necessarily equal between retailers and suppliers.

## 2.4. Implications

The model in Fig. 1 is presented as an integration of previous studies. It associates channel communication with EDI use and IOR status. Given that EDI is not the sole communication medium for retailers and suppliers, is the effect of EDI use on the IOR bond (relationship ‘‘C’’) significant when it is compared to how partners exchange information outside EDI (relationship ‘‘B’’)? How does EDI use interact with channel communication practice (relationship ‘‘A’’)?

Therefore, the research questions are:

 What is the relationship between channel communication practice and EDI use?

 What is the impact of communication practice and EDI use on the state of the IOR bond?

 And finally, are the patterns of relationships between communication practice, EDI use and the IOR bond the same for retailers and suppliers?

## 3. Research framework

According to Merriam-Webster’s Collegiate Dictionary [34], the central meaning of the verb ‘‘communicate’’ is ‘‘to convey knowledge of or information about,’’ or simply ‘‘to make known.’’ In other words, the effectiveness of communications first shows up in the level of the knowledge gained by the recipient. For this study, the key foci for assessing communications impact are: how frequently communication takes place, how much knowledge and EDI use results from this communication, and how this impacts the IOR bond. The overview of the hypothesized relationships is shown in Fig. 2.

## 3.1. Communication variables

According to mechanistic communication theory, the key dimensions of communication are frequency, direction, communication medium, and content [35]. In addition, organizational communication involves potentially numerous contact points between the two entities. Because our unit of analysis is the organization, we chose to focus on two levels: top management and operations.

A common empirical approach in social network studies is to analyze each communication activity at the individual level by focusing on a few organizations (e.g. [24,31]). However, such an approach is not suitable for industry-wide, cross-sectional empirical studies. Although EDI has standardized communication content, the majority of the communication by two organizations is between individuals and/or groups in different business units. Worse, the communication content varies in each instance. If the aim of a study is to identify relationships between the cross-sectional organizational communication practice and the IOR bond, assessing the individual content of numerous communication messages and aggregating their meaning at the organizational level is theoretically ambiguous. It is also difficult, on a practical level, to access and collect empirical data. Thus, our study looked at communication frequency and communication venues at the organizational level, while controlling the direction of the communication.

While each firm designs its organizational structure differently, all firms have top management that makes firm level decisions and operations that handle procurement and distribution of products. At the operations level, trading partners exchange purchase orders, invoices, remittances, payment receipts, and order status inquiries to complete their transactions. They also exchange information to coordinate and optimize sales/marketing activities. For example, suppliers ask retailers how well a particular product group is selling in terms of sales volume, price, shelf inventory level, and customer demographics. Suppliers need this information to formulate and/or optimize product distribution and marketing strategies. Retailers, in turn, expect suppliers to help them sell more products. Thus, retailers ask suppliers to tell them of any change to product distribution and marketing strategies. These types of information exchanges, which are characterized by volume and repetition, are typically performed at the operations level.

In contrast, information exchanged at the top management level is neither voluminous nor repetitious. These exchanges usually concern major resource allocation and formal firm level trade agreements. They help top management to make decisions. Although such information exchanges do not always result in making or revising formal trade agreements, their existence, per se, sends a powerful signal of a trading relationship’s importance to the personnel handling day-to-day transactions.

## 3.2. IOR bond variables

One obvious concern for retailers and suppliers is the economic state, such as trading volume and its impact on profitability. However, previous studies did not find a direct relationship between information sharing and the economic state. Thus, our study focuses on the status of the IOR bond, defined as the descriptor for the pattern of interaction between the organizations. Specifically it uses conflict, conflict resolution, joint decision making, and cooperation as the focus because these are commonly used in marketing channel studies. A favorable, strong or positive bond has fewer conflicts, a higher conflict resolution rate, more joint decision making, and cooperation.

Conflict is ‘‘characterized by mutual interference or blocking behavior . . . , while incompatibility of goals, aims, or values exist’’ [47]. Anderson and Narus defined it as ‘‘the overall level of disagreements in the working relationship,’’ and also note that a main concern of managers is resolving disagreements. They argue that disagreements are even beneficial when they are used to clear potentially harmful tensions and ill-will. At all other times, disagreements negatively affect working relationships. Thus, the study assesses conflict resolution by the effect of the outcome of disagreements (positively or negatively) on the bond between the trading partners.

Cooperation is defined as ‘‘similar or complementary coordinated actions taken by firms in interdependent relationships to achieve mutual outcomes or singular outcomes with expected reciprocation over time.’’ In channel operations, such coordinated actions include making operational decisions together (joint decision making) and lending help when a partner firm requests it (cooperation).

With respect to conflict and cooperation, some authors view them as opposite ends of a continuum, while others feel that they are distinct, orthogonal constructs. Recent empirical studies suggest that the two can co-exist in a relationship and are separate constructs, even though they tend to be negatively correlated (e.g. [44]). This study regards conflict and cooperation as two different constructs.

## 3.3. Hypotheses

At the outset, the study hypothesizes that the patterns of relationships among communication variables, EDI use and the IOR bond variables are not equal between retailers and suppliers. Previous studies note that the benefit of EDI use is not necessarily equal because suppliers control the upper stream of marketing channel operations.

H1. The patterns of relationships for communication frequency, partner knowledge, EDI use and the IOR bond are different between retailers and suppliers.

The higher frequency of channel communication relates to the higher level of partner knowledge between two trading firms. The following hypotheses directly test the effectiveness of channel communication at two managerial levels:

H2a. At the top management level, higher communication frequency with a trading partner results in greater partner knowledge.

H2b. At the operations level, higher communication frequency with a trading partner results in greater partner knowledge.

The next two hypotheses deal with top management and operations communication. The top management provides direction and guidance to operations.

H3a. Higher communication frequency with a trading partner at the top management level results in higher communication frequency with the trading partner at the operations level.

H3b. Better partner knowledge at the top management level results in better partner knowledge at the operations level.

The last two sets of hypotheses form the basis of the channel communication process—the relationships between communication frequency and partner knowledge at two managerial levels. Then, how does EDI-use fit into the communication process model?

Past EDI studies frequently found that top management support is a significant factor in the successful adoption and implementation of EDI [19,38,42]. Besides, a number of studies on Efficient Consumer Response (ECR) note that top management commitment to successful EDI implementation is a key driver for adopting an EDI system in retail marketing channels (e.g. [21]). Because a sufficient level of partner knowledge is vital for making such commitment, we hypothesize:

H4a. Better partner knowledge at the top management level results in more EDI use with the trading partner.

Once EDI is implemented, it provides a steady stream of detailed transactional information to operations. If so, EDI use should increase knowledge on the operational status of any partner firm.

H4b. The higher use of EDI with a trading partner results in the higher level of partner knowledge at the operations level.

Nelson [37] reports that low-conflict organizations have more contacts between work units. Some channel firms hide or disguise financial results from their operations personnel in order to influence wholesale price negotiations. When a firm perceives that its trading partner exchanges sufficient trade information, the firm develops a positive IOR bond with its partner. Then that firm becomes more cooperative. Such a view aligns well with field observations that EDI implementation facilitates more communication in various departments between two firms and results in less conflict and more cooperation in bargaining situations. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5. The level of communication frequency with a trading partner results in a positive IOR bond.

H6. Higher level of partner knowledge results in a favorable IOR bond.

Finally, we set forth a hypothesis on the effect of EDI use on the IOR bond.

H7. The higher use of EDI results in favorable IOR bond outcomes.

However, certain EDI transaction sets favor suppliers in this respect. For example, product activity in the X12 EDI standard gives suppliers the information on how efficiently products are sold at the store level. On the other hand, there is no EDI transaction set that informs retailers about the warehouse status of their suppliers.

## 4. Research method

To test the hypotheses, this study collected data from a survey sent to retailers and suppliers. Prior to the survey, two pilot studies were conducted to develop and validate survey instruments.

## 4.1. Unit of analysis

Some past channel IOR studies utilized a dyadinformant method (e.g. [11,23]). That is, their unit of analysis was the combined view of suppliers and buyers (retailers). Our study, however, did not use dyads, because the research focus is not to investigate the on going relationship between retailers and their suppliers but rather to find whether and how the patterns of hypothesized relationships differ between retailers and suppliers.

## 4.2. Samples

EDI implementation requires significant investment. This study avoided retailers with annual sales of US\$ 1 million or less, because they are unlikely to be EDI users. Also, it excluded automotive dealers, apparel and accessory stores, and eating and drinking places—the retailing sectors where the Vijayasarathy and Robey’s survey indicated low levels of EDI use. Furthermore, this study asked respondents to limit their answers to their relation with their top EDI trading partner based on dollar transaction volume. This is because our pilot study indicated that EDI use is not pervasive in retailing. Also, studies indicate a slower-than-expected penetration of EDI in the retailing industry even with the availability of web-based EDI (e.g. [33,43]).

With these criteria, endorsement of the survey from national level, retailers’ trade associations were asked to help maximize the response rate of this unsolicited survey. The study, in fact, obtained endorsements from National Association of Chain Drug Stores (NACDS), North American Retail Dealers Association (NARDA),

National Grocers Association (NGA), and Food Marketing Institute (FMI).

Each association had its endorsing cover letter attached to the survey packet, which was then distributed to its member firms. The questionnaire was distributed through the US mail in October 1998. Three weeks after the survey packet mailing, follow-up contacts were made using fax. Data collection was completed at the beginning of December 1998.

The study had 99 participants from four retail sector of the economy: 30 chain drug stores (response rate of 24.3%); 32 independent, non-food retailers (14.2%); 31 independent grocers (4.2%); and 6 chain grocers (11.1%). In addition, the study had 72 NACDS member suppliers (usable response rate of 11.5%). About one-third of the supplier participants were pharmaceutical firms. The remaining suppliers were the vendors of diverse product types, including healthcare products, food, beverages and office products.

The survey respondents were an executive or an operations level manager in either sales/marketing or the MIS area at these firms. The summary of survey responses from each sector is shown in Table 3.

## 4.3. Testing the hypotheses

Path analyses were performed to test the hypotheses using the partial least square (PLS) method. This needs less demands on measurement scales, sample size, and residual distributions [9]. The PLS method is suitable for this study because some variable constructs are latent variables whose measurement items predict or cause them (formative) while others are the latent variables whose measurement items are caused or predicted by them (reflective) [8,10]. We followed the PLS analysis and reporting procedures that are consistent with those seen in recent IS studies (e.g. [1,28,39,51]).

## 4.3.1. Communication variables and EDI use

Communication frequency was measured using the media that the pilot study identified. It indicated that communication frequencies had a significantly uneven distribution that was driven by irregular events and needs. Respondents consequently had difficulty in specifying their communication frequency in absolute terms. For this reason, communication frequency was measured with a relative scale (e.g. 1, not at all to 5, very frequently) as opposed to an absolute scale (e.g. 1, a few times a day to 5, once in a few months).

Partner knowledge assesses how much in-depth knowledge is obtained by managers from trading communications; these focus on the partner’s strategy, product strategy, and operational situations.

Our study captures the level of EDI use in two ways: the number of EDI transaction sets used and the frequency of EDI exchanges. Among the four facets of EDI use proposed by Massetti and Zmud [32], volume (proportion of EDI documents over all documents exchanged) and diversity (number of EDI document types) are relevant for this study.<sup>3</sup> Concerning the ‘‘volume’’ of EDI use, the pilot study revealed that various documents were exchanged as-needed on occasions through various media, such as face-to-face meetings, fax and email. Such irregularity makes it difficult for respondents to estimate the ‘‘volume.’’ Therefore, our study focuses on EDI exchange frequency while all the irregular non-EDI document exchanges are assessed through the communication frequency.

## 4.3.2. IOR bond variables

Several variables were used to describe the status of an IOR bond. Conflict was assessed by how often disagreements occurred. Conflict resolution was evaluated by how the outcomes of disagreements positively affected the productivity of the working relationship. The extent of collaboration between the two parties was measured in two ways: (1) the degree of joint decision making and (2) if and how much trading partners help each other (cooperation). The pilot study indicated that most relevant IOR communications occurred in a firm’s marketing and sales functional areas. Therefore, five purchasing and marketing items were selected to measure conflict resolution and the resulting cooperation (see Appendix A).

Figs. 3 and 4 show the results of PLS analyses for retailers and suppliers, respectively. Overall, the pattern of relationships among communication frequency and partner knowledge at two managerial levels are the same for both retailer and supplier samples. However, these two samples give different results for the relationship between communication and EDI use as well as how they impact the IOR bond.

## 5.1. Retailer sample

The significant paths indicate a positive impact from top management communication to how operational personnel communicate and to EDI use. The path coefficients between the top management and operational personnel were $0 . 5 1 \ ( \alpha \leq 0 . 0 1 )$ for communication frequency and $0 . 2 7 \ : ( \propto \le 0 . 0 1 )$ for partner knowledge. This supports H3.

The path coefficients between communication frequency and partner knowledge were 0.43 $( \alpha \leq 0 . 0 1 )$ for top management and $0 . 5 1 \ ( \alpha \leq 0 . 0 1 )$ for operations. Thus, H2 was affirmed.

As hypothesized, partner knowledge of top management had a significant path coefficient path of 0.19 $( \alpha \leq 0 . 1 0 )$ to EDI use in terms of the number of transaction sets used. In turn, EDI use enhanced partner knowledge of operations with a path coefficient of 0.13 at $\alpha \leq 0 . 1 0 .$ , supporting H4.

## 6. Implications

## 6.1. Assessment of the results

Overall, the results show that retailers and suppliers not only use communication media differently, but the impact of EDI and communications also vary in their patterns.

The results raise two major questions. First, why does the study find a negative impact from EDI use for retailers but a positive impact for suppliers? Second, why does top management communication have no significant relationship to the variables that make up the IOR bond for either the retailer or supplier?

One possible explanation for the first question is that retailers and suppliers use more vendor-managed inventory (VMI) in which store level information is transferred to suppliers. That is, suppliers collect more retail store-shelf information with greater EDI use. Thus, they apparently perceive that the information gained from EDI can resolve disagreements positively and facilitate the operations managers’ day-to-day communication with the retailers. In contrast, operational decisions tend to go in the opposite direction with VMI. This gives retailers an impression that suppliers try to design and execute channel operations only from their vantage point.

There are two possible explanations for the lack of direct relationships between top management communication and the IOR bond. First, the role of top management is to set the stage for a trading relationship by agreeing to the ground rules, while the bulk of the actual trading negotiations are performed by operational managers. Second, the IOR bond variables here focused primarily on ‘‘operational items,’’ such as wholesale pricing, promotions planning, and payment schemes. It may well be that operations usually handle these items and top management intervenes only when critical ‘‘exceptions,’’ such as serious trading conflicts, occur. Thus, neither side sees a direct relationship between top management communication and the variables that comprise the IOR bond.

Looking at the retailers’ pattern, we find EDI use is meshed with the channel communication process. Increased top management communication enhances its partner knowledge as well as operational level communication. Increased top management partner knowledge results in more EDI use and improves operational level partner knowledge, which is also enhanced by EDI use. Thus, EDI use can be regarded as a facilitator for the retailer’s communication process. Nevertheless, retailers find the more EDI use, the less helpful the supplier partner is. This suggests that EDI use helps internally but it can have a negative impact on trading relationships.

Now with respect to the suppliers’ pattern, when top management is communicating frequently with its retailer counterparts that not only improves top management’s partner knowledge but also facilitates the communication process at the operational level. Improving top management’s partner knowledge also leads to more EDI use, which reduces conflict. The improved partner knowledge at an operational level leads to more joint decision making with retailers. Yet, unlike the pattern for retailers, EDI use does not enhance partner knowledge at the operational level.

There are a few other notable differences between retailers and suppliers. Although joint decision making is impacted by the operational level communication for suppliers, retailers provide a different pattern. Communication frequency, but not partner knowledge, is a significant factor, whereas suppliers find operational level partner knowledge significant. In addition, retailers do not find wholesale pricing and payment schemes to be significant dimensions for joint decision making while suppliers find all five dimensions of trading negotiations significant (wholesale pricing, promotions planning, sales strategy, logistics coordination, payment schemes).

Telephone, fax, and email are the significant communication means for retailer executives. In contrast, the significant communication channels for supplier executives are trade shows, associations, and industry task forces. Interestingly, operations for both retailers and suppliers find face-to-face meetings and non-formal business settings, such as meals, golfing, or other recreational activities are significant means of communication. Finally, for EDI use, retailers find significant the number of transaction sets used, but not the frequency of EDI exchanges. In contrast, suppliers find the exact opposite significant.

## 6.2. Limitations

These results show that EDI use has a complex and complicated relationship with both the channel of communication and the IOR bond. At the same time, both communication channel and EDI use are not the sole determinants of the IOR bond. This is not surprising; previous marketing studies find that the channel of communication can impact cooperation, conflict, or opportunistic behaviors, but it is not the only factor influencing the IOR bond. For example, Chwelos et al. [7] report that EDI adoption is strongly influenced by external pressures, such as competition and bargaining power.

There are other limitations of this study. First, the supplier sample was drawn from only one retail-segment (chain pharmacy). Second, the study evaluated only EDI use, ignoring other types of channel IT use, such as extranets and shared databases. The other types had not established a level of technological maturity comparable to EDIs. Finally, the scope of the study did not include the contextual evaluation of channel IOR bonds. For example, a pair consisting of a retailer and a supplier may be in contention or in strategic alliance with each other. Does such a situation impact the channel of communication model that this study used?

## 7. Conclusion

This study extended the scope of previous EDI research from a channel communication research perspective. Historically, the IS research stream tended to regard IT use as a main, if not sole, explanatory variable. Our work places non-IT variables (e.g. communication frequency, partner knowledge) side-by-side with EDI use when empirically examining the relationship between channel communication process, EDI use, and the IOR bond.

The analysis of survey data showed that retailers and suppliers do have different views on EDI impact on the IOR bond. This study gives insights on how EDI use impacts differently the trading communication process and, in turn, on the IOR bond for suppliers and retailers. For retailers, EDI facilitates the internal channel communication process but has a negative impact on cooperation. In contrast, suppliers enjoy a positive impact of EDI use on conflict. Yet, EDI does not have any direct effect on internal communications. Overall, channel communication has a positive impact on joint decision making.
