# The effect of task uncertainty, decentralization and AIS characteristics on the performance of AIS: an empirical case in Taiwan

Ruey-Dang Chang<sup>a,\*</sup>, Yeun-Wen Chang<sup>b</sup>, David Paper<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Business Management, National Sun Yat-Sen University, Kaohsiung 804, Taiwan <sup>b</sup>Providence University, Taichung, Taiwan <sup>c</sup>Utah State University, Logan, UT, USA

Received 26 March 2001; received in revised form 20 March 2002; accepted 17 August 2002

## Abstract

This study investigated the fit between contingency variables (e.g. task uncertainty and decentralization) in accounting information systems (AIS); a good fit is assumed to improve user satisfaction. We divided task uncertainty into two dimensions: variability and analyzability. We chose information scope, timeliness, and aggregation as the AIS characteristics to be studied within each dimension. User satisfaction was adopted as a surrogate to measure the performance of AIS. The responses of 126 managers were drawn from a cross-industry sample in Taiwan. Multiple regression models were employed to test the hypotheses. The results of the empirical test led us to two results: (1) broad-scope information (external environment or future-oriented information) promotes user satisfaction in high task variability situations, and (2) in a highly decentralized organization, broad-scope, timely, and aggregated information will also facilitate user satisfaction. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Accounting information systems (AIS); Information system (IS); Information characteristics; Task uncertainty; Decentralization; Contingency theory; User satisfaction

## 1. Introduction

The issue in the design of accounting information systems (AIS) has long received the attention of AIS researchers and their users. Many studies have examined the effects of contextual variables on AIS design and performance (e.g. [6,8,9,21,23,24]). These suggest that there must be an appropriate ‘‘fit’’ between contextual variables (e.g. environment, task, and structure) and AIS information characteristics to enhance managerial performance. However, empirical results lack the necessary consistency to draw appropriate conclusions.

Barney [4] suggests that effective information processing helps information flow more smoothly to decision makers, who can make more informed decisions more quickly, thereby giving the organization a substantial competitive advantage. However, Galbraith [14] warns that information overload can result from even the most effective system. This is why many studies stress the importance of the fit between task and technology [34]. Matching information technology with business tasks and user needs can significantly upgrade managerial performance and user satisfaction [19,43].

AIS-oriented researchers have been primarily concerned with an organization’s information system (IS) within the context of given environmental and organizational conditions. Under this view, information systems are seen as facilitating decision-making within organizations and should be tailored to an organization’s environment, requirements of task, and structure [6,8]. Because AIS are part of a company’s IS, they should reflect its operational activities accurately [47]. Over the past few years, we conducted informal interviews with managers of information departments in many organizations. From these, we found that reliable and accurate accounting information was considered very important in facilitating decision-making and was therefore given top priority by most managers. However, these interviews were not part of a formal survey, but served as an a priori information gathering exercise to determine the validity of our study.

We believe that AIS play a major role in providing decision-makers with appropriate information concerning management, auditing, and control functionality. Consistent with the literature and our presumptions about the decision-making importance of AIS based on our experiences and informal interviews with managers, we decided to examine the fit between the type of information provided by AIS and the level of task uncertainty and decentralization.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses

## 2.1. The information characteristics and performance of AIS

Wilkinson suggests that AIS provide three different functions:

\- Recording, processing, and analysis of economic data and display of the results in financial formats.

\- A summary of an organization’s operating activities.

\- Enterprise-wide information summaries to facilitate managerial decision-making.

Chenhall and Morris suggested that the characteristics of AIS were: scope, timeliness, aggregation, and integration. Scope refers to the dimensions of focus, quantification, and time horizon. Narrow-scope information is concerned with events internal to the organization, and provides financial and historic data. Broad-scope information includes external, non-financial, and future-oriented material. Timeliness refers to the ability of an AIS to provide information on request from systematically collected information. Aggregation of information provides summarized information that covers periods of time or diverse management areas. Integration of information puts together data that crosses functional boundaries. Hence, it is useful to managers in decentralized subunits with high organizational interdependence.

While these characteristics are useful to management, they may not be incorporated in an AIS. Since it may be impractical to incorporate all these, we embarked on this study to uncover the most important ones. We decided to examine the fit between characteristics of AIS and contingent variables (task uncertainty and decentralization) that promote performance of an AIS. We chose AIS performance because it seemed the most logical link toward promoting organizational effectiveness.

Kim [30] defines a successful IS as one that helps the organization obtain its objectives. Utilization and user satisfaction are the most frequently cited factors used to measure IS success [1,3,7]. However, many studies report that utilization and performance had no significant relationship when users were told to use IT [10,16]. Goodhue and Thompson argue that even voluntary use of IT does not guarantee increased performance, especially when the system is used inappropriately. Therefore, our study did not use utilization to measure the performance of an AIS. We concentrated on user satisfaction as a measure.

## 2.2. Task uncertainty and AIS

Galbraith [15] suggests that task uncertainty can be defined as the difference between the amount of information needed to complete a task and the amount of information already possessed. Perrow [38] proposes two basic dimensions of task uncertainty: variability and analyzability. This is one of the major determinants of information processing requirements for an organization. Ghani [18] suggests that task variability affects the amount of information required to handle unexpected events and task analyzability affects the form of information necessary to resolve ambiguities.

A number of researchers argue that the capacity of information processing must fit the processing requirements to obtain high managerial performance [27]. For example, AIS that provide broader information under high task uncertainty can help and probably increase managerial performance. On the other hand, the extent of use of broad-scope AIS information led to information overload in low task uncertainty situations; this was dysfunctional to managerial performance. MacIntosh and Daft [33] indicated that cause and effect relationships were not well understood under high task uncertainty, and therefore multiple focused or aggregated information may be needed. Finally, under high task uncertainty, managers needed more timely accounting information to control the daily operation and solve unusual problems or events.

Mauldin and Ruchala suggest a matching process between requirements of task and AIS design alternatives at multiple levels of analysis. This is important, because over-capacity results in information overload and wastes precious resources. However, capacity shortages may not provide the necessary information flow to managers. Hence, many studies focus on the relation between task-technology fit and performance.

MacIntosh [32] used task knowledge and task variety to classify the unit of task into four types: routine technology, technical–professional technology, craft technology, and research technology. He suggested that different types of tasks should match different types of IS. Goodhue and Thompson reported that IT must be fully utilized and match with task characteristics to enhance individual performance. Many studies embrace contingency theory to study the relationship between organic design and AIS performance [13,35].

In sum, the literature therefore suggests that performance is linked to a fit between contextual variables (e.g. environment, task, and organization structure) and the characteristics of AIS.

According to contingency theory, the organization’s information processing capacity or control systems (e.g. AIS) should match the business requirements of the organization [17]. Consequently, a fit should provide more effective information flow that improves managerial performance. The first question we examined was whether the fit between task uncertainty and characteristics of AIS can really enhance the performance of AIS.

According to Perrow, task variability is the number of exceptions encountered in work, and the methods or procedures required for completion. It also can be defined as the frequency of unexpected and novel events that occur when operating a task [42]. Specht further indicated that when the task variability is high, broad-scope information is needed to cover various exceptional events, and the need for manipulated information (this includes analyzed or aggregated data (means, ranges, etc.) and decision aids (e.g. graphics)) may also increase to reduce the time required to make a decision. Chong also showed that in a high task uncertainty situation the extent of use of broad-scope information led to effective managerial decisions and hence to improved managerial performance. Therefore, information users may have to process and receive more broad-scope, timely, and aggregated information to deal with higher variable tasks. We thus developed the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1a. When the task variability is high, AIS that provide broad-scope information will have a positive impact on the performance of AIS.

Hypothesis 1b. When the task variability is high, AIS that provide timely information will have a positive impact on the performance of AIS.

Hypothesis 1c. When the task variability is high, AIS that provide aggregated information will have a positive impact on the performance of AIS.

Task analyzability can be defined as the availability of concrete knowledge about task activities and the degree of complexity of the search process in performing the task. This study adopted task non-analyzability to replace task analyzability in order to obtain the same analysis direction as the task variability.

First, when the task non-analyzability is high, the task is not well understood, and there are no objective and correct procedures or rules to follow. Therefore, in high task non-analyzability situations, users need broad-scope and timely information to facilitate their decision-making. Second, cause and effect relationships are not well understood under high task non-analyzability and multiple focus or aggregated information may be needed. Because aggregated information is less specific, it may be more helpful when searching for multiple answers or solutions. From this, we formulated the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2a. When the task analyzability is high, AIS that provide broad-scope information will have a positive impact on the performance of AIS.

Hypothesis 2b. When the task analyzability is high, AIS that provide timely information will have a positive impact on the performance of AIS.

Hypothesis 2c. When the task analyzability is high, AIS that provide aggregated information will have a positive impact on the performance of AIS.

Table 1 shows how we formulated hypothesis sets 1 and 2. The literature defines task and AIS characteristics, but the cause–effect on performance is not well understood. All of the studies in the AIS column assume a connection with task uncertainty. Here we use satisfaction as the surrogate measure of performance, to be consistent with existing literature. Although previous studies suggest that the interaction effects of task and AIS characteristics as they relate to performance should be explored, we could find no studies that did explicitly do so.

## 2.3. Decentralization and AIS

In some organizations, top managers make all of the decisions and direct lower-level managers to carry them out. At the other extreme, decision-making is pushed down to those who are closest to the action. The former organizations are described as highly centralized, and the later as decentralized, though an organization may never be truly either one [39]. Under highly decentralized organizations, managers tend to require aggregated information to control and coordinate the activities of interdependent subunits. Broadscope information is also needed to serve the diversity of decisions faced by the decentralized manager [40]. We examined the relationship between decentralization and AIS characteristics, given the broad-scope of information required in these situations.

The issue of structural decentralization and AIS characteristics has generated much debate and subsequent research [11,20]. The contingency view of AIS design proposes that AIS should suit the extent of decentralization within the firm. Chenhall and Morris indicate that aggregated and integrated information is perceived favorably by decentralized managers, who should put emphasis on the coordination of the subunits given highly decentralized organizational environments. They also argue that AIS should provide aggregated and integrated information that facilitates the coordination of the subunits. Gul and Chia suggest that AIS provide broad-scope and aggregated information that improves managerial performance under high environmental uncertainty and decentralization. Choe reports that broad-scope, timely, and aggregated information with high user participation in the design of AIS has a positive influence on the performance in a highly decentralized organization. However, Gordon and Narayanan report that, after controlling for the effect of the environment, AIS and structure do not appear to be significantly interrelated.

Consistent with the literature, we posit that broadscope and aggregated information provided by AIS aids the coordination of subunits and improves user satisfaction in highly decentralized organizations. Choe also indicates that more time is apt to be spent on producing information for alternatives and decision-making as a result of coordination and control problems in a decentralized organization. As such, manager response time may increase. Hence, high performance AIS should produce timely information to counteract information delay.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. The instrument and sampling procedure

The survey instrument consisted of a 29-item questionnaire. It was first constructed in English with the assistance of three information technicians. The English version was then translated into Chinese using a two-stage procedure recommended by Werner and Campbell [45], Brislin [5], and Farh et al. [12]. Initial translation was conducted by two of the researchers (rather than a volunteer or paid professional). A panel of three experts reviewed the translated Chinese version and the original English version of the questionnaire and revisions were made based on this. A pilottest was then performed using eight EMBA students of a major university in southern Taiwan. The sample was purposeful in that we chose the eight students because they had the expertise in the business domain. That is, they averaged at least eight years of managerial experience. In addition, they were willing to participate in the pilot and the post-pilot interviews. Each student completed the entire 29-item questionnaire and was then interviewed to ascertain their answers to the following questions: (1) Did you understand the questions? (2) Were the instructions clear? (3) Was the length of the survey reasonable? We used their responses to pre-validate the instrument. The pilot-test results indicated that the instrument contained no ambiguities and its length was reasonable. The survey was then mailed to high-level managers of 300 randomly selected companies that had implemented AIS and were listed on the Taiwan Stock Exchange.

Approximately 1 week after the survey packets were sent, phone calls were made to ensure that the selected companies had received the packets. Using the technique advocated by Oppenheim [37] and Armstrong and Overton [2], non-response bias was evaluated by comparing the subject responses received before the follow-up reminder (76) with the responses received after the follow-up reminder (50). No significant differences were found between these groups regarding the variables or the demographic data.

## 3.2. Measurements

## 3.2.1. Task uncertainty

3.2.1.1. Task variability. The items used to measure task variability were obtained from previous studies [29,46]; they included: (1) In your opinion, to what extent are your daily tasks routine? (2) To what extent is your work routine? (3) To what extent do you complete your work the same way most of the time? (4) To what extent do you perform repetitive activities in doing your job ? and (5) How repetitious are your duties? These five items were measured by a seven-point Likert-type scale and reversely scored.

3.2.1.2. Task analyzability. Task analyzability measures are also taken from previous studies; we measured it using five items with a seven-point Likert-type scale. The items include: (1) To what extent is there a clearly known way to do your major work? (2) Is there anyone who can guide you to finish the work? (3) To what extent do you understand the sequence of steps in doing your work? (4) To what extent can you actually rely on established procedures and practices? (5) To what extent is there an understandable sequence of steps that can be followed in carrying out your work?

## 3.2.2. Decentralization

Decentralization provides managers with greater responsibility over planning and control activities and greater access to information not available to the corporate body [44]. The items used to measure it were again obtained from previous studies [25]. The items were based on the extent to which authority has been delegated to the appropriate managers for each of the following four types of decisions: (1) development of new products or services, (2) selection of large investments, (3) budget allocations, and (4) pricing decisions. We also included two additional items: (5) to what extent have decisions been delegated to appropriate employees overall? and (6) to what extent will the company support employees to fulfill the decisions that they make? A seven-point Likert-type scale was used to measure these six items.

## 3.2.3. The characteristics of AIS

3.2.3.1. Scope of information. This refers to the dimensions of focus, quantification, and time horizon [22,31], measured with three items using a seven-point Likert-type scale. These items were also formulated by reviewing and integrating methods developed in previous studies. They are: (1) the amount of non-financial information (e.g. the rate of output, demographic factors, and scale or growth of the market), (2) the amount of external economic information (e.g. competitors’ actions, information of industries and technological advances), and (3) the amount of future information.

3.2.3.2. Timeliness of information. This refers to the speed and frequency of information provided by the AIS. Consistent with Chenhall, Morris and Choe, we measured timeliness with two items using a sevenpoint Likert-type scale. The items include the speed and frequency of AIS reporting.

3.2.3.3. Aggregation of information. This involves summarizing information across different periods and departments (it could also include specific information for formal decisions). We measured aggregation with two items using a seven-point Likert-type scale. The items are the spread of information across different departments or responsibility centers and the dispersion of detailed information.

## 3.2.4. The performance of AIS

Choe argues that user satisfaction is a viable measure of AIS performance. Our measures are based on a set of six items developed by Kim et al. Each uses a seven-point Likert-type scale. The items include the following: (1) AIS helps me do my work better, (2) AIS allows me to carry out my work more easily and efficiently, (3) AIS enables me to carry out my work more effectively, (4) AIS enables me to make better decisions, (5) the information provided by AIS is extremely useful for me, and (6) overall, I am now satisfied with the AIS.

## 4.2. The interaction between task variability and characteristics of AIS

Next, we measured the interactions between the characteristics of AIS and task variability (Hypotheses 1a–c). We used the following regression model as a basis for examining the effects:

$$
P = b _ {0} + b _ {1} X _ {1} + b _ {2} Y _ {i} + b _ {3} X _ {1} Y _ {i}\tag{1}
$$

P represents the performance of AIS, b the regression coefficients, $X _ { 1 }$ the task variability, $Y _ { i }$ the characteristics of AIS (scope i ¼ 1, timeliness i ¼ 2, aggregation $i = 3 )$ and $X _ { 1 } Y _ { i }$ the interaction effects between task variability and characteristics of AIS. The regression models for testing breakdown into:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \bullet P = b _ {0} + b _ {1} X _ {1} + b _ {2} Y _ {1} + b _ {3} X _ {1} Y _ {1 (\text { task   variability } + \text { scope })}, \\ \bullet P = b _ {0} + b _ {1} X _ {1} + b _ {2} Y _ {2} + b _ {3} X _ {1} Y _ {2 (\text { task   variability } + \text { timeliness })}, \\ \text { and } \end{array}
$$

$$
\bullet \quad P = b _ {0} + b _ {1} X _ {1} + b _ {2} Y _ {3} + b _ {3} X _ {1} Y _ {3 (\text { task   variability } + \text { aggregation })}.
$$

Tables 7–9 provide the empirical results of the multiple regression models. Hypotheses 1a–c test the impact of task variability and AIS characteristics (taken together) on performance. The coefficient of interaction $( X _ { 1 } Y _ { i } )$ term needs to be a positive value $( b _ { 3 } > 0 )$ to be significant. As can be seen, the only significant interaction term is from Hypothesis 1a $( b _ { 3 } = 0 . 6 0 8 ;$ ; P-value ¼ 0.014). This means that there is evidence that task variability and information scope (taken together) have a positive effect on the performance of AIS. In other words, performance is positively impacted when broad-scope information is used to accomplish tasks that are dynamic and not routine. People that are required to complete tasks with high variability (non-routine) do so in a more effective manner (i.e. they perform better) when they have access to broad-scope information to address issues and problems as they arise. Thus, Hypothesis 1a is supported.

## 5. Conclusion

The study examined two main effects: whether task uncertainty interacting with information characteristics had a positive effect on performance and whether decentralization interacting with information characteristics had a positive effect on performance. We found that non-routine tasks when supplemented with broad-scope information, can improve performance. We also found that performance can be improved in decentralized organizations when broad-scope, timely, and aggregated information is provided.

We used user satisfaction as the surrogate variable to measure AIS performance. We did not, however, measure the effect of AIS on managerial performance. The main reason we used satisfaction is that this variable was used as a surrogate in the literature.

There are several limitations, which may need to be taken into consideration when we try to derive any implications from the study. The use of a self-reporting methodology makes it possible that the results were biased due to the use of respondents’ perceptions and common method variance [26]. Another limitation is that the researchers were not present to observe sample selection and subject participation, and this diminished our control over the administration of sample criteria. Finally, subjects were from one country and this may restrict the ability to generalize the results to others.
