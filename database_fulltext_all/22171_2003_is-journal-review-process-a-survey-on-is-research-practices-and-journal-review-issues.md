# IS journal review process: a survey on IS research practices and journal review issues

Chang E. Koh

Department of Business Computer Information Systems, College of Business Administration, The University of North Texas, P.O. Box 305249, Denton, TX 76203-5249, USA

Received 16 September 2001; received in revised form 16 February 2002; accepted 13 October 2002

## Abstract

Journal publication is an important indicator of research productivity for individual researchers as well as academic institutions. It is also a contentious issue as various stakeholders have different and often conflicting interests and perspectives. This study explores how IS researchers view journal review and publication process based on their professional status, institutional mission, and role orientation. It also investigates publication practices of IS researchers, such as frequency of article submissions, acceptance/rejection rates, number of revisions required before publication, and publication outlets. <sup>#</sup> 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information systems research; Information systems publication; IS journal review process; Journal quality

## 1. Introduction

The importance of journals in an academic field is fully understood as they play three critical roles: (1) a repository of important intellectual work; (2) a communication channel for scholars within the field, as well as various stakeholders outside the field; and (3) a recognition and reward mechanism for researchers and institutions who made a contribution to the advance of the field [22]. Journal publication is considered the most important indicator of research productivity for individual researchers as well as academic institutions [16,18]. With the career of a researcher and the reputation of an institution at stake, scholars and schools need pertinent journal publication strategies. Numerous studies have approached issues associated with journal publication in IS and IS-related fields. For example, they measured perceived quality of various IS journals using different subjects (e.g. IS researchers, university administrators, and the editors of journals) and different methods (e.g. citation analyses, mail surveys, and interviews). Table 1 summarizes such studies on IS publications. Undoubtedly, all these efforts have positively contributed to shape IS as a coherent research field and helped researchers and institutions better plan and implement sound publication strategies [2,3,12]. However, the focus of these studies has been on the quality of IS journals as final products and little or no attention has been given to the process by which papers are submitted, reviewed, and selected for publication. Information about journal publication practices and experiences of IS researchers is scarce, while questions abound. How many articles do IS researchers submit and how often? Where do they submit? How many articles are accepted for publication, after how many revisions? How long does it take for an article to be reviewed, revised, and accepted for publication?

How articles are submitted, reviewed, and selected for publication is perhaps as important as what articles are actually published. While the field of IS has matured rapidly, issues and problems associated with journal publications persist. The journal review process has always been a contentious issue and intensely debated among various stakeholders. Some critics of current journal review systems even charge that the current review process is flawed to a point that it requires drastic changes such as the abolition of the blind review requirement from the process [21]. The objective of this study was to provide stakeholders of the journal review process with a common framework and sounding board for further discussion by: (1) identifying various issues and problems associated with IS journal review process; and (2) assessing how different stakeholders perceive these issues and problems. The study also investigated journal publication practices of IS researchers.

## 2. The study

The study was conducted in two phases. A preliminary survey with 15 IS journal editors and 57 IS researchers provided an initial list of journal review issues and problems. The second phase surveyed 133 IS researchers on their perception of journal review issues and journal publication practices using a survey instrument developed from results of the preliminary study.

## 2.1. Research framework

Journal review is a highly contentious and subjective matter as various stakeholders have different and often conflicting interests and perspectives. To study the similarities and differences in the perception of the journal review and the publication process held by different stakeholders, I adopted the three-factor model used by Walstrom and coworkers in their studies [9,19,20] (see Fig. 1). Based on Howard and Sheth’s buyer behavior model [13,17], Walstrom et al. conjectured that the three factors affect the IS researcher’s perception of IS journals: (1) specialized education (i.e. underlying discipline of the researcher); (2) role orientation (e.g. publication experience and editorial experience); and (3) life style (e.g. academic ranks). They argue that, just like consumers who exhibit different buyer behaviors depending on their various backgrounds, researchers may draw different conclusions about the quality of a journal according to their predisposed characteristics.

In the same line of reasoning, I proposed a threefactor model to study the IS researchers’ perception of the IS journal review process as shown in Fig. 2. Researchers at different stages of their career, with affiliation to institutions of different missions have different needs, expectations, and standards and are likely to develop different attitudes toward the IS journal review process. For example, junior faculty members, fresh from a graduate school, should have different perspectives on journal review issues than their senior counterparts [6]. The mission of the school with which the researcher is affiliated is also likely to have an impact on the research and publication strategies. A faculty member at an institution with a greater emphasis on research should have different publication expectations from a counterpart at a more ‘‘teaching-oriented’’ school [23]. A researcher’s attitude toward a journal review process is also likely to be affected by his/her role in the process. In a journal review process, a researcher plays one or more of the three roles: (1) an author who submits a paper to a journal for publication; (2) a reviewer who provides independent feedback regarding the paper’s merits and demerits and makes a recommendation to the editor; and (3) an editor who sets and implements rules and standards for contributing authors and reviewers. Most researchers play a dual role of author and reviewer throughout their research career, but relatively few have editorial experience. Having or not having such experience can have a substantial influence on their perception of various review issues. Fig. 2 depicts my journal review process research model. Note that this replaces the first factor (i.e. specialized education or underlying discipline) in the Walstrom model with a new institutional factor. Walstrom et al. hypothesized that IS researchers with different discipline associations would have different opinions about IS and ISrelated journals. For example, researchers with a management science background tend to read and value a different set of journals from those with a marketing background. Today IS is far more established as a discipline than before. IS is still one of the most interdisciplinary fields and its research community representing the widest array of disciplines, IS is now matured to be viewed as an independent, unified field of study. For this reason, I removed Walstrom’s first criterion and replaced it with the institutional mission factor.

## 2.2. Preliminary survey

A short survey questionnaire was first sent to editors of 20 major IS journals. It asked them to evaluate seven journal review issues in terms of their importance (Table 2). The editors were invited to add any other issues they felt important but missing. The same questionnaire was also mailed to 100 IS researchers randomly selected from the list of participants of a major IS conference. Fifteen editors (75% response rate) and 57 researchers (57% response rate) responded to the survey.

Of the seven journal review issues presented in the questionnaire, both the editors and the IS researchers who responded rated the quality of review comments as the most important issue. Other journal review issues considered important included objectivity of the review process, selection of reviewers, review turnaround, and the blindness of the process. On the other hand, the respondents rated the number of reviewers and the consistency of review as less important. Some researchers suggested that consistency does not necessarily imply a higher review quality, because conflicting reviews may reveal the strengths and weaknesses of the paper. An editor even suggested that he often intentionally choose reviewers with potentially different views to represent different perspectives. Overall, however, there was considerable consensus on what are important issues between the two groups—editors and contributing researchers. The only exception was the issue of blind review. The researchers considered the blindness more important than did the editors. To further investigate this and other issues along with factors that may affect the importance of the issues, a full-scale survey was designed and administered in the second phase.

## 2.3. Main survey

Based on the findings, comments, and suggestions, a full-scale questionnaire was developed. This asked the respondent to evaluate 14 statements regarding various issues of the process and to indicate the extent to which he/she agrees or disagrees with the statement using a Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The 14 questions are presented in Table 3.

The objective of the main survey study was to assess how IS researchers perceive various journal review issues differently, based on their personal and institutional attributes, such as the respondent’s academic rank and the highest degree offered by the institution. The survey also asked the respondent to assess the number of papers submitted and the number accepted during the previous 3 years. The respondent was also asked to report the number of revisions before a paper was accepted or rejected. It also asked respondents how many papers they had reviewed during the same 3-year period. Finally, it asked if the respondent was a current editor or had served on the editorial board of an IS journal.

## 2.4.2. Institutional characteristics

The sample, with regard to the research versus teaching orientation, appears to be skewed toward more research-oriented institutions. The composition of institutions as reported in the Directory of MIS Faculty is almost evenly divided among the three groups as shown in the last row of Table 5. It is possible that researchers affiliated with a researchoriented institution had a higher level of interest in the study and responded at a higher rate.

## 2.4.3. Editorial experience

A larger percentage of the respondents (59.2%) reported having served on the editorial board of an IS journal. A surprisingly high number of the respondents—43 respondents or 32.6%—reported that they were currently an editor of a journal. This may reflect the strong research propensity of the researchers who participated in this study.

## 2.5. Research publication practices

## 2.5.1. Publication profile

The average number of papers submitted to journals by the respondents within the past 3 years was nine. Of the total number of papers submitted, 5.4 had been accepted for publication while 2 were still in review. Table 6 summarizes the fate of the nine papers in terms of their status and the number of revisions. About 46% of the papers accepted for publication were after one revision and 26% after a second. While 1 out of 5 papers was accepted without revision, about 10% required more than two revisions before they were published. On the other hand, the majority of rejected papers (69%) were rejected outright. About 19% of rejected papers were rejected after the first revision. Thirteen percent of the rejected papers were rejected after two or more revisions.

## 2.5.3. Review practices

The intellectual development of a field heavily relies on the service of reviewers who objectively assess the strengths and weaknesses of an intellectual work and determine its relevance and value for pub lication. Collectively, it is the same group who contribute papers to be published and those who oversee the process that ensure only quality papers are published. In order to assess the level of involvement of IS researchers in the review process; I asked the respondents how many papers they had reviewed during the past 3-year time period. On an average, a researcher reviewed 14.5 papers. The number varied according to professional rank and institutional mission. As shown in Table 10, the number of reviewed papers increases with the rank and the level of highest degree offered.

Every researcher seems to acknowledge and often complains that the journal review is not fast enough. I asked the respondents what they consider acceptable review time. On average, the respondents consider 2 months as a reasonable amount of time for the first round of review and 1.6 months for the second and subsequent review. Table 11 breaks down this by professional rank and institutional mission.

The lack of conclusive evidence should be considered as an indication of a serious discord between perception and the reality in journal review performance.

## 2.6. Journal review issues

Participating IS researchers considered receiving timely feedback on their papers the most important issue. This item received a score of 4.7 on a five-point Likert scale with a score of 5 representing strongest agreement. The low S.D. (0.56) also indicates strong agreement among the respondents. The next issue of importance was provision of detailed and constructive comments by reviewers. The respondents felt that receiving detailed comments from the editor when a paper was rejected was also very important. They also considered blindness of the review process important. This result was particularly surprising since in the preliminary study this was not considered very important, especially among editors (see Table 12).

## 2.7. Differences among different groups

## 2.7.1. By academic rank

Table 13 summarizes and compares the responses to the questions concerning journal review issues according to academic ranks. A casual examination of the data suggests that junior researchers generally regard journal review issues with greater concern than their senior counterparts. With some issues, statistically significant difference $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ exists among researchers of different ranks. These issues are highlighted in the table. The widest gap exists with the issues relating to review comments. While all ranks believed that receiving sincere and productive comments from reviewers and editors was important, the assistant faculty group exhibited the strongest yearning for such feedback. They were particularly anxious to get feedback from editors than other groups when the paper was rejected. These findings can be expected: burgeoning junior researchers normally have the highest need for such feedback. Other areas of incongruence include blind review requirements and perceived fairness of the review process. Interestingly, while junior researchers believed that concealing author’s identify was more important than their senior counterparts, they did not regard concealing reviewer identity from authors as important as did the senior faculty group.

## 2.7.2. By institutional mission

The respondents have shown considerable agreement on many journal review issues. However, researchers at institutions that offer only bachelor’s or master’s degrees considered the number of reviewers assigned to a paper more important than those at institutions that offer doctoral degrees (see Table 14). Those at bachelor-only institutions were more convinced that a larger number of reviewers would lead to a better review process. Researchers also differed in their opinions about the blind review requirement. Researchers at bachelor- and masterinstitutions regarded concealing the identity of an author more important than those at doctoral institutions. Researchers at less research-intensive institutions may be more concerned that their reputation or the lack of it may negatively affect their chances for favorable reviews. While it was not statistically substantiated by a small margin $( p = 0 . 0 5 4 )$ , researchers at bachelor- and master’s-institutions showed a stronger desire for feedback when their paper was rejected. Overall, researchers at less research-oriented schools appear to have a higher level of concern with the journal review process.

## 2.7.3. By editorial experience

Contrary to the presumption that researchers with editorial experience would have different attitude from researchers with no such experience, the study found that both researcher groups seemed to agree on most issues (see Table 15); they differ in two general areas: (1) review comments; and (2) number of reviewers. In both areas, researchers with no editorial experience displayed a stronger desire for more comments and reviewers. One big surprise—although not statistically substantive—was that researchers with editorial experience had a less optimistic view of the journal review process than those without, particularly researchers on a editorial boards at the time of the study.

It could also validate (or invalidate) variables used in the study.

## 3. Conclusions

The study provides insights into research and publication practices of IS researchers. The information should help researchers in developing effective research strategies and administrators in establishing research reward and guidance policies. Although there was across-the-board consensus among all researchers on what journal review issues are important, their opinions diverged depending on their personal and institutional dispositions.

However, some of the results may not be generalizable to IS researchers of different backgrounds and affiliations. Although the sample was obtained from one of the most pertinent sources that represents the IS researcher population, the composition of the sample in terms of individual and institutional attributes may not accurately reflect the real IS research community. I also acknowledges that the study did not capture recent developments that may have affected some of the findings, the most notable being the use of the Internet.

Chang E. Koh is a faculty member at the Business Computer Information Systems Department at the University of North Texas. His current major research and consulting focus is on electronic commerce, e-government, and end user computing issues. His most recent research explores organization’s readiness and critical success factors for e-business in both the private and the public sectors. His previous articles

appeared in MIS Quarterly, Information and Management, Journal of Computer Information Systems, International Journal of Information and Management Science, and Industrial Management and Data Systems. He holds a PhD from the University of Georgia in Management Information Systems.
