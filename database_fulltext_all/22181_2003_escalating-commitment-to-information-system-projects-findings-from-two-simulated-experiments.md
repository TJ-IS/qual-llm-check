# Escalating commitment to information system projects: findings from two simulated experiments

Rajiv Sabherwal<sup>a,\*</sup>, Maung K. Sein<sup>b</sup>, George M. Marakas<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>CCB 206, College of Business Administration, University of Missouri-St. Louis,

8001 Natural Bridge Road, St. Louis, MO 63121, USA

Received 1 November 2000; received in revised form 1 March 2002; accepted 1 October 2002

## Abstract

Commitment to an information system (IS) development project is essential for system success. However, some projects exhibit escalation of commitment, i.e. allocation of additional resources to a failing course of action. This paper describes two simulated longitudinal experiments examining the effects of project, psychological, social, and structural factors during four stages of an IS project. The results support escalation in IS projects. Moreover, project factors and psychological factors, but not structural factors, seem to aid escalation. Project, psychological, and social factors also have different effects during variou stages.

## 1. Introduction

‘‘We have made some contractual commitments, some of which have been fulfilled and we have got some significant sunk costs in the software package . . . so it’s clear we are going to proceed’’ (interviewee comment in [39]).

The above remark illustrates the phenomenon that has been variously described as ‘‘escalation of commitment’’ [20,37,42], ‘‘runaway information technology (IT) projects’’ [1], and ‘‘entrapment’’ [8,18]. This phenomena has been studied by social psychologists and organizational behaviorists for a long time [51,52,56] and has recently been examined in the context of information systems (IS) projects, because managers, over time, make decisions on allocation of resources to a particular course of actions (such as a development project) [32]. Escalation occurs when managers who made the initial decision continue, or further increase, their commitment when faced with substantial setbacks [12]. Researchers in this area have examined the factors that engender escalation, offered and compared several theoretical explanations for it [7,59], and provided suggestions for reducing it [43]. The factors influencing commitment have been classified into four broad determinant types: project, psychological, social, and structural [54]. These four were also observed in two case studies of escalation in IS projects. Here, we contribute to the understanding of escalation in IS projects by systematically assessing their effects.

A temporal sequencing of the effect of these four determinants has been suggested. This has been supported in non-IS cases (Expo 86 and the Shoreham Nuclear Power Plant), but not in cases on escalation in IS projects. We empirically investigate whether any such ordering should be expected.

A third objective of the paper specifically concerns two determinants—personal responsibility for failure (a psychological factor associated with one’s own perception of responsibility) and responsibility for failure (a social factor related to the organizational consequences of one’s responsibility). Prior examinations of these determinants have produced conflicting results.

In order to address the research questions, we conducted two experiments using student subjects that simulated an IS project in trouble. This allowed us to examine the effects of specific variables using a large sample of subjects and to examine escalation under a variety of controlled circumstances that would not have been possible in field studies. While our reliance on student subjects limits the generalizability of the results, we still gained valuable insight into the phenomenon of escalation, especially when our results are considered in conjunction with prior studies.

## 2. Theoretical background

Three major arguments are implicit in the literature. First, escalation of commitment does occur in some IS projects. Second, it depends on a number of factors, which may be classified into project, psychological, social, and structural determinants. Third, the dynamics of commitment will change: different factors influence commitment during the various stages of a project.

## 2.1. Runaway is projects and escalation of commitment

IS development projects continue to encounter obstacles for technological, organizational, and economic reasons [24,36,49]. These may cause the project to be abandoned before completion [15] and this may well be the correct decision. But in several cases, IS projects are continued too long, despite clear and repeated indications of poor progress. A key factor that determines whether the IS development project is abandoned or continued is the level of commitment to the project [55].

Commitment has been defined in two ways. Some consider it as a psychological state that holds people and organizations to a line of behavior, e.g. [25,33]. They consider it to be distinct from, but affecting, the persistence of behavior [48]. Other authors focus on the behavioral aspect, viewing persistence itself, rather than the psychological state that underlies such behavior, to be commitment. For example, commitment to an IS development project has been described as ‘‘doing what is necessary throughout the stages of system development, installation, and use to assure that the problem is understood and that the system development solves that problem’’ [22].

The level of commitment of several stakeholders is believed to influence the eventual success of the IS project. Top-management commitment influences the implementation of IS plans [19], the development of strategic information systems [41], and the organization’s effectiveness in utilizing IT [58]. However, the commitment of line managers and users is also crucial to IS development [6], especially for systems that cross departmental boundaries [29].

Escalation of commitment involves situations in which decision makers choosing between discontinuing an unproductive line of behavior and investing additional resources to make that course of action work adhere to, or ‘‘persist with,’’ the failing course of action. According to Drummond [13], escalation is concerned with why organizations ‘‘seem to persist with failing ventures long after any sensible person would surely have given up’’. Keil considers project escalation ‘‘to occur when there is continued commitment and negative information’’. Although, escalation involves resource commitment in the face of negative interim outcomes, the eventual outcomes may or may not be negative. Also, escalation involves the allocation of additional resources, but not necessarily at an increasing rate. In some cases, the escalation of commitment might be reflected in the continuation of the planned funding for a project that is no longer relevant due to the changed business situation. For example, the Concorde project was continued despite its irrelevance due to the launch of Boeing’s widebodied mass aircraft.

Some IS projects, called runaway projects, continue despite clear indications of their likely failure [44]. These exemplify the phenomenon of escalation of commitment, wherein decision makers commit additional resources to a failing course of action. Keil and Newman and Sabherwal describe two cases of IS development that encountered escalation of commitment. Drummond examined the decision by the London stock exchange to develop the multi-million pound system called Taurus, and the decision, 3 years later, by the stock exchange commission to cancel it while still under development. Escalation was observed here when the decision makers persisted with the project in the face of negative feedback despite at least two opportunities to withdraw commitment. In addition, several other examples of runaway IS projects have been described in the literature, e.g. [34]. We therefore proposed the following proposition and a corresponding testable hypothesis:

Proposition 1. Escalation of commitment occurs in IS development projects.

Hypothesis 1. When an IS project encounters problems, decision makers recommend commitment of additional resources.

## 2.2. Factors affecting escalation of commitment

Whether a manager is aiming to increase or wishes to decrease commitment, it is important that he or she understand the factors determining the level [40]. Several theories have been used to explain escalation of commitment, including self-justification theory, [53], prospect theory [30,31,60], expectancy theory [57], decision dilemma theory [5], self-presentation theory [23], modeling theory [2,9], and agency theory [14,26]. Appendix A briefly summarizes these theories and their links to escalation. A number of factors explaining commitment have been drawn from these theories. Moreover, a typology of these factors has been proposed and used by several authors. It includes four broad types of influencing factors: (a) project: attributes of the project; (b) psychological: characterize the individual participants in the process; (c) social: pertain to the various groups involved; and, (d) structural: concern the organization and its relationship with the project.

As shown in Table 1, several influencing factors have been discussed and empirically examined. However, except for case studies, there has been no reported systematic investigation of these categories and their change with time. The following proposition, and the subsequent hypotheses, were drawn:

Proposition 2. Four types of factors (project, psychological, structural, and social) affect the level of commitment of a manager to IS projects.

In order to keep the study manageable, it was necessary to focus on a subset of the factors. We selected two from each of the four categories. In making these selections, we focused on factors proposed by Staw and Ross. Moreover, we selected those that have received some empirical support in the literature.

Project factors reflect its costs and benefits. The way in which it is economically structured has been argued to affect, at least partly, decision makers persistence with the project. The project is likely to be continued when it is perceived as a long-term investment, is expected to provide a large payoff but at a low cost, and has a long-term payoff structure. Moreover, continued commitment is likely when the costs of closing or stopping the project are high, the salvage value is low, and no feasible alternatives are available. Commitment may also depend on ambiguity associated with the project [21] and the project’s expected duration [10].

Both the project factors—size and cost of payoff— characterize the economics of the project. One that seems more attractive would be more likely to be continued, in spite of problems. A large payoff, quantitative or qualitative, is believed to increase commitment because the decision maker may perceive abandonment as a loss of opportunity [45]. On the other hand, low cost of payoff makes it easier to justify continuing the project. This leads to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2. A decision maker is more committed to an IS project when project payoff is large and the cost of payoff is small rather than when the project payoff is small and the cost of payoff is large.

Psychological factors may cause managers to feel optimistic about the situation. They reflect their tendency to continue an apparently failing project due to their unwillingness to admit that previous decisions were wrong. Therefore, if the individual who originally approved or championed an IS project is involved with it when it runs into difficulty, then that individual is likely to continue to support the system. Psychological determinants also include information-processing errors, such as overestimation of the chances of success [3]. Framing the current project status, positively or negatively, may also cause varying levels of commitment.

The two variables studied were (1) personal responsibility and (2) framing of the project status. A decision maker who has a high personal responsibility is considered to be more committed to continuing the project. This is self-justification. Previous investigations have produced mixed results on the effect on escalation in IS development projects. The influence of personal responsibility has found considerable support in IS and non-IS cases, as well as in non-IS experimental research [27].

According to prospect theory, individuals are risk seeking when choosing between two losing options but risk averse when choosing between two winning options [35]. Consequently, the decision to invest additional resources depends on how the problem is framed. When a project gets into trouble, the situation is inherently one with negative interim consequences, thereby leading to risk-seeking behavior [4]. Under such circumstances, positively framing the current status of the project (60% of the users are satisfied) would enhance commitment: things are not as bad as they actually are. A negative framing (40% of the users are dissatisfied) makes it appear worse and may lead to withdrawal of commitment. The effect is consistent with prior research [38]. For example, Bateman and Zeithaml found that subjects in two different experiments make more reinvestment in positively-framed situations than in equivalent but negatively-framed situations. We therefore proposed the following:

Hypothesis 3. A decision maker is more committed to an IS project when they have supported it in the past and the decision is framed positively than when they have opposed it in the past and the decision is framed negatively.

In contrast, social factors originate from the group involving the individual, and the members hold that individual to a course of action, regardless of his or her personal beliefs. Some social determinants arise from the individual’s actions being observed by an audience. The individual’s public identification with the project and responsibility for failure are two such factors. Social determinants may also arise from the decision-making group’s competitive rivalry with another social group or from their considering another group as a role model. For example, if an organization has developed and used a system successfully, its success may make other organizations more optimistic about problems they face in developing similar systems.

We examined two social factors: modeling and need for external justification (specifically, job security/ insecurity). Modeling arises from the experience of a peer group, such as a competitor with a similar project; it also plays a role in facilitating this behavior, as individuals are more likely to invest in a course of action when others who committed themselves to a similar one express satisfaction rather than regret. A competitor’s prior success may therefore lead to higher commitment.

Escalation of commitment is also considered more likely when the decision maker’s fate in the organization seems to depend on project success. The organizational consequences faced by the decision maker can be in the form of a punishment (demotion, being fired, etc.) or neutral (nothing happens). Staw and Ross argue that negative consequences represent greater need for external justification and lead to increased commitment. Moreover, Fox and Staw found that one specific factor contributing to a need for external justification, job insecurity, lead to escalation of commitment. However, there is also some counter-evidence in the literature. For example, Newman and Sabherwal suggested that the organization they studied had ‘‘a climate of waste and slackness.’’ Similarly, Keil indicated that the organization he studied had loose management controls and ‘‘formal reviews were seldom performed.’’ He also attributed persistence with the project to ‘‘strong champions’’ who apparently had high job security. The continuation of another IS project [46] also seemed to benefit from supporters with high levels of job security.

We tested both possibilities, i.e. escalation being facilitated by job insecurity or job security. By switching the combination of the two social factors, we could separately test each factor’s effect. Therefore, we proposed two different hypotheses. Hypothesis 4a relates only to modeling. Hypothesis 4b included job security as it was combined with modeling in Experiment 1, but we also tested the reverse possibility as well.

Hypothesis 4a. A decision maker is more committed to an IS project when competitors have had a satisfactory experience rather than an unsatisfactory experience with similar systems.

Hypothesis 4b. A decision maker is more committed to an IS project under high job security condition rather than under low job security condition.

Finally, structural factors represent the context of the project. They may be attributes of the organization or its environment. They include political support and administrative inertia [47]. They also include the motivation to persist due to economic and technical side bets, such as hiring of new employees.

We examined are top-management support and side bets associated with the project. In the IS literature, top-management support is widely believed to lead others in the organization to support the project [28]. On the other hand, if top-management does not support a project, commitment of other stakeholders may also be diminished. The second structural factor, economic and technical side bets, represents costs that are incurred in conjunction with the project, but are not part of the project itself. Although, the effect of side bets was apparently not observed in the two cases that examined escalation in IS projects, this may reflect the specifics of these cases rather than representing a general conclusion. Potentially important side bets in IS projects include training employees on system design methodologies that will be useful in later projects as well. Decision makers may lump these expenses with the project and may tend to increase commitment when side bets are high. Based on this, we proposed:

Hypothesis 5. A decision maker is more committed to an IS project when the CEO strongly supports it and it involves ‘‘side bets’’ (e.g. hiring, training) than when the CEO only moderately supports the project and the project does not involve ‘‘side bets.’’

## 2.3. The dynamics of commitment and its determinants

The level of commitment may change during the course of a project. Decision makers who are committed to it may continue to invest additional resources, and thereby further increase their commitment, even when faced with indications that the project may be failing; indeed, an individual’s commitment may change over time. It is therefore impor tant to examine the determinants at different stages.

Staw and Ross argued that, early in the project, managers focus on costs and payoffs of the project, and their level of commitment thus depends on project factors. As the project runs into trouble, decision makers who originally approved the project become biased toward continuation. As the project slides further behind, individuals who have supported it find that they must continue to do so also due to social factors related to their group and the organizational context. Finally, when the situation looks really bleak, structural factors assume great importance, possibly due to the project becoming intricately linked to the organization’s success. By this time, psychological and social factors become less important as earlier investment decisions fade into memory. However, along with the increased importance of structural factors, project factors may again become important in later stages.

While the dynamics of determinants of commitment were evident in the two cases studied by Ross and Staw, this temporal ordering was not observed in the two IS project case studies. Instead, Newman and Sabherwal concluded that project determinants are key to obtaining the initial commitment to the project; social, and structural determinants affect the withdrawal of commitment, while psychological and project determinants influence escalation of commitment. We proposed:

Proposition 2. The stage of the IS development project moderates the relationship between the four types of determinants and the commitment to the ISD project.

Hypotheses 6–9. In different stages of an IS project, a decision maker’s level of commitment to the project depends on project determinants (H6) psychological determinants (H7), social determinants (modeling— H8a; job security—H8b), and structural determinants (H9) to different extents.

## 3. Data collection

We empirically assessed the effects of the determinants at different stages of the IS project using a simulation. Test subjects interacted with specially written software that provided them scenarios for four different stages and asked them a series of questions about each. Haunschild et al. used a similar method to study escalation of commitment to corporate acquisition decisions. Our subjects were student volunteers who received extra credit for participating in the experiment. For the simulation, we divided the IS project into four stages: (1) project approval, (2) design, (3) programming, and (4) installation (including testing). Subjects were students in graduate or undergraduate MIS courses. At the start of the simulation, they were given a welcome, and an introductory screen indicating that they would play the role of a decision maker as a member of the IS planning committee of a company. Following this, the scenarios were presented. In each scenario, two factors within each of the four categories were manipulated as ‘‘high’’ or ‘‘low’’.

We altered the two variables within a category similarly, i.e. both as ‘‘high’’ or both as ‘‘low.’’ Appendix B provides an illustrative scenario for stage 1 where each determinant was manipulated as ‘‘high’’. Appendix C illustrates the ‘‘low’’ counterpart. As there are four types of determinants, and each is manipulated as ‘‘high’’ or ‘‘low,’’ 16 combinations are possible. Each subject started from one of these cells. As the subject progressed through the simulation, the project, social, and structural determinants remained the same, although the text used in the manipulations was changed to reflect the activities involved in each stage. Unlike project, social, and structural determinants, psychological factors could change across the project stages because ‘‘personal responsibility’’ changed, depending on the subject’s recommendations during previous stage. For the first stage, we manipulated responsibility by telling the subjects that they had supported or not supported the decision to approve the feasibility study for the project. We then matched the second psychological determinant (framing) with personal responsibility. We changed the scenarios at each stage to reflect the stage. After each scenario, the subjects answered the following three questions.

\- Question 1: ‘‘How much funding are you willing to commit to stage 1 of the project?’’ The response to this question was on a five-point scale with 1 as ‘‘stop funding,’’ 2 as ‘‘reduce funding slightly,’’ 3 as ‘‘maintain original level of funding,’’ 4 as ‘‘increase funding slightly, but less than requested,’’ and 5 as ‘‘increase funding as requested.’

\- Question 2: ‘‘In your opinion, what is the likelihood that the project will succeed?’’ The response to this question was on a five-point scale with 1 as ‘‘highly unlikely,’’ 2 as ‘‘unlikely,’’ 3 as ‘‘don’t know,’’ 4 as ‘‘likely,’’ and 5 as ‘‘highly likely.’’

\- Question 3: ‘‘Justify your answers in words.’’ This was an open-ended question.

Questions 1, 2, and 3 appeared on successive screens. We used the subjects’ response to questions 1 and 2 as measures of their commitment. Finally, after going through the four stages, the subject was asked eight questions on manipulation checks and some questions on demographics.

Experiments 1 and 2 were similar, except in two aspects: (1) the way in which the two social factors (modeling and job security) were combined. To examine the mutually opposite possibilities of either job security or job insecurity facilitating escalation, we combined job security with modeling in Experiment 1, and job insecurity with modeling in Experiment 2 (i.e. the two sentences on job security in Appendix B and the two sentences on job insecurity in Appendix C were interchanged); (2) the manipulation checks.

Table 5 summarizes the results of regression analysis for the entire sample, with three dummy variables used to represent the four stages<sup>1</sup>, and with interaction terms for these dummy variables and the various influencing factors included to examine changes in the factors’ effect across project stages. Since each of the 208 subjects provided data for four stages, there were a total of 832 data points in this analysis. But the data points for each subject would be statistically interrelated due to within-subject commonalities and a correction had to be applied. Cohen and Cohen [11] provided a thorough analysis of this issue, and described a way of correcting the F-ratio and the t-statistic to address this problem. We applied the suggested correction to the F-ratio and various tstatistics.

The project factors produced mixed results. As the F-ratios and the cell means show, the project factors had the expected effect on commitment in stages 1 and 3 (significant at $P \leq 0 . 0 5$ level in Experiment 2 and the aggregate sample, and at $P \leq 0 . 1 0$ level in

Experiment 1). But the effects were weaker in stages 2 and 4. The regression results did show support of the overall effect of the project factors, and there is thus moderate support for Hypothesis 2. However, the regression did not find any significant interaction between stage dummies and project factors. There is thus mixed support for Hypothesis 6, with ANOVA results showing significant effects in stages 1 and 3 but not in stages 2 and 4, and the regression not producing any significant interaction effects. Maybe the relationship could not be detected because it was curvilinear.

The two psychological factors significantly influenced commitment in each stage (at $P \leq 0 . 0 1$ or 0.001). Moreover, the cell means indicate that not only was the direction of this effect as expected in Hypothesis 3, with greater commitment when the psychological factors were high, but the difference between the commitment scores for high and low levels of psychological factors also increased as the project moved toward later stages (consistent with Hypothesis 7). These results were consistent across the two experiments and the aggregate sample. The regression results provide further support for these findings; the psychological factors significantly influenced commitment. The interactions of the three dummy variables with the psychological factors had a significant negative effect. Together, they indicate that the effect of the psychological factors increases with stage, consistent with the cell means in Table 4. Thus, results support Hypotheses 3 and 7.

The social factors provided interesting results. In Experiment 1, where modeling (specifically, competitor’s success with a similar system) was combined with job security, they affected commitment in stage 1 $( P \le 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , but not in the other three stages $( P \ge 0 . 1 0 )$ . In Experiment 2, where modeling was combined with job insecurity, social factors affected commitment in each stage $( P \le 0 . 0 5$ in stages 1, 2, and 4, and $P \leq 0 . 1 0$ in stage 3). Also, in all four stages in Experiment 1, commitment was higher when the social factors were high (i.e. under high modeling and high job security). In contrast, in Experiment 2, commitment was higher for low levels of social factors (i.e. for low modeling and high job security). Together, these results (summarized in Table 6) suggest that job security, rather than job insecurity, facilitates commitment (consistent with Hypothesis 4b). This finding was supported by the regression. There was some support for Hypothesis 8b, as the regression analysis showed job security to have greater effect in stage 1.

The results are less clear for modeling alone, or specifically, competitor’s success with a similar system. In Experiment 2, competitor’s lack of success combined with high job security consistently produced high commitment, suggesting that competitor’s success with a similar system may reduce commitment. This is also supported by the regression results. In Experiment 1, where competitor’s success with a similar system (which, based on the results for Experiment 2 and the regression, seems to reduce commitment) was combined with high job security (which apparently increases commitment), the opposite effects of the two factors may explain the lack of significant effects in stages 2–4. The significant effect in stage 1 in Experiment 1 reflects the effect of job security. Also, the effect of the interaction of competitor’s success with the dummy for stage 1 was significant and in a direction opposite to the main effect of competitor’s success. This (along with the stage 1 result for Experiment 1) may mean that competitors’ success with a similar system may have a positive effect on commitment in stage 1, but a negative effect later. However, overall, the opposite results for modeling in Experiment 1 and 2 suggest a lack of support for Hypothesis 4a and 8a.

## 5. Conclusions

Before we discuss the implications of our findings, identification of some limitations of the study is in order. First, our use of student subjects can raise questions about how closely tied we are to reality [50]. The generalizability of our results to a population of experienced managers can be questioned. However, we took several precautions to mitigate this issue. Our subjects were not naive about either IS or project issues in general, with most of them having between 3 and 5 years of full or part-time job experience in IS, and an average of 1–1/2 years of experience with real-world project teams. Manipulation checks to assess whether the subjects were indeed involved in the experiment indicated that the subjects paid attention to the experimental tasks. We also tracked the amount of time spent by the subjects on the experiment. The average was over 36 min, which might suggest that the subjects took the task seriously. Still, doubts may remain about whether the subjects’ behavior reflects those of a ‘‘real manager.’’ The study required them to play a senior executive role and presented them with cues that may not be ‘‘real’’ to them. We therefore interpret our findings with caution.

Another limitation of the study is that although the project was multi-stage, and the scenarios were presented in a sequence, running the simulation in one sitting may not have provided the true flavor of a multistage project. The amount of time taken by the subjects is consistent with the desire to balance between the objectives of maintaining subjects interest (which might decline if the experiment takes a very long time, say 2 h) and obtaining meaningful answers (which would not have happened if each subject spent say 5 min on the experiment). However, a truly longitudinal laboratory experiment can be designed to simulate different stages, for example, by asking subjects to make decisions on the stages in different sessions separated by several weeks.

Recognizing the above limitations, we offer some tentative implications for practice. First, IS literature provides some evidence based on case studies or anecdotal examples that escalation of commitment can occur during IS projects and that a ‘‘runaway’’ project may consequently take on a life of its own. Our research results are consistent with prior case studies, suggesting that escalation takes place in IS projects as well. The following comment by a subject in stage 4 is illustrative:

‘‘I have invested so much in this project. I am going to go through with it till the end to make it work. To stop the project now would be a tremendous waste.’’

Second, the results suggest that personal responsibility for failure facilitates commitment. This is in accordance with findings from prior field studies, and suggests that an individual’s close association with a project may lead to escalation. A possible implication of this is that to avoid escalation it is important not to identify individuals too strongly with projects.

Third, job insecurity seems to diminish commitment during the early as well as the later stages. This, which is consistent with case studies on IS project escalation, suggests that while top-management’s tough treatment of people involved in failed projects helps, it might drive decision makers to abandon projects at the slightest sign of trouble. However, an encouraging aspect is that such abandonment is more likely to be at an early stage.

Fourth, competitor’s success with a similar system may reduce commitment. This suggests that managers may be more committed when one of their competitors has unsuccessfully pursued a similar project or no competitor has attempted a similar project.

Finally, our results also suggest that factors have differential effects on commitment over time. For example, large payoff and low cost of payoff, have a greater effect in initial stages of the project. However, these project characteristics have lesser impact on commitment during later stages.

In conclusion, we reemphasize that the implications presented above are tentative and based on the behavior of student subjects who might have lacked experience with the scenarios presented in this study. However, we believe we have gained further insights into a key aspect of IS development: the decision concerning whether to increase, maintain, or decrease funding for IS development projects facing problems.
