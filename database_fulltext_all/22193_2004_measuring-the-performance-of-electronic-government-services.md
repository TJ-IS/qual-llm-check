# Measuring the performance of electronic government services

Joan C. Steyaert

5113 Scarsdale Road, Bethesda, MD 20816, USA

## Abstract

The World Wide Web (WWW) and the Internet have streamlined government information, products, and services. Electronic government now includes on-line filing of documents, notification of entitlements, permits, registration, and disaster relief. This paper addresses the question, can a marketing model be used by Federal and state agencies to improve the content and value of electronic services to the public? Six Federal and state e-service programs were analyzed—the National Institutes of Health (NIH), the US Mint, the Internal Revenue Service (IRS), the US Postal Service (USPS), and the e-State-government systems of California and New Jersey. Five marketing indicators were used—consumer awareness, popularity, contact efficiency, conversion, and retention. Awareness deals with the number of visitors to a site. Popularity refers to the rank of the site. Contact efficiency indicates site usability and content. Conversion refers to customer satisfaction, transactions and time on the site. Retention deals with customer loyalty. Web traffic reports and customer surveys were used as proxies to analyze and compare a sample of Federal and state e-service agencies. The results support the use of a marketing framework in organizing and evaluating these sites. The case studies demonstrate how statistical and survey data were combined for a robust assessment of the e-services.

## 1. Introduction

The Bush Administration focused public attention on the development of ‘‘citizen-centered’’ electronic services [4]. The work reported in this paper used a marketing framework to identify the factors that increase the number of electronic transactions between government and the public. We applied an e-commerce model to analyze individual and electronic self-service, something that is still relatively rare in the IT literature.

Case studies reported here were conducted at the National Institutes of Health, the US Mint, Internal Revenue Service, the US Postal Service, and on sites for two States: California and New Jersey. In addition to the case studies, web traffic on 45 federal and 39 state agencies were analyzed. The paper applies e-commerce principles to public sector services.

## 2. Methodology

## 2.1. E-commerce marketing indicators

We adapted the e-commerce model of Watson et al. [14] to six case studies. In Electronic Commerce— The Strategic Perspective, Watson et al. propose five e-commerce performance indicators: awareness, popularity, contact, conversion, and retention. The first indicator is consumer awareness. It refers to the visibility of a web site in terms of a targeted audience. An agency can market $_ { \mathrm { o r } }$ ‘‘brand’’ its site to increase awareness $( \eta _ { 0 } )$ . The second indicator is site popularity $( \eta _ { 1 } )$ measured by the number of visitors to a site. Popularity can provide an early indication of effective advertising and marketing. The third indicator is contact efficiency $( \eta _ { 2 } ) ;$ ; it refers to site content and convenience. After visitors register, they should be able to e-mail a request for information, file a claim, buy an item or make a transaction. The fourth indicator is conversion $( \eta _ { 3 } ) ;$ it refers to transactions and customer satisfaction. Sales increase when a site is convenient and has multiple offerings, payment options, competitive prices, personalization, and multi-channel delivery. The fifth indicator is retention $( \eta _ { 4 } )$ or customer loyalty. A successful site must be able to move a one-time visitor to a return customer. Retention is measured by repeat transactions or repeat sales relative to all transactions or sales.

## 2.2. Analysis of case studies and sample

We used Watson’s e-commerce framework to analyze the six agencies and two Federal and state government samples. Data sources included: comScore web traffic reports [2,3] and overall e-government and customer satisfaction scores by West [15–17] and Fornell et al. [6–8]. The analysis period for the comScore sample was January–July 2001. E-government and customer satisfaction scores were analyzed for 2001 and 2002.

The number of agencies in the Federal and state samples remained constant throughout this period.

The five efficiency indicators used in this study are presented in Table 1. The first indicator consumer awareness $( \eta _ { 0 } )$ measures the number of unique Internet visitors to a government site relative to the total number of agency consumers. A visit usually involves a page request or a series of page requests to a given domain. Visitors are only counted once per month. The second indicator $( \eta _ { 1 } )$ is site popularity based on agency rank, measured by the number of unique visitors to a site as compared to other agencies in the sample. The popularity index is inversely proportional to the rank. We used comScore reports to measure awareness and popularity. The third indicator $( \eta _ { 2 } )$ is contact efficiency. It measures site content and convenience. We used West’s e-government scores to measure Federal and state contact efficiency. West measures the availability of contact information, publications, databases, portals, and services. The fourth indicator conversion $( \eta _ { 3 } )$ refers to the process by which a shopper becomes a buyer, or moves from individual government service provision to electronic self-service. We used the American Customer Satisfaction Index (ACSI) and visitor time to measure conversion on Federal sites. The ACSI is a consumer satisfaction score for Federal and private sector organizations. We used West’s self-service scores and visitor time to measure conversion on state sites. The time count began when a page was requested. If 30 min elapsed without another request, the visit was closed. The fifth indicator is retention $( \eta _ { 4 } )$ measured by counting comScore repeat visits and repeat transactions.

## 3. Case study results

## 3.1. Federal cases and sample

The four US Government cases had more visitors and higher popularity than the normal (average) federal sample due to the market size of the cases selected. For example, the National Institutes of Health, Internal Revenue Service, and US Postal Service serve a worldwide market. The four case studies averaged 3 million visitors/month (or six times the average), 15 page views per visitor (twice the average) and 13 min per visit. Table 2 presents the data in the case study analysis. The Federal sample averaged: 500,000 unique visitors, 1.2 return visits/month, six page views per visit, and 8 min per visit. The US Treasury (http:// www.ustreas.gov) was first in popularity and fourth in contact efficiency (at 84%). The Bureau of Land Management (http://www.blm.gov) was 45th in popularity and contact efficiency.

There were 2.4 million visitors from the US and 1.9 million from overseas. The majority of visitors were female (67%), 40 years or older (44%), and college educated (66%). Both http://www.medline.gov and http://www.cancer.gov have powerful search engines and personalized information by topic, clinical trial and research program.

## 3.1.2. Internal Revenue Service (IRS), Department of Treasury

IRS receives 24 million tax filings each year, but only a fraction of IRS filers submit their forms electronically. Given the popularity of the site, IRS focused on contact efficiency. Demand for IRS services is seasonal: from January through April, IRS is the most popular Federal site, hosting 5 million visitors per month. By May, visitors have declined to 1.4 million and the site is ranked 10th. Even with variability in demand, IRS has maintained high performance levels. In May 2001, http://www.keynote.com, the Internet performance authority, ranked IRS as one of the top 10 performing agencies for the ninth consecutive week. In 2002, West ranked http://www.irs.- gov 76 in contact efficiency. The site offers ‘‘one-stop filing, immediate acknowledgement (within 48 h of efiling), faster and safer refunds, direct bank deposits, and flexible payment options’’. In 2001, IRS converted consumers to self-service. Visitors increased 57%, page downloads increased 100%, home filings went from US\$ 35 million to US\$ 39.5 million, and direct deposit tax refunds increased 14.5% [12]. In 2002, the ACSI for electronic filing was 78 versus 53 for paper filing.

## 3.1.3. United states postal service (USPS)

The US Postal Service’s http://www.usps.com is a successful provider of electronic information but has faced challenges in selling its products on-line. Each month, four million unique visitors access http:// www.usps.com, primarily for zip code information (75%). Reports by the General Accounting Office [9,10] and survey data [3] indicate low contact efficiency and conversion. Few consumers visited the USPS e-commerce sites, such as http://www.stamps.- com. Eighty-five percent of the visitors that accessed http://www.stamps.com did not buy stamps. From 2001–2002, USPS met only 1% of its US\$ 104 million sales target. Customer satisfaction was 68 compared to Federal Express (82) and United Parcel Service (78). An e-commerce framework that focused on awareness and contact efficiency would increase USPS e-commerce sales.

## 3.1.4. US Mint, Department of Treasury

http://www.usmint.gov is a popular site for commemorative and numismatic coin collectors. Many e-commerce offerings, e.g. ‘‘50 state quarter’’ and ‘‘dollar coin’’, are tailored to specific customer segments, e.g. women and teenagers. US Mint focuses on awareness and contact efficiency. The site offers end-toend ordering, electronic forms, secure transactions and credit card sales. Conversion has increased: electronic purchases grew from 7 to 36% and total sales grew from US\$ 1,000,000/month to US\$ 1,000,000/day [5]. The Mint ACSI was 86 in 2000 and 88 in 2001.

Web traffic on the case studies and sample diverged, mainly because of the state’s population. California averaged 3 million visitors/month, 14 pages per visitor, 2.29 return visits, and 17 min per visit. The web traffic for the two states was seven times greater than the sample in terms of visitors/month, 40% greater in page views, 60% in return visits, and 35% in minutes per visit. ## 3.2.1. State of California

In June 2001, California had approximately 5 million visitors on its two state sites, http://www.california.gov and http://www.california.us. Over the study period, the State ranked third (54.8) in contact efficiency and fourth in self-service transactions. The state’s portal allowed residents to personalize web pages, browse the site using handheld Personal Digital Assistants (PDAs), and select on-line state services, category links and state news. A resident could select a service by community (resident, business, media, state employee, student, tourist) or delivery channel (e-mail, wireless or mail). Traffic information, payment, insurance ratings, filings, and records were all frequently updated.

Thirty-eight percent of California sites offered selfservice transactions. Employers electronically filed taxes, accessed records and reconciled forms from their desktop. California IT managers converted consumers: individual services declined 50%, and on-line transactions increased from 30 to 80%. The Center for Digital Government ranked California sixth in digital services and ninth in digital democracy. Ninety-five percent of e-service customers were satisfied according to state on-line services and many returned to the site (3–7 return visits/month).

## 3.2.2. State of New Jersey

In 2002, the New Jersey ‘‘one-stop’’ portal ranked second in contact efficiency (55) and 19th in selfservice transactions. Twenty-five percent of New Jersey sites offered on-line tax filing, publications, vehicle registrations, licenses, forms, vital records, and shopping. Consumers could register and access data with four clicks regardless of their certification level. They could also customize their web pages, access video content, jump to other agency sites, and process credit card transactions. The Center for Digital Government ranked New Jersey first in managing intergovernmental information, electronic law enforcement and the courts [1].

## 3.2.3. Comparison of California and Texas e-services

California and Texas web traffic reports were normalized based on population. Results for California indicated that one out of five residents visited the California sites each month versus one in seven for Texas. Californians also returned more frequently and stayed longer. Results of this study showed a strong correlation between state population and the performance of Federal and state e-service programs [16,17].

## 4. Findings and conclusions

This effort supported the use of a marketing framework to analyze web sites. The framework was flexible and could incorporate multiple quantitative and qualitative data sources to help understand consumer behavior at government sites. The case studies illustrated the importance of contact efficiency in converting consumers to self-service transactions. The US Mint designed its e-commerce site to increase the Agency’s reach to include women and teenagers. IRS reduced processing time for electronic tax filers. NIH incorporated powerful search engines to increase convenience in accessing research and clinical trials underway in multiple organizations. New Jersey and California were working to standardize data formats across agencies to increase efficiency, convenience, and security.

We believe, based on our results that Government leaders should use a checklist in planning, designing, and maintaining their e-service sites to take into consideration the customer’s perspective. Questions that should be considered by IT managers in web design include:

 Consumer awareness—estimating the actual and potential e-service market, identifying services that offer savings and convenience as well as new markets, and developing a plan for multi-channel customer service and delivery (performance metric for this is the monthly unique visitors to the site).

 Popularity—comparing the rank of the site to competitors based on unique visitors, content and offerings (performance metric is monthly rank of the site compared to that of the competitors).

 Contact efficiency—defining the enterprise architecture as well as the systems that need to be integrated in the supply chain and transaction chain, testing usability and monitoring downloads, navigation, and security (performance metrics are four clicks to complete a transaction and 30–40% of services on-line e.g. e-mail, purchasing, filing, or accessing information).

 Conversion—understanding the factors that move a visitor from seeking information to transacting business, posting customer service targets and conducting customer surveys to improve ratings (performance metrics, the number of electronic transactions or sales).

 Retention–continuously improving content, services, and features to increase customer loyalty (performance metrics, return visits/month, repeat transactions).
