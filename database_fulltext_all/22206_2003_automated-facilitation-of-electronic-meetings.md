# Automated facilitation of electronic meetings

Zachary Wong<sup>a</sup>, Milam Aiken<sup>b,\*</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Business and Economics, Sonoma State University, 1801 East Cotati Ave., Rohnert Park, CA 94928, USA <sup>b</sup>School of Business, The University of Mississippi, Oxford, MS 38677, USA

Received 22 May 2001; received in revised form 7 January 2003; accepted 17 February 2003

## Abstract

Much group support system (GSS) research has focused on the impact of group size, leadership, tasks, and other variables on the effectiveness and efficiency of meetings, but few studies have investigated the effect of different facilitation modes. An automated facilitator could assist or possibly supplant a human facilitator in complex or geographically distributed meetings. Our study of electronic meetings showed that automated facilitation was as good as expert-human and better than novice-human facilitation for simple idea generation and ranking tasks. <sup>#</sup> 2003 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Electronic meetings; Group support systems; Group decision support systems; Group facilitation

## 1. Introduction

A review of the literature of group support systems (GSS) shows that the facilitator plays a major role in an electronic meeting’s success [6]. Further, expert facilitators are scarce, because they not only have to be proficient with all facets of the technology, but they also must have an appreciation of group dynamics [17]. Automating this process may alleviate many of the administrative burdens placed upon a human facilitator, thus reducing the cost and possibly improving the efficiency and/or effectiveness of the meeting. Automation may be even more important for electronic meetings in which group members are dispersed geographically, where coordination and control through traditional means of facilitation are very difficult, if not impossible.

However, automation may involve some risks. For example, the loss of the ‘‘human touch’’ could decrease satisfaction among group members. In addition, it is not clear how novice- and expert-human facilitators compare with automated facilitators. Very few studies have investigated the relative effectiveness of facilitation support on group meetings, and apparently, only one study included completely automated facilitation. The purpose of our study was to provide some insights into the effectiveness of the different types of facilitation support in GSS meetings. In addition, it was hoped that it would reveal some evidence as to the feasibility of augmenting or supplanting human facilitators with a completely automated approach.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Role of the facilitator in a GSS environment

Secondary to group characteristics, the quality of facilitation is vital to meeting success [5]. The facilitator must assume many roles, including that of an agenda preparer, technology integrator, and meeting recorder and timekeeper [16]. Depending upon the experience of the facilitator as well as the group size, nature of the meeting objectives, complexity of the GSS software, and other variables, the burden may be overwhelming. Although some meetings have included a chauffeur (a staff member at a separate computer who has access to the GSS administration programs and ‘‘drives’’ the software) to manage the technical aspects and a facilitator to lead the group, staff personnel may be scarce and/or expensive. Automating as many routine, frequently occurring tasks as possible can reduce this burden, leaving more time for the facilitator to focus on more important duties that require greater skill. However, it is not clear what tasks can be automated and what effect human facilitators have on groups. For example, do groups led by expert facilitators perform better than those led by novice facilitators? Several studies summarized in Table 1 have investigated the role of the facilitator.

## 2.2. Human facilitation in GSS

In one study of facilitation [13], researchers investigated the differences between a scripted facilitator and a user-driven approach in groups of five members. Facilitated groups followed a structured process to arrive at a decision, had the facilitator activate the software, and had the facilitator modify or delete items in the group’s common knowledge pool, but the facilitators were not allowed to provide any expert advice or process counseling. User-driven groups were allowed to devise their own processes and activate the software tools themselves, whenever desired. The inflexibility of the process imposed by the facilitator was assumed to cause lower levels of consensus. Flexibility in the user-driven groups allowed more rapid feedback on alternatives through a vote, once the group members felt that feedback was needed. However, facilitated groups did achieve higher quality decisions (possibly due to use of a formal structure via an imposed meeting agenda to encourage groups to consider the issues more carefully than they otherwise would). Provision of flexibility tends to cause groups to make premature decisions, because group members are less inclined to consider all aspects of the problem. In groups with leaders, it was found that the leaders had a significant role in leading the group to its final decision. Group members also tended to look to the leaders for direction, and the alternative proposed by the leader was never out-voted.

A later study of groups of three to five people explored three facilitation modes in GSS meetings: facilitated, chauffeured (staff-driven), and user-driven [10]. In all modes, a human was used to offer guidance. In the facilitated mode when following a predefined script, a facilitator directed the group on the software tools to use at any specific time and how to use them. In the chauffeured mode, however, the facilitator merely implemented the features/software tools of the GSS upon direction from the group. In the user-driven mode, complete control of the GSS was available at each user workstation. Group consensus improved in all three modes, but there was a difference among treatments. The chauffeured groups were found to achieve the highest level of consensus, followed by facilitated groups and user-driven groups, respectively. Facilitated groups had significantly less consensus than groups with only technical, nonscripted, chauffeured support, because many groups resisted the structure imposed by the facilitator.

In a more recent study [4], facilitated groups experienced improved group processes and greater cohesion, but groups without a facilitator did not. When groups act on their own, and they are the only source of structure, participants engage in less effective interaction behavior than those supported by some form of external process structure. Facilitators significantly influence cohesion and process outcomes by including the most appropriate structures and varying the restrictiveness of intervention.

In another study [19], three treatments (facilitation, GSS configuration, and training) were used to test groups’ decision making by limiting interactions with the activities, sequences, and philosophies specified by a heuristic or structured group decision technique. Groups often ignore the techniques and technology available to them in favor of familiar decision processes, and results indicated that each mediator increased their use of a heuristic that in turn, improved decision quality. Facilitator ability, facilitation strategy, choice of process structure, experience with GSS use, group member training, degree of group adherence to prescribed process structure, group characteristics, and tasks also affected meeting success.

## 2.3. Automated facilitation in GSS

In one study of the tradeoffs associated with human and automated facilitation in GSS meetings, groups were exposed to one of the three conditions: no facilitation, human facilitation, or automated facilitation [15]. In the first treatment, group members decided when and how to use the different tools, but a human facilitator was available to perform editing and other tasks on request. In the second group, a person who was responsible for the technical aspects of the process aided the facilitator. In the third treatment, groups used a GSS in which the decision model was enhanced with several built-in (automated) facilitation mechanisms. Results showed that human-facilitation groups and automated-facilitation groups experienced significantly higher post-meeting consensus and perceived decision quality than non-facilitated groups. No statistically significant difference was detected between human- and automated-facilitation groups for the two outcome measures. Without facilitation, groups had difficulty performing well using advanced, complex GSS features, and a human chauffeur alone may not be enough.

In perhaps the only instance of completely automated facilitation [2], groups in two case studies used a GSS without a human facilitator. The automated facilitator started and stopped a brain-writing program for each meeting phase, showed a countdown of the time remaining on a screen at the front of the room, sounded a bell when 1 min remained in the phase, and archived all comments in separate files.

In the first case study, five students used the GSS in a seminar to discuss two assigned papers for 5 min each. The students were familiar with the technology through prior study and use, and they were generally satisfied with the meeting, thought that the automated facilitator kept the meeting phases on-time, and liked the displayed countdown. In addition, some of the participants said that a human facilitator is sometimes distracting during a meeting.

In the second case study, 14 faculty and staff used the GSS to discuss five information technology topics for 5 min, each in a different brain-writing session. Some participants preferred a human facilitator for the meeting because they did not understand a few of the meeting topics and they needed further explanation during the idea generation session.

## 3. Research methodology

Due to the limitations of small sample sizes and no independent variables in these case studies, an experiment was performed to determine the differences between human and automated facilitation using the research model shown in Fig. 1.

## 3.1. Independent and dependent variables

The independent variable was the type of facilitator employed in GSS group meetings. The first four dependent variables were assessed by a post-meeting questionnaire consisting of four groups of pooled items to measure four of the five dependent variables (see Appendix A). Based on seven-point linear scales that were used and validated in earlier studies, re-use of questionnaires saves time and promotes crossexperimental comparison. No attempt was made to label the intermediate points on the scale, because a neutral alternative may lead the majority of respon dents to select that answer just because it seems a safe and easy choice [18]. The dependent variables were:

1. Process satisfaction: A subject’s perception of his or her interaction process was expected to be a sensitive indicator of a facilitator’s influence on meeting effectiveness; the construct has high reliability (Cronbach’s $\alpha = 0 . 8 1 \mathrm { \Omega }$ ). Questions for this construct were employed in previous studies (e.g. [9]). Item 2, however, was reworded for brevity and simplicity and item 5 was reworded to improve clarity.

2. Group cohesiveness: This construct was measured using three items found in two earlier studies (e.g. [8]) and has a high reliability (Cronbach’s $\alpha = 0 . 8 5 )$ . In addition, two new items were added for greater exploration of the construct.

3. GSS ease-of-use: GSS ease-of-use was measured using six items adapted from an earlier instrument for measuring system ease-of-use. The construct has high reliability (Cronbach’s $\alpha > 0 . 9 0 )$

4. Perceived effectiveness: Subjects’ perceived meeting effectiveness was assessed by five items adapted from [12].

5. Number of ideas: The numbers of unique, and unique, quality comments produced by each group were extracted by two independent, objective raters using coding rules defined by [7]. A quality idea was defined as any good idea related to the topic of the meeting [11]. The inter-rater reliability was considered adequate (unique: 0.96; unique, quality: 0.91).

## 3.2. Subjects

Ninety undergraduate business students were assigned randomly to 9 groups consisting of 10 members. Three groups were assigned to each treatment condition. Prior studies have shown that a minimum group size of about eight is necessary for successful use of a GSS [1]. As done in many prior studies of GSS, students participated as part of an assigned class activity, and incentives were provided in the form of extra credit for participation. Subjects of each group completed a pre-meeting questionnaire that asked for their age, sex, prior computer experience, computer skills, prior GSS meeting experience, and other individual measures. There were no statistically significant differences among the groups in any of these measures.

## 3.3. Facilitator treatments

1. Expert-human facilitator: Groups assigned to this treatment were guided through the meeting process by a facilitator who had conducted over 50 GSS meetings in 8 years.

2. Novice-human facilitator: Groups assigned to this treatment were guided by a facilitator who had only recently received training on operating the various GSS tools and had not conducted any GSS meetings; both the human and novice facilitators performed administrative tasks including saving files, notifying group members, starting and stopping software, etc. Thus, the human facilitators performed the same administrative tasks as the automated facilitator.

3. Automated facilitator: Groups were guided through the process by an automated facilitator modeled on earlier systems that had two principal features:

 Automated individual comment monitor: This feature encouraged meeting participants to contribute more frequently (to minimize free-riding) by tracking the rate of comment submission per individual. If the rate fell below an arbitrary threshold, the program reminded the group member to contribute more (though a pop-up window). The frequency threshold depended on the tool used, the planned duration, individual GSS experience, individual typing speeds, and other meeting parameters. This feature was especially useful for monitoring GSS meetings in which participants were geographically dispersed or when there was an extremely large group.

 Automated system operator: The automated system operator started and stopped the programs, provided technical instructions for using the programs, notified group members when the session was expected to end, saved program output, and performed other administrative tasks. Prior to the meeting, a script could be recorded specifying the sequence of tools and the amount of time each was to be used. One or two minutes before the meeting phase concluded, an audible alarm and a pop-up window on the screen notified each participant to finish writing any comments and prepare for the transition. In addition, a countdown was displayed on participants’ screens showing the remaining time. Simplified training was provided at the beginning of each phase by a screen that contained the purpose of the session, the program name, and clear instructions on how to use the software. Once begun, the automated facilitator required no human intervention to conduct the meeting.

## 3.4. Experimental setting and method

Our study focused on the most commonly used GSS setting, the Synchronous Decision Room, in which a small group meets at the same time in the same place (face-to-face). At the beginning of each meeting, subjects were told the purpose of the study and what to expect.

The decision tasks consisted of two unstructured problems: (1) ‘‘How can the parking problem be solved at the University?’’ and (2) ‘‘Where can additional parking lots be built?’’ These problems have been used in many prior studies.

Subjects typed comments anonymously and simultaneously using electronic gallery writing, a technique that allowed all group members to see all comments at the same time [3]. In addition, the meetings concluded with a ranking of solution alternatives through another GSS program. Each of the three meeting phases (two idea generation and one voting) lasted 10 min. Following the experiment, subjects completed the questionnaire.

## 3.5. Study results

There was a statistically significant difference in the three group types’ perceived process satisfaction $( F =$ $5 . 8 1 , P < 0 . 0 5 )$ , effectiveness $( F = 1 2 . 9 1 , P < 0 . 0 5 )$ group cohesiveness $( F = 7 . 0 0 , P < 0 . 0 5 )$ , and GSS ease-of-use $( F = 1 0 . 0 1 , P < 0 . 0 5 ) $ ; the specific differences among modes were investigated using pairedcomparison analysis [14]. The Tukey test (at $\alpha = 0 . 0 5 )$ showed no significant differences between expert-human and automated facilitator groups for satisfaction, effectiveness, and ease-of-use, but the novice-human facilitator groups were significantly different from the other two (less satisfaction, effectiveness, and cohesiveness). Comparing ease-of-use, expert-human facilitator groups rated the system easiest to use, followed by automated facilitator and novice-human facilitator groups. There was a significant difference between ease-of-use ratings in experthuman and novice-human facilitator groups, but no significant difference between either and automated facilitator groups. In addition, there was a clear difference among the three group types in the number of unique and unique, quality ideas generated (Table 2). Members of the expert-human facilitator groups contributed the most unique and unique, quality ideas, followed by automated and novice-human groups. Using the number of ideas contributed per subject (recorded by the software), paired comparisons using the t-test were conducted. While there was no significant difference (at $\alpha = 0 . 0 5 )$ between experthuman and automated facilitator groups in the number of unique and unique, quality ideas generated, there was a significant difference in the numbers between automated and novice-human groups and between expert-human and novice-human facilitator groups.

There was no appreciable difference between the expert-human and the automated facilitator, but the expert-human was superior to the novice-human (as expected), and the automated facilitator was also superior to the novice-human. These expert-human and automated meetings were conducted efficiently with no missteps or perceived problems, while the novice-human meetings were somewhat more awkward. Real or perceived facilitator inadequacies can influence participants’ satisfaction and performance during the meeting. Therefore, for routine, administrative tasks, the results indicate that an automated facilitator might be able to replace a human facilitator.

## 4. Conclusion

Different facilitation modes have significant impacts on participants’ perceptions of process satisfaction, meeting effectiveness, group cohesiveness, and GSS ease-of-use in electronic meetings faced with relatively simple idea generating and ranking tasks. In general, there is an insignificant difference in these measures between human expert and automated groups, but groups using these two facilitation modes perform significantly better than novice-human groups. One limitation of the research, however, is the use of undergraduate business students as subjects. Thus, the meeting behavior might not reflect that found in a business environment. A second limitation is sample size: 30 subjects per treatment were sufficient for statistical analysis but are not a large community. A third limitation is that personal biases for or against the two human facilitators may have affected subjects evaluations. A final limitation is the use of relatively simple idea generating and ranking tasks; also no attempt was made to automate more cognitively complex facilitation tasks such as ensuring that all parties are heard, that one ‘‘faction’’ does not take over the meeting, that all ideas are discussed fairly, intimidating or derisive comments are discouraged, and that new ideas are considered.
