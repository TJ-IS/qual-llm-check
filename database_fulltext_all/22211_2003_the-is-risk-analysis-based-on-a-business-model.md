# The IS risk analysis based on a business model

Bomil Suh<sup>\*</sup>, Ingoo Han

Graduate School of Management, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology, 207-43 Cheongryangri-Dong, Dongdaemun-Gu, Seoul 130-012, South Korea

Accepted 26 December 2002

## Abstract

The disruption of operations due to IS failure becomes more important as IS has become an increasingly essential component of the organization’s operations and can affect its strategic objectives. Nevertheless, traditional IS risk analysis methods do not adequately reflect the loss from disruption of operations in determining the value of IS assets. Quantitative methods do not measure the loss from disruption of operations. Qualitative methods consider the loss, but their results are subjective and not suitable for cost-benefit decision support. There is a lack of systematic methods to measure the value of IS assets from the viewpoint of operational continuity.

This study presents an IS risk analysis method based on a business model. The method uses a systematic quantitative approach dealing with operational continuity: the importance of various business functions and the necessity level of various assets are first determined. The value of each asset is then determined based on these two levels.

The proposed method adds the first stage, organizational investigation, to traditional risk analysis. The process of the method utilizes various methodologies such as paired comparison, asset–function assignment tables, and asset dependency diagrams. <sup>#</sup> 2003 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

IS was introduced to the business as a means of improving operational efficiency. It was then treated only as a tool for performing an organization’s operations. Now, it is, however, an essential component of an organization’s survival. Almost no operations of an organization can be performed without IS. Organizations have become so dependent on IS that even a relatively short loss of the availability of a critical system can lead to a total failure of the business. The emergence of e-business accelerates this trend. IS managers, therefore, have to place an emphasis on the IS risk analysis and management.

The disruption of operations can also become more important than the replacement of IS assets. The support of the operation is a major part of risk analysis. So, IS assets should be valued from the viewpoint of operational continuity in addition to their replacement costs.

Traditional IS risk analysis methods cannot adequately reflect the loss due to disruption of operations. Quantitative risk analysis methods measure the value based on its replacement cost. Due to the measurement difficulty, such methods do not measure the loss from the disruption of operations. One of the disadvantages of these methods, the inappropriateness of monetary asset value, is due to the difficulty in measuring the costs. Qualitative risk analysis methods determine loss based on the knowledge and judgment of a risk analyst rather than on precise monetary values. This results in a lack of cost-benefit decision-making and the subjective results.

IS risk analysis requires the identification of mission critical assets, the potential threats that might undermine the mission capability, and the consequences of loss of mission critical assets [10]. To meet these requirements, this study presents an IS risk analysis method based on a business model.

## 2. IS risk analysis

## 2.1. Overview

The final stage is the assessment of the risk to the current IS. The security profession views risk as having two attributes: injury and probability [1,2]. To measure a risk, each must be determined. Injury is caused by a situation that adversely affects assets, resulting in costs or limitations on an organization’s ability to achieve its mission, regardless of its mitigation by safeguards. Therefore, the injury level is the loss if the threat is successful. The asset value determined in the asset valuation stage is used for the injury level [26]. Risk probability is the chance that a loss or harm will occur over the lifetime of an asset or within a specified period of time, for example, a year. Therefore, the risk probability is the likelihood of threat occurrences determined in the threat and vulnerability assessment stage.

The purpose of risk management is to minimize the expected loss. It is a systematic process. Risk analysis is the basis on which risk management decisions are made. The process has three stages [5,16]:

\- asset identification and valuation;

\- identification and assessment of threat and vulnerability;

\- risk identification and valuation.

The IS assets include hardware, software, data/ database, personnel, documentation, and facilities. The hardware, software, and various facilities are tangible while the others are intangible. The boundary of the risk analysis should be established during the asset identification stage to determine the protection priority of each asset. Then, the overall worth of the identified assets can be assessed. The values of hardware and facilities are the replacement costs minus the insurance coverage in traditional risk analysis. Also, in a relatively simple manner, the value of software is calculated as the replacement or development costs minus insurance coverage. However, the worth of the intangible assets cannot be easily determined.

## 2.2. Risk analysis methods

IS risk analysis methods are classified into: quantitative and qualitative. The quantitative methods develop a level of risk for each hazard. These methods normally calculate the cost of damage when a risk becomes a reality. They usually calculate annual loss expectancy (ALE) for each threat. Quantitative methods include the Courtney method, Livermore risk analysis methodology (LRAM), stochastic dominance method, ALE using program evaluation review technique (PERT), and simulation [19,21].

Qualitative methods assume that a loss cannot be expressed in monetary value or as discrete events. These methods, therefore, attempt to express the risk in terms of descriptive variables (assessment on a Likert scale from 1 to 5, for example) based on the knowledge and judgment of an analyst. They include Delphi techniques, scenario analysis, fuzzy metrics, comparison risk ranking, and the questionnaires.

## 3. Risk analysis based on a business model

In this method, the importance level of various business functions of the business model and the necessity level of various IS assets are determined. The process of the proposed method selects the quantitative approach and presents a systematic procedure for valuing all kinds of assets. The process has similarities to the traditional risk analysis process. The only differences are the introduction of an organizational investigation stage and the consideration to operational continuity.

## 3.1. Stage 1: the organizational investigation

The investigation into the organization under review is a prerequisite for risk analysis from the viewpoint of operational continuity. It involves determining what needs to be managed. This method regards the business function as the most important one to be managed. This stage has five steps:

\- identification of the organization’s mission;

\- identification of the organization’s objectives;

\- development of the business model;

\- identification of the objectives of each business function;

\- determination of the relative importance of each business function.

To understand the aim of an organization, it is necessary to understand its strategies and plans. The first step is, therefore, in understanding the organization’s mission, which is a general statement of the purpose and nature of the organization [28]. The second step is the identification of the organization’s objectives according to its mission. An objective is a broad, longer-term result that the organization wishes to achieve to support its mission. These may be obtained from existing strategic plans or from interviews with senior management.

Risk analysts should understand the underlying business activities of the organization. The business model is developed after this. It is the representation of the business functions that are necessary in running the organization [12,17]. A business function is a group of business activities that completely support one aspect of the organization. A business function can be broken into several sub-functions. Each of which is further split, and so on, until IS assets supporting the lowest level of activities can be clearly represented. This process is referred to as functional decomposition. The next step is the identification of the objectives of each business function at each level by the line manager who is familiar with his/her business activities and their intended objectives [20].

The final step is to determine the relative importance of each business function. This refers to the degree to which each contributes to the organization’s objectives. The relative importance may range from 0 to 1. Because we have the hierarchic structure of business functions, we can use a successive paired comparison technique presented in Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) [24]. This method takes a business function and compares the effect of its two sub-functions on its objectives without concern for other objectives or business functions [23].

## 3.2. Stage 2: asset identification and evaluation

Three components composing the risk are the asset, the threat, and the vulnerability. As stated in the previous section, assets should be identified and valued first among these components of risk in the traditional risk analysis methods. This is the same in the risk analysis method based on a business model. The value of assets is, however, determined from the relationship with business functions in the risk analysis method based on a business model, while the traditional risk analysis methods determine the asset value considering only the asset itself. As the basis of the asset valuation, we calculate the relative importance of the asset in this stage. The relative importance of an asset is determined using the relative importance of the business functions related to the asset and the degree to which the asset contributes to the objectives of the related business functions, the necessity of the asset. In this stage, the following five steps are included:

\- asset identification;

\- assignment of assets to business functions;

\- determination of initial asset importance;

\- asset dependency identification;

\- determination of final asset importance.

IS assets are categorized into seven groups: hardware, software, data/database, personnel, documentation, and various facilities. All assets within the review boundary determined in Stage 1 should be identified. When assets within the review boundary are identified, the replacement cost of each asset should be determined.

Each asset in an organization supports one or more of the organization’s business functions. All assets within the review boundary are, therefore, related to the supported business functions after they are identified. This assignment of assets to the supported business functions leads to an asset–function assignment table. Table 3 is an example of this kind of table. It is also effective to have the line manager carry out this task [27].

The next step of this stage is to determine the relative necessity of each asset for the related business function. The relative necessity of an asset refers to the degree to which the asset contributes to the objectives of a business function. The value of the relative necessity may range from 0 to 1. The judgments are also given in the form of paired comparisons by the Delphi team or the manager of the business function. The paired comparisons in this step are made among assets related to the same business function. The relative necessity $( N _ { i j } )$ can be obtained from the priority calculated in AHP.

Because the relative necessity is the degree to which an asset contributes not to the organization’s objectives, we should calculate the degree to which an asset contributes to the organization’s objectives using the relative necessity of the asset and the relative importance of the business functions supported by the asset. The degree is the relative importance of the asset. The relative importance of an asset is calculated through weighted-summation with the relative importance of all business functions:

$$
\mathrm{AI} _ {i} = \sum_ {\text { all } j} (N _ {i j} \times \mathrm{FI} _ {j})\tag{1}
$$

Risk probability is the chance that a loss or harm will occur over the lifetime of an asset or within a specified period of time. Threats and vulnerabilities should be identified and assessed to determine risk probability.

Threats can be categorized based on the source and perpetrators’ intent [25]. Accidental and internal threats include error by people, and malfunction of equipment and software. Accidental and external threats include natural events. Intentional and internal threats include modification, destruction, and disclosure of system by dishonest employees. Intentional and external threats include willful damage and theft by external offenders. Cerullo and Cerullo [4] enumerated the threats and their consequences.

The probability of threat occurrence is determined with regard to the vulnerabilities of an organization’s control system. The vulnerability is a control deficiency that makes it possible for a threat to materialize [14]. The probability of threat occurrence should be, therefore, determined in the consideration of the effectiveness and weakness of the control system. The probability of threat occurrence can be determined using various methods: questionnaires, the IBM approach, Delphi techniques, brainstorming, threat scenario approach, and the statistical approaches [22].

This study focuses on the development of the systematic measurement method of IS asset value from the viewpoint of operational continuity. Injury level is determined based on the IS asset value. Therefore, the various methods presented in the previous studies can be used for determining the probability of threat occurrence.

## 3.4. Stage 4: annual loss expectancy calculation

Injury is any circumstance caused by a threat event that adversely affects assets, resulting in costs or limitations on an organization’s ability to achieve its mission, regardless of any mitigation of such adverse effects by safeguards. Therefore, injury level is the resulting loss if a threat is successful, and can be substituted by asset value. Asset value is composed of replacement cost and income loss. The replacement cost of an asset is determined in Stage 2.

For calculating the income loss, risk analysts should estimate the recovery time of each asset if each threat is successful and calculate the daily average income of the organization. The recovery time is the number of days between an asset failure and the full restoration to normal operation of the asset. The formula of PERT can be used to estimate the recovery time [11]. The formula is based on three time estimates: the optimistic time, realistic time, and pessimistic time.

$$
\mathrm{RT} _ {i j} = \frac {o _ {i j} + 4 r _ {i j} + p _ {i j}}{6}\tag{3}
$$

where $\mathrm { R T } _ { i j }$ is the recovery time of an asset i when a threat j is successful, $o _ { i j }$ is the optimistic recovery time of the asset i when the threat j is successful, $r _ { i j }$ is the realistic recovery time of the asset i when the threat j is successful, and $p _ { i j }$ is the pessimistic recovery time of the asset i when the threat j is successful.

The daily average income can be calculated through dividing the annual income by the number of business days. If the organization under review is a profit organization, the averaged annual income of the last 3 or 5 years can be used for the annual income. If the organization is a nonprofit organization, the monetary measure indicating the organization’s annual outcome can be substituted for the annual income. The con tribution to daily average income of each asset can be calculated through multiplying the daily average income and the relative importance of the asset calculated in Stage 2. Thus, income loss when an asset fails due to a threat is the product of this contribution and the recovery time of the asset:

$$
\mathrm{IL} _ {i j} = \mathrm{AI} _ {i} \times \frac {I}{\mathrm{BD}} \times \mathrm{RT} _ {i j}\tag{4}
$$

where $\mathrm { I L } _ { i j }$ is the income loss when an asset i fails due to a threat j, $\mathbf { A I } _ { i }$ is the relative importance of an asset i calculated in formula (2), I is the annual income or the annual monetary outcome measure of the organization, BD is the number of business days in a year, and $\mathrm { R T } _ { i j }$ is the recovery time of an asset i when a threat j is successful calculated in formula (3). Asset value is the sum of the replacement cost determined in Stage 2 and the income loss calculated in formula (4). The asset value is used for injury level.

Annual loss expectancy is calculated by multiplying injury level and the probability of threat occurrence determined in Stage 3:

$$
\mathrm{ALE} _ {i j} = \left(\mathrm{RC} _ {i j} + \mathrm{IL} _ {i j}\right) \times \mathrm{P} _ {j}\tag{5}
$$

where $\mathrm { A L E } _ { i j }$ is the annual loss expectancy of an asset i due to a threat $j , \mathsf { R C } _ { i j }$ is the replacement cost of an asset i when a threat j is actualized, $\mathrm { I L } _ { i j }$ is the income loss when an asset i fails due to a threat j, and $P _ { j }$ is the probability of threat j becoming a reality over a 1-year period. ## 4. Conclusion

Traditional risk analysis methods cannot adequately reflect the loss from the disruption of operations resulting from asset failure:

\- Quantitative methods measure the value of assets based on their replacement costs. They do not measure the impact of the disruption of operations due to the measurement difficulty.

\- Qualitative methods consider the impact of the disruption. The methods, however, provide subjective results and are not suitable for cost-benefit decision support.

This study presented the new IS risk analysis method based on a business model. This is a systematic quantitative approach from the viewpoint of operational continuity.

The study proposed a risk analysis method that considers the replacement of assets and the disruption of operations together. IS continuation is the essential condition for the organization’s survival. Even a relatively short loss of availability can lead to total failure of the business. The disruption of operations becomes more important and costly than the loss of assets.

The proposed method determines the value of assets by considering the relationship between assets and business functions. The value of assets, therefore, reflects the value of the business functions that the assets support. This sees assets as one of the essential conditions for performing the business functions.

In addition, the method adopts a quantitative approach. This method, therefore, inherits the advantages of a quantitative approach and overcomes the disadvantages of the qualitative approach. Thus, it produces objective results. Moreover, the method provides a systematic approach to calculate the mone tary asset value and therefore overcomes one of the disadvantages of the quantitative approach: inappropriateness of monetary asset value.

The method also leads to the involvement of various field managers as well as the IS manager, increasing all managers’ understanding of the risks and threats. This leads field managers to accept the risk analysis and resulting security systems.

The damages resulting from the disruption of operations do, however, include several elements, such as customer confidence, trust, and goodwill. Nor does our method consider partial dependency between assets. For example,itispossiblethatoneassetis50%dependenton another and this sort of effect is ignored in our analysis.
