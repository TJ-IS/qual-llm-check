# Effects of multimedia on document browsing and navigation: an exploratory empirical investigation

Albert H. Huang \*

Eberhardt School of Business, University of the Pacific, 3601 Pacific Avenue, Stockton, CA 95211, USA

Accepted 12 March 2003

## Abstract

As intranets become more widely adopted by business organizations, a growing number of IS are being developed or retrofitted to hypermedia style in order to provide standard Web browsers as the user interface. This allows business documents to be organized and connected by hyperlinks. As a result, the retrieving of information has become easier and more useful in the decision-making process. A field experiment was conducted to assess the effects of multimedia on decision makers’ browsing and navigation behavior. Data was collected using a study group of forty professionals with management responsibilities. To identify possible business threats and opportunities, participants searched and analyzed information stored in an experimental Executive Support System (ESS). Participants’ information-browsing and navigation behaviors were recorded for analysis. The results suggest that the use of multimedia:

 affects decision makers’ browsing and navigation behavior in terms of total browsing time, average browsing time per document, and total number of documents retrieved;

 does not affect navigation efficiency;

 cognitive styles of users do not affect their navigation efficiency.

Keywords: Information retrieval; Browsing; Navigation; Multimedia; Executive support systems; User interface; Presentation format

## 1. Introduction

Managers’ interpretation of information about the business environment are essential for organizational change, and the way management responds to changed information has often been documented as being the first step taken in response to external forces [23,33,62]. Previous studies have also shown that significant time is spent scanning the business environment for information that will affect business operations [34]. Consequently, the ability to scan and identify key information efficiently and effectively is a critical skill for business managers who want to succeed in their careers and help their organizations thrive in today’s information-intensive environment [4,28]. With the increased use of technology, many managers now conduct environmental scanning activities using various types of IS.

Due to the increasing popularity of intranets, many mission-critical or decision support systems have been developed using hypermedia that uses the standard Web browser as its user interface [10,12,21,55]. As a result, increasing numbers of business managers are using such information to help them make important business decisions [26,47]. A hypermedia-style system organizes information into a hierarchic or network structure with documents connected by hyperlinks. Users browse and navigate documents in such systems by clicking on words and buttons, just as they would browse a Web site. Hypermedia ESS or other decisionsupport systems (DSS) are capable of carrying information in various formats [39,48]. Multimedia can be used to carry information items, such as news, briefings, product introductions, business analyses, etc. [9].

Although many studies have investigated the potential benefits of multimedia [3], the effects of multimedia on the various stages of the decision-making process are not well understood. Most of the research papers have focused on decision outcomes [27,54]. Fewer studies have focused on the individual aspects of the decision-making process, such as how individuals use computer systems to gather, scan, or browse information. The goal of our research was to discover the potential effects of multimedia on information browsing and navigation behavior when decision makers used a hypermedia-style ESS or DSS.

## 2. Significance

Previous studies have shown that most business executives are highly systematic in the ways they scan for information [19]. As a result, information presentation formats that influence their information-scanning habits may significantly affect the decisionmaking process. The phrase ‘‘information presentation format’’ here will refer to the way that information is conveyed to users in order to satisfy both their personal style and their task requirements; so the formats are of critical interest to designers of ESS, DSS, etc. In numerous early studies, researchers altered information presentation formats to observe the impact this had on decision outcomes [7,17,31,41,65]. Although many of these studies concluded that the information presentation formats did affect decision outcomes, conflicting evidence was also found [6].

This study investigated the impact of multimedia on information browsing and navigation processes using a hypermedia ESS. It was different from previous research in several ways:

1. Information gathering is an essential first step in decision making, yet relatively little effort has been expended on understanding it [13]. With a hypermedia-style ESS, decision makers can choose from a number of strategies to navigate, browse, and scan stored documents; thus it was judged essential to understand how these processes might be affected by various factors.

2. The study addressed the link between the presentation format and the information gathering process. Presentation formats have often been shown to affect decision outcomes and task performance. Yet, how this occurs is still not fully understood, so this study attempted to investigate the issue.

3. As intranets and Web-enabled systems continue to gain acceptance, more ESS and other decisionrelated systems will certainly be designed using the hypermedia structure. Outcomes should provide insight into the design of hypermedia-style ESS, particularly as they relate to the use of multimedia.

## 3. Previous research

ESS and DSS have been the focus of a great deal of IS research. Their basic function is to provide decision makers easy access to business information; to supply analytical models for structured decisions, etc. and to integrate models into systems to aid decision makers [60,61]. For unstructured problems, decision makers normally have to work through a more convoluted process [5,16].

## 3.1. Information scanning and gathering

Information scanning, an important step in decision making, is the process of gathering and learning about the various events and trends that have occurred in an organization’s environment. It also reduces uncertainty by collecting useful information [63]. For routine or structured decisions, the process is normally well defined: the number of required inputs is usually limited, and the inputs are normally quantifiable measures. For unstructured decisions, however, the process involves a larger number of subjective steps. Researchers have devoted effort to understanding the process for unstructured decisions under the topic of ‘‘environmental scanning’’ [42,49,56]. During this process, decision makers are frequently unable to describe exactly what they are looking for. It is an ‘‘I’ll know it when I see it’’ process. Information input may be qualitative as well as quantitative, making it difficult to determine a value for it. Previous studies have identified many factors that affect this process, such as personal knowledge, user expectation, personal belief system, tasks, and even the relationships of information gatherers with their supervisors [22,29,30].

## 3.2. Information retrieval from hypermedia systems

In hypermedia systems, information retrieval can be accomplished by either: index searching or browsing. Index searching, also know as querying, involves using an indexing system in which users enter relevant keywords to locate information. The process may require several iterations. With each trial, the user narrows the scope of information searched. Browsing is a retrieval process that requires users to navigate a database by following links from one item to another. An information item may be a paragraph, a picture, a media clip, or a page. Browsing has two advantages: first, it allows the user to recognize what is wanted based on his or her own interpretation of the link. Second, it allows users to evaluate a large amount of information rapidly and determine which is useful [25]. Therefore, browsing is particularly useful for unstructured decision-making.

## 3.3. The effects of multimedia

Recently, multimedia applications have appeared in many areas such as: computer-based training, business presentation, merchandising, and communications [18,35,43]. There have been many investigations into the effectiveness of multimedia use, particularly in a learning environment. Studies have demonstrated that multimedia significantly improves comprehension, or even increases users’ problem-solving capability [20,45]. Many studies have found multimedia training methods to be more effective than traditional ones. Other studies have shown that multimedia users had a higher retention rate and a shorter learning curve [50]. Still other findings have indicated that multimedia training is generally more effective than conventional training. The effect was also found to be non-industryspecific. Because multimedia allows users to see and to hear simultaneously, user motivation and stimulation are increased, and learning is enhanced [24]. Decision makers may exhibit different browsing and navigating behaviors when using a hypermedia ESS or DSS.

## 3.4. Characteristics of media

In many studies, the selection of media has been assumed to affect decision making and communication [40]. A concept used to explain the effects of various media is media richness theory (MRT) [14,15,37,44,53]. This represents the ability of information to change a recipient’s understanding within a given amount of time; i.e. ‘‘rich’’ information can change a recipient’s understanding more quickly than ‘‘lean’’ information. Thus, a medium capable of providing immediate feedback is better than one that only provides unidirectional communication; a medium that carries more cues (e.g. expressions, gestures, tones, etc.) is better than one that carries fewer cues. Based on this, text is a leaner medium because it does not support the same level of richness as face-to-face conversation or multimedia, which can transfer facial expressions, body language, or vocal tones.

Also, a multimedia ESS should be richer than text because it would have multiple channels and could transmit more cues in a given period. Thus, it would have the potential to change decision makers’ understanding of the task and circumstances more rapidly. Decision makers who use multimedia ESS would be less likely to revisit a document, may be able to determine the hypermedia structure more rapidly, etc.

## 3.5. Cognitive fit theory

Another theoretical perspective is cognitive fit theory [58,59]. It argues that the representation of a problem, the desired solution to the problem, and the recommended method for solving the problem should all be presented to the subject using the same technique: when there is a good ‘‘fit’’ between the problem representation, the solution, and the method, the problem-solving process will be more efficient and accurate. For instance, if a task required the acquisition of spatial information, a spatial problem representation would probably simplify the problemsolving process.

Therefore, because multimedia presentation formats are less analytical and more intuitive than numerical tables, there may be a cognitive mismatch between a multimedia presentation and an analytical task. As a result, users of hypermedia ESS may need to take more time on each document, or more likely, will become lost in the system.

## 3.6. Bounded rationality

Bounded rationality explains the information gathering behavior of decision makers [32,51,52]. According to bounded rationality theory, decision makers often are faced with complex situations that require the intake of a large amount of information. Yet, practical constraints and cognitive limitations often restrict decision makers from obtaining all the information they need, and so they make decisions with whatever information they have available.

Based on it, multimedia may adversely affect decision makers’ browsing and navigation behavior in that they may lose control of the pace of information presented. Multimedia, especially audio, presents information in a paced sequence. In addition, skipping ahead in an audio presentation is more cumbersome than scanning and skipping ahead over unrelated text. This may affect information browsing and navigation behavior [1].

## 4. Research hypotheses

A preliminary study was conducted to observe the browsing and navigation behavior of several graduate students in a large public university in the American Southwest. Several indicators were observed in the preliminary study that would serve as surrogates for the main research construct—browsing and navigation behaviors: the first was total browsing time, which measured the total time decision makers spent in gathering information before making decisions; the second was the average document browsing time (the average time a decision maker spent on each retrieved document); the third was the number of documents retrieved (the number of documents browsed by the decision maker before making the decisions); the fourth was navigation efficiency (the efficiency of a decision maker navigating the hypermedia system, defined as the minimum path length divided by the total user path length).

$$
\mathrm{NE} = \frac {\mathrm{MPL}}{\mathrm{TUPL}},
$$

where NE is the navigation efficiency, MPL the minimum path length, TUPL the total user path length.

The total user path length is the total number of links a decision maker used to browse the documents retrieved. The minimum path length is the least number of links needed to access them. The maximum value for NE is one, occurring when the decision maker used the shortest path to browse the documents. As the decision maker wasted time going back and forth, the value of TUPL would become larger, and the value of NE would become smaller. NE was not found to have been used in any previous studies, and so it was an untested measure. However, in a previous study of information retrieval, the number of times users returned to the top of a hypertext hierarchy was used as an indicator of disorientation in the system [11].

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 } }$ . The use of multimedia does not affect the number of documents browsed.

The amount of information decision makers obtain before making a decision is of critical interest to researchers [36]. In most situations, they cannot search and gather all available information before making a decision; so an experiment was constructed with more documents in the system than decision makers needed. The excess made it possible to test whether the use of multimedia affected the number of documents they could browse to perform the experimental task of identifying threats and opportunities.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 } .$ The use of multimedia does not change the average browsing time on each document.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 3 } .$ The use of multimedia does not affect the total browsing time.

Business managers spend a large portion of their work time scanning information in order to make decisions. Studies suggest that the time spent continues to increase as the business environment becomes more dynamic. The total opportunity cost is significant. Thus, the amount of time spent on ESS is of critical importance to their overall effectiveness and productivity.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 4 } .$ The use of multimedia does not affect navigation efficiency.

Navigation efficiency is a measure of how efficiently a decision maker can retrieve a document without using unnecessary links. It may be affected by the use of multimedia because the effects studied included increased media richness and enhanced retention. Hypothesis $\mathrm { H } _ { 4 }$ is therefore exploratory because navigation efficiency (NE) was not investigated in previous research.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 5 } .$ The cognitive styles of decision makers do not affect their navigation efficiency.

Subjects took the Group-Embedded Figures Test (GEFT) to determine their cognitive styles. GEFT classifies individuals into two parts: field-independent and field-dependent, one of the most-used dimensions of cognitive style in MIS research [8]. Field-independent individuals are usually analytically oriented: they tend to use structured methods to deal with problems and are more likely to identify a target object in a complex environment. It has been suggested that a ‘‘field-independent person is more able to find salient features in a complex field and to react to ambiguous stimuli in an analytical manner’’ [46]. In contrast, field-dependent persons usually do not perform as well as field-independent individuals in structured tasks [38]. Studies show that the GEFT has high construct validity, reliability, and desired measurement characteristics [57,64]. The $\mathrm { H } _ { 5 }$ hypothesis tests the possible differences in navigation efficiency between fieldindependent and field-dependent decision makers.

## 5. Research method

The experiment used a three-group randomized design. Group A was the control group; its subjects used a text-based ESS. Groups B and C were the experimental groups; their subjects used different versions of multimedia ESS, with Group C using audio, but Group B used no audio. All three groups performed a task commonly performed by decision makers—analyzing information regarding a business proposal and identifying potential threats and opportunities.

## 5.1. Instrument design and validation

The three experimental ESS contained information about a business proposal, and all three used the same hypertext or hypermedia structure to store it. Each ESS prototype contained three subsystems: an External Information System, an Internal Information System, and an Electronic Communication System. The External Information System contained information on the business environment (vital statistics, crime statistics, and industry trends). The Internal Information System contained information about the locations of existing restaurants, customer profiles, current policies, and a market expansion plan. The Electronic Communication System contained important e-mail messages.

The text version of the ESS prototype used only text fields and tables to present information. The fields contained non-numerical information, such as descriptions of events, mission statements, and e-mail messages, while tables were used to display numerical information, such as vital statistics, business statistics, financial and operational data, and other numerical data. The multimedia ESS used various graphics, including bar charts, pie charts, graphical icons, and illustrations. It also included animations and speech output consisting of pre-recorded human voices speaking the numerical information. The sound clips played automatically, but users could replay them as often as they wished.

## 5.2. Subject

The subjects were forty management professionals. To increase the validity of the study and qualified subject pool, hypotheses were tested at multiple sites. Participants were employed by: a leading aerospace company, a major airline, and a regional healthcare organization. All performed managerial duties and had adequate information-scanning experience. The organizational descriptions of the participants’ positions ranged from ‘‘professional,’’ to ‘‘vice-president,’’ to ‘‘executive,’’ with one participant being known as a ‘‘director’’ in his company.

These participants were deemed suitable for the experiment because they all performed some type of environmental scanning tasks [2].

## 5.3. Procedure

All participants were treated equally and randomly assigned to one of the three groups. Participation was voluntary. Participants were told to browse the system to identify potential threats and opportunities in their business proposal. No time limits were set. Participants’ screen activities were recorded on a VCR by converting the computer’s digital output to analog for later viewing. Participants could see the VCR and were aware that their activities were being recorded.

## 6. Statistical results

The investigator collected data by reviewing tapes of participants browsing and navigating the system. Data was recorded on a structured data collection form to reduce errors. After collection, the data were analyzed using ANOVA in the SPSS statistical analysis package (Table 1).

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 } } .$ . The use of multimedia does not affect the number of documents browsed.

The test rejected the null hypothesis. Based on the ANOVA, a significant difference was found between the numbers of pages browsed by the three groups; the quantity of information gathered varied depending on the version of the ESS used. Most decision makers who used the text-based system browsed more documents in the system, and they rarely skipped any intentionally. In contrast, those who used multimedia systems browsed a significantly lower percentage of documents. Based on observation, many participants who used multimedia systems appeared to skip documents intentionally.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 } .$ The use of multimedia does not change the average browsing time per document.

The result was also to reject the null hypothesis. The average time spent on each document in the three groups was compared using an ANOVA test. There was significant variance: decision makers spent less time on text documents than on multimedia documents.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 3 } .$ The use of multimedia does not affect the total browsing time.

Again the null hypothesis was rejected. Users spent more time with multimedia systems than with textonly systems.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 5 } .$ The cognitive styles of decision makers do not affect their navigation efficiency.

Again the analysis failed to reject the null hypothesis. Of the 40 participants, 20 were field-independent and 20 were field-dependent (Table 2.). ## 7. Discussion of statistical findings

The total time each decision maker spent on browsing the hypermedia ESS varied according to the system used. Intuitively, the difference can be explained by the time needed to understand the information passed by various media. The results also suggest that participants were not totally in control of the way their time was spent: it was driven by the type of media. Besides this, participants tended to skip documents with multimedia ESS when they perceived they would need to spend more time on each document. In other words, they would have to reduce the number of documents browsed to compensate for the increased amount of time they would have to spend on each. This is consistent with the bounded rationality theory that decision makers tend to gather as much information as environmental and cognitive constraints allow.

## 8. Non-quantitative findings

In addition to the statistical results, several findings derived from observing the processes are worth mentioning. However, they can be used only as anecdotal evidence.

First, users in the audio-multimedia group appeared to become impatient with the audio presentation and interrupted it. In post-experiment interviews, participants said they could read faster than the audio presentation played, and so they finished reading the text while the audio presentation was still running. Users seemed to be even more impatient with the multimedia presentation of numerical information. For example, many users would allow the audio presentation of system instruction or descriptive information to finish, but would interrupt the statistical information. This suggests that some types of media are only appropriate for certain information; for example, text may be more suitable for factual or numerical material. This finding is inconsistent with the information richness theory, which argues that rich media are always better than lean.

## 9. Limitations

This study had several limitations: first, the experimental systems were custom-built for the study and thus they may not have been a true representation of hypermedia ESS used in business organizations; second, the study was conducted in a setting where there were no external factors, such as interruptions; third, the sample size was small, because it was difficult to get qualified managerial users to participate. In addition, the experiment utilized a single task in an artificial environment; fourth, while the researcher was able to observe the number of documents retrieved, he was not able to observe the actual amount of information obtained from each and could not tell when users retrieved a document but did not read it; fifth, the study did not solicit subject comments during the experiment (to avoid interfering with the browsing process) The only data available to help explain the statistical results are the general comments from the post-experiment interviews and the video tapes. Thus, the actual causes for the differences in subjects’ document browsing behaviors are not clear.

## 10. Conclusion

The use of multimedia in a hypermedia ESS affects both average browsing time per document and total browsing time per system. It also affects the number of documents retrieved by users. With multimedia, subjects appear to spend more time on the system, more time on each document, and browse fewer documents. But navigation efficiency was not affected by either multimedia or by users’ cognitive styles. Based on these conclusions, ESS developers would be wise to exercise caution when using multimedia as a presentation format because it may cause decision makers to spend more time to obtain less information, which would adversely affect decision quality.
