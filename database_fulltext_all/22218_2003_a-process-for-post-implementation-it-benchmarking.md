# A process for post-implementation IT benchmarking

William J. Doll<sup>a,\*</sup>, Xiaodong Deng<sup>b</sup>, Joseph A. Scazzero<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of E-Business Strategy, The University of Toledo, 2801 West Bancroft Street, Toledo, OH 43606, USA

Received 8 April 2002; received in revised form 26 March 2003; accepted 3 May 2003

## Abstract

Post-implementation learning involves continuing improvements in how effectively information technology (IT) is utilized. This kind of IT learning is a core competency that may determine the competitiveness of firms in information intensive industries. While many chief information officers (CIOs) may have some experience with infrastructure, user satisfaction, or business process benchmarking, few firms benchmark how effectively IT is utilized or its impact on the user’s work.

This paper proposes a web-enabled process for benchmarking IT outcomes (effective use and impacts) and diagnosing problems with the user’s learning. This process is based on an explicit causal model of how induced and autonomous learning factors drive IT usage and impacts. The proposed process enables internal versus ‘‘best-in-class’’ causal analysis. We discuss the common considerations and key issues involved in implementing this IT benchmarking process. ②2002.F1 DU

Keywords: Benchmarking; Post-implementation; Learning; Induced learning; Autonomous learning; IT usage; IT impact

## 1. Introduction

As the effective utilization of information technology (IT) becomes recognized as a core competency, benchmarking [4] is increasingly important to chief information officers (CIOs). To react successfully to the moves of competitors [27,36], CIOs need to know how effective these competitors are in implementing IT-enabled process changes or new Internet-enabled business models. Benchmarking can help these CIOs make full use of IT by comparing how well their firms use IT against their competitors, or against the best-inclass users of IT in other industries with similar processes. The goal of post-implementation IT benchmarking is to make full use of the technology and maximize its impact on the work of employees and the organization.

Most of what we know about IT learning focuses on the efficacy of training or support during implementation (i.e. before the application becomes operational) [2,5,26,30,32]. While implementation learning is important, today’s rapidly changing business environment and IT’s powerful and integrative applications require users to continually learn new skills [7]. The term post-implementation learning is used to describe this continuing learning after the application is in operation. This post-implementation learning often represents ‘‘firm-specific’’ knowledge that must be developed internally. Without this continuing IT learning, there will always be a gap between how technology is actually used and the realization of its full potential [8].

There is an extensive literature on individual learning theory [3,14,22,24,28,31,33], but there has been no application of this theory to the context of postimplementation IT benchmarking. In emphasizing the need to focus on the process of transferring best practices, Zairi and Ahmed [40] have pointed out the difference between ‘‘doing’’ and ‘‘learning’’. They advocated benchmarking learning and/or knowledge management practices. Focusing on organizational learning, Goh and Richards [16] described measures for benchmarking learning capacity. Zairi [41] described how benchmarking could be used to close performance gaps by focusing on the processes that produce outcomes. Building upon these contributions, we propose a process for benchmarking IT outcomes (effective use and impacts) and diagnosing problems with the user’s IT learning environment.

## 2. Developments in information technology benchmarking

The competitive and organizational implications of IT benchmarking efforts have evolved through four distinct, but overlapping, stages. The illustration (Fig. 1) of these stages is adapted from original work by Watson [38], as further elaborated by Ahmed and Rafiq [1]. Our focus is on benchmarking IT outcomes and learning drivers in a post-implementation context rather than benchmarking in the IT development context. Thus, we do not include reverse benchmarking, i.e. the reverse engineering of software.

In the 1970s, benchmarking efforts focused on investments in IT infrastructure [20]. Comparative studies of infrastructure investments focused on how much a firm spends on hardware, software, training, and support in comparison to other firms in their industry. The purpose of infrastructure benchmarking is to justify investments in technology and/or to make sure that overall IT investments are in line with what others in the industry are spending.

In the 1980s, many firms engaged in user satisfaction benchmarking using self-developed instruments or taking advantage of published instrument for measuring user satisfaction [10,15,21,29,34]. User satisfaction benchmarking enabled the CIO to see how well the IT department was satisfying key stakeholders in user departments.

In the 1990s, Hammer and Champy [17] popularized the idea of reengineering or business process redesign (BPR). BPR benchmarking efforts focused on identifying best-in-class processes. While these operating process redesign efforts were often user led, the IT department played an important role in using technology to enable these new business processes. Developments in IT such as client–server architectures, data warehousing, graphical user interfaces, the Internet, and the increasing power of computers enabled new, yet relatively inexpensive, integrated work flow designs that would not have been possible without the enabling power of IT [19].

By the end of the millennium, firms had made heavy investments in people, process, and technology, yet there was a perception of a growing gap between how IT was actually used and its full or potential use. It has become increasingly apparent that the critical issue was not what technology users have, but rather, how users learn to use that technology effectively in their work. Effective management of this gap requires IT learning benchmarking. The fourth stage of IT learning benchmarking is just now emerging and new tools and benchmarking processes are currently being developed. In this stage, IT effectiveness depends on the knowledge and skills of users and these, in turn, depend on the factors that drive IT learning.

IT learning in an organization is an uncertain emergent phenomenon that is influenced by the dynamic interaction of people, work processes, and technology [23,39,42]. Benchmarking this phenomenon requires the identification of the processes by which individuals learn. It is focused on how effectively individuals use IT and how their usage patterns affect their perceptions of the technology’s impacts on their work. Post-implementation IT benchmarking requires an explicit model of the complex processes by which people learn to use computers in their work.

Successful change programs begin with clearly specified goals [35]. Increasingly, CIOs are looking for benchmarking tools and processes that enable them to assess how effectively their firm is using IT compared to best-in-class competitors in their own industry. CIOs are also interested in evaluating IT’s impact on organizationally important outcomes such as task productivity, task innovation, management control, customer satisfaction, and supplier management. Finally, they are interested in diagnosing underlying problems/causal factors in the user’s IT learning and prescribing remedies for alleviating these problems.

The model of autonomous and induced learning [12,25] is useful in benchmarking post-implementation IT learning. Induced learning reflects conscious efforts of an individual to improve. An individual’s efforts to enhance his or her skills in using the software, to improve the software itself, or to improve their work process are indicators of induced learning. Autonomous learning happens naturally or without awareness through repetitive use of an application. Learning capacity, empowerment enablers, and learning motivation are factors that cause autonomous learning. Fig. 2 depicts IT learning driver benchmarks (see on the left-hand side) that contribute to the phenomena of induced and autonomous learning. Ovals are latent factors and rectangles are benchmark measures.

It also illustrates how induced and autonomous learning relates to IT learning outcome benchmarks such as IT usage and IT impacts. IT is effectively utilized in an organizational context when it facilitates functions such as problem solving, decision support, work integration, or work planning [11]. The more IT is used, the more likely users will perceive that IT has an impact on their work. Possible IT impacts on work include task productivity, task innovation, management control, customer satisfaction, and supplier management [37].

Technology must be used effectively if it is to have important impacts on the work of individuals in the organization. The more users continue to learn, the more effectively IT is used and, thus, the greater its impact on work. Our static model is a simplification of an inherently dynamic process. We recognize that IT’s impact on work may also encourage learning and, thus, increased usage.

This explicit causal model enables CIOs to identify problems in the user’s IT learning environment and identify possible remedies. This causal model can be used to diagnose IT learning problems in different environments or contexts, i.e. different firms, departments, or work groups. Thus, it enables a comparison of what causal factors are dominant in each context. Causal analysis and the ability to compare causal models between contexts can enhance our understanding of post-implementation IT learning.

## 3. Benchmarking post-implementation IT learning

While Elmuti and Kathawala [13] provide a ‘‘typical’’ benchmarking process, it must be adapted to the unique challenges and requirements of postimplementation IT learning. For example, the typical benchmarking process involves selecting a bestin-class company and measuring its performance. Often industry information or competitive award processes like the Malcom-Baldridge Award can identify this. In benchmarking how effectively users utilize IT, selecting an a priori best-in-class can be difficult.

In contrast to the hard data often available in benchmarking larger units of analysis such as manufacturing processes or IT infrastructure, benchmarking post-implementation IT learning involves soft or perceptual data and latent variables at the level of the individual user. With so many individual users, it is hard to identify the best-in-class performer for a specific application. The number of applications in a typical firm magnifies the problem.

The large number of applications, processes, and organizational units require a large database to make appropriate comparisons. The database requirement may make it hard to arrange benchmarking partnerships in order to obtain comparison data. Where motivated partners can be identified, benchmarking studies initiated by these firms may not have used the same metrics for measuring IT usage, IT impacts, and learning drivers.

Identifying causal factors and prescribing remedies are also more difficult in benchmarking post-implementation IT learning. In benchmarking a firm’s business processes, we normally assume that other firms in the same industry have similar processes and that substantial shared knowledge exists in the industry. If we can identify the best process design, we can implement or copy this into our firm to become more competitive. In IT learning, shared industry knowledge is not available. We need an explicit causal model for analyzing situations and prescribing remedies. We also need the ability to validate this model by comparing causal models between internal and best-in-class benchmarks. Finally, to maintain confidentiality for individual respondents as well as the participating firm, a third party is often needed.

## 3.1. Determining the purpose(s) of IT benchmarking

IT benchmarking is complex. A study can be designed in a number of different ways. Zairi argues that the firm should first clearly identify its purposes and what strategic objectives it hopes to achieve through its efforts. To achieve these objectives, the firm needs to make appropriate comparisons. Generally, the types of comparisons a firm wishes to make are either internal or competitive.

For example, one global automotive firm was using nine different software packages for computer-aided engineering (CAE). The software manager wanted to reduce the cost of providing and maintaining nine different software packages. To do this, he needed an independent comparative assessment of each software package. By identifying the software package with the greatest impact, he hoped to create a consensus on eliminating some software packages.

In another example, an entrepreneur who started his own engineering firm believed that the effective use of IT was a key competency. He wanted to compare how effectively his firm was using IT relative to competing engineering service firms and he wanted to identify the causal factors that might enrich the learning environment for his engineers. He hoped to identify learning drivers that would create the continuing learning necessary for a sustainable competitive advantage.

## 3.2. Determining what objects to benchmark

Secondly, the firm needs to determine the objects that must be included in their sampling plan. There are at least three objects (entities) in IT benchmarking— organizational units, business processes, and software packages. Classifying respondents by these three enables powerful comparisons and improves analysis. For example, if a firm is using an integrative software package for enterprise resource planning (ERP), it might be invaluable to compare how effectively people use the software for different sub-processes; i.e. manpower planning, production planning and control, financial analysis, etc. In integrative applications, the results might identify learning opportunities.

This sampling plan is operationalized using a spreadsheet file containing the names of the individual respondents, their e-mail addresses, their organizational units, the names of the software packages being included in the survey, and the business process for which the software is used. At times, firms want to survey the same individual for different software packages or processes. When this happens, the individual’s name is included in the sampling plan several times; i.e. a row/line associated with each unique software package or process.

The names are included in the sampling plan to make sure that all appropriate respondents are identified and included in the list. The e-mail addresses are used to notify the respondents that the survey has been posted on the web and is ready for their response. This e-mail also asks respondents to click on an embedded link to easily access the web site containing the survey. Finally, the e-mail notifies the respondents of the name of the focal software package and/or process that they should use as a point of reference while answering the questions.

## 3.3. Identifying outcome/process attributes to benchmark

The next step involves identifying the outcome and process attributes to be included in the benchmarking study. Many traditional studies tend to focus on either outcome or process benchmarks, not both [18]. Because an explicit causal model drives our process, we recommend benchmarks for both learning outcomes and the drivers that create these outcomes.

The IT outcome benchmarks include the effectiveness of IT utilization and its perceived impacts on the individual’s work. The IT usage dimensions include problem solving/decision support, work integration, and work planning. In their 1998 article, Doll and Torkzadeh describe the development and validation of these usage measurement scales. There are also five impact attributes that can be benchmarked. They are: impacts on task productivity, task innovation, management control, customer satisfaction, and supplier management. The development of these impact measures is described by Torkzadeh and Doll in their 1999 article.

In special circumstances, some firms may prefer to use only the outcomes benchmarks. For example, they may be trying to screen several software packages and select the most effective. We have found that as firms gain experience with IT benchmarking and identify specific areas where they have performance problems, they then become increasingly interested in improving the user’s learning environment through process benchmarking. The development and validation of the process benchmarking instruments described below are reported elsewhere [9].

## 3.4. Collecting internal data

The process we recommend for benchmarking postimplementation IT learning creates substantial value for the firm and its CIO. The CIO can use the survey results to answer the following important questions.

\- Are we using IT as effectively as our competitors?

\- Are we making full use of the technology or is there a gap between how IT is actually used and its potential or full use?

\- Can changes in the user’s learning environment enhance post-implementation IT learning?

\- What are the key aspects of the IT learning environment that most facilitate or inhibit IT learning?

By providing answers to these important strategic questions, the CIO can make the effective use of IT a core competency of the firm.

Collecting internal data focuses on issues related to obtaining a good response rate, simplifying the data collection, and lowering survey costs. Ways include gaining top management support, having the study cosponsored by a professional association or university; designing the questionnaire so that most people can answer it in 15–20 minutes; providing immediate feedback to respondents; ensuring confidentiality; and using a web-enabled questionnaire.

We recommend a web-enabled approach to simplify the process and lower costs. We normally send potential respondents a personal e-mail message that asks possible respondents to click on an enclosed link and answer the questions online.

## 3.5. Selecting external comparison data

The selection of external comparison data focuses on issues related to standardizing data, identifying the most appropriate comparisons for competitive benchmarking, and ensuring confidentiality. A questionnaire for benchmarking IT outcomes and learning drivers has been standardized by us to facilitate comparisons with other firms as they become available in our database. This benchmarking process has a network effect. The more firms participate, the better the comparison, and the greater the value of the studies to all.

To identify the most appropriate comparisons, we review the firms in our database and select those who are direct competitors. Where a direct competitor is not available, we look for other firms in the same or similar industries that have similar processes and/or use the same software packages.

As a third party, we treat the names of the firms as confidential. Each firm is asked whether they will permit their results to be made available anonymously to other firms. If they are not willing to make their results available anonymously, they do not get information on how well they are doing relative to their competitors. So far, all firms have given permission so that they can benefit from competitive comparisons.

## 3.6. Making competitive comparisons

A ‘‘Competitive benchmarking for core competencies in technology learning’’ report is illustrated in Table 2. The sample report in Table 2 focuses on the use of computer-aided design (CAD) and computeraided engineering software in five engineering service firms. The focal firm is industrial engineering technology (IET). IET has given us permission to publish their reports. The italicized columns indicate the two firms selected by the researchers as most comparable to IET. They are referred to as ‘‘Company $\mathbf { A } ^ { \prime \prime }$ and ‘‘Company $\mathbf { D } ^ { \prime \prime }$ to maintain anonymity. Companies C and E are also engineering service firms but their size and resources do not make them direct competitors. This competitive benchmarking report enables IET to evaluate their performance against the overall sample and their key competitors.

We have found that firms are interested in knowing how well they have scored in each outcome or learning benchmark compared to their competitors. Each row of Table 2 reports average scores and standard deviations (SD) of each of the five engineering service firms in the sample for one outcome or process benchmark. Each row also contains the mean and standard deviation for the overall sample of 164 respondents from the five firms.

We calculated percentile scores for 20, 40, 60, and 80 percentile categories for each benchmark (see ‘‘adjusted percentiles’’) based on the sample size of the focal firm [6]. The mean score for a firm with a large number of responses has less variance than for a firm with fewer responses. To be valid for comparison purposes, these five percentile categories must be adjusted for the sample size of the focal firm. The adjusted percentile scores were used to create five categories labeled: excellent (80% and higher), above average (60–80%), average (40–60%), below average (20–40%), and poor (0–20%). These labels made more sense to the respondents than percentile ratings. If the percentile categories were not adjusted for the sample size of the focal firm, almost all benchmark scores would be rated average. This would leave management with a mistaken perception of how well their firm scored on each outcome or process benchmark.

This report enabled the CIO to identify their strengths and weaknesses. For example, IET was rated ‘‘excellent’’ in the overall impact and utilization sub-totals. Their major weakness is in learning capacity. Within learning capacity, the major problem areas related to the autonomous learning are the firm’s scores in systematic problem solving style, intuitive problem solving style, and prior knowledge of computers. This kind of detailed information on outcome and learning benchmark scores can help IET better evaluate their firm’s scores relative to the scores of their principle competitors. It can help the CIO set outcome and/or learning driver goals and plan a strategy for enhancing IT’s effective use until it becomes a core competency with strategic implications.

## 3.7. Making internal organizational comparisons

The purpose of internal organizational comparison is to analyze data by organizational unit, business process, software package, and/or least-effective versus most-effective users to identify points of strength or weakness within the firm. The same analytical process is used as in external competitive benchmarking but, because the information is internal, the CIOs have access to greater detail and thus richer data. It can help identify the specific organization units, business processes, or software packages that need improvement.

By analyzing the learning benchmarks that distinguish the problem units from the exemplary models, the CIO can better understand what learning driver changes are most critical to improved performance. A helpful tool is a report that compares the learning drivers for the firm’s least-effective and most-effective users. This report divides a firm’s users into two categories by their score on the sub-total for technology utilization (i.e. the bottom 25% or ‘‘least-effective users’’ versus the top 25% or ‘‘most-effective users’’). Contrasting the profiles of the two groups on the learning capability, learning motivation, and learning empowerments variables often provides useful insights.

## 3.8. Analyzing causal models

To identify the causal factors that drive technology utilization, we use step-wise regression (see Table 3). The percentage of variation explained in each of the dependent variables is reported in the $R ^ { 2 }$ row. For example, the value for overall technology utilization was 0.60, suggesting that approximately 60% of the variation in technology utilization was explained by the autonomous and induced learning drivers.

Where an independent variable entered the final regression equation, Table 3 shows the standardized regression coefficient (b) and its P-value. This identifies the variables that best explain the variation in a particular dependent variable. For example, the decision support column has two variables in its regression equation—systemic problem solving style (b ¼ 0:43, P < 0:005) and autonomy (b ¼ 0:52, P < 0:001). This helps identify the dominant learning drivers that are operating in the focal firm.

Table 4 repeats this type of regression analysis using overall IT impact and its sub-dimensions (task productivity, task innovation, management control, customer satisfaction, and supplier management) as dependent variables. In these impact regressions, we include technology utilization dimensions as explanations of technology’s impact on work. It shows that, in this company, technology usage plays a major role in determining its impacts.

Often, the critical learning drivers for improving IT utilization and impacts may not be obvious. Many data sets have incongruities that make interpretation or causal inferences difficult. Often, these incongruities make it useful to analyze causal relationships in several data sets before settling on an improvement strategy. Crossvalidation can improve confidence in the findings.

We recommend the use of step-wise multiple regressions to compare the causal relationship in at least two data sets. First, we use regression on the focal firm’s data to identify the variables with the greatest explanatory power. Then, we repeat this analysis for the bestin-class competitor. If we find the same variables entering the regression equations in both data sets, we have greater confidence in our findings and, thus, the resultant improvement strategy. Over the time, analysts develop considerable expertise in helping CIOs interpret their data, sort out the dominant causal relationships, and develop detailed action plans.

## 3.9. Developing and implementing action plans

The overall outcome and learning benchmark goals set by the CIO during the competitive benchmarking and the rich information from the internal benchmarking set the stage for developing an action plan. The action plan details what needs to be done to enhance use and/or impacts for specific organization units, software packages, or business processes.

Developing the action plan generally takes several sessions with the CIO and the heads of user departments. To facilitate this action planning, we provide a table that defines each of the learning benchmark variables and provides recommended actions for improvement (see the last column of Table 1). The ‘‘best-in-class’’ causal analysis is very useful, shedding light on what the best companies do to enhance IT use. In these sessions, the step-wise regressions results for internal and best-in-class cases are reviewed and discussed with the management team.

The group strives for a consensus interpretation of ‘‘why’’. Achieving this management buy-in facilitates the funding and implementation of the action plan.

The recommended actions are offered only as suggestions. Once the problem is well defined by outcome and learning benchmarks and goals are set by the CIO, management knows their own situation better than anyone else. Through this process, management can develop an action plan, make the required changes, and conduct follow-up studies to confirm whether the outcome and/or learning driver goals were achieved.

## 3.10. Monitoring improvements

The objective of monitoring improvements is to verify that post-implementation learning is occurring and to determine whether the gap between the potential full use and actual use of technology has been reduced. Where effective IT utilization is a core competency, monitoring improvements is absolutely essential.

## 4. Discussion and conclusions

IT benchmarking is important to CIOs because it can justify investments in infrastructure, provide a window to user satisfaction, improve the redesign of IT-enabled business processes, and enhance user learning or knowledge management. This paper has reviewed developments in IT benchmarking and proposed a new benchmarking approach focusing on post-implementation IT learning.

This benchmarking approach should be viewed as complementary rather than competitive with traditional IT benchmarking methods. First, it focuses on different but related issues that build upon each other. For example, full use is not the critical issue if the infrastructure investment is inadequate, if the users do not accept the technology, or if the business process design is not competitive. Achieving the full potential of IT is a function of adequate investment, user acceptance, competitive business processes, and learning.

This benchmarking process includes an explicit causal model of IT learning that can be validated in the firm and cross-validated between firms. Thus, management is not required to base their action plans on a leap of faith that corrective actions will be effective. The benchmarking process itself provides evidence of causal relationships. However, interpreting causal relationship is always perilous. Interpretive field studies involving interviews with users, managers, and IT professionals can complement this positivistic approach and enhance our interpretation of causal relationships in the context of a particular firm.

This benchmarking process utilizes a third party database to facilitate competitive benchmarking against ‘‘best-in-class’’ competitors. This third party approach is easier, less costly, and more confidential than establishing bilateral benchmarking partnerships to exchange information. This database feature has a substantial consortium or network effect. The more firms in an industry participate in the IT benchmarking process, the better the points of comparison, and the greater the value of the benchmarking studies to all participating firms.

These features distinguish this benchmarking approach from earlier or more traditional IT benchmarking efforts. It is unique in its underlying assumptions. Its underlying assumptions are not based on technology alone (e.g. infrastructure benchmarking), or people alone (e.g. user satisfaction benchmarking), or business processes alone (e.g. IT-enabled business process redesign). Rather, it assumes that IT use is an emergent phenomenon that depends upon the dynamic interaction of people, process, and technology. Stated another way, this new benchmarking approach focuses on how well people use technology in their work processes to achieve business goals.

His research has been published in Decision Sciences, Information Resources Management Journal, and Journal of Intelligent Manufacturing. His research interests are in post-implementation information technology learning, information systems benchmarking, and information technology acceptance and diffusion.

Joseph A. Scazzero is an associate professor in the Finance and Computer Information Systems Department at Eastern Michigan University. His research interests include experimental design, quality control, and software engineering. His publications have appeared in Communications in Statistics, Journal of Statistical Planning and Inference, International Journal of Quality and Reliability Management, Total Quality Management, and Information Systems Management Journal.
