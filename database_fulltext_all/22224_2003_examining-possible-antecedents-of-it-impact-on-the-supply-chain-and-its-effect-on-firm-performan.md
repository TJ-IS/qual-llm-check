# Examining possible antecedents of IT impact on the supply chain and its effect on firm performance

Terry Anthony Byrd<sup>a,\*</sup>, Nancy W. Davidson<sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management, College of Business, Auburn University, Auburn, AL 36849, USA

<sup>b</sup>School of Business, Auburn University at Montgomery, 7300 University Drive, Montgomery, AL 36117-3596, USA

Received 29 September 2002; received in revised form 14 December 2002; accepted 13 January 2003

## Abstract

Our study examined the impact of information technology (IT) on the supply chain through a survey of 225 large for-profit US firms. Specifically, it involved the determination of IT antecedents to IT impact on the supply chain and the effect that these relationships had on overall firm performance. The respondents were primarily chief information officers and other top IT executives. The factors in the study were validated using confirmatory factor analysis. A model featuring the IT antecedents, IT impact on the supply chain, and firm performance was evaluated using a structural equation analysis. The data used fit well with the hypothesized relationships in the model, as all links were significant. The findings here suggested that the antecedents, IT department technical quality, IT plan utilization, and top management of IT positively affected IT impact on the supply chain. The results also revealed a positive relationship between IT impact and firm performance. <sup>#</sup> 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Supply chain management; Value of information technology; Structural equation models; Information technology impact; Survey research/design

## 1. Introduction

Supply chain management has come to the forefront of organizational practice over the last decade as companies link to their suppliers electronically, to form inter-functional operations within their organi zations and to forge electronic connections with key customers [17,49,63]. Managers in organizations are also seeking evidence that these efforts produce better firm performance [3,66,68,74]. Because information technology (IT) has become an integral component in supply chain management, it is important to examine its impact on today’s organizations. Empirical research on this topic is extremely sparse, if not non-existent.

This study is concerned with investigating possible antecedents of the impact of IT on the supply chain with the central question: what are some of the antecedents that will positively affect IT impact on the supply chain? A related question is: does IT impact on the supply chain lead to positive firm performance?

To answer these questions, we first searched the research literature for IT constructs that are likely candidates and then defined a structural model for analysis of IT impact on the supply chain and firm performance measures. The process-oriented approach used here has recently been promoted as a technique to study the impact of IT at a more detailed level than simply using firm-level IT investment data and relating it to firm performance measures, such as Brynjolfsson and Hitt [8,12]. Process-oriented IT studies provide the opportunity to investigate how IT use or action in one stage affects a downstream IT and other organizational effects. Data to test our model were obtained through a survey of 225 high ranking IT executives in large US firms. Fig. 1 shows the research model.

## 2. Literature review and research framework

The interest in the impact of IT has intensified since Roach [58,59] introduced the concept of the ‘‘productivity paradox.’’ This stated that, although business was investing huge sums of money in IT, positive results could not be observed in the US economy. Strassman [67] also failed to find positive returns from IT investments in his study of IT expenditures in the 1980s. Other researchers during this same time period found similar outcomes [51].

However, more recent evidence has challenged this and demonstrated that large benefits result from IT investments. Barua and Lee [7] uncovered significant productivity gains from IT. Other recent studies (e.g. [33,46,47]) have argued persuasively that enough evidence has been gathered on the positive effects of IT that the productivity paradox can be labeled a myth of the past.

Although there seems to be evidence now that IT investment does pay off, determining just how it does remains a mystery. Barua and Mukhopadhyay noted that IT value research had ignored the synergistic effects of IT with other organizational factors, such as business strategies, mass customization, and supply chain management. IT does not operate in a vacuum; it works very closely with other organizational assets (e.g. [1,37,43]). In light of this, we examined several possible IT related antecedents and their effects on the supply chain.

A company’s supply chain divides an organization into a sequence of primary activities, inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service, along with support activities [52]. Supply chain management involves the integration of these business processes, and provides products, services, and information that add value for customers. The management of the supply chain is revealed in this holistic approach. The goal is to reduce uncertainties, such as demand, delivery times, quality, and competition in the supply chain. Almost by definition, a supply chain includes the flow of information up and down the chain to all the entities that are involved. The heart of many of the uncertainties is the result of poor flow of information: it is often inaccurate, untimely, and improperly managed.

Porter and Millar [53] argued that the diffusion of IT into the activities of the supply chain amplifies its value-creating potential. The ability to manage the information flow is one of the critical weapons in today’s leading edge organizations. IT has the potential to manage the flow and to impact many of the dimensions of the supply chain such as cost, quality, delivery, flexibility, and, ultimately, the profits of the firm [11]. IT provides links that support communication and collaboration along the supply chain. Handfield and Nichols [29] noted the significance of IT in determining the ultimate success in managing the supply chain and cited evidence that IT was a necessary factor in the survival of supply chain management projects.

Since 1991, Wal-Mart has used its retail link as part of its technologically-enhanced supply chain infrastructure. Retail link integrates the company’s electronic data interchange (EDI) networks with an extranet used by Wal-Mart buyers and some 10,000 suppliers to gather and disseminate information about sales and inventory levels in every store. These systems have become increasingly important to supply chain dynamics, overall cost efficiency, and customer satisfaction [34].

IT is also helping Dell Computers eliminate inventory from its manufacturing plants and has been a key in establishing ultra-close ties with its suppliers [28]. Dell has been able cut inventory to just three to five hours. The reduction has been so profound that Dell has been able to turn factory storage space into production lines. Additionally, Dell is using IT to help push the benefits of lean manufacturing up the supply chain so that suppliers can eliminate excess inventory.

The proficiency of the use of IT in managing information flow within the chain can be measured by examining how IT impacts the supply chain. This measure can give an indication of just how effective IT resources are being used to help manage the activities in the supply chain. It is also important to investigate the antecedents of this measure of IT impact on the supply chain to be able to improve the value of this impact. The identification of such antecedents would help practitioners better manage the IT investment for the supply chain and probably enhance overall firm performance. The antecedents having a possible positive effect on the IT impact on the supply chain in this study are IT department quality, top management support of IT, and IT plan utilization.

In a process-oriented model of the effect of IT on intermediate level variables and, ultimately, on firm performance [6], these three variables have often been proposed as ‘‘upstream’’ variables that affect other ‘‘downstream’’ variables [14,35,60,64,70,75]. It is also necessary to investigate if IT impact on the supply chain also leads to better firm performance. Case studies of successful uses of IT in a firm’s supply chain to give it a competitive advantage have fueled a desire by top managers to enhance their own supply chain management with the use of IT. Therefore, we tested to see whether IT impact on the supply chain also was positively related to firm performance in the firms we surveyed.

## 2.1. IT department technical quality

IT department technical quality is defined as the technical value of the products and services that are rendered by the department compared to the IT departments of the firm’s nearest competitors. Value measures include: ‘‘hardware and operating systems performance,’’ ‘‘business applications software performance,’’ ‘‘communications services efficiency,’’ ‘‘communications service performance,’’ ‘‘application development cycle time,’’ ‘‘end user support,’’ ‘‘IT investments and expenditures,’’ and ‘‘software maintenance efficiency.’’ Determining the value of the IT department is crucial in today’s environment, because it can directly affect how this entity is managed by the executives. It could lead to reorganization, restructuring, or even outsourcing of the IT department. Because of the central position of the IT department in the initiation, development, and implementation of IT resources, the influence of the department on the supply chain should be profound. There is evidence to support this view.

Weill and Broadbent [76] found that base-level IT components were converted into useful IT infrastructure capabilities and business applications with the skills of the IT department in their study of 26 firms. These skills bind the IT components into a set of shared IT infrastructure capabilities. They also found some evidence that the higher skilled IT department were associated with IT infrastructures with more capabilities and services, and ultimately, more valuable business applications. Byrd and Turner [13] found that IT departments with high technical skills led to better IT infrastructure flexibility, a feature of strategic importance to IT and business managers [15].

They also discovered that high technical skills increased the competitive advantage in key business management areas. Duncan [25] interviewed CIOs and high-level IT executives from 21 Fortune 500 firms and found that numerous respondents identified the quality of IT personnel skills as being an important enabler of key IT services. Harkness et al. [30] found that Bose Corporation had to increase the depth and scope of the skills of its IT personnel before it could develop an integrated infrastructure. Likewise, Cross et al. [21], in their case study at British Petroleum, found that its IT organization had to be improved significantly to develop and support more integrated and flexible applications.

Mata et al. [45] argued that the skills of the IT department were critical in maintaining a sustained competitive advantage from an organization’s IT resources. Clark et al. [19] reported in their in-depth case analysis on Bell Atlantic that to build changeready IT capabilities required a highly skilled, empowered, and energized IT workforce, with an entrepreneurial orientation toward leveraging technological knowledge into business applications. In fact, the importance of technical skills to the development of IT in all areas of an organization has been found from early studies of IT departments [18,32] to more recent studies [71]. The importance of the quality of an IT department should be just as high in its effect on IT applications in the supply chain. Therefore, the following hypothesis is given.

H1. The quality of the IT department’s technical skills directly and positively affects the IT impact on the supply chain.

## 2.2. Top management support of IT

Top management support has been investigated in several areas of IT implementation and IT value research [42] and has long been recognized as a key to success. It should include both involvement and participation. It reflects, in many ways, the importance that the top management executives place on IT. Top management involvement could also be measured by the level of funding for IT. It might also include the facilitation of technology transfer throughout the firm.

Essentially all IT literature has expressed the importance of top management executives to the implementation, use, and success of IT in organizations [16,26], IT case studies [56]; and empirical studies [31] have promoted and demonstrated its value. Specifically, it has been shown to be one of the most important factors in the success of IT initiatives and effective use of IT investment (e.g. [36]). It improved the quality of IT, the progressive use of IT, radical IT innovation, the relationship between strategic IT and firm performance, IT effectiveness in small businesses [69], and the assimilation of IT in firms [4]. This serves as the foundation for the second hypothesis.

H2. Top management support directly and positively affects the IT impact on the supply chain.

## 2.3. IT plans and IT impact on supply chain

Past scholars have noted the importance of IT plans to the successful applications of IT resources [10,73]. Some empirical work has upheld this and found a positive association between IT plans and the successful use of IT. Sabherwal reported on a study of 36 companies, where 16 of 18 successful users of IT had formal IT plans. Raghunathan and Raghunathan [55] empirically tested the hypothesis that IT planning predicted IT success and found support for their hypothesis. Premkumar and King [54] also empirically examined the link between IT planning and ITs contribution to the organization’s performance and discovered a positive relationship. Finally, Doll [24] reported that firms with successful information systems were three times more likely to have and use formal plans for systems development.

The impact of IT on the supply chain is a measure of the influence of IT applications across many activities, some integrating functions of the organizations with each other and some with external entities such as customers and suppliers. Use of IT plans should increase the communication between IT executives and business executives and increase the possibility for effective application of IT resources in the organization, including them in the firm’s supply chain [57]. Enterprise-wide plans would probably find support with line managers in developing and implementing IT applications in the supply chain [44]. These arguments and evidence from previous studies lead to the following hypothesis.

H3. IT plan utilization directly and positively affects the IT impact on the supply chain.

## 2.4. IT impact on the supply chain and firm performance

One of the weaknesses of some previous studies was the lack of context for IT and its consideration in the abstract. Mukhopadhyay et al. found that the use of electronic data interchange reduced the operating costs significantly if JIT (just in time) practices were designed to fit the information from the EDI imple mentation. Barua and Mukhopadhyay noted that close proximity between the IT implementation and firm performance measures are likely to yield better estimates of the value of IT in organizations. Here, the general question is whether the IT applications that are used to manage and facilitate the operations and activities in the supply chain have a direct effect on firm performance. These IT applications include appli cations that may assist in some way to buying raw materials, selecting suppliers, managing operations, and interacting with customers. Kotha and Swamidass [39] found that advanced IT applications in manufac turing led generally to higher growth rates and higher profitability. Brandyberry et al. [11] discovered that more advanced IT applications led to more integrated applications and an increase of the quality and timeliness of production information. All of these features have been linked to overall firm performance in past studies (e.g. [40,50]). Mukhopadhyay et al. [48] reported that IT aided in increasing both the output and quality of mail sorting in the US Postal Service and resulted in a considerable influence on perfor mance. Banker and Kauffman [5] found that ATMs had significant strategic effects on a bank by increasing local deposit market share during the early stage of the implementation of that technology. Bharadwaj [9] found that superior IT capability in firm operations was associated with significantly higher profit ratios and significantly lower cost ratios. Tallon et al. discovered that when business executives had more focused goals for IT in their operations, they perceived greater payoffs from IT in the supply chain. Hence, we have the final hypothesis.

H4. The IT impact on the supply chain directly and positively affects overall firm performance measures.

## 3. Research methodology

To develop the research instrument, a pool of items was developed from the literature. Data from a survey sample were used to assess the instrument’s validity and reliability, and to test the hypothesized relationships of our research model.

## 3.1. Content validity

The first phase of our study addressed validity and reliability of our research measures. An extensive content analysis of the literature was first performed. We searched periodical indices in the business and social science (ABI/INFORM and Wilson DSOC) using the above criteria, as well the Dissertation Abstract database. We also reviewed top academic journals (Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, Journal of MIS, Communications of the ACM, Information & Management, Decision Sciences, and Management Science). Books and monographs that have the stated criteria were also included.

We found and used the items for our research factors; explicit characteristics of each factor were also extracted. Three faculty members reviewed the lists in an iterative fashion until agreement was reached on the items.

## 3.2. Pre-test

We performed a pre-test with seven faculty members and eight Ph.D. students at a major university in the southeast of the USA. We asked them to complete our questionnaire and then to review and comment on both its content and appearance. The respondents comments were reviewed and used to update the instrument.

## 3.3. Pilot test

We contacted several large IT intensive companies to help pilot test the instrument. A contact person in each participating firm was asked to distribute the packet to IT managers in their firms. It included a cover letter explaining the research objectives, the questionnaire, and a stamped, return-addressed envelope. Participants were asked to complete the instrument and provide comments regarding the wording of the items, especially understandability and lack of ambiguity [38]. Additionally, we asked the participants to comment on the overall appearance and organization of the instrument.

The responses suggested only minor ‘‘cosmetic’’ changes. No questions were dropped. Since there were only minor changes and with further review by two other University faculty members, the instrument was deemed ready to be sent to a large sample to gather data to evaluate our research model. The items are shown in Table 1.

## 3.4. Data collection

Each section of the questionnaire contained instructions for completing that section. The instrument contained a statement stating that all responses would be held in the strictest confidence: only the researchers would see individual responses. The respondents were offered a summary report of the study if they either included their business card or sent their business card under separate cover. A cover letter was included introducing the researchers and explaining the research objectives. No further incentives were offered.

We compiled a mailing list of top computer executives from the Directory of Top Computer Executives [23], published by Applied Computer Research, Phoenix, Arizona. Due to the difference in profit motive for private and public firms, we eliminated all public hospitals, public educational institutions, and governmental agencies from consideration as participants. Firms also had to meet the criterion of having 50 or more employees in their IT department. This removed effects from outsourcing.

We then generated random numbers for each firm and they were sorted in ascending numerical order. The qualified population of firms was divided into homogeneous strata based on industry type and placed in each type in ascending numerical order. We calculated a reasonable ratio for each category using 1000 total firms as the target number. We selected entries for the mailing list by starting with the first entry per industry category and continuing down the list until the desired number of entries was obtained. Thus, a proportional, stratified random sample of fairly large, for-profit firms was developed.

Three mailings of the instrument were made. We targeted the top information executive in each firm as the questionnaire recipient. The first mailing was sent to all target sample firms. A second mailing was sent to non-respondents approximately 8 weeks after the first mailing. The cover letter for this mailing was an abbreviated version of the first letter; it also stated that this was a second mailing and asked for a response if the recipient had not already returned the survey. We sent a third mailing to non-respondents of the first two mailings approximately 8 weeks after the second mailing. The cover letter indicated that this was the third mailing and asked again for participation. A total of 225 completed questionnaires were returned from the three mailings, approximately a third of the total from each mailing. This resulted in a response rate of 22.5% of the original 1000 firms. This was in line with response rates of other IT studies targeting top IT executives [27,61,65] and other studies targeting senior executives [62]. Chi-square analysis of the industry distribution of the respondents showed no difference from the industry distribution of all the firms used in the survey. This suggested an absence of non-response bias in the returned questionnaires relative to industry association. Some salient characteristics of the responding executives and their firms are given in Table 2. As can be seen, the CIO or the Information Services Director answered more than 83% of the surveys. Over 70% of the companies were 500 million dollars in sales or greater, with over 50% at the \$1 billion mark and above. Manufacturing companies made up 22% of the survey respondents with insurance and financial services at 15% each.

## 4.2. Assessment of convergent validity

Convergent validity is defined as the existence of one latent trait or construct underlying a set of measures [2]. We examined the measurement properties of each factor separately with the objective of isolating and locating any misspecification in each of the factor. The results are given in Table 3. All the loadings for the items of each factor were high and above the cutoff of 0.40. All of the chi-square values were below 5, which was adequate, and the comparative fit index (CFI), the primary goodness of fit indicator in EQS, was above the recommended 0.90 for all factors. The root mean square residual (RMSR) was less that the recommended 0.08 for all of the factors. The reliability measure for each factor was 0.80 or above indicating high internal consistency among the measurement items. Overall, the goodness-of-fit indicators seemed to suggest that each scale was capturing a significant amount of variation in the factors of our IT model.

## 4.3. Assessment of discriminant validity

Discriminant validity is inferred when measures of each factor converge on their respective true score, which is different from the scores of other factors [20]. Following Venkatraman, we assessed the discriminant validity by testing to see if correlations between pairs of factors were significantly different from unity. The evidence for this comparison can be obtained from the estimates of an unconstrained model. The difference between the chi-square values with a degrees of freedom equal to one is also a chi-square variate. A significant difference in this indicates that the unconstained model is a better fit than the constrained model. Such a result implies the presence of discriminant validity between the pair of factors. This test was performed on all possible pairs of factors in the model. Table 4 reports the results of all six pairwise tests among the four IT factors. The chi-square difference was evaluated at 1 d.f. For example, IT plan utilization comparison with top management support had a constrained chi-square of 562 with 37 d.f. while the unconstrained model for this pair was 167 with 36 d.f. The difference is 395 with 1 d.f. This provided evidence that IT plan utilization and top management support were indeed two different factors. All the pairwise comparisons produced similar results and, therefore, all the factors were deemed separate and distinct factors of IT.

## 4.4. Evaluation of the model

The fit for the structural equation model for IT success was very good. The relationships and the fit indicators are shown in Fig. 2. The primary goodnessof-fit indicator (CFI) for the model was 0.91 and was above the recommended lower limit of 0.90. The adjusted chi-square was less than 2, which was considered satisfactory. The RMSR was a little above the recommended 0.08 at 0.18. All of the hypothesized relationships were supported at the 0.05 level. The IT impact on supply chain was positively influenced by IT department technical quality, top management support, and IT plan utilization at magnitudes of 0.26, 0.16, and 0.20, respectively. These results supported hypotheses H1, H2, and H3, respectively. Hypothesis H4 was supported as the link between IT impact on supply chain and firm performance as measured by ROE, ROI, and market share was at 0.18.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

The confirmation of the overall proposed model was important because it provided empirical evidence that IT impact on the supply chain did lead to better overall performance, at least, as measured by ROI, ROE, and market share. Additionally, this study showed how intermediate level IT variables can affect other IT variables and, ultimately, lead to better firm performance. Barua et al. [6] utilized the concept of a stage model of IT impact to show how immediate IT impact variable may affect each other and contribute to an overall performance. However, we believe this is the first study to look specifically at IT impact on the supply chain and its relationships to other IT variables and firm performance. Nevertheless, this approach does seem to be a good way to study the value of IT in organizations.

In considering the results of this study, first, the measures used for the IT variables, IT impact on the supply chain, IT department technical skills, IT plan utilization, and top management support of IT, seemed to be good candidate to be used in other studies examining any of these issues. Although the validation of these variables came from data of large for-profit firms, the nature of the factors appeared to be universal. The validation and reliability of this measure were very good. The measure also took into account many different facets of the supply chain from relationships with suppliers to relationships with customers and the activities in between.

Given the importance of managing information flow with IT in the supply chain, it is critical that antecedents be identified. This study confirmed that IT department technical quality, IT plan utilization, and top management support of IT are all related to the impact of IT in the supply chain. Some past studies have suggested that IT personnel needed to be trained in business skills and interpersonal skills as well as technical skills (e.g. [41]). These suggestions may be valid, but excellent technical skills are still valuable in developing IT applications.

The development and utilization of long-term IT plans were beneficial to the effective use of IT according to our findings. The content of IT plans includes distinctive competences of the firm, the dominant types of IT in the firm, and integrative concepts surrounding the firm’s IT infrastructure [22]. Evidently for the companies in our survey, IT plans provided directions for the integration of IT applications in the supply chain as indicated by the positive relationship between the two. In many organizations, IT plans have not been developed but have not been utilized: this may be a mistake—the IT plans could be valuable if they are used.

Top management support has been shown to be important is. Ultimately, the true value of any organizational resource is its effect on firm performance. Included in the questionnaire were measures of ROE, ROI, and market share that were combined to measure the firm performance.

## 5.1. Limitations

The study developed measures for four IT success factors for the IT supply chain model. Although validity and reliability checks were performed on the measures, there is still room for improvement. We also only used three measures for firm performance and these measures were self-reported.

The sample consisted of large US manufacturing and service corporations, generally members of the Fortune 2000. Therefore, it might be difficult to generalize the results to small and medium size firms. Finally, on the same note, the findings might not apply to large or small public organizations.

Our survey study, like many others, used a single respondent from each organization. However, the research was framed to query the top IT executive and measure the factors from that viewpoint. In fact, some measures like IT impact on supply chain might be more accurately reported by function managers or middle managers that are closer to the operations in the company than senior IT managers.

Finally, this study uses cross-sectional data and is static in nature. Although the causal interrelationships were analyzed and may imply temporal aspects, our study did not directly examine the temporal aspects of the model that were developed.
