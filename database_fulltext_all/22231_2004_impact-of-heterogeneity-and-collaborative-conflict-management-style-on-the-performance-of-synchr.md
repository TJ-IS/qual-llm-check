# Impact of heterogeneity and collaborative conflict management style on the performance of synchronous global virtual teams

Souren Paul<sup>a,\*</sup>, Priya Seetharaman<sup>b</sup>, Imad Samarah<sup>a</sup>, Peter P. Mykytyn<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management, Southern Illinois University, Mail code 4627, Carbondale, IL 62901, USA

<sup>b</sup>Department of Management Information Systems, Indian Institute of Management, Calcutta, India

Received 31 July 2002; received in revised form 12 January 2003; accepted 6 July 2003

## Abstract

Virtual teams cut across organizational cultures, national cultures, and functional areas, thereby increasing group heterogeneity, which may result in increased conflict among team members and less effective performance of the team. Our study explored the relationships that might exist among the heterogeneity of the virtual teams, their collaborative conflict management style, and their performance outcomes. The paper reports the findings of a laboratory experiment in which homogeneous and heterogeneous virtual teams, consisting of subjects from the USA and India, worked independently on a decision task involving the adoption of a computer use fee by an online university. Team members, used a web-based group decision support system (GDSS) that allowed them the opportunity to discuss task options, critique suggestions, and vote on the result. The data analyses suggested that collaborative conflict management style positively impacted satisfaction with the decision making process, perceived decision quality, and perceived participation of the virtual teams. There was weak evidence that links a group’s heterogeneity to its collaborative conflict management styles.

Keywords: Virtual teams; Collaboration; Group decision support systems; Diversity of team members; Group heterogeneit

## 1. Introduction

Virtual teams are work groups whose members are spread over geographic and/or organizational boundaries but are linked together via computer and communication technologies. These teams interact, either in a synchronous or asynchronous mode. In synchronous teams, members collaborate in real-time, whereas in asynchronous teams, members perform their assigned tasks at different times, at their own pace, and according to their own time limitations.

Given today’s fast-paced globalization of commercial activity, it is not uncommon for organizations, especially those that span across nations, to group people from different locations into virtual teams. As interorganizational cooperation is becoming a common phenomenon in the business world, newer organizational forms and structures that facilitate such cooperation are increasingly being sought. Virtual teams may be the answer to the evolving organizational needs. For example, with supplier and customer organizations located across national boundaries and intense time and market pressures for new product development, virtual teams can provide a vehicle for global collaboration and coordination of R&D-related activities. In fact, the need to be able to transfer expertise no matter where qualified experts are located has led to widespread workforces. Work environments have shifted from a predominantly production-oriented set-up to a service- and knowledge-oriented one. Pressures for proactive actions and rapid responses to a fast-paced business environment necessitate the possession of a high degree of organizational flexibility and structural agility. Virtual teams can help organizations adapt better [56]. In fact, they may provide firms with other advantages, such as increased utilization of employee-time, round-the-clock workforce availability, and the opportunity to leverage knowledge and expertise around the world.

Nonetheless, the use of virtual teams leads to many challenges. The need to manage team heterogeneity, use technology efficiently, and develop trust among the members are some of the challenges. Virtual teams may cut across organizational cultures, national cultures, and functional areas, thereby increasing their heterogeneity which may result in less effective performance [64]. Virtual team members communicate and collaborate using different types of groupware technologies. The interactions usually offer lower levels of social presence and information richness than face-to-face (FTF) meetings. Some technologies, such as video conferencing, are more effective than others, such as electronic and voice mail. Proper assessment of these two factors is crucial in the selection of an appropriate groupware technology and its subsequent use by virtual teams. Trust is another critical factor [31]. While a trusting alliance usually develops in long-term relationships, many virtual team members work on short term or temporary projects. The team members may never meet in an FTF setting. Hence, development of trust becomes a major challenge.

This study focuses on heterogeneous virtual teams that include participants from different cultures. In the information systems (IS) field, researchers have examined culture as a factor that influences group performance. However, our understanding of how heterogeneity or homogeneity may impact the performance of virtual teams is far from complete. Prior research has compared FTF and computer-supported groups [8,50] and the performance of homogeneous groups in the US vis-a\`-vis groups from other cultures [63]. Many studies have also used heterogeneous groups to understand other issues, such as the utility of maintaining mutual knowledge in virtual teams [11]; the effect of shared mental models on coordination in teams [15]; the strategies employed by team members to overcome the ‘‘time-space divide’’ in virtual teams [49]; the communication and collaboration in global virtual teams [34,54]; the implications of geographical, functional, identity, and organizational boundaries of virtual teams [16]; and antecedents of trust in virtual teams [25]. Studies have also been undertaken to compare heterogeneous and homogenous teams, but the global nature of organizational work and the increasing mobility of employees have ushered in the need to study heterogeneous groups in varied settings.

Additionally, diversity among team members can cause variations in their attitudes, values, and overall performance, perhaps giving rise to conflicts when team members interact [44]. Furthermore, conflicts are far more evident in problem solving and decision making situations, which frequently involve many knowledge workers today. These types of interactions may increase as well, given globalization, the growing number of multinational organizations, and e-commerce. In addition, the characteristics of communication technology, especially in a virtual team, may contribute to team conflict [29]. Thus, the very nature of the electronic exchanges within virtual teams may be a source of conflict; when the level of information richness is low because of a lean medium of communication. The end result may be confusion, differing interpretations, and ultimately conflicting points of view. It is therefore important to understand the relationship between diversity in virtual teams, their conflict management style, and their performance.

Prior studies on computer supported virtual teams [62] have primarily looked at asynchronous teams using e-mail or other tools. Our work adds to this body of knowledge by focusing on virtual teams performance in a synchronous mode.

## 2. Literature review and hypotheses

## 2.1. Virtual teams and group decision support systems

Virtual teams are considered different from FTF teams due to the physical and, sometimes, temporal separation of the members. One of the prime reasons for the need for asynchronous virtual teams is that of time differences among team members in various geographical regions. This separation can be advantageous, especially when organizations need to utilize available workforce time effectively over a 24 h time span. But, asynchronous virtual teams face hurdles due to problems in communication, such as delayed feedback, long pauses, and discontinuous discussions. The recent interest in the use of synchronous virtual teams may be largely attributed to the availability of improved communication technology. But synchronous teams are not free from problems either. Synchronous virtual teams are likely to have problems of time coordination, especially if the team members are from time zones that are substantially different [13]. Yet, organizations may opt to use synchronous virtual teams, as there is an opportunity for immediate feedback and active interaction.

In order to study the use of virtual teams, it would be helpful to begin by highlighting the work done in the area of group decision support systems (GDSS) as virtual teams may depend on an effective one. A GDSS may combine communication, computing, and decision support technologies to facilitate group decision making activities. For more than a decade, IS researchers have studied numerous aspects of GDSS [9,17,40,51]. Various factors impact a group’s performance, including its size, proximity of members, task type, and culture. Group performance is generally assessed through efficiency measures (e.g. decision time), effectiveness measures (e.g. decision quality), satisfaction measures (e.g. satisfaction with decision process and outcome), participation, and consensus. More closely oriented toward our research, Ho et al. [22] examined the effects of culture. Although experimental studies concerning its effect on the use of GDSS have been conducted (see [58] for an extensive review), the two relatively less well explored areas are the contexts of heterogeneous groups and synchronous virtual teams.

With respect to specific virtual teams research, earlier work emphasized the performance of virtual teams through measures such as decision time, member satisfaction, participation, consensus, and perceived decision quality [1,32]. Our study extends prior research by examining the performance of virtual teams through the assessment of satisfaction with the decision making process, decision quality, participation, and member agreement in a synchronous environment. The choice of these variables stemmed from the view that the assessment of group performance can be enhanced by measuring both process- and outcome-related measures. Satisfaction with the decision making process is a process-related measure while perceived decision quality is an outcome-related measure. The latter measures the perceived freedom of participation. Often, studies measure consensus or complete agreement of group members on the final decision or choice of the group. But in many group tasks, such as those involving brainstorming or planning tasks, consensus may not be desired or necessary. Agreement, on the other hand, refers to a less than unanimous concurrence on the final decision. Our choice of the four performance variables was included to cover the effectiveness, satisfaction and consensus factors of group outcomes.

## 2.2. The heterogeneity of virtual teams

Heterogeneity in group composition is an important issue in GDSS research. Most of the prior research has focused on gender mix or personality profiles [23,53], and cultural diversity [10,18,52]. In the area of GDSS, researchers examined the impact of culture on the use of group support systems in different nations [12]. Anderson and Hiltz [5] examined the performance of culturally homogeneous and heterogeneous groups. Often, the heterogeneity of groups arising out of cultural diversity was based on dimensions such as power distance, individualism/collectivism, masculinity/femininity, uncertainty avoidance and long-term/short-term orientation where people from different nations may vary in the way they perform [24].

In the context of computer-supported virtual teams, the issue of work group heterogeneity has become more complex. As a result of rapid proliferation of web and groupware technologies and with the growing importance of multinational organizations and globalization, it is possible to form groups with members residing in different countries and engage them in collaborative work.

Johnson et al. [28] indicate that virtual team composition can cross national boundaries. Likewise, it is often possible that team members form homogeneous groups based on the fact that they live and work in one location, e.g. the United States, yet one or more members may be from different ethnic or cultural backgrounds. Thus, the work here examines performance of virtual teams whose members’ country of current residence adds to the heterogeneity of the team’s composition.

There has been a growing interest by IS researchers in global virtual teams, resulting in a number of studies that examined heterogeneous virtual teams [33]. Earley and Mosakowski [14] examined the functioning of transnational teams in two confirmatory laboratory studies, primarily focusing on FTF groups, and they demonstrated that homogeneous and highly heterogeneous teams outperform moderately heterogeneous ones in the long run.

## 2.3. Conflict and conflict management

Conflict has many definitions. Interpersonal conflict refers to discord among two or more people who differ from each other in their preferred outcomes, attitudes, values, or behavior. Here, conflict refers to the case when two or more individuals or groups believe that they have needs that cannot be mutually satisfied or that cannot be reconciled or integrated. In fact, one can look at conflict as a perceived phenomenon. Still, the presence of conflict might have positive consequences, an issue that has been the subject of much debate in the management literature. There is evidence that interpersonal conflicts that are related to personality issues are damaging to group effort while task-based conflict is actually helpful and improves group decisions [2,3,27]. While a taskrelated conflict may not always be bad for a group, the way conflict is handled contributes greatly to the group’s success.

There is no doubt that conflict resolution is an important subject for organizations. Numerous studies have addressed the issues in several disciplines. In fact, one can argue that it is the way conflict is handled, rather than conflict itself, that is of crucial importance for work groups. The different ways of handling internal group conflict have been termed conflict management styles. Mortensn and Hinds [38] examined conflict in ‘‘distributed teams’’ and discussed the possibility that cultural heterogeneity might have an impact on intra-team conflict and how it might be related to performance. However, there was little evidence that heterogeneity and conflict were positively related whether in FTF or distributed teams. Evidence suggests that heterogeneity arising from cultural differences can hinder the resolution of conflicts between managers with different cultural backgrounds [65]. In the IS field, conflict management styles have been the focus of several recent studies; e.g. the conflict management behaviors were found to be related to satisfactory resolution of conflicts in IS development [21]. Conflict management is a topic that is of great significance for the success of virtual teams in general and heterogeneous teams. Fortunately, conflict management has been a topic of research in social psychology for a long time [6,48,57], and the following well-defined styles of conflict management have been identified [47]:

\- Avoidance style (low concern for others and low concern for self) is associated with intentional withdrawing from the conflict situation.

\- Accommodating style (high concern for others and low concern for self) refers to focusing on areas of agreement and thus smoothing over differences.

\- Competition style (low concern for others and high concern for self) described as enforcing one’s own views on others.

\- Collaborative style (high concern for others and high concern for self) pertains to integrating the views of all involved.

\- Compromise (moderate concern for all) is associated with finding a middle ground solution.

Similar to FTF teams, members of virtual teams supported by GDSS are expected to follow these conflict management styles. But studies have reported increased conflict due to the use of GDSS for group decision-making [42], and group conflict has often been found to have both positive and negative effects on group performance. High levels of affective or relationship conflict have been found to be detrimental to group performance, a moderate amount of task conflict can be beneficial to the group task accomplishment [26], and low levels of task conflict may also lead to inactivity [60]. Anonymity in the use of GDSS may ensure low levels or absence of relationship conflict. On the other hand, freedom to participate and to express one’s opinions and views may increase task conflict. Thus, the use of GDSS may encourage more constructive conflict [46]. The contextual circumstances (virtuality and dispersion of time and place) that are present in virtual teams might even lead to heightened levels of conflict, especially when there is group heterogeneity.

Based on our review of the literature, we propose the following two major research questions:

Research question 1: How does heterogeneity of the virtual team influence the conflict management style of the team?

Research question 2: How does the virtual team’s conflict management style affect its performance?

## 2.4. Hypotheses and research model

Heterogeneous groups set-up based on personality profiles have experienced process problems [4]. Culture also seems to have a significant impact on group processes. Members of a culturally heterogeneous virtual team are likely to be influenced by their cultural backgrounds when working together. In this regard, Chidambaram [7] argued that modes of communication vary across people from different cultures. Cultural diversity in groups also results in diversity in cognition, values, and demeanor [20]. Thus, cultural diversity is likely to affect the style by which the team manages conflicts that arise during group task execution. There are, however, variations in the conflict management style of homogenous workgroups of different cultures [39]. It is our belief that computer-supported virtual teams having group heterogeneity based on the country of residence (i.e. transnational virtual teams) will offer similar impacts on the group process.

Group tasks are characterized by differences in opinions and interpretations, and group members manage conflicts using one or more of the five-conflict management styles. Though virtual teams would not be very different from FTF teams in managing conflict, it is quite possible that certain factors, such as anonymity, synchronicity, or geographic dispersion may make some of the conflict management styles more dominant than others. The use of GDSS supports anonymity and parallel communication in the group process, thereby decreasing group process losses, such as conformance pressure, evaluation apprehension, and domination in the group and increasing process gains, such as more objective evaluation of problems and group learning [43]. Thus, we expected that computer-supported (or more specifically groupware-supported) virtual teams will be engaged in more collaborative conflict management style than avoidance or competition styles. Prior studies concluded that groups using GSS technology tend to have greater amounts of collaborative behavior in conflict management than groups not using it [36]. Also, Gross and Guerrero [19] found that collaborative (integrative) style of conflict management was generally perceived as the most effective and situationally appropriate strategy.

Members of virtual teams formed with participants from different countries of residence seem likely to have diverse cultural as well as socio-political backgrounds and, hence, dissimilarity in their attitudes, values and beliefs towards problems. These heterogeneous teams seem likely to encounter difficulty in identifying priority of issues and hence integrating diverse views of the members. Thus,

Hypothesis 1. Heterogeneous synchronous virtual teams will have lower levels of the collaborative conflict management style than will homogeneous synchronous virtual teams.

We measured performance through satisfaction with the decision making process, perceived decision quality, perceived participation, and group agreement. Prior literature [35] suggested that computer-mediated communication had marked effects on most of these variables. The use of GDSS reduces conformance pressure, evaluation apprehension and domination in groups and facilitates free participation in group activities and more objective evaluations of problems. Higher levels of collaborative conflict management style will increase the level of participation and the active involvement in the group’s activity and will leave a feeling of greater satisfaction amongst the team members regarding the decision making process. Thus,

Hypothesis 2. The higher the level of the collaborative conflict management, the greater is the level of satisfaction with the decision making process of the synchronous virtual team.

The collaborative style induces higher levels of involvement in group discussions prompting members to raise more issues and exchange multiple perspectives on issues related to the decision. As a result members may have the impression that the decision involved ample discussion and the integration of multiple views. Greater focus of efforts on task should lead to increased perceived quality of decision [45]. Hence,

Hypothesis 3. The higher the level of the collaborative conflict management style, the greater is the level of perceived decision quality of the synchronous virtual team.

The use of GDSS increases participation among group members [55]. Moreover, it is assumed that collaborative conflict management style would promote higher levels of participation by the members of virtual teams. Collaborative members should find it natural to work with others and be active participants in group activities. We expect that cooperative behavior encourages team members to contribute more vigorously to group work. Hence,

Hypothesis 4. The higher the level of the collaborative conflict management, the greater is the level of perceived participation of the synchronous virtual team.

When groups adopt a collaborative conflict management style, the solution is reached after integrating multiple views. Thus, the group’s decision should be more collective rather than individualistic in nature. We expect that members would tend to concur on the decision outcome and will cooperate in achieving agreement. Thus,

Hypothesis 5. The higher the level of the collaborative conflict management, the greater is the level of agreement of the synchronous virtual team.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Subjects

The nature of this study, virtual collaboration among homogeneous and heterogeneous virtual teams in a synchronous mode, dictated a need for a way to identify and obtain subjects. Subjects enrolled in graduate MBA programs at a major Midwestern US university were identified and selected along with graduate students in a major university in India. All subjects were experienced with information technology, including basic office-type skills as well as internet/Web skills (as measured by a questionnaire completed by all subjects).

The students enrolled at the US University were from different countries, in addition to being from the US. However, the international students had been enrolled in other courses and had been involved in other group studies and projects also. This is consistent with our previous discussion of the heterogeneity of virtual teams. Thus, for purposes of the design of the study, all students enrolled at the US institution were considered ‘‘US’’ whereas the students enrolled at the Indian institution were ‘‘Indian.’’ A total of 63 persons, 40 US and 23 Indian, completed all phases of the study.

## 3.2. Research design

The original design was based on a combination of homogeneous, (i.e. US or Indian) and heterogeneous, (i.e. US and Indian) groups. Initially, the study was designed to accommodate 16 four-person groups; however, in the end, one person from the Indian university was unable to participate. Thus, 63 persons completed all phases of the study, including 15 fourperson groups and one three-person group.

For the 15 four-person groups, 7 consisted of all US students, 3 included all Indian students, and 5 were mixed/heterogeneous. The heterogeneous groups included several combinations, i.e. two Indian and two US, three US and one Indian, and three Indian and one US. The one three-person group consisted of Indian students only.

Due to the nature of the study, the approximate 10 (1/2) h time difference between the two locations, and the schedules of the students in each, completely random assignment of subjects to groups was not possible. However, once the availability of all the students was known, accounting for the time differences, class schedules, etc. students were randomly assigned to either homogeneous or heterogeneous groups, depending on their availability. Additionally, all subjects participated in a training session that dealt with the use of the GSS to work on a task that was similar to the experimental task.

## 3.3. Task identification and description

A number of different tasks were considered. However, given that the participants were students, it was felt that their involvement would be stronger if the task was one to which they could easily relate. Accordingly, the task chosen was the selection of a computer use fee for students enrolled in an online university.

Students in all groups were provided with both a printed copy of the task and an online description that was integrated into the software used. The task description identified reasons why the online university’s administration was considering the implementation of a fee, e.g. needing to maintain an online help desk and hiring student workers to staff them. The task, including a description of the fee options, is presented in Appendix A.

As part of the experimental procedures, students discussed fee options among themselves and selected one option as that to recommend to the university’s administration.

## 3.4. Variable identification

The study examined the influence of two independent variables on four dependent measures. The first independent variable was group heterogeneity, and it consisted of two values: homogeneous or heterogeneous. Homogeneous groups were defined as either all US students or all Indian students. Heterogeneous groups consisted of a mix of US and Indian students.

The other independent variable was conflict management style. This had five different values: avoidance, collaborativeness, competitiveness, accommodation, and compromise. As noted earlier in the literature review, only collaborativeness was investigated; it consisted of five items measured by a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). It was measured as the average of the five items, which is a common practice in research involving multi-item instruments.

Four dependent variables were measured; they are identified in detail in Appendix B. Satisfaction with the decision making process was assessed by taking the average of the first seven items (items 8 and 9 were used to assess predictive validity) using a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). A second dependent variable was perceived decision quality, assessed by the taking the average of the first four items (item 5 was used to assess predictive validity) using a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The third dependent variable was perception of participation by each group member. Participation was measured by five items (item 6 was used to assess predictive validity) using a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). The agreement among the group members was measured in terms of the votes cast in favor of the final decision made by a group.

## 3.5. Experimental procedure

The experimental sessions were carried out over a 3-day period. Subjects provided their 1st and 2nd choices for days and time available to participate. Using this s, the subjects were randomly assigned to groups. All subjects were informed by email of the day and time that they would participate. Subjects were not given data about the other members of their group.

It was important to preserve a virtual environment. In the computer labs, there were never more than eight persons involved in the experiment at any time. In addition, each of the labs contained a sufficient number of workstations to allow dispersal of subjects. Finally, none of the subjects knew whether they were on part of a homogeneous or heterogeneous group or whether their group members were in the US or India.

Each session was scheduled for approximately 90 min. Subjects arrived 15 min before and were provided with an instruction sheet similar to the list of commands given them during the training session. Additional information included a flow chart depicting the expected series of activities of each group as part of each the session. Each subject was also provided with a typed description of the computer use fee task.

The task consisted of the following three activities (Fig. 2):

\- Activity 1: Each group member read a file that explained the nature of the activity. This was to be an on-line discussion regarding advantages, disadvantages, comments, questions, etc. about each of the five computer fee options (Fig. 3). The collaboration software allowed students to read each option, comment as desired, comment on another group member’s statements, etc. (Fig. 4). When all commenting was completed, each group member rated the five fee options from 1 to 5, with 5 representing the most appropriate/best option and 1 the least. After this, each group member was able to view the rating results for his/her group. A typical rating screen is presented in Fig. 5.

\- Activity 2: Each group member read a second file that outlined the steps. During this phase, each member commented on the options chosen in Activity 1. This Activity centered on discussions about the choice, etc. (Fig. 6). The discussion was not as encompassing as that of Activity 1. Near the end, each group member voted ‘‘yes’’ or ‘‘no’’ to accept the group’s top/best option. A typical voting screen is presented in Fig. 7.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Reliability and validity

Reliability assessments were computed for the self-reported variables of collaborative conflict management style, satisfaction with the decision-making process, perceived decision quality, and perceived participation. Four experts conducted an initial review of these measures to establish their face validity. Subsequently, Cronbach alpha coefficients were calculated. Since the measurement scales used had not been tested and validated before and in view of the exploratory nature of this research, a cut-off value of 0.70 was considered acceptable. An alpha of 0.85 was found for collaborative conflict management style, 0.83 for satisfaction with the decision making process, 0.88 for perceived decision quality, and 0.84 for perceived participation.

Each item of each instrument was carefully examined to ensure its relevance to the property of the construct being measured. In addition, the evidence of content validity was established by examining the correlation matrix of the indicator variables for a construct [41]. For the collaborative conflict management style scale, the coefficients were 0.35 or better (P-values were 0.0005 or better). For the satisfaction with the decision-making process scale, with the exception of five cases, all inter-item correlation coefficients were 0.22 or better (P-values were 0.08 or better) indicating the need to improve this scale. For perceived decision quality scale, the coefficients were 0.57 or better (P-values were 0.001 or better). For perceived participation scale, all inter-item correlations were 0.36 or better (P-values were 0.004 or better).

In order to establish construct validity of the measures, we followed two methods suggested by Kerlinger [30]: the correlations between the corrected total score and each item score and conducted factor analyses. We subtracted each item score from the total of the scores of each measure. The corrected total score, thus obtained, was correlated with individual item scores. The correlations of the five individual items measuring collaborative conflict management style with the corrected total score of the instrument ranged from 0.56 to 0.87 with significance levels better than 0.0001. The correlations of the seven items representing satisfaction with decision making process with the corrected total score of the instrument ranged between 0.38 and 0.71 with six items correlating with the corrected total score at levels above 0.50. All correlations were significant at 0.002 levels or better. For the four items representing perceived decision quality, the corresponding correlations ranged between 0.69 and 0.83 with significance levels better than 0.0001. The correlations of the five items representing perceived participation with the corrected total score ranged from 0.46 to 0.72 with four items correlating with the corrected total score at levels above 0.60. All were significant at the 0.0001 level or better. The correlations of individual items with corrected total scores of the measures support the construct validity of the measures. However, the underlying assumption was that the total score of each instrument was valid.

We conducted factor analysis employing VARI-MAX orthogonal rotation for each instrument. The factor analysis of five items representing collaborative conflict management style loaded on a single factor and resulted in factor loadings ranging from 0.57 to 0.91. The factor analysis of seven items representing satisfaction with the decision-making process scale produced a single-factor structure with factor loadings ranging from 0.50 to 0.81. The factor analysis of four items representing perceived decision quality produced a single-factor structure with factor loadings ranging from 0.82 to 0.86. The factor analysis of five items representing the perceived participation scale produced a single-factor structure with factor loadings ranging from 0.65 to 0.88. Based on these analyses, all of the self-reported scales possessed sufficient convergent validity.

Further, to examine discriminant validity, the seven items measuring the satisfaction with decision making process, the four items representing perceived decision quality, and the five items measuring perceived participation were combined and a factor analysis with VARIMAX rotation was carried out on this combined set of 16 items. With the exception of two items (item 2 of the satisfaction with decision making process scale and item 5 of the perceived participation scale), the items of the three scales loaded on three different factors with most of factor loadings far exceeding 0.50. The results established reasonable discriminant validity of the self reported scales measuring three outcome variables of this research. As collaborative conflict management style is an independent variable in this research, the items measuring this variable were not combined with those of the three dependent variables.

Predictive validity was evaluated by examining the correlation between the pre-defined/emergent multiitem scale (measuring the construct) and a second overall measure of the construct. For perceived decision quality item 5 measured the overall perceived decision quality. The correlation between the fouritem scale and the overall perceived decision quality item was strong $( r = 0 . 7 4 )$ indicating quite a high predictive validity. The seven-item satisfaction with the decision making process scale correlated with a second measure (items #8 and 9); satisfactory predictive validity was achieved $( r = 0 . 6 1$ with #10 and 0.66 with #11, $P \leq 0 . 0 0 0 1$ for each). For perceived participation the corresponding correlation with the overall perceived participation item (item 6) was 0.38 $( P = 0 . 0 0 3 2 )$ indicating the need to improve this scale. As the collaborative conflict management style was adopted from a validated measure of conflict management style [37], the predictive validity was not tested for this scale.

## 4.2. Potential covariates

Person-specific demographic data (age, gender, number of years of residence in USA or India, computer skill, and groupware skill) were collected, and the variables were statistically tested for random distribution of subjects across the two experimental groups. Chi-square statistics for categorical variables (e.g. gender) and analyses of variance (for metric variables) were performed to ascertain random assignment of study subjects. ## 5. Discussion

In this research we examined two important aspects of global virtual teams: group heterogeneity and collaborative conflict management style. Consistent with the prior research we found that collaborative conflict management style has a positive influence on the performance of virtual teams. We also established weak support for the hypothesis, which predicted a negative influence of group heterogeneity on collaborative conflict management style of the teams. In that heterogeneity of virtual teams is a multidimensional construct, it appears that some underlying elements of group heterogeneity influenced collaborative conflict management style. Heterogeneity may arise because of group members’ diverse cultural backgrounds, functional expertise, experience levels, etc.

As expected, we found that the collaborative conflict management style has a positive influence on the performance of virtual teams in a synchronous mode. This supports the interesting finding that collaborative conflict management style is key to improving the performance of the virtual teams.

It seems that a higher level of collaborative conflict management style results in active participation in the group decision making process. Participants are likely to perceive that the decision making process is thorough and complete. As a result their satisfaction with the decision making process improves. This is reflected in the strong support for Hypothesis 2 $( \beta = 0 . 8 0 , P \le 0 . 0 0 5 )$ .

The groups with higher levels of collaborative conflict management style attempt to integrate the views of all participants. They felt that the final decision is fair and the best outcome that the group can offer. As a result the members’ perception of decision quality improves. This has also been confirmed by the results of Hypothesis $3 \ ( \beta = 0 . 4 0 , P \le 0 . 0 5 )$

In virtual teams that have higher levels of collaborative conflict management style, each participant can express his/her opinion freely and can respond to the views of other members in the group. This is confirmed by the support for Hypothesis 4 in this study $( \beta = 0 . 5 8 7 , P \le 0 . 0 1 1 )$

The results of the study, however, did not support our expectation that that the group agreement level improves with higher levels of collaborative conflict management style. We carried out further statistical analyses and found that group agreement is weakly influenced by the interaction between group heterogeneity and the collaborative conflict management style (Table 4). Although further statistical analyses did not establish any moderating role of group heterogeneity, its possibility can not be ruled out.

The findings have several implications for managers supervising virtual teams. The key to improving the performance of virtual teams is to enhance the collaborativeness of the members and make them strive for integrative solutions. Managers need to state explicitly the task objectives and procedures to the members of virtual teams and promote the importance of collaborativeness to them. Another challenge is to introduce appropriate facilitation mechanisms. Virtual team members communicate using groupware technologies which are usually lean on certain communication cues (such as voice inflection, body gestures) and have less personal focus than FTF interactions. As a result, the equivocality about the preferred outcome that may be inherent in heterogeneous teams may be further aggravated. Facilitators may also have to coordinate the different activities of synchronous virtual teams to ensure that members work together and follow the sequence of activities laid down in the agenda of group work. The issue of coordination becomes more complex when virtual teams interact asynchronously. Team members may work independently of others throughout the duration of group work; or may follow a structure (such as voting) only to formulate results; or may work in a sequential manner during the group work. Accordingly, various modes of coordination are feasible for asynchronous groups using distributed GDSS [59]. Managers, thus, have to be extra careful to determine the appropriate coordination mechanism for asynchronous virtual teams. Some team members may have to work at inconvenient hours, i.e. early morning or late night. Here, we attempted to motivate this category by highlighting the novelty of the work with participants from other countries. While this seemed to have worked for one-time participation in virtual work, managers may have to follow a different approach for virtual teams that interact repetitively.

## 6. Limitations

Though India and the US. vary in their cultural dimensions, and a heterogeneous group consisting of members from just these two countries would be heterogeneous in nature, it is not enough diversity to make general conclusions.

The study assumes that conflict management in virtual teams is in some ways similar to conflict management in FTF teams. This may not be the case.

The subjects in the experiment in India worked at night to participate and this might have resulted in the perception of sacrifice among them.

Research involving virtual teams focuses on an emerging socio-technical environment. The use of students in a laboratory experiment may thus be viewed as a limitation of the research. Other research methods, such as field and case studies, may be more appropriate though laboratory experiments using student subjects on virtual teams are not uncommon [61].

Finally, the limitation of using a particular software tool may have affected the way in which the groups interacted and actually managed conflicts.

## 7. Conclusion

Groupware supported virtual teams is an important area of IS research. While the issues are diverse, we focused on group heterogeneity and collaborative conflict management style. Several conclusions can be drawn. First, the study demonstrated that collaboration among the members of virtual teams improves their satisfaction with decision process and perception regarding decision quality and participation. Virtual team members do not see one another; they are not sure whether others pay attention to their messages. Most importantly, it is unclear how the other members view the decision situation, especially when they come from diverse cultural background. The significance of collaborative conflict management style improving members’ perception about decision process, decision quality, and participation is therefore paramount. The importance of collaboration in the team should be a major consideration when choosing members of a virtual team. Members could be trained to be collaborative and facilitation is essential to ensure collaboration among members.

Another important issue is the heterogeneity of the virtual teams arising out of the members’ countries of residence. Although not conclusively established, this research indicates that heterogeneous team members have a lower level of conflict management style than their homogeneous counterparts.

## Appendix A. Computer use fee program

As a student enrolled at OnlineUniversity.edu, you have come to realize how important information technology is in the completion of your courses and related academic experiences and requirements. Since all of your courses, discussions, and interactions with faculty and classmates are conducted over the Internet, you can understand how important it is for our University to have the best information technology available.

To this end, the University is considering whether to impose a computer use fee. The purpose of the fee will be to provide instructional support to students enrolled in the University. Instructional support can include the following:

\- Maintaining an online help desk. Persons staffing the help desk would be available via email and chat lines to answer general computer- and softwarerelated questions.

\- Hiring student workers to monitor specific courses. For example, a certain number of students could be hired to be available at different times to answer specific course-related questions.

\- Upgrading telecommunications equipment as needed to ensure the fastest and most reliable Internet connections.

\- Purchase of appropriate software licenses for particular courses as well as for general online computer use.

The University Provost has requested input from a committee of students to determine the best way to implement the Computer Use Fee Program. In this regard, there are a number of options that are available. They include the following:

\- Assessing a flat fee to every undergraduate and graduate course offered by the University. Each student taking the course would pay the fee.

\- Assessing a flat fee to every undergraduate and graduate course offered by the College. Students enrolled in undergraduate courses would pay less than students enrolled in graduate courses.

\- Having a graduated type of fee structure. This would mean that courses that make little use of computers and software in the course would carry a lesser fee; courses, such as those in MIS, where computer use is much higher, would carry a higher fee.

\- Assessing a ‘‘fee for use’’ type of structure. Each time a student requested any kind of support, e.g. asking a question from a support person working the help desk, the student would be required to input an ID and password. The student’s University account would be assessed a fee. Students would have to pay for all such assessed fees by the end of the semester.

\- Assess the fee on the basis of where the student lives. The rationale is that a student living, say, in most parts of the United States may be more capable of paying a higher fee than those residing in countries NOT on the ‘‘high income’’ list of the World Bank.

Since the University Provost has appointed you to this Committee, it is your responsibility to make a recommendation to the Provost as to the best alternative. You and other members of your committee may choose from among the alternatives listed above. However, you must provide the Provost with only one alternative.

## B.1. Satisfaction with decision making process

1. I was able to evaluate a number of alternatives during the decision-making session.

2. Our group was able to reach a consensual solution without any major conflict.

3. I feel that the group members converged on the final decision.

4. I did not rush to provide my solutions.

5. I was not rushed by others in the session.

6. The decision making process of the group was complete.

7. The progress of the group towards the stated goals of the task was satisfactory.

8. Overall, as a member of our team, I am satisfied with the process I employed in arriving at the final solution.

9. Overall, I am satisfied with the solution process our group employed to arrive at the final decision.

## B.2. Perceived decision quality

1. The decision made by my group is practical.

2. The decision made by my group is fair.

3. I am confident that the final decision we came up with is the best decision.

4. I feel that the quality of the group’s decision would have positive effects on the performance of the university.

5. Overall, it is my opinion that our final decision is of high quality.

## B.3. Collaborative conflict management style

1. I collaborated with my teammates to come up with decisions acceptable to us.

2. I tried to bring all our concerns out in the open so that the issues could be resolved in the best possible way.

3. I tried to work with my team members to find solutions to a problem that satisfy our expectations.

4. I exchanged accurate information with my teammates to solve a problem together.

5. I tried to investigate an issue with my team members to find a solution acceptable to us.

## B.4. Perceived participation

1. I always felt free to voice my comments during the meeting.

2. Other members appeared to have felt free to make positive and negative comments.

3. Everyone had a chance to express his/her opinion.

4. Team members responded to the comments made by others.

5. The group members participated very actively in today’s meeting.

6. Overall, the participation of each member in the chosen task was effective.
