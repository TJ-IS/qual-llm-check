# Developing a set of management needs for IS managers: a study of necessary managerial activities and skills

Jen-Her Wu<sup>a,\*</sup>, Yi-Cheng Chen<sup>a,b</sup>, Hsin-Hui Lin<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Information Management, National Sun Yat-Sen University, 70 Lien-Hai Road, Kaohsiung 804, Taiwan, ROC <sup>b</sup>Department of Business Administration, Mei-Ho Institute of Technology, Ping-Tung 912, Taiwan, ROC

Received 23 September 2002; received in revised form 12 March 2003; accepted 3 July 2003

## Abstract

This study presents an activity competency model (ACM) that was developed to investigate the perceived importance of managerial activities and skills/knowledge required of three levels of information systems (IS) managers from three types of industries. Our findings indicate that, not surprisingly, the importance of IS managerial activities varies significantly for different levels of management but does not vary significantly for different industries. The results of this study have implications for management development, training, and IS management career planning. They can also serve as a guideline for recruiting the right IS managers.

Keywords: Activity competency model; IS manager; IS management development; Management activity; Skills and knowledge

## 1. Introduction

Today, data resources and information systems (IS) are critical corporate resources. IS managers are therefore becoming one of the important assets of the firm. To meet a firm’s information needs, IS managers must effectively manage their IS human resources and to achieve this objective, IS managers must know exactly what they should be doing in their positions and thus operate effectively, having the requisite skills and knowledge.

The necessary ability of an IS manager is determined by the tasks that must be accomplished, the organizational environment in which they operate, and the information technology (IT) with which they work. However, educational institutions are being challenged to produce job candidates with the skills and knowledge that are in demand. IT applications in the business environment have changed and continue to change dramatically [15]. This forces IS educators and practitioners to reevaluate and upgrade their professional skills on a regular basis. IS professionals must therefore decide what seminars and tutorials will best position them for career advancement. IS managers must make decisions about the needs of their groups and match the job tasks with personnel skills, making long-term resource commitments based on their perception of tomorrow’s IS environment and their organization’s future needs [1]. Therefore, obtaining and retaining IS managers with the skills and knowledge required to support the organization effectively is an important challenge.

To address this, management development must be provided to IS managers. A successful program is based on clear management development requirements with systematic training on design and effective program implementation. However, in practice, training for management development is often considered to be bottom-up or ad hoc; that is, the training courses are often proposed by employees based on their own feelings and not necessarily their real needs or are suggested by external consulting firms based on popular current issues. While both approaches may provide some people with needed training, neither is sufficient or efficient as an approach to meeting real IS challenges in a firm.

Therefore, a systematic study of IS management development requirements is needed. The objectives of our effort were to investigate critical IS managerial activities and skills requirements based on the appropriate managerial level, as suggested by Grover et al. [7]. The key research questions were:

(1) What constitutes a typical set of critical management activities performed by IS managers at various organizational levels?

(2) What are the skills required to perform these man agement activities at each IS management level?

(3) Do the set of critical IS management activities and the needed skills differ among various management levels and industries?

An empirical investigation was undertaken to obtain a better understanding of the managerial activities and skills important to IS managers and to assess the relationships among the management activities and levels.

## 2. Conceptual model and research hypotheses

## 2.1. Activity competency model

An IS manager must adopt various managerial roles and functions that require effective communication with other functional areas and organizations, management of information resources, influencing or proposing changes in organizational strategy, or helping by applying IT planning to cope with the firm’s competitive environment. One way to characterize an IS manager’s work results from understanding the roles that an IS manager plays, while another is based on the functions that he or she performs.

From the perspective of role, Mintzberg [21] concluded that a manager’s work could be described in terms of 10 job roles. These consist of three interpersonal roles (figurehead, leader, and liaison), three informational roles (monitor, disseminator, and spokesman), and four decisional roles (entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, and negotiator). For a detailed description of each role, see [7]. According to Mintzberg, these 10 roles are common to all managerial jobs, regardless of their functional or hierarchical level.

As managers take on these roles, they perform some or all of the functions identified originally by Fayol: planning, organizing, commanding, coordinating, and controlling (POCCC). Urwick [33] had previously separated forecasting from planning, although he felt that it could be a useful basis for planning; he also identified viewing investigation as an important function. Other observers later extended these ideas. For instance, Miner [20] added three other functions: staffing (the organization), representing (to the outside world), and bargaining [9].

To understand IS managers functions in more detail, job characteristics theory (JCT), from Hackman and Oldham [8], provided a very good base for us to further break down the managerial roles and/or functions into specific professional and managerial activities in the course of structuring an organization’s information resources. The JCT argued that skill variety is one of the critical characteristics that contributes to the manager’s feelings about the work and whether it seems to be meaningful. Skill variety is the degree to which a job role requires a variety of different activities when carrying it out; it may involve the use of several skills and knowledge. They suggested that knowledge and skills are the job characteristics that foster people’s psychological states and, through them, enhance internal work motivation and job performance. People with sufficient knowledge and skills to perform well should feel positive about their job performance, while people lacking sufficient knowledge and skills will have negative feelings [5]. Proficiency in each of the work activities depends on the manager’s command of critical competencies at the skill and knowledge level; they contribute to organizational success. A skill implies an ability that can be developed, not necessarily inborn, and that is manifested in performance, not merely in potential.

Katz [13] pointed out that managers should possess technical, human, and conceptual skills. He also claimed that technical skill has the greatest importance at the supervisory level of management. Human skill is essential to every level of management and at the top level, conceptual skill becomes the most important.

Sandwich [29] followed Katz’s research and thought and proposed that managers acquired conceptual/creative, leadership, interpersonal, administrative, and technical skills that could be applied to work situations to achieve effective results. The conceptual/creative skill includes too broad a band of the organization’s related domain and can now be seen to consist of creativity, adaptation, and planning skills. Similarly, the interpersonal skill can be seen to consist of communication and coordination and social skills.

Furthermore, Mandt [16] proposed technical and professional, interpersonal, and managerial and administrative skills. Duffield [3] proposed analysis, planning, organization, and control abilities. We focused only on managerial competency in our research. The study therefore did not include factors such as technical skill. We further considered the multi-lingual requirement favored by most hi-tech companies in Taiwan. Twelve managerial skills for IS managers were then identified.

Drawing on these viewpoints and the job characteristics theory, we established the working perspective of an IS managerial activity that guided our exploration of IS manager performance and what skills and knowledge they need. We called this the activity competency model (ACM); it is shown in Fig. 1.

At the apex of the ACM is the IS manager’s roles or functions; these are vital to the success of the organization. At the next level, the IS manager must have mastered the work activities needed to fulfill his or her roles or perform the functions. Proficiency in each of the work activities depends on the manager’s command of the fundamental competencies at the skill and knowledge level; they can be considered as contributing to organizational success. Obviously, a competency at a lower level is likely to support more than one competency at a higher level. Similarly, many competencies at the lower levels may be needed to support a specific competency at a higher level.

## 2.2. Conceptual framework and hypotheses

IS managers typically exhibit managerial roles requiring effective communication with other functional areas and/or organizations, managing information resources, influencing organizational strategy, and responsibility for IT planning to cope with the firm’s competitive environment. Mintzberg [22] concluded that a manager’s work could be described in terms of 10 job roles and top-level managers focus more on external roles while supervisory-level manager focus more on their internal responsibilities. He also pointed out that top-level managers focus more on external roles while supervisory-level manager focus more on their internal responsibilities.

Furthermore, Paolillo [24] classified these roles into three management levels. Top management plays the informational roles of spokesman, disseminator, and monitor. Middle management plays the roles of leader and liaison. Supervisory management plays the role of disturbance handler. Pavett and Lau [25] also felt that supervisory-level managers focus more on the internal roles of disturbance handler, negotiator, and leader (supervising) while a chief information officer (CIO) emphasizes planning and strategy. Ives and Olson [11] studied the daily work and oral communications of six IS managers. They observed that IS managers play the roles of coordinator, motivator, and planner. Based on this review and the ACM, we hypothesize that different management levels perceived the relative importance of managerial activities and the needed skills and knowledge differently.

In addition, industry comparisons showed that IS managers are impacted by many factors such as the organization culture, design, characteristics, strategies, goals and so forth. Several empirical studies found that managers from different functional areas differ in the relative importance they place on managerial roles. Other researches [19,27] suggested that different industries influence the IS manager roles in different ways. IS managers in more dynamic, competitive industries will spend more time scanning because they need to be prepared to handle environmental contingencies [34]. Thus, we predict that IS managers in different industries perceived the relative importance of managerial activities and the needed skills differently.

Although previous research has studied the relative importance of managerial roles in various functional areas, virtually no research has focused on the perceived importance of each managerial activity and the needed skills based on different levels of management. To address this important issue, the ACM concept is intended to anchor our research and the conceptual framework of this study is shown in Fig. 2. As a result of then, we formulated the following research hypotheses:

1. Effects of industry type and management level on management activity

1.1. H1.1: There is no interaction between management level and industry type on the importance of the management activity.

1.2. H1.2: There is no difference among the management levels regarding the importance of the management activities.

1.3. H1.3: There is no difference among the industry types regarding the importance of these management activities.

2. The importance of management activities to management level and industry type

2.1. H2.1: Each management activity is unimportant to each level of management.

2.2. H2.2: Each management activity is unimportant to each type of industry.

3. The relationship between managerial activities and skills

3.1. H3: The management skills are unimportant to each managerial activity for each one of the three management levels.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Instrument development and pilot study

A survey instrument was used to collect the data used in this study; the questionnaire contained two parts: basic data and management activities and their needed skills. Data were collected using a five point Likert-type scale, where 1: strongly disagree, 2: disagree to some extent, 3: uncertain, 4: agree to some extent, and 5: strongly agree.

The basic data portion asked IS managers to give the name of their organization and circle their current management position. In the management activity and skill part, the IS managers were asked to evaluate and show their degree of perceived importance for each activity, based on their current position. If the degree of importance for the activity ranked 3 or above in importance, they were asked to describe the skills needed to accomplish that activity effectively.

This empirical study used multiple item measures to structure Mintzberg’s roles and Fayol and other researchers’ functions and adapt them for use within the IS management context. Twenty activities from the functional perspective were identified. Another 49 activities came from Mintzberg’s management roles. These 69 activities and 12 skills were identified, based on an intensive literature review and IS manager job analysis, and became the preliminary list.

Once the list was generated, an iterative process involving personal interviews with domain experts was conducted to refine the instrument. To make the results more generalizable, we attempted to gather data from several firms as a pilot study. A sample of 29 IS managers from 6 organizations was obtained. These organizations included one systems integration firm, one software firm, one hospital, and two manufacturing firms. Managers at various levels from these organizations were interviewed using the critical event method. Each subject was asked to recall past and current important events that they had encountered, how they handled the event, and what managerial skills were needed to do this successfully.

Data were gathered by researchers in personal interviews with IS managers, using the questionnaire to structure the questions; the interviews were recorded to improve the quality of data collection. From this, we were able to gauge the clarity of the tasks presented, assess whether the instrument was capturing the phenomenon desired, and verify that important activity and skill aspects were not omitted. This process was continued until no further modifications to the questionnaire were necessary. Feedback from this pilot study therefore served as the basis for correcting, refining, and enhancing the experimental scales. For instance, scales were eliminated if they represented the same aspects with only slightly different wording or modified if the semantics were ambiguous. Twentyone management activities and nine managerial skills remained, as shown in Tables 1 and 2. All questions on the survey come from the literature and were verified, based on the interviews.

## 3.2. Sampling

Data for this study were collected across various industry types and management levels using the questionnaire administered in Taiwan. Firms from the Taiwan Top-2000 in manufacturing, information technology, and banking were considered, but only those with a formal MIS department were included. Through a stratified sampling method, 547 companies were selected. Each received an initial phone call explaining the purpose of the research and inquiring about the firm’s willingness to participate. A contact person was identified at each company and was asked how many managers there were in their MIS department. They were then asked to distribute the selfadministered questionnaires. Participation in the study was voluntary and people were assured that their individual responses would be treated as confidential. A follow-up phone call was used to increase the response rate.

## 4. Data analysis

## 4.1. Sample characteristics

Five hundred and forty-seven companies agreed to participate in this study. Their industries included semiconductor (57), electronics (164), computer equipment manufacturing (64), computer and communication (132), entertainment (6), software (3), security (47), insurance (25), and banking (49). They were classified into three industry types: manufacturing (semiconductor, electronics), information technology (software, entertainment, computer and communication), and banking (security, insurance and banking). We sent out 1168 questionnaires to the IS managers at these companies and received 316 questionnaires in response. Fifty-two invalid responses were discarded. This left 264 valid responses for statistical analysis, a response rate of 22.6%. The respondents represented a broad crosssection of industry and management levels as shown in Table 3.

The ratio of sample size to scale number, 10.5:1, was higher than the recommended minimum ratio, 5:1 [4].

## 4.4. Testing of the hypotheses

Next, a more detailed analysis was carried out to test the effect of management level and industry type on the five management activity categories. A twoway MANOVA was used to analyze the data for the industry type and management level effects on the management activity. The other effects were analyzed using ANOVA, t-test, or binominal test. The results were expressed at the 0.001, 0.01 and 0.05 significance levels and represented by \*\*\*, \*\* and \*, respectively.

## 4.4.1. Effects of industry type and management level on management activity

Prior to the two-way MANOVA analysis, a Box’s M-test showed that the observed covariance matrices for the dependent variables were equal across groups (Box’s M-value ¼ 134:36, F ¼ 0:97, P ¼ 0:588). This test result suggested that further MANOVA and ANOVA tests were worth pursuing. The twoway MANOVA analysis indicated that there was no interaction between the management level and industry type for all five management activity categories (Wilks’ l ¼ 0:91, P ¼ 0:224). The two-way MAN-OVA result shown in Table 5 indicated that the perception of the importance of IS managerial activities was significantly different among the management levels, but not significantly different for various industry types.

## 4.4.3. Analysis of relationship between managerial activities and skills

A binominal test analyzed the relationship between managerial activities and skills. The results are shown in Table 10, showing which of the management skills are required to perform each of the 19 management activities, based on each level of management.

Specifically, performing human resource management activities need the skills of leadership, monitoring and controlling, and communication and coordination for each level of management. However, for middle and top managements needed additional planning and organizing skill. Performing resource allocation and operational standard setting activities needed the skills of planning and organizing and communication and coordination for each management level. Performing marketing strategy activities needed the skills of analysis and judgment and creativity for each level. However, for middle management needed additional planning and organizing skill.

In general, the findings indicated that performing each of the activity involving the use of a number of different skills for each level of management and the needed skills among the management levels were quite similar. Among the nine management skills, planning and organizing (C3) was the most widely required, followed by the communication and coordination (C6). For each skill, there appeared to be no difference in terms of number of rated (i.e. frequency) for the activities among different levels of management.

## 5. Implications and conclusions

Our findings provided empirical insight into the ACM to explain the perceived importance of managerial activities and the associated managerial skills for each level of IS management. The perceived importance of the IS managerial activities were viewed in significantly different ways by different levels of management and the degree of importance for these activities was not significantly different in different industry types. One explanation of this may be that, in general, for any organization the IS manager typically exhibits managerial roles or functions in managing information resources, influence on organizational strategy, or responsibility for the planning of IT to cope with a firm’s competitive environment. Therefore, despite IS managers operating in different types of industry, there was a remarkable degree of homogeneity in the types of management activities among them.

However, different levels of management play different roles and therefore there is a different emphasis on a particular management activity in carrying it out, as shown in Table 8. Furthermore, performing each of the activities involved the use of a number of different skills for each level of management, but the needed skills for each activity among the management levels are quite similar, as shown in Table 10.

The findings of this study have several implications for practitioners. The ACM approach provides a systematic method that allows an organization to shift from a somewhat fragmented and incomplete management development process to one that is more orderly and complete. Based on Table 11, the management development needs of IS managers become clear, while the gaps in the process of filling vacancies becomes more obvious.

For instance, training for the supervisory level needs to focus more on the activities of leveraging internal and external resources, standard operation procedure design, and so forth. For the middle level, in addition to the scope of the supervisory level, more emphasis needs to be placed on recruiting, crisis management, etc. For the top level, in addition to the scope of the middle level, more emphasis needs to be placed on organization design. Programs developed to account for these gaps help the organization to develop a pool of qualified candidates for succession into all positions.

The organization can further develop job profiles that accurately account for the complete set of knowledge and skills required for all managerial positions. Once the job competencies for all levels of management have been identified, a parallel hierarchy of required training responses can be identified. The resulting hierarchy of job competencies then guides all the organizational management develop ment activities.

In addition, the ACM makes it easier to discuss competency development with lower-level managers. Both parties will be better able to identify the types of training and development needed to improve each individual’s managerial performance. The ACM can also be of value for individual career planning. Interested career aspirants can review the different available jobs and their profiles to identify areas of career interest and their development needs. Managers also use such job profiles to assist in making succession-planning decisions by evaluating the competency levels and development needs of their employees.

Understanding the relative importance of managerial activities and the associated managerial skills for each level of management may be critical in successful management of the IS enterprise. While this study has produced some interesting results, they should be interpreted with caution. Although the surveyed managers represent a wide diversity of organizations, the sample total of 264 respondents may not be large enough to generalize on the entire population of IS managers. The disproportionate sample size among the three management levels could also lower the significance of the study results.
