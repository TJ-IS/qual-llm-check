# Understanding the local-level costs and benefits of ERP through organizational information processing theory

Thomas F. Gattiker<sup>a,\*</sup>, Dale L. Goodhue<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Richard T. Farmer School of Business Administration, Miami University, Oxford, OH 45056, USA <sup>b</sup>Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA, USA

Received 6 February 2002; received in revised form 13 March 2003; accepted 27 June 2003

## Abstract

Using organizational information processing theory (OIPT), we suggest several factors that influence some of the enterprise resource planning (ERP) costs and benefits that organizations are experiencing. Though we do not attempt to address all important factors that contribute to an ERPs impact, we suggest two organizational characteristics that may have received insufficient attention in other ERP literature: interdependence and differentiation. High interdependence among organizational sub-units, contributes to the positive ERP-related effects because of ERPs ability to coordinate activities and facilitate information flows. However, when differentiation among sub-units is high, organizations may incur ERP-related compromise or design costs. We provide a case study that explores the viability of this framework. The case describes some local-level impacts of ERP and provides some evidence of the validity of the model. Unexpected findings are also presented. <sup>#</sup> 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Enterprise resource planning; ERP; Enterprise system; Standardization; Integration; Interdependence; Differentiation; Manufacturing planning and control systems; Organizational information processing theory

## 1. Introduction

Companies have experienced mixed results when using enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems: along with some inspiring success stories come a con siderable number of conspicuous failures. In between these extremes are many ERP implementations that are partly successful and partly problematic. While much of the literature focuses on implementation practices as the critical determinant of success, this paper uses several constructs from organizational information processing theory (OIPT) as a basis for exploring an additional complementary explanation for why some ERP implementations are more successful than others.

Our IP-based framework suggests that, depending upon the amount of interdependence and differentiation among sub-units of an organization, ERP systems may ‘‘fit’’ some organizations better than others. To explore these ideas further and to see whether these constructs make sense in the context of an actual company, we conducted a case study of a manufacturing organization involved in a multi-plant ERP implementation. The investigation suggests that OIPT is a worthwhile lens for understanding ERP systems, and the case study provides evidence that the concepts of interdependence and, especially, differentiation may be important in predicting the impact of ERP, particularly at the local level.

The goals of our research were to develop some rich descriptions of ERP impacts at the local level and, in particular, to refine our conceptualization of differentiation and the ways in which it appears to affect ERP systems within organizations. Differences among sub-units can be assessed only by examining them. Therefore, this paper focused on lower levels of the organization, specifically, on the manufacturing facility.

We acknowledge that concentrating on a limited view of the organization misses important ERP costs and benefits that occur at the organization-wide level. However, we also note that a great deal of the existing discussion of ERP focuses at that level and perhaps under-represents local-level costs and benefits.

## 2. Why ERP?

ERP systems are highly standardized systems. They typically employ a single logical database for the entire enterprise. This feature requires data standards across the enterprise [1,5,7]. ERP implementations also entail a great deal of process standardization [13,22]. This is partially a result of data standardization. Furthermore, managers in many firms view ERP-driven reengineering of business processes to company-wide standards as beneficial from a business standpoint [20]. Finally, ERP systems themselves are limited in the processes that they can model. For example, one estimate is that 20% of the typical company’s legacy processes cannot be modeled in SAP [23]. As a result, an organization is sometimes limited to the collection of options imbedded in the package or to modifying the business practice and not the ERP code [3,29].

Integration—the linking together of the information and processes of distinct subsets of the organization— is one benefit of standardization. Indeed, ERP systems link all (or many) business functions and operating locations so all have access to relevant information as transactions occur.

## 3. Organizational information processing theory

OIPT posits that resolving uncertainty is the central task in organizational design. The theory conceptualizes uncertainty as a lack of information about statuses of tasks, the environment, and so on [10,12]. The amount and types of uncertainty vary across organizations and among individual sub-units within organizations. Numerous modes of coordination exist and these differ in the degree to which they are suited for coping with various types and amounts of uncertainty. For example, the theoretical literature states that mechanisms, such as hierarchical referral and standard operating procedures, are appropriate when uncertainty is low, while computerized IS and lateral relations are better choices in high uncertainty situations. Therefore, in order to prosper, the organization must match the appropriate mode(s) of coordination with its particular uncertainties [11].

It follows that ERP, being a type of computerized information system, is a coordination mechanism that is appropriate under many circumstances but less so under others. A recent study [9] examined the impact of various uncertainty reduction mechanisms within manufacturing plants. Interestingly, IS were the only mechanism that did not significantly moderate the negative relationship between uncertainty and performance. However, the researchers examined IT investment in the aggregate, rather than considering potentially differing effects of different types of computerized systems (e.g. computer integrated manufacturing, ERP, advanced planning and scheduling software, etc.). Since these technologies differ so widely, their impacts on performance probably differ. Therefore, it may be more revealing to narrow the focus to a particular technology, such as ERP.

Information processing theorists have suggested various sources or types of uncertainty, including: the characteristics of the self-contained tasks that sub-units must execute, instability of the external environment, interdependence with other sub-units [27], and differentiation among sub-units [6]. Because integration and standardization are two major characteristics of ERP, we focus on the two sources of uncertainty that are most related to them: interdependence and differentiation. Theory suggests that greater interdependence among organizational sub-units is associated with greater benefits from ERP. On the other hand, differentiation among organizational subunits can lead to some significant ERP-related costs. This framework is depicted in Fig. 1.

## 3.1. Interdependence influences ERP benefits

OIPT posits that the benefits of a highly integrated, standardized system, such as ERP, are influenced positively by the level of interdependence among organizational sub-units [30]. Interdependence is the degree to which sub-units must exchange information or material in order to complete their tasks [21]. Interdependence increases the need for ‘‘mutual adjustment’’ and decreases the degree to which activities can be pre-planned. When interdependence is low, simple coordination modes, like standard operating procedures, suffice. By contrast, high interdependence increases the need for a common formalized language in order to enable the exchange of information among sub-units [26].

Managing interdependence and improving the flow of information across sub-units is a major reason many firms have implemented ERP [4]. If sub-units of a firm need to exchange information and materials, then ERP should facilitate this flow. After all, data standards eliminate the burden of reconciling or translating information that is inconsistently defined across two or more sub-units [18]. Data standards also do away with the potential for translation or reconciliation errors as well as ambiguity about a field’s true meaning [24]. Finally, ERP improves the timeliness of information.

## 3.2. Differentiation influences ERP costs

On the other hand, OIPT predicts that the costs of a standardized system, such as ERP, increase in proportion to the degree of sub-unit differentiation, which is the uniqueness of tasks, technologies, environment, goals, etc. across sub-units [19]. According to a model posited by Goodhue et al. [14], when an integrated IS is implemented across a number of differentiated subunits, two types of costs result: design and compromise costs as shown in Fig. 2. Designing a single common language (or a single common system) to accommodate the diverse needs of sub-units that differ greatly from one another is a difficult and expensive task, compared to designing a system for a collection of relatively homogeneous sub-units. These design costs include the time and money spent developing common understandings across very different points of view, creating complex IS designs, and possible database structure redesign. On the other hand, when standardized systems are implemented across differentiated sub-units without such complex designs, some sub-units will experience compromise costs. These costs include decreased operational performance or decreased data relevance due to one or more sub-units’ having to use an IS that is not well-tailored to the peculiarities of the tasks that the subunit must execute. Hong and Kim [17] found that the fit of an organization’s ERP system with its task, data, and related needs explains a significant amount of the company’s ERP success. Our model extends this by suggesting that differentiation impacts fit.

A recent study of small businesses found that the greater the variety of functions within a business, the less the effect of ITadoption on the amount and quality of information made available to organization members [8]. One explanation is that greater functional variety creates greater variety in information needs across sub-units and thus greater compromise costs if a single, standard system is broadly deployed. While this is only one explanation, it does add some empirical basis for investigating our framework.

## 4. Differentiation in a manufacturing context

Manufacturing plants often exhibit high differentiation, making them a good domain for studying the impact of differentiation on ERP implementation. Plants within an organization often differ significantly and observably in products produced, technologies employed, and markets served.

The manufacturing strategy literature provides several very similar frameworks for characterizing plants and thus for comparing them in order to assess differ entiation among them. These characteristics include volume and standardization of outputs. The manufacturing technologies (e.g. general purpose versus specialized, level of automation) that plants employ internally should be consistent with these output characteristics [15,16]. Furthermore, to be successful, manufacturers must match these characteristics with the configuration of their computerized planning and control systems, including ERP [2]. For example, job and batch approaches require features like detailed order tracking, while these features impede productivity for repetitive and process manufacturers. Processors often require the capability to maintain multiple bills of material for a single end product and to track individual lots of raw material [25].

This line of thinking has strong implications for ERP. A particular ERP implementation imposes a particular set of computerized planning and control system characteristics (configuration decisions that are made when implementing ERP modules like material requirements planning, master scheduling and purchasing) on a manufacturing plant. This configuration is usually determined at the division or company-wide level. Thus a particular ERP configuration may be a good fit for the majority of plants in a company but may be a bad fit for one plant (or a few plants) that differ substantially from the others.

## 5. Research methodology

In order to explore these ideas, we conducted a case study at a manufacturing plant that was part of a large

ERP implementation. We interviewed eight individuals at the site: Plant Manager, Controller, Accounting Clerk, Purchasing Manager, Customer Service Representative, Master Scheduler and Production Supervisor, Shipping Supervisor. Most interviews lasted approximately 1 h and followed a semi-structured format. Most were taped and transcribed. We also toured the facility and reviewed documents, such as organization charts and ERP planning documents. Case write-ups were reviewed by the plant manager for accuracy and amended as necessary. The company identity is disguised.

## 6. Case description: forest products corporation

Forest products corporation (FPC) manufactures construction materials—primarily engineered lumber. FPC has approximately \$1.2 billion in sales annually, generated from approximately 20 manufacturing plants, all in North America. Finished goods are held at the plants and shipped directly to customers and distributors. Our focus in this case was on FPC’s Augusta plant.

In the early 1990s management felt FPC’s systems were inadequate for several reasons. Current systems were not Y2K compliant. Furthermore, the plants were running various systems that did not communicate. Thus customers had to place and track orders with multiple plants. In fact, it was difficult to even find out how much inventory FPC retained of a particular item without making telephone calls to many plants.

In 1993 a decision was made to implement a variety of SAP modules (version 2.1) throughout the company. The modules chosen were manufacturing and materials, finance and accounting, and sales and distribution. Access to and control of the SAP system were to be maintained at the corporate IT level. Most plants, including Augusta, did not have IT staff.

With the help of outside consultants, a project team from corporate IT began implementing SAP, starting with the Augusta plant. Because every plant was considered to have different needs, the company implemented a different configuration of SAP at each plant. This included significant customization of the SAP code.

The approach created several unforeseen problems. The high levels of customization required considerable resources. In fact, the team spent the entire implementation budget on just four plants, and the project was then frozen. Shortly thereafter SAP began releasing revisions to version 2.1, some of which allowed SAP to handle the three-dimensional nature of FPC’s items. Unfortunately, having already customized the code to meet the needs of different plants during implementation, FPC felt that installing the upgrades was not feasible.

At this point a new IS vice president was hired. One of his major charges was to supervise ERP implementation. He pointed out the problems in implementing a unique configuration of SAP at each plant and stressed the importance of developing one business ‘‘vision’’ for the entire company prior to moving forward with ERP. In 1996, the Blueprint Team (including the manager of the Augusta plant) was formed to develop this vision. A group of mid- and top-level managers was assigned to this team for 6 months.

The plan assumed that after the company-wide blueprint had been created, SAP 3.1 was to be implemented in all 20 plants in the following 2 years (prior to the year 2000), with the help of a different outside consultant. In contrast to the approach taken during the first round, FPC did not allow its plants to modify SAP code. In fact, to facilitate implementing the software on the tight 2-year timetable, a template-based approach to implementation was used. This approach stressed standardization and allowed for limited configuration to meet individual plants’ needs.

The first implementation occurred at the Augusta plant and was completed in August 1998. After the system was implemented, 20 office staff plus 20 operating managers and associates used the SAP system at Augusta. A shop-floor data collection system called FACE was also implemented companywide at this time. FACE provided a more user friendly interface to upload data to SAP.

## 6.1. The Augusta plant

The Augusta facility produced engineered beams and had four production departments. In the first, logs were received, prepared, and ‘‘unrolled’’ into sheets of veneer. In the next, the veneer was dried and then stored in bundles. In the press department, bundles of veneer were cut into strips, mixed with glue and fed into a press. As material left the press it was cut into

15–50 ft billets. In finishing, these were cut into final dimensions, and these finished beams were bundled and sent into the finished goods storage yard.

There were a number of stock items (standard depth, width and length) but many products were cut to order. Lower volume sawed-to-order material was termed N-STOK. After being cut and bundled in the finishing department, stock and non-standard (N-STOK) products were placed in finished inventory. Off-cuts (leftover sections of billets) and rejects containing some usable material were put into recovery– reclaim inventory for use later. Partially complete orders (partial bundles of identical items) were also stored in recovery–reclaim, since, from this point in the process onward, the organization only tracked complete bundles.

The plant’s customer service department took orders. Orders that could not be fulfilled from finished goods inventory were promised against future capacity. Non-standard orders were sawed to customer specifications (from recovery/reclaim inventory material or new production).

The wide variety of finished goods configurations it produced set the Augusta plant apart from other facilities in the company. In its product line, it was the one plant that handled non-stock lengths. For example, Augusta manufactured all European orders, which was a significant business segment but treated as N-STOK because of the metric sizing.

## 6.2. Benefits from ERP

According to the interviewees, the system resulted in several important benefits in the Augusta plant. SAP kept perpetual inventories of finished goods and of some intermediate materials. These increased the plant’s ability to make customer commitments and to keep them. Additionally, several interviewees stated that SAP boosted accountability and self-discipline within the plant. SAP provided visibility of open production orders and it provided accurate inventories of intermediate materials, such as veneer.

At the company level, SAP improved coordination among plants for fulfilling customer orders. A customer service representative in one plant could view all other plants’ inventories using the SAP system; some plants could enter finished goods orders against another’s inventory. Before SAP, both these activities were conducted via telephone, which was time consuming and error prone. The vision was to centralize customer service and order management.

Three of Augusta’s four production departments were satisfied with SAP. Unlike the finishing department, these three production areas were very straightforward in terms of manufacturing planning and control and they were typical of most operations in FPC. The dryer department manager’s evaluation of SAP was representative of them. He stated that the simplicity of their processes had much to do with their satisfaction:

When you take a log and you peel it, and you make a stack of 4 - 8 veneer, all SAP is doing is tracking: that’s worth 90 cubes, 90 cubic feet. Then when the dryer uses it, through the FACE system, we withdraw that from inventory and it minuses out and it gets added to the next stuff along the line.

Furthermore, SAP eliminated many reporting and data entry chores. For example, prior to the first SAP implementation, the dryer department employees had to calculate the volume of each bundle of veneer removed from green inventory and the volume of veneer entered into dry inventory. This involved measuring bundle height and multiplying by a factor. At 100–200 bundles per shift, this practice resulted in many errors and, ultimately, in poor inventory accuracy. The first SAP implementation eliminated the errors by performing the calculations automatically, and the second implementation went even further by almost eliminating data entry through bar-coding each bundle.

The dryer department manager stated that the latest implementation of SAP was a ‘‘piece of cake,’’ and that, ‘‘Bottom line, it’s saving us an enormous amount of time now not having to do all those hand calculations.’’ However, there were some valuable reports he received under the previous version of SAP that the current implementation eliminated.

## 6.3. Problems from ERP

Many problems stemmed from the poor visibility of information regarding N-STOK orders and recovery– reclaim inventory. The accuracy and understandability of performance measurement and accounting data were also an issues. The problems often resulted in the use of informal systems or ‘‘going without.’’.

## 6.3.1. N-STOK part numbers

One objective of the SAP 3.1 implementation was to enable available to promise calculations. Doing so without adding unacceptable levels of complexity (and IS excessive IS staff) required drastically reducing FPC’s number of stock model numbers. The largest part of the problem came from the many stock model numbers used for non-standard, lower volume products.

Since most plants had little actual volume of nonstandard products, a compromise solution was found. Starting with the SAP 3.1 implementation, FPC instituted configure-to-order or N-STOK part numbers for all low volume, non-standard products. A particular number was used for all non-standard items with a given width and depth, regardless of length. For example, all items that were 3.5 in. wide and 12 in. deep were assigned part number 897 686, regardless of their length. Since N-STOK part numbers do not indicate length, they could not be used as a way to identify exactly what had been ordered on a particular sales order. The only way to determine that was to pull up the individual line item of the sales order and look at the text description. Nor was it clear what ‘‘quantity on hand’’ or ‘‘quantity produced’’ meant for N-STOK items. Thus, a bundle of N-STOK product in the plant could not be fully described by its part number; the only way to identify the configuration of product in such a bundle was to indicate its order number and line number.

For most plants in FPC, this way of handling nonstandard products worked well enough. However, because of its heavy volume of non-standard business, relative to other plants, Augusta experienced problems. For example, to create the schedule, the master scheduler needed to know what orders had been placed and what recovery–reclaim was available for cutting. For any customer order consisting of N-STOK, the SAP standard reports he received indicated the quantity, depth, and width of the material needed, but not the length. Discerning the length required opening up the appropriate line item of the specific sales orders. Thus the master scheduler spent approximately 30 min per day looking up N-STOK requirements in sales orders. N-STOK created the same type of visibility-related problems in the customer service area also.

Using sales order number as part of the identifier meant that identical items were not interchangeable, e.g. when physically assembling orders, the picker was required to locate the distinct package of N-STOK associated with the order being picked. There could be (and often were) numerous identical packages in inventory, but the picker could not know this without accessing the text description on the order; this was a time-consuming task. For many products this insistence on obtaining the exact bundle would be no great inconvenience. However, it greatly complicated operations at the Augusta plant, because the size of the items (often 15–50 ft in length) made moving them difficult. Furthermore because N-STOK was identified by sales order, a bundle of N-STOK material could not easily be moved from one sales order to another. A customer service representative explained:

It’s locked in, all the way down to that package number. You might have 9 packages out there that are 39 foot, the same size and everything, but each one is assigned to a specific order and it has to ship with that order. So, if the customer calls and wants to change his mind or he wants to take that same package and move it to another one of his trucks, that is chaos.

## 6.3.2. Recovery–reclaim inventory

Off-cuts were stored in recovery–reclaim inventory until they could be used on orders. Similarly, partially completed bundles of product were kept in recovery– reclaim until they could be completed. Because of the high variety of configurations it produced, Augusta had some of the ‘‘toughest cuts’’ in the company. This resulted in many leftover billet sections and thus a large recovery–reclaim inventory to manage.

Since FPC’s ERP system was part-number driven, the only way to track the contents of recovery–reclaim inventory would be to assign a part number to every piece that was placed into recovery–reclaim. FPC management did not consider the costs of doing so to be worthwhile because of the sheer variety of lengths of off-cuts and rejects. Therefore, all material placed into recovery–reclaim was inventoried under a single part number and SAP only tracked the total cubic feet in this inventory.

This poor visibility into recovery–reclaim resulted in problems for the plant. To avoid wasting usable material, the master scheduler needed to schedule cuts from material in recovery–reclaim whenever possible; he wanted to keep a perpetual recovery–reclaim inventory in a spreadsheet; however, doing so would require a full-time clerk, which the plant could not afford. Therefore, making the best use of recovery–reclaim was difficult and the master scheduler estimated that working to do so added several hours to his work week.

The finishing department, too, needed to perform manual calculations in order to manage the daily material flow into and out of recovery–reclaim and the associated reporting. This activity required about one man-day per day and the department hired a clerk solely to work on it. The department manager spent 60–90 min per day on such tasks when the initial, customized version of SAP was installed, and approximately 30 min per day using their old, totally customized system before any ERP was installed.

## 6.3.3. Performance reporting

In addition to information visibility issues, there was also a problem with the meaningfulness and accuracy of SAP reports. This manifested itself in a number of different areas and was often addressed by maintaining alternate parallel reporting systems.

For example, in the finishing department, production and cost numbers generated by SAP were not useful to the department manager. The major performance metric for the finishing department manager was recovery (net finished goods production divided by the amount of material received to be sawn into finished goods). However, because of the way the system handled recovery–reclaim, SAP did not provide a meaningful recovery metric that the department manager could use to gauge daily performance. The problem with the system (as implemented at FPC) was that it only considered, as production, those completed bundles placed into finished goods inventory; it did not count material in recovery/reclaim, including partial bundles even though (from the standpoint of measuring the department’s efficiency) they were finished production. As a result, yield was understated some days and overstated on others. Because the SAP daily production report was not managerially meaningful, the finishing department manager and others depended on a manually generated department production report maintained on Excel. One department manager said:

Even though SAP is what the plant’s performance is based on, I don’t really use a lot of the numbers that we’re entering. They’re not easy to get to even though I know my way around the system, and I don’t trust the numbers that it gives me.

Similarly, the senior plant accountant and the plant manager had reservations about the accuracy of SAPgenerated reports. Some problems were a function of the way FPC configured SAP. For example, because an N-STOK part number did not signify length, it was difficult to track the cost per cubic foot produced. This created difficulties valuing certain business segments, such as European sales. Other deficiencies were due to errors in the logic implemented. The plant manager and accountant noted many discrepancies between SAP-based reports and historical averages and also the plant’s manually generated reports. Corporate investigated some of these and found the plant’s internal figures or estimates to be correct.

As a result, the plant manager and his subordinates made many decisions based on the manually generated plant production report. This report drew data from the FACE system, and from various departmental reports, some of which were generated from tally sheets on the plant floor.

## 6.4. Complexity and understanding

Even though the senior plant accountant had years of experience in diverse manufacturing environments, he had difficulty in understanding how costs were calculated and passed on from one department to another in the SAP system. Furthermore, because SAP limited his access to information and because it restricted the format into which this information could be organized, SAP made it more difficult to investigate and resolve concerns.

The senior plant accountant believed that eventually the problems would be fixed (and that reports tailored to the needs of the plant would then be available) but that resources to do so were scarce. However, he noted that, in previous systems, he would have done much of this customization himself. Because of ERPs integration of plants and the associated complexity, he had to rely instead on the corporate SAP team. He stated:

If you tinker with something, you’re tinkering with something that 19 other plants are using, too, and you just can’t do that. Not all the plants are exactly the same, so you don’t know what you’ll screw up somewhere else.

## 7. Discussion of the case

Because many other studies have focused on global impact, we chose to look more closely at local impacts here. Guided by OIPT, we looked for evidence of relationships between interdependence and ERP benefits and also between differentiation and ERP costs. However, because of the focus on the local level, we were better able to surface evidence about differentiation. Additionally, we sought to refine our understanding of differentiation in a concrete business situation. We found evidence supporting the contention of Goodhue et al. that integrating in the presence of differentiation results in either design costs or compromise costs. Furthermore we developed some understanding about the sources of differentiation in manufacturing organizations.

## 8. Differentiation and ERP-related costs

## 8.1. Sources of differentiation among manufacturing plants

As a construct in OIPT, differentiation is often used in general and abstract terms; in order to apply the construct to a specific domain, we suggested frameworks from the manufacturing strategy literature. A key in both these is the level of standardization (or variety) of products produced. We found that standardization did set the Augusta plant apart from others in the company. The plant differed due to the wide variety of non-standard finished products produced. This included custom dimensions (such as those specified by architects) and products for export (sold in low volumes and not conforming to American standards).

As a check on the accuracy of Augusta interviewees reports about their plant, we visited another plant and conducted several interviews. We refer to this plant as Stockton. Stockton and Augusta used the same production technologies and produced the same family of products. Furthermore, both plants underwent both of the company’s SAP implementations (versions 2.1 and 3.1). However, while Augusta manufactured a significant portion of non-standard items, almost 100% of Stockton’s product mix was standard material for residential construction. Stockton management confirmed this; the manufacturing manager contrasted his plant with Augusta by saying, ‘‘This plant is easier to manage with SAP than some of the other plants . . .. At Augusta there are more exceptions and so they have more potential to stub their toe.’’ Experiences with SAP at Stockton were overwhelmingly favorable. Employees did comment on the system’s rigidity and on the cumbersomeness of some of the user interfaces but SAP was clearly not creating problems of the same magnitude as those at Augusta. For example, N-STOK created significant problems at Augusta, whereas Stockton employees described SAP’s handling of N-STOK as ‘‘somewhat clunky’’ but a minor problem. The Stockton Master Scheduler spent less than 30 min per week dealing with N-STOK parts and often no time at all. Furthermore, recovery–reclaim was not a problem, because the plant was able to keep this inventory to almost zero.

For another perspective, we also interviewed four individuals at company headquarters (over the telephone). These quoted numerous metrics that suggest that, overall, the project was a success. For example, capacity utilization went from 60 to 85%, the order fill rate improved, and the end month close cycle was reduced from 20 to 5 days. Interviewees stated that SAP has worked well for most plants, and these metrics seem to confirm it. Furthermore, the interviews indicated that the company followed a sound methodology during the ERP configuration process. Therefore, it would be difficult to attribute Augusta’s difficulties to configuration blunders. For example, the company utilized a high level steering committee; the project general manager was a respected former plant manager and users were heavily involved. In fact, there were many indications that teams went to great lengths to solicit input from all plants when making important decisions. However, there were also instances when a small number of plants would protest a configuration decision but would be overruled.

Interestingly, the impact of custom products was not felt in three of the four departments of the Augusta plant. Operations in these three departments (green, drying, and press) are virtually identical from plant to plant regardless of the degree of customization of the plants’ final products. Only in the finishing department did custom products make Augusta truly different from the other plants in FPC, and there they forced a difference in operations. It is problems that originate in the finishing department that have caused the Augusta plant its greatest difficulty.

## 8.2. Compromise costs

OIPT predicts that ERP will impose costs when differentiation exists among entities in an enterprise, because it increases the likelihood that the ERP will be a poor fit with the operational and informational needs of some local units. At FPC, the finishing department of the Augusta plant differed substantially from its peers. When faced with high levels of differentiation, companies may implement sophisticated ERP configurations in order to attempt to accommodate them. To the extent that these attempts fail or are not made, compromise costs will be incurred. Forest products did not attempt such accommodations with respect to the Augusta plant. Thus the plant experienced compromise costs.

The higher end-product variety at the Augusta plant resulted in the need to make more extensive use of N-STOK part numbers and recovery/reclaim than its peers. When FPC configured SAP to handle N-STOK and recovery/reclaim, their decision-makers assumed that non-standard cuts and non-standard products were rare. This was a valid assumption for most plants but not for Augusta. The organization made a deliberate decision to accept the ‘‘ungraceful’’ manner in which the ERP system handled N-STOK and recovery– reclaim. This trade-off was worthwhile at the other plants, because they seldom had to deal with nonstandard business. But at Augusta this solution led to operational problems for the master scheduler and the finishing manager, as well as reporting problems that extended up to the plant manager level. The Augusta plant responded by utilizing several manual systems, but these were resource intensive and still did not solve the information accuracy problems.

## 8.3. Design costs and the role of resources

In supporting the needs of locally differentiated sub-units, SAP and other ERP systems may well have more flexibility to model a wider variety of business processes and conditions than was used at FPC. This functionality often exists within the configuration options of the ‘‘vanilla’’ ERP. Additionally, systems may be customized, and/or ‘‘bolt-ons’’ may be added to provide desired functionality. However, sufficiently skilled personnel (consultants or employees) and the time needed to analyze problems and potential solutions to difficult local-level problems are extremely costly. Thus even when the technical potential for such accommodations exists, local tailoring or configuring requires substantial design costs—the costs associated with meeting the idiosyncratic needs of a differentiated sub-unit.

In part because of the need to install ERP before Y2K and partially because of management’s implementation philosophy, FPC followed a deliberate strategy of avoiding design costs. The plant manager at FPC’s Augusta plant acknowledged the possibility that the SAP system might be capable of meeting the plant’s needs if it were configured differently. However, FPC’s IT staff concentrated on issues common to the majority of manufacturing facilities, not to the idiosyncratic problems plaguing a single unique plant. Because of a desire to economize on time and personnel costs, FPC decided not to attempt to accommodate the differences of the Augusta plant. For example, one interviewee stated:

Nobody’s going to back you up now because you’re the only plant with a problem. The problems that are getting fixed are problems that are pretty similar throughout all plants. The problems that aren’t getting fixed are the ones that are individual problems, specific to plants.

## 8.4. Unexpected findings: closing the gap versus bridging the gap

A significant gap resulted between the business processes that the plant needed to follow and the business processes supported by the ERP systems, as implemented. This gap needed to be closed somehow. Most of the discussion today focuses on two types of responses to such misfits: (1) change the business process or (2) modify the ERP system. However, the plant we studied did not have the authority or resources to modify the ERP system. Moreover, changing business processes to conform to ERP standards was not viable, because these processes were dictated by relatively immutable factors: the heavy use of recovery/reclaim and N-STOK was determined by the nature of the products that the plant produced and the markets it served.

Because neither modifying the ERP system nor changing business processes were options, the plant could not close the gap. Rather plant employees were forced to bridge the gap instead. Gap-bridging behaviors were based on reliance on alternative IS (Excel, paper logs) and using more labor (longer hours, hiring additional personnel).

## 9. Interdependence and ERP benefits

Following OIPT, we suggested that interdependence would contribute to ERP benefits. Many ERP benefits which exploit interdependence, such as improved centralized decision making, manifest themselves most clearly at the global level. Because our study focused on the plant level, we were not able to discern all corporate-wide benefits.

However, the case manifests one very significant locally visible benefit influenced by interdependence: At FPC, the ERP provided better coordination among plants by allowing customer service representatives to see inventory across multiple plants and then take cooperative action. The ERP thus supported a small degree of reciprocal interdependence between the plants because each plant’s actions could be influenced by their knowledge of conditions at others. However, there was minimal interdependence invol ving the plants.

Our work was well positioned to discern both benefits and costs at the local level, and we saw some laudable local benefits. For example, the ERP improved coordination and discipline between departments within the Augusta plant and it automated some manual tasks. We do not claim that interdependence is the only predictor of all ERP benefits, and FPC’s gains are certainly cases in point.

## 10. Limitations of this study

This paper presents a model that uses interdependence and differentiation as predictors of some important costs and benefits of ERP. Understanding differentiation among sub-units requires the study of sub-units. Thus we focused at this level. Although we did gather some data at the corporate level, we did not concentrate our efforts there. Thus, we are missing many of the benefits (and perhaps some costs) that accrue at the global level, e.g. how is the aggregated information that ERP provides being used for highlevel decision making?.

We made an effort to confirm our observations about the Augusta plant (especially regarding differentiation and the impacts of ERP) by examining a contrasting plant and by interviewing employees at corporate. However, it remains possible that we overestimated the level of differentiation at Augusta or that the problems are widespread throughout the company. One way to eliminate this concern would be to study each plant in the company. The fact that we have not done so is a limitation of our work.

Case studies are useful for their exploratory value, including investigating the applicability of a particular theoretical framework to a specific domain. However, confirming theoretically specified relationships requires confirmatory work.

## 11. Conclusion

As a collection of highly integrated subsystems, ERP systems can be described as ‘‘tightly coupled’’, in contrast to the ‘‘loosely coupled’’ subsystems that make up most of the installed base of pre-ERP systems in organizations. To be sure, tight coupling facilitates coordination among subsystems, and this is typically critically important in today’s competitive business environment. On the other hand, Weick [28] argues that loosely coupled subsystems have potential advantages as well:

 They adapt more easily to unique local requirements.

 They may provide a more sensitive boundary sensing mechanism.

 They provide more room for innovation and adaptation to environmental change by accommodating more variety and novelty.

 A ‘‘breakdown’’ in one subsystem is not automatically transmitted to others.

 Finally, since they interact little with one another, changing them requires fewer resources.

For many organizations, implementing ERP means moving from a confederation of loosely coupled systems to a tightly coupled one. Drawing on OIPT, we have suggested that tightly coupled systems produce significant benefits, including better coordination among sub-units and administrative efficiencies. The more interdependence among sub-units in an organization, the more we expect this type of benefit to accrue. However, we have also argued that in some cases ERP systems will create either compromise or design costs because of the standardization that they require. The severity of these costs increases with differentiation among sub-units. Here, we have provided a description of differentiation in a manufacturing context and a better understanding of some of the costs.

We do not suggest that the case presented here is representative of all ERP experience. Rather, we argue that organizations differ in the amount of interdependence and differentiation among their sub-units, and that these two organizational characteristics merit consideration.

Differentiation does not make it impossible to implement ERP well, but it may make doing so more costly. In most situations, firms will find that the benefits of integration are sufficient to outweigh compromise and/or design costs. Occasionally, this will not be the case and wise firms may opt for looser coupling by choosing to omit a plant or some other sub-unit from an ERP implementation. However, firms are in no position to make such determinations without an understanding of differentiation and its effects in the ERP context.
