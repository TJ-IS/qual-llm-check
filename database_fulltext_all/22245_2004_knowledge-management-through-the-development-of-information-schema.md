# Knowledge management through the development of information schema

Shouhong Wang , Godwin Ariguzo

Department of Marketing/Business Information Systems, Charlton College of Business, University of Massachusetts Dartmouth, North Dartmouth, MA 02747-2300, USA

Received 20 November 2001; received in revised form 9 March 2003; accepted 25 June 2003

## Abstract

Knowledge management (KM) has been receiving considerable attention in the human-systems research community in the past few years. This paper discusses the key concepts of user–computer interaction for knowledge development and proposes a model of an information schema. Such an information repository for KM must be organized into a domain schema. Users of an information repository play an active role in searching through information to coordinate their actions. An example of information schema for KM can be found in a student advising system. This will be used to illustrate the concept of the development of an information schema.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, knowledge management (KM) has become one of the important topics of study in management literature [42]. Several new journals have emerged devoted entirely to its research. In addition, a number of traditional human-system journals have recently published special issues focusing on KM. It is an overarching concept covering a variety of disciplines. It extends into general management [4], information systems development [30], information resource management [23], decision support systems [14,24,26], artificial intelligence [19], and human resource management [27].

Today, KM is viewed as one of the most important topics for academic research and industrial practice. However, in the executive offices and boardrooms, many mangers have yet to appreciate its real value [8]. On the managerial side, organizations are seeking the support of information technology specialists to help foster business process reengineering and electronic commerce through KM. It and business process management are interrelated, and KM itself is a business process [28]. On the scientific research side, artificial intelligence techniques (e.g., [32,34]), developed in the past few decades, need to be woven into the fabric of general practices available to managers. On the technical side, generic KM tools and software available at present have little power beyond traditional information management or groupware systems [29].

Widely used keyword search engines and structured menu-driven user–computer interfaces do not meet the challenges of KM. These approaches often mislead managers into following universal checklists for all situations and discourage them from differentiating one course of action from another, as circumstances may dictate [22]. The mismatch between the expectations of enterprises and the capabilities of information technology becomes the central focus of KM, and thus raises new questions for academic researchers and practitioners.

To support KM effectively, development of its support systems for enterprises is imperative. KM support systems are used by knowledge workers to navigate the information repository of an organization for the effective coordination. This paper proposes a model of KM support system.

## 2. KM and information management

KM has as many definitions as there are writers on the subject; this has caused some confusion about its true nature and functions [20]. In addition there is a wide array of discourses of KM in the IS literature, each one presented within its own context and assumptions [31]. One of the definitions comes from Zeleny [43]. According to him, knowledge is the purposeful coordination of actions. He states that knowledge cannot be separated from the process of knowing. Based on this, one could suppose that individuals construct their own knowledge through experiencing and interacting with the surrounding world. Hence, given the capability of current IT, any item stored in a computer could be classified as data, or information, or knowledge representation, but should not be defined as knowledge. Generally speaking, expert opinions (production rules), data mining results, and decision-making algorithms are highlevel information or knowledge representations but they do not constitute knowledge. These pieces of information are only inputs for human actions, they are not knowledge. In this sense, humans, not computers, possess knowledge.

Due to the power of IT, some information is considered useful for solving human problems and coordinating actions, especially when the actions are structured and simplified. IT may give one an illusion that one can acquire knowledge from computers without doing so or practicing it. The danger of this misperception lies in its over-emphasis on ‘‘knowledge discovery’’, and de-emphasis on the role of user interaction with computer technology in developing knowledge through the performance of ill-structured actions.

Recently efforts have been made to develop new theories of design information systems to support KM (e.g., [6,21]). However, these efforts have not made any distinction. Given this lack of attention, we argue that the basic theories and models of KM ought to be re-examined and developed in such a way that a distinction is made between these two important variables: knowledge and information.

The KM life cycle model (KMLCM) is one of the KM models commonly circulated in the KM field [3,7,9]. According to KMLCM, knowledge goes through six phases: create, organize, formalize, distribute, apply, and evolve. This model tends to confuse information with knowledge. The real problem with KMLCM is not limited to its definition. Its primary limitation is in its limited real world application. Here, people often find that ‘‘knowledge’’ gained from computers does not always work in all situations, particularly when ‘‘knowledge’’ is applied to complicated situations, such as business process reengineering. To reflect this, the KMLCM has been adapted to include a sixth phase called ‘‘knowledge evolution’’. This sixth phase solves the problem of real world application and establishes the solution to how KM can work in a closed loop system. However, since the fundamental concept of knowledge in the KMLCM has been questionable, this ‘‘knowledge evolution’ has never before been recognized in the literature. In fact, the KMLCM is actually a model for the development cycle of a comprehensive information repository, and this has failed to recognize the user’s need for information to develop his/her knowledge.

In extending the KMLCM, Holsapple and Joshi [15–17] provided a conceptual framework for unifying the KM literature. Their descriptive framework articulates the complex interactions among a variety of knowledge related activities. Our study takes it one step further by exploring the interaction between the user and the information depository, probably the most important aspect of KM. It is generally understood that KM has two basic cycles. One is the information repository development cycle and the other is the human knowledge development cycle. The intersection of these two cycles is known as the application phase. The KM model that incorporates the user-centered knowledge development cycle is shown in Fig. 1.

In the user-centered knowledge development cycle, there are four phases:

(1) Learn. Learning is vital for the user’s effective undertaking of actions through KM. To develop knowledge, the user must learn the schema of the information repository, that is, how the comprehensive information repository for knowledge development is organized and formalized on the storage medium. The user must then understand the exact meaning of the retrieved pieces of information for the particular action and the effectiveness of the information repository in coordinating his/her actions.

(2) Apply. To apply knowledge, the user must search the information repository and match the schema of the information repository with his/her schema.

(3) Act. The ultimate objective of KM is to support the actions of the user. An action could be an activity related to decision-making, or it could be an operation sequence. A decision-making action emphasizes what should be done, and an operation sequence focuses on how it can be done.

(4) Enforce and unlearn. To develop knowledge, the user must observe the outcomes of the actions taken after applying the information repository. The observations re-enforce the learning and understanding of the information repository and further develop the KM. Unlearning processes might also be required. Unlearning makes inappropriate information in the repository obsolete.

The information repository development cycle, known as the knowledge engineering cycle, has been widely discussed in the KM literature. In this cycle, the information repository for KM is created, organized, formalized, distributed, applied to knowledge development, and evolved based on the users’ new knowledge.

The point at which the apply phase joins the two cycles, is crucial for understanding knowledge development. Effective knowledge development depends upon the appropriate use of the information repository. This, in turn, depends upon the understanding of the schema of the information repository, namely, the structure of the information repository and the interpretation of the relationships among the individual parts of the repository. The schema is the mutual instrument of the two development cycles. It must be easy to use for all users and knowledge engineers. This paper, therefore, proposes a model of a visual schema of information repositories to support their effective use.

## 3. The schema of information repository for KM

Traditionally, research into the schema has focused primarily on database design, high-level information processing, and natural language processing. There have been many schematic models designed for databases [18] and many models of semantic networks structured for artificial intelligence [33]. However, few have been applied directly to KM.

The ER (entity-relation) model [5] is a simple semantic network model for designing databases. However, it has limitations for the following reasons:

(1) The ER model does not provide for multiple perspectives on the underlying information set. It does not have explicit contractors for aggregation. The schema based on the ER model does not permit information relativity and has little power over navigation of the schema.

(2) The attribute-based approach of the ER model emphasizes structured normalization of data entities. However, information repositories are different from databases, in that they are highly unstructured and include diversified data formats including rules, documents, Web sites, images, etc. Data normalization is not a central issue in an information repository. In many cases, cardinalities between information entities are difficult to determine. Static definition of cardinalities often causes semantic inconsistency in a large information repository used for different application contexts.

To address these limitations, this study extended the ER model to a schematic model for KM by adding an object-oriented feature of inheritance and method aggregation. The schema of the information repository was actually the interface between the user and the information repository system. Based on principles of object-oriented user–computer interaction design ([39,41]), we propose a model of a schema for information repositories. The proposed model of information repository schema would, therefore, have the following two characteristics:

(1) rich semantics to represent the complex structure of the information repository;

(2) a schema that can be easily employed by the user to visualize the structure of the information repository and its navigation.

The scenario of the use of a schema is that the user would initially see the schema at the very top level of the screen, where the information repository is used for knowledge development. The schema, therefore, is a ‘‘road-map’’ for the user to access information. The user is allowed to drill down the schema to a subschema by exploring various parts of it. The model provides a variety of ways of drilling down, including decomposition, hyperlink, menus, and keyword search.

Although the real knowledge structure in the human brain remains by and large an enigma, a general schema of the information repository can be relatively represented in an organization’s knowledge structure. Generally, the schema of an information repository can be a network of connected objects. Each object has certain connections with other objects. Such an interconnected network is a type of semantic network [25]. Historically, they have been used in natural language processing and reasoning. Here, we plan to use semantic networks to construct visual schema of information repositories for the user–computer interface.

## 3.2. Class and object

An object in the information repository has its data and generic methods, such as displaying, as shown in Fig. 3(a). One of the main advantages of the object oriented paradigm is the inheritance structure among the classes. In an inheritance structure of class, objects share common attributes and methods. Constructing an inheritance structure is primarily based on problem context or domain. Using inheritance hierarchies, the information repository user is able to explore the class to find its sub-classes and objects in the information repository. Here, the notation of class is similar to object, except for the unique method of exploring, which translates to exploring the inheritance structure to find its sub-classes or objects (see Fig. 3(b)).

## 3.3. Object categories in information repositories for KM

According to Beckman [2], Alter [1], Tobin [35], and van der Spek and Spijkervet [36], an information repository for KM contains data (facts), information (summarized data and cases), and knowledge representations (procedures, rules, and ideas that guide actions and decisions). However, in the broader literature, there is a lack of formal object-oriented description of schemata of information repository for KM. Furthermore, since the information entities (objects) are semantically linked into a network, the entire semantic network of the information repository is usually large. To provide a large semantic network visual and manageable to the user, the entire semantic network must be partitioned. This is done through categorizing objects and developing the inheritance relationships. A formalized generic class/object category (or meta-classes) can help the organization in developing aspects of its schema, especially when the information repository is incorporated into comprehensive information systems development. Next, we discuss a generic class/object category for schemata of information repositories for KM. The foundation from which we derive this category of object classes is the research on organizational learning [37,40].

## 3.3.1. Goal object

The ultimate objective of KM is to assist the user in reaching a goal. If object-oriented specifications are applied, a set of goals and sub-goals is a class. A goal structure is a type of assembly structure. Since a goal (or sub-goal) is an object, it possesses its own attributes and operations. Attributes of a goal object include: goal states and strategies to achieve the goal. Technically, the strategies of a goal are general statements that can be used as a guideline for the operational procedures for the user to take action or access the information repository itself.

## 3.3.2. Physiomorphic objects

Physiomorphic objects are physically existing entities. Human entities, physical resources, and organizations are examples of such objects. Generally, entities targeted by traditional relational databases are physiomorphic objects in KM.

## 3.3.3. Procedure objects

Knowledge development is the process of doing and leaning. One of the major objectives of KM is to support the user in conducting unstructured actions (or decisions) through decomposition of the unstructured actions into structured procedures, and executing these structured procedures in accordance with the environment and circumstances.

Each structured procedure is formalized by defining the protocols (e.g., when, where, and who) and instructions (e.g., how). The formal descriptions of the procedures represent explicit expertise in the organization, while the selection and combination of the structured actions are at the user’s discretion.

## 3.3.4. Episodic objects

KM is designed toward the support of human cognitive activities. Human knowledge development is often story-based. Therefore, the core competences of knowledge are drawn from episodic events, where an event is represented by an episodic object in the information repository. In fact, case-based reasoning is an approach to problem solving based on the retrieval and adaptation of cases, or episodic descriptions of problems and their associated solutions.

## 3.3.5. Cognizance objects

Cognizance concepts are powerful methods of symbolizing knowledge representations for KM. In artificial intelligence and operation research, there has been a plethora of literature on cognizance concepts, such as production rules, cognitive maps, and semantic networks. These have been used to represent human expertise, rules of thumb, beliefs, and views of the real world. The unit of these knowledge representations is the cognizance object.

## 3.3.6. Instrument objects

An elementary model that can be used for operations, deriving conclusions, testing hypotheses, or discovering abnormal phenomena is called an instrument object, which could be a guideline, a statistical model, an operational research model (mathematical programming, networks, inventory model), a nondefinitional model (reasoning logic, simulation, inference engines), an accounting/financial model (spreadsheet), or a visual model.

## 3.3.7. Note objects

A free-format document other than the objects discussed above is called a note object, which could be a reference document, meeting minutes, or a Web site.

## 3.3.8. Construction of a schema

A schema of information repository for an organization is a synthesis of these seven categories of objects based on the contingency of information sharing in the organization. The first step in the development of a schema is to identify the seven independent categories of object-oriented descriptions (goal, physiomorphic, procedure, episode, cognizance, instrument, and note). The second step is to synthesize these categories of objects and implement the construction.

Although the relationships between the objects could be diversified, there are generic semantics that commonly exist among them; they could be used for general purposes of KM. For instance, in terms of inheritance relationships, a top-level goal ‘‘has\_a’’ low-level goal. Semantically, a goal ‘‘is\_achieved\_through’’ a procedure, and a procedure ‘‘achieves’’ a goal. Using such a schema, tacit knowledge can be modeled as objects. The user conducting an activity that requires tacit knowledge will have to rely on his/ her experience and cognition by filling the implicit attributes and methods. The schema model does not tell how to convert tacit knowledge into objects, but it would help the user to search the classification of tacit knowledge objects by reducing the mental search space.

From the viewpoint of information retrieval using hyperlinks, the schema approach is similar to the widely discussed topic maps [13,38]. However, the proposed schema model has explicit descriptions of goals for action coordination. Compared with topic maps, the semantic network represented by the schema is formalized for a particular domain of problem solving. From the viewpoint of objectoriented modeling for KM, the schema model can be related to the theory of generic design patterns for KM systems [10,12]. Specifically, the proposed schema model can be considered to be an approach to implement the framework of generic design patterns through the identification of meta-classes [11] and their relationships for KM systems.

## 4. A case study: the schema of an information repository for academic advising

Here, we present a prototype of the schema model used for KM in academic advising. Faculty members generally provide advising as an important activity. It is part of an array of services provided to students. However, some have argued that academic advisement tends to siphon off limited time resources from the faculty. One of the serious problems confronting it is that there are a wide variety of rules and regulations and a diversified set of circumstances facing faculty advisors and students. Academic advising requires a considerable level of knowledge by the faculty, and is customarily a challenge for KM experts in higher educational institutions.

Academic advising typically involves a variety of activities. Academic advisors help students select courses that best satisfy their academic requirements, such as prerequisite requirements, general education requirements, distribution requirements, minimum and maximum course loads, graduation and major requirements, other special requirements, and available courses and sections. Advisors also help students find optimal academic paths, as well as helping students select or change a major, counseling them on their career interests, recommending courses/professors by matching the student’s interests and strength, and suggesting to students ways of resolving academic conflicts.

Although faculty member are frequently required to participate in academic advising, ironically, academic advising activities are generally viewed by faculty as unstructured. KM for academic advising then becomes an important issue. As an example, the authors used their institution’s academic advising system as a prototype for the schematic model. This prototype can be viewed as a KM support system for academic advising. It is based on a master manual consisting of the traditional paper documents and some electronic on-line materials (Student Information System) that are shared by all faculty members.

The information repository for advising therefore contains a massive amount of information stored on two media: online and paper. Information related to students, courses, catalog, majors and minors are available on the Student Information System. However, most of the rules and notes for providing advisement for students are provided to the users in the form of off-line paper documents. This prototype of the semantic model constructs pointers to the information sources. It is possible for the academic advisors to obtain the needed object entity by clicking the object icon on the visualized semantic network provided that the object entity is computerized and linked to the icon.

The tool used to build the prototype was Microsoft Visual Basic. Each segment of the semantic network was stored in a form. Fig. 4 shows the top level of the schema. As shown in the semantic network, the goal, procedure, physiomorphic, instrument, cognizance, and notes objects are semantically linked and represent the information repository. Each object icon has a number of methods associated with the command buttons (e.g., Global Search, Local Search, and Explore) that allow the user to navigate the entire semantic network through one entry point. The schema provides a variety of ways for the user to search the information repository, a well as provides for sophisticated users and novice users to meet diversified needs of knowledge development.

From the top-level schema, the user is able to drill down to a sub-schema. For example, the user is able to view the sub-goals, as shown in Fig. 5. Each sub-goal can have relationships with other object types; however, these relationships are often too complicated to display on the same screen. By clicking the Explore command button of a sub-goal, the user is able to further access a sub-schema for this sub-goal with the similar layout.

Fig. 4. The top-level schema of information repository.

A sub-schema is a semantic network in which the user is able to find information for his/her actions. For example, all objects/classes related to Procedure of Register, a required course for Business Information Systems (BIS) majors, are organized in the semantic network as shown in Fig. 6. The outline shows that, the Procedure deals with the Student and Course objects, applies Option Sheet as an instrument, and applies various Rules. The part of the information repository corresponding to this semantic network can be navigated if the user wants to select a course for a student in BIS major.

This schema can help a user coordinate various actions related to academic advising. The primary lessons learned are:

(1) The schema of the information repository for the problem domain provides the associations among the information entities. It is high-level information, formalized through soliciting human knowledge. Hence, it reflects the organizational memory.

(2) The schema provides a map for the user to navigate the information repository so that he/she is able to take appropriate actions for the purpose of providing academic advisement.

(3) To reduce information overload, the large schema is partitioned into parts so that each part can appear on the screen for the user. The principle of partitioning is that each part of the schema keeps a semantic unity.

(4) There might be multiple entries for the user to reach a particular information entity through a search of the entire semantic network. The design of each screen for the segments of the semantic network might be individualized for different types of users. For instance, the layout of display could be adjusted, and short-cuts can be created for sophisticated users to access a specific segment of the schema. Each type of user can see his/her own sub-schema.

(5) Finally, the schema of an information repository can be shared, or even modified, by all authorized users on the Internet.

The schema has been used in a trial. We found that a brief training session was needed for the user to be familiar with the semantic network. Once the user was well versed in the ER diagram style, the schema was much more efficient than the paper-based master manual in advising students.

## 5. Concluding remarks

This paper presents the crucial concepts of KM, and explains that no knowledge can exist without doing it. The competence of KM depends not only on the abundance of information, but includes the effectiveness of the user–computer interaction. It recognizes the problem of lack of schematic models, and proposes a framework of schemata for information repositories. The schema model is based primarily on objectoriented semantic network techniques and the premise of user–computer interaction. The contribution of this study is in the conceptual construction of the information schema model that can be used for actiondriven KM.

![](/api/attachments/QBB9VDJH/fulltext/images/7ca87afa6a4779360aba712e926a73f8bcb6c28551c03fcf95a1f425fb340f38.jpg)  
Fig. 6. A semantic network for the procedure.

An information repository schema does not replace the user’s activities in determining actions; rather it will aid the user to employ the information repository to conduct unstructured actions and develop his/her own knowledge. As a result, the use of the schema would allow the user to utilize information in an efficient manner.

This study has primarily focused on the semantic aspects of information repository for KM, and has shown the way of computer support for KM in the information era. It raises new questions for all parties involved in KM. For enterprises, there is an organizational need to develop a schema that contains semantic information about the repository. The schema of the enterprise is maintainable: the knowledge engineers need to maintain it by deleting components (objects and linkages) for obsolete knowledge and adding new ones for future knowledge. For software developers, new techniques and tools for developing and using schemata for KM are imperative. Simple database management systems and naive search engines are no longer adequate. In our view, the schema proposed here can be used for actual development of KM software. For the users of information repositories, new skills of information literacy are required. They must clearly understand the semantic structure of the organizational information repository, and transform unstructured actions to structured tasks by applying the information repository based on their own a priori knowledge in order to develop their new knowledge.
