# Functional characteristics of commercial web sites: a longitudinal study in Hong Kong

W. Lok Yeung<sup>\*</sup>, Ming-te Lu

Department of Information Systems, Lingnan University, Tuen Mun, Hong Kong, PR China

Received 4 May 2002; received in revised form 23 January 2003; accepted 27 June 2003

## Abstract

A set of quantitative web site attributes and their corresponding metrics are proposed for better understanding of the evolution of functional characteristics of commercial web sites. This set of metrics can facilitate an objective comparison of commercial web sites developed over time and in different regions of the world. Their use would also help studies on functional classification of web sites. The result of a longitudinal study using the proposed metrics is then presented. This involves a stratified systematic sample of 98 Hong Kong-based commercial web sites measured on three separate occasions during a period of 2.5 years. The results show that, while the sampled web sites generally grew larger in content, their functions were only marginally enhanced over time, in contrast to the general impression of fast growing e-commerce activities. Cluster analysis revealed that the sample was dominated by web sites with low information intensity. The results obtained in Hong Kong were compared with those of related studies in the US and Europe.

Keywords: Commercial web sites; Functional characteristics; Longitudinal study; Quantitative attributes; Cluster analysis

## 1. Introduction

Retail sales on the Internet have been increasing drastically in recent years. The US Census Bureau estimated that the total online retail sales in the US for year 2000 had reached US \$28 billion, up from US \$17.3 billion in 1999, and US \$7.7 billion in 1998. Similar increases have been reported in Japan, Europe, and other parts of the world. Yet, it is generally agreed, that Internet commerce is still in its infancy and we have to learn how to successfully generate commercial activities and profits from the Internet with well-constructed web sites. To this end, a comprehensive set of web site attributes in quantitative terms need to be developed to provide a better understanding of the changes in the functional characteristics of commercial web sites over time or in different economies. The set would facilitate studies on functional classification of commercial web sites. Prior studies are either qualitative in nature or lacking in a comprehensive view.

## 1.1. Research aims

Our main aim is to derive a set of functional characteristics, operationalized as quantitative web site attributes and corresponding metrics, from the qualitative functional classifications proposed in prior research. They would provide objective measures which can be used to compensate or complement the qualitative classifications of commercial web sites in order to gain a better understanding of their functional characteristics. This set of metrics may be used to study the changes in the functional characteristics of commercial web sites over time and space. Furthermore, in order to illustrate the use of the metrics developed and to test its validity, we have applied the proposed metrics to a sample of Hong Kong web sites. It is hoped that this study would discover trends and patterns in their evolution during a period of Internet ‘‘gold-rush’’ in Hong Kong before the end of the last century. Results should shed light on whether these web sites had experienced any substantial growth. In addition, a comparison was made with related studies of commercial web sites carried out in the United States and Western Europe. The comparison not only puts our results in the global context, but also helps to highlight certain regional differences in e-commerce development due to differences in their macroenvironments.

## 1.2. Hong Kong economic and IT background

With a population of 6.5 million, the World Bank ranked Hong Kong the 26th largest economy in terms of GNP in 1997, with a per capita GNP of around US \$25,000 (the 13th highest), compared with a per capita GNP of US \$28,740 in the US. When Hong Kong was handed over back to China in July of 1997, the domestic economy was dominated by the service sector, accounting for 85% of the GDP. The rates of change of GDP during the period of this study swung from 5.1% in 1997 to -5.0% in 1998 and then back to 3.4% in 1999 (compared with 4.1–4.4% in the US during the same period). In this period, the Hong Kong economy underwent some upheavals, including the Asian Financial Crisis and a 50% crash in local property prices.

Despite the economic difficulties, Hong Kong responded enthusiastically to the worldwide Internet phenomenon during the same period. By the end of 1997, the number of Internet Service Providers (ISPs) had increased from just one in 1994 to 124. According to Network Wizards (http://www.nw.com), the number of registered hosts in Hong Kong grew faster than anywhere else in the world in 1996 with a 178% rise, while the average growth rate worldwide was just 70% during the same period. Internet traffic volume on the PSTN network grew by 142% to 665 million minutes during 1998 and by 69% to 1.1 billion minutes during 1999. During the period of our study, the Hong Kong Government initiated a number of major developments related to Internet commence, including a public key infrastructure (PKI) backed by legislation for online business transactions.

From government to business community, to stock market investors, Internet-based electronic commerce was tipped as a key to Hong Kong’s move to a new hitech economy. Sentiment towards the new economy was so strong in the stock market that the Hong Kong stock market index reached its all-time high in 2000 before the technology recession of 2001–2002. It would be interesting to see whether commercial web sites in Hong Kong also experienced any growth in functionalities and technical sophistication during the same period as a response to the fast growing Internet user base and governmental initiatives. The study based on the quantitative web site attributes and the metrics developed here should provide a clear picture of the evolution of web sites during this period.

## 2. Research framework

## 2.1. Previous studies

Huizingh [7] conducted an empirical study of commercial web sites in an attempt to determine how firms in different industries use the web and how their web sites were structured. He focused on the content and design of commercial web sites and identified a number of features relating to information, transaction, entertainment, site navigation, etc. His sample included 651 commercial web sites in five industry groups selected from Yahoo and the Dutch Yellow Pages (DYPs). The results revealed differences between web sites of different sizes and between different industry groups, with size being a prominent distinguishing factor.

Many frameworks have been proposed for classifying commercial web sites from different points of view, e.g. [1,10,18,21]. From an integrated marketing point of view, Hoffman et al. [5] proposed a classification of commercial web sites into six distinct functional categories: online storefront, Internet presence, content, mall, incentive and search agent. The first three categories are also referred to as destination sites that compete for customers’ visits, and the remaining categories, called web traffic control sites, direct consumers to various destination sites. Cappel and Myerscough [2] classified the business use of the web, based on their own experience, into marketplace awareness, customer support, sales, advertising, and electronic information services (EISs).

Spiller and Lohse [19] carried out an empirical study of online shopping web sites in an attempt to clarify the different strategies adopted by online shopping web sites. They identified a number of key distinguishing attributes and features of online storefronts based on analogies from three domains: the design and evaluation of paper-based catalogs, shoppers’ store choice behavior [9], and usability principles of computer interfaces [13]. These attributes and features were operationalized into 44 variables which were then measured and coded manually. An overall picture of online women’s apparel stores was provided by descriptive statistics of the variables. Cluster and factor analysis was applied to 35 of the 44 variables, identifying five meaningful groups of online stores: superstores, promotional stores, plain sales stores, one-page stores, and product listings.

The web characterization project (http://wcp.oclc.org) is a longitudinal study of web sites; it conducts an annual web sample to analyze trends in the numbers of web sites of different industries (based on the North American Industry Classification System). However, this project did not examine the functional characteristics of web sites from a commercial point of view.

## 2.2. Functional classifications

The general framework of this research was derived from the various functional classes of commercial web site as proposed by Hoffman, Novak, and Chatterjee and Cappel and Myerscough. Furthermore, these properties were operationalized in terms of quantitative web site attributes following Spiller and Lohse’s approach. Table 1 identifies the functional classes with their properties in terms of the selected quantitative web site attributes. Overlapping classes of the two classifications were placed side-by-side. Note that these properties (or classes) are not mutually exclusive. A web site may possess two or more of the properties and belong to several functional classes simultaneously. The predicates ‘‘is high’’ and ‘‘is low’’ are to be interpreted in the statistical sense and their magnitudes were determined empirically.

While having at least one product for online ordering is sufficient for classifying a web site as an ‘‘online storefront’’ or a ‘‘direct sales’’ site, a ‘‘mall’’ is distinguished by its having many different categories of goods for sale by multiple sellers. In contrast, the web site of a ‘‘virtual company’’ which sells exclusively on the web is indistinguishable from a ‘‘direct sales’’ site, unless we look beyond the web for its other sales channels. ‘‘Indirect sales’’ sites together with ‘‘market awareness’’ sites, which create marketplace awareness for companies and their products, are essentially ‘‘Internet presence’’ sites, which are subdivided into ‘‘flat ad,’’ ‘‘image,’’ and ‘‘information’’ sites. A flat ad site is simply a single-page web site. The distinction between an image site and an information site lies in the information intensity [12,16,22] of the products involved. Information intensity ‘‘pertains to the proportion of an organization’s market offering and/or value chain that is information-based [15].’’ According to Hoffman, Novak, and Chatterjee, an image site is suitable for products with low information intensity, whereas an information site is better suited to products with high information intensity. A content analysis of 250 randomly selected web sites of the United States Fortune 500 listing found that those firms with information-intensive products used significantly more internal hyperlinks and text in their web sites [14]. One interpretation is that an image site uses relatively more images in describing products, whereas an information site contains relatively more detailed rational information about the firm and/or its products expressed in a catalog of web pages. Hence, the average number of internal hyperlinks per web page and the average text size of a web page are used for characterizing image and information sites here.

Typically, a customer support site provides frequently asked questions and technical specifications for products. Technical support has also been found to be significantly more common in web sites for information-intense products. Hence, a customer support site is also identified as an information site in our framework.

Fee-based content sites and subscription-based EIS sites are, essentially, web sites selling downloadable products (e.g. news, music). While both sponsored and searchable database sites involve content sponsorship, the former provides their own content coupled with sponsored advertisements. The latter derives its content from the sponsors (as in a classified advertisement). Sponsored and searchable database sites can also be considered as the advertising sites in the Cappel and Myerscough classification.

The attribute number of external links can be used for identifying ‘‘incentive’’ and ‘‘search agent’’ sites, since they are web traffic control sites. As there is virtually no limit to the number of destination sites to which a search agent, such as Yahoo, may point, it is convenient to stipulate that a search agent has an infinite number of external links. Finally, a broadcast site, like the CNN web site, is best characterized by its prolific video and/or audio content.

## 3. Research design

## 3.1. Sampling

Various sampling methods, ranging from convenience sampling to quota sampling and (stratified) random sampling, have been employed in previous empirical studies on Internet commerce [4]. Our study involved a stratified systematic sample of Hong Kongbased, commercial web sites taken from two major comprehensive online directories of Hong Kong web sites: the Hong Kong World Wide Web (HKWWW) database (http://www.cuhk.hk/hkwww.htm) and the Hong Kong Internet Yellow Pages (HKIYP) (http:// www.great-china.net/yellow/) of May 1997. They were the two main web site directories at the time that the sampling for this research began. Other prominent local directories such as Yahoo Hong Kong went online in 1999.

The HKWWW database and HKIYP listed 1655 and 752 commercial web sites, respectively. Merging the two directories gave 2046 unique commercial web sites. The merged list was then divided into 12 business categories based on the classification schemes of the two original directories (Table 3). Web sites in each category were sorted in alphabetical order and the first of every 10 web sites (i.e. the 1st, 11th, 21st, and so on) were selected for the sample. The web sites were then screened to remove any inaccessible, overseas-based, or non-commercial web sites. After the screening, 156 web sites were left in the sample. This number was further reduced to 98 during data collection for various reasons.

## 3.2. Data collection

A copy of the entire contents of each sampled web site was made by downloading all its web pages using an offline browser. The attributes of the web site (content) were then measured from these pages. The main advantage of creating such a ‘‘snapshot’’ of the sample was that the schedule of measurement and data collection would be flexible and repeatable, without having to worry about the fluidity of online web site content. A snapshot of these sampled web sites was examined once every 15 months beginning June 1997 on three occasions (June 1997, September 1998, and December 1999). However, due to various technical problems (e.g. network timeouts, server errors, etc.), not all sampled web sites were successfully downloaded. Also, a handful of the web sites simply ceased to exist after the first or second downloads. Ultimately, the number of web sites successfully downloaded three times was 98 and the results here are based on these 98 web sites. Apart from the attrition bias, the representativeness of the sample was affected by the fact that the population of commercial web sites in Hong Kong expanded substantially over the study period.

## 4. Analysis of web site attributes

## 4.1. Volumetrics

The size of each sampled web site was measured by its page count. Table 5 shows the page count distribution of the 98 sampled web sites over six size ranges during the study period, together with the distribution for Huizingh’s sample. The proportions of the sampled web sites among the six size ranges did not undergo any significant changes over the study period $( \chi ^ { 2 }$ test, $P = 0 . 9 8 )$ . The ‘‘2–10’’ size range remained the largest throughout the study period. In contrast, Huizingh’s sample had the largest number of web sites in the $" 1 1 - 2 5 "$ size range. Our sampled web sites seem to move away from Huizingh’s 1996 sample in terms of size distribution over time $( \chi ^ { 2 }$ tests, $P = 0 . 2 9 6 ,$ 0.065, 0.0055 for 1997, 1998 and 1999, respectively).

The mean page count of sampled web sites for each industry group is shown in Table 6. The IT group tended to have the largest web sites, on average, whereas the Finance and Insurance and Services groups tended to have the smallest. This is similar to Huizingh’s findings. The table also shows the mean text volume of web sites in kilobytes (KB) in each industry group. All groups showed increases in mean text volume throughout the study period. Wilcoxon Signed Rank test confirmed that the overall increases in text volume were significant (P ¼ 0:000 for 1997– 1998 and P ¼ 0:001 for 1998–1999). Text volumes and page counts of the sampled web sites correlated significantly in all 3 years (Spearman rank correlation coefficients: 0.884, 0.897, 0.883 for 1997, 1998, 1999, respectively). The IT web sites were by far the largest group in terms of both page count and text volume. Notice that the mean page count rose from 66.4 to 80.7 and then dropped slightly to 77.2. Wilcoxon Signed Rank test confirmed that the 1997 page counts were significantly lower than those of 1998 $( P = 0 . 0 3 0 )$ and 1999 $\left( P = 0 . 0 3 4 \right)$ . On the other hand, no significant difference (Wilcoxon Signed Rank test, $P = 0 . 1 0 7 )$ was found between these two later years. It appears that the average size of the web sites had stabilized after 1998.

## 4.3. Sponsorships

A total of 19 (19.4%) of the sampled web sites displayed some sort of advertisements or sponsored content, including banner ads (9 sites), click-through icons (14 sites), advertising sections (5 sites) and classified sections (1 site in 1999 only). Nine sites had only one or two pages, mostly their home pages, with banner ads or click-through icons. None of them provided any search facility specifically for their sponsored content. All five web sites with an advertising section had their sponsored content enlarged over the study period.

We measured sponsorships by counting web pages with sponsored content. Distinction was made between web pages with (add-on) advertisements (e.g. banner ads) and those web pages of sponsored content, i.e. pages whose content was entirely spon sored by a third party. The results, expressed as a percentage of the site’s page count, ranged from 0 to 100%. Table 8 shows the mean values of these percentages among the industry groups. The figures suggest that web pages with advertisements were generally more common than web pages of sponsored content. Also, as far as online advertisements are concerned, the IT and Services groups tended to involve more sponsorships than other groups throughout the study period, and the observed differences were significant (Kruskal–Wallis test, P ¼ 0:038, 0.010, and 0.032 for 1997, 1998, and 1999, respectively).

## 4.4. Linkage

With the help of software tools, we counted the number of internal and external hyperlinks on each sampled web site. The average number of internal links per web page (3.66, 2.83, and 3.74 for 1997, 1998, and 1999, respectively) was much higher than the external linkage figures (0.72, 0.77, and 1.01 for 1997, 1998, and 1999, respectively). Wilcoxon Signed Rank test confirmed that the differences were highly significant (P ¼ 0:000 for 1997–1999). Furthermore, the internal linkage figures correlated significantly with page counts and text volumes throughout the study period, confirming that bigger sites had more complex linkage structures than smaller sites. The linkage results are relevant to the information intensity of the sampled web sites.

## 5.2. Sponsorships-related classes

A few sponsorships-related classes are characterized by a high percentage of pages with advertisements and/or a high percentage of pages of sponsored content. Instead of assigning arbitrary percentage values to these characterizing properties and classifying the sampled web sites accordingly, we applied cluster analysis to the attribute data. Apart from the two sponsorships-related attributes, page count was also included as an attribute in the cluster analysis to account for size differences. The flat ad sites were excluded as extreme cases.

Spiller and Lohse used cluster analysis in a study of 137 retail web sites and classified them into five groups based on 35 web site attributes. Following their approach, attribute data were standardized to an interval of 0–1 and transformed into normalized Euclidean distances. Using Ward’s minimum variance technique, different numbers (2–5) of hierarchical clusters were derived and interpreted, resulting in a four-cluster solution for all 3 years of data. The choice was based on the subjective judgment of the authors as cluster analysis has been regarded as ‘‘. . . much more of an art than a science’’ [3]. The actual calculation was performed with the help of the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software [20]. Table 10 shows the mean attribute values and profiles of the clusters.

Cluster A consistently included the same pair of web sites that belonged to a trading company and a travel agent throughout the study period. They were characterized by relatively high page counts and very high percentages of pages of sponsored content (involving product catalogs and travel information). Cluster B included relatively small sites with relatively high mean percentages of pages with advertisements, but low mean percentages of pages of sponsored content. Hence, we can associate clusters A and B with advertising sites per Cappel and Myerscough and, in particular, cluster B with sponsored content sites according to Hoffman, Novak, and Chatterjee. Although cluster A fulfils the criteria for searchable database sites, we found no search facility within the two web sites of this cluster. Finally, clusters C and D are low in both advertisements and sponsored content and therefore do not represent any sponsorships-related classes.

## 5.3. Linkage-related classes

In a similar way, we applied cluster analysis in an attempt to classify the sampled web sites into image, information, and incentive sites, based on the relevant web site attributes. All sampled web sites, except the flat ad sites, were included in this analysis. The mean, minimum, and maximum attribute values, as well as brief profiles of the computed clusters for the three sample years, are given in Appendix A. Although the clusters exhibited somewhat diverse profiles in different years, the following observations were made:

(A) Each year, a majority (57–70%) of web sites (clusters D, C, and B in 1997, 1998, and 1999, respectively) could be identified as low in both textual content and the number of internal linkage, i.e. what Hoffman, Novak, and Chatterjee called image sites. As the number of internal linkages correlate with page count and text volume, the image sites were also relatively small.

(B) There were clusters identified as high in either textual content, or number of internal linkages, but not both. Hence, there was no recognized group of information sites in the sample. In fact, none of the sampled web sites provided any substantial online customer support, as associated with information sites.

(C) Small clusters of web sites with a high number of external linkages were identified in the 1997 (cluster E) and 1999 (cluster E) data. These sites can be regarded as incentive sites and they included two IT suppliers, an ISP, and a travel agent, all of which displayed some sort of advertisements or sponsored content.

## 5.4. Unidentified classes

The following classes were not identified within the sampled web sites:

 Fee-based content,

 Subscription-based EIS,

 Broadcast,

 Search agent.

Since no sampled web sites sell any downloadable products, the first two classes do not apply here. Although in each year there were 10 sampled sites with audio/video clips, the number of clips per site were rather small (1–10). In fact, our sample did not include any News & Media web site.

## 6. Summary and discussion

We have derived a comprehensive set of quantitative web site attributes and the corresponding metrics which can provide an objective measure of the functional characteristics of commercial web sites. Using the proposed metrics, one can gain a better understanding of the evolutional changes of the functional characteristics of these web sites due to technological advancement and economic influences. The metrics are also useful for the study of functional classification of web sites.

The use of the proposed metrics was demonstrated by a study of 98 Hong Kong commercial web sites grouped under four industry headings over a period of 31 months. It was found that in terms of web page count, the sampled web sites grew significantly larger from 1997 to 1998, but stabilized after that. Advocates of a limitation on the number of web pages associated with a web site have suggested that customer attention [8] and the webmaster bottleneck [17] are limiting factors. More pages could mean more time and effort from customers to find what they want, and the lack of search facilities hampers goal-directed shopping [11]. There may be evidence to support this in practice. On the other hand, the mean text volumes (KB) of the sample increased substantially overall (61–75%) and within each industry group (16–141%) throughout the study period. It is important to point out that text volumes were measured at the source level—a web page source typically consists of text for display as well as HTML tags for page formatting. Our text volume results were somewhat inflated by the widespread use of advanced web page editors, such as Microsoft FrontPage, with increasingly versatile formatting features.

Only about 10% of the sampled web sites had audio/ video features and the usage was very limited, with no more than 10 audio/video clips per site. This tended to support the notion that rich media consumes scarce consumer attention, leading to fewer purchase decisions [23]. The long download time associated with video has often been regarded as undesirable for commercial web sites [6]. This was not helped by the fact that broadband Internet connections were still rare and mainly restricted to corporate users in Hong Kong in 1999. Nevertheless, the situation has rapidly been improving as the degree of home Internet users in Hong Kong with broadband connection climbed to 40% in 2001.

## 6.1. Comparison with related studies in the US and Europe

Huizingh selected his sample from the Yahoo and DYPs directories that can be regarded as representative of companies in the US and the Netherlands, respectively. Palmer and Griffith studied 250 web sites of the US Fortune 500 companies in 1997. Spiller and Lohse selected their convenience sample of 137 online apparel stores in 1996 from Internet search engines such as Yahoo, Alta Vista, and Infoseek. It is useful to draw comparisons between our results and those of these studies.

Compared with Huizingh’s 1996 sample, the mean page counts of our sampled web sites (66.38, 80.71, 77.15 for 1997, 1998, 1999, respectively) are very much higher than those of his sample (3.3 and 3.2 for Yahoo and DYP, respectively). The size distributions revealed that large web sites with over 100 pages occupied higher percentages (13.3, 17.4, 19.4 for 1997, 1998, 1999, respectively) in our sample than in his (8.8%). The higher and increasing percentages of large Hong Kong web sites may simply reflect a worldwide trend since 1996. On the other hand, we obtained some results similar to Huizingh in the analysis of web sites by industry groups: the IT group tended to have the largest web sites, on average, whereas the Finance and Insurance and Services groups tended to have the smallest. In addition, 35.3% of Huizingh’s sample offered sales ordering features, compared with only 11.2–12.2% of our sample.

Unlike Spiller and Lohse’s sample which had been screened for having more than five items of any kind of clothing, only a dozen web sites in our sample provided some sort of sales ordering features. 62% of their sample, compared with 42% of our dozen of online stores, had fewer than 50 products for sale. Spiller and Lohse found the largest store, Shoppers Advantage, had nearly 250,000 items for sale whereas a travel agent was the top in our sample with over 3500 products. 59% of their sample accepted credit cards for payment, compared with 4 out of the 12 online stores in our study.

Palmer and Griffith advocate the importance of media richness for the web sites of informationintensive firms but the percentages of their sampled web sites with audio (8.7%) and video (7.3%) contents were similar to ours.

## 7. Conclusions

Our research developed a comprehensive set of quantitative web site attributes and corresponding metrics for a better understanding of the evolution of functional characteristics of commercial web sites. The set of metrics were then used in a longitudinal study of commercial web sites in Hong Kong to illustrate its use and to test its validity. Association of the set of metrics and functional classification of web sites proposed in prior research was also elaborated.

The longitudinal study began in June 1997 and was strategically planned to end before the turn of the century in anticipation of the Y2K problem. During this period, Hong Kong experienced the handover to the Peoples’ Republic of China (PRC) and the Asian Financial Crisis. The same period also saw the ‘‘dot-com fever’’ and the rapid expansion of Internet infrastructure and user population in Hong Kong. Yet, the sampled web sites of this study showed only marginal increases in their commercial provisions, and lagged behind other studies in the area of online sales.

On the other hand, our study served as an empirical test of the two functional classifications of commercial web sites on which the research framework was based. Not all classes could be identified from our sample and web traffic control sites were clearly in the minority.

Attrition bias was a major limitation of our research. The loss of all web sites in the News and Media category from the original sample not only led to the absence of broadcast sites in the sample, but also affected its overall representativeness. This was also affected by the fact that many high-profile ‘‘virtual companies’’ and numerous ‘‘late-comers’’ launched their web sites after the start of this study. Hence, the population of this sample is better regarded as early adopters of Internet commerce in Hong Kong.
