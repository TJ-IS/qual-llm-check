# Centralization as a design consideration for the management of call centers

Marco Adria<sup>a,\*</sup>, Shamsud D. Chowdhury<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Faculty of Extension, 210-C University Extension Centre, University of Alberta, Edmonton, Alta., Canada T6G 2T4 <sup>b</sup>School of Business Administration, Dalhousie University, 6152 Coburg Road, Halifax, NS, Canada B3H 3J5

Received 18 July 2002; received in revised form 13 January 2003; accepted 13 June 2003

## Abstract

A call center and its associated information technology (IT) provide an opportunity to redesign and improve service-delivery operations. Managers at all levels should understand the role of organizational design as call centers are established or expanded, in particular the relative centralization (distribution of authority) associated with delivering services to customers. This article argues that centralization moderates and influences the organization’s efforts to improve customer service through the implementation of the call center and its IT. If managers fail to capitalize on the particular way that centralization moderates between IT and competitive strategy, the organization may not enjoy an important benefit of the call center, which is competitive advantage through increased efficiency and improved customer service. Based on survey responses from 68 call-center managers, the authors found that both centralization and decentralization are associated with call-center service operations. While the call center provides managers with the ability to influence decision-making (centralization), there are also opportunities for agents in the call center to exercise authority in managing the organization’s communications with customers (decentralization). Implications for organizational practice are considered.

Keywords: Call centers; Centralization; Decentralization; Organizational design; Customer service

## 1. Introduction

Productivity gains, much of them driven by information technology (IT), have become what Smothers refers to as one of the ‘‘creative’’ forces of global competitiveness [27]. Although the relationship between IT and productivity is not entirely clear [9,16,19], better productivity through the appropriate implementation of IT can result in more competitive cost structures and the ability to offer more competitive prices, products, and services. An organization’s immediate ability to pursue the strategy of low cost or differentiation depends on its capacity to increase both the efficiency of its operations and its responsiveness to customers. For an example of how IT-driven pro ductivity can contribute to an organization’s competitive position, consider knowledge-leveraging. Knowledge-leveraging is facilitated by IT and involves sharing and integrating cross-functional expertise [30]. Knowledge-leveraging can take place at any level of the organization and can lead to efficiency. Efficiency can lead, in turn, to cost effectiveness.

Furthermore, knowledge-leveraging can enhance the organization’s responsiveness to its customers. Because the delivery of value-added services, or products, can be the basis for service differentiation, knowledge-leveraging can be seen to contribute directly to such differentiation. It is not surprising, then, that customer service has become so important in organizations that ‘‘[c]ompetition is shifting away from how companies build their products to how well they serve customers before and after they build them’’ [8]. Consistent with this shift, some writers emphasize the need for managers to create a customerservice culture throughout the entire organization [2] and to strive to create what Lucas has called the ‘‘Tform organization’’, which is the ‘‘flat and fast’’, technology-based organization [18].

In this article, we focus on call centers as a means of improving an organization’s customer service. Specifically, we examine the relationship between a call center and the two organizational outcomes of efficiency and customer service, especially in connection to the organizational design variable of centralization. There are several definitions of organizational centralization, but we use centralization to mean the distribution of authority in an organization [13]. The greater the proportion of authority assumed by higher levels of the organization, the greater the degree of centralization.

A call center typically replaces a more informal approach to service activities in an organization. Centralization is a particularly important organizational design variable, because the relative centralization of some service activities is likely to change after a call center is introduced into an organization. The imperative of customer service can become lost in the technical and organizational flurry of establishing a call center. Our goals in this article are therefore to accomplish the following:

1. describe some of the ways that call centers are being deployed in North American business and government organizations;

2. discuss how call centers can potentially improve an organization’s competitive strategy, particularly by strengthening customer service;

3. explore centralization as a variable that moderates between the call center and the organization’s competitive position.

The call center will influence the efficiency and customer service of an organization, both of which should be regarded as outcome, or dependent, variables. The organizational design (OD) variables of specialization, formalization, and centralization help to translate the input of the call center into the outputs of efficiency and customer service.<sup>1</sup> Thought of in another way, the organization’s specialization, formalization, and centralization help to explain why the effects of IT are not universally positive. IT is effective only when it is implemented appropriately through the right kind of organizational design. Executives and senior managers increasingly confront issues surrounding how to ensure an appropriate organizational design. They are required to manage a virtual, serviceoriented, distributed workplace that has the capability to deliver improved productivity and enhanced customer service. Fig. 1 provides a schematic of the relationships, as we see them, between the call center, organizational design, and competitive position.

Specialization, formalization, and centralization moderate the relationship between the call center and the organization’s competitive position. Our scheme is different from that of Huber [17], who treats several OD variables as dependent variables, with IT as the independent variable, or from that of Dewett and Jones [15], who consider IT as a moderator of the relationship between OD variables and organizational outcomes.

We argue that centralization should be a significant consideration for those designing, implementing, and maintaining call-center operations and that there is evidence to show that there is movement both towards and away from a more intensively centralized service and support operation. Call centers represent one part of an increasingly bureaucratized IT environment, and there is a shift to move decision-making upwards in the organizational hierarchy. However, we argue that decentralization, too, is associated with the call center’s activities. While our findings do not demonstrate how a call center changes a particular organization’s structure, they do provide a compelling portrait of how centralization may be approached as a design variable by managers who are either establishing or managing call centers.

## 2. The depth and scope of call-center operations

The call center provides telephone access to information services that are delivered by a human operator (agent), who in turn has access to an information resource (database). Typically, a call center allows a customer to call a toll-free number and to receive information with the assistance of an agent. A call center may have one agent or many hundreds of agents. Agents providing information to customers may rely on a simple printed script or a comprehensive electronic database. The call center’s dedicated combination of an agent with a database allows the organization to increase the reliability of the information services that it provides to customers and to assist in better managing the relationship between organization and client.

While a call center may use printed or other nonelectronic information sources, most employ an information system that is integrated with other corporate information sources, an arrangement known as an intranet. The corporate intranet is a communication network internal to the organization, by which information is made widely accessible within the organization. Information is disseminated through the intranet, thereby improving communication among employees [29]. The intranet connects ‘‘all employees through common, hyperlinked interfaces to documents, messages, and multimedia information sources’’ [4]. Electronic forms can be created, circulated, and filed in integrated databases that encompass newsletters, human resources information, calendars, product inventories, recruitment updates, and so on, thereby developing a dynamic information base accessible to many people in the organization. Increasingly, customers can be given access to the corporate intranet as well so that they can find information on their own. Intranets also enhance corporate efficiency and organizational learning. At DHL Systems, for example, an intranet was established in part to facilitate communication among 20,000 employees about corporate activities, thus supporting organizational learning [21]. At the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, MN, physicians post links on the clinic’s intranet to web sites that provide up-to-date and authoritative information about current medical treatments for use by clinic practitioners [11].

Call centers are expanding rapidly in scope, number, and size in the US and Canada. A 1998 study estimated that there were 6500 call centers in Canada, employing 330,000 Canadians, with estimated annual operating expenditures of \$15 billion [22]. Call centers are associated with generating or facilitating some \$50 billion in sales each year in Canada [7]. In the US, in which toll-free telephone services have been more widely and intensively adopted, the teleservices industry is estimated to be worth \$90 billion per year [5].

Organizations of all sizes are establishing call centers, from very small to very large. There are estimated to be between 1.7 million and 2 million agent workstations in the US alone [5]. However, larger organizations are more likely to establish call centers than are small organizations. A US survey of the industry found that the average number of people employed by a world-wide organization that has a call center is less than 1000 in about half the cases [10]. The average size of an organization with at least one call center is about 15,000. Larger organizations with multiple locations were more likely to establish call centers than are smaller organizations with one location, reflecting the capacity of the call center to coordinate the delivery of customer service across multiple sites [10]. The average number of agents working in a call center is about 660. About 45% of survey respondents said their call center had fewer than 25 agents on staff [10].

Many people have the impression that call centers operate mainly to complete ‘‘outbound’’ sales calls. Many of us have become familiar, especially at dinner time, with telemarketing agents working in outbound call centers. Yet a recent study found that a call center was likely to be involved in several activities at once, most of them involving inbound calls, such as billing inquiries and new account applications. About 86% of call centers in the US are involved with ‘‘inbound customer service’’, while only 25% of them are involved in ‘‘outbound’’ sales [10].

Call centers can increase the quality of information provided to customers and the process by which it is delivered through the concept of customer relationship management (CRM). CRM is a technique for managing the organization’s communications with customers by providing agents with access to rich information about customers. IT can be used to streamline the flow of this information to the customer [1,6,12]. Some of the services provided through call centers are professional, some are technical.

An example of a professional services call center is that of ChaseMellon Shareholder Services, a joint venture between Chase Manhattan and Mellon Bank. It provides stock transfer and other shareholder services to publicly held companies [28]. The joint company has become the largest shareholder services company in the US, serving some 2000 corporate clients and some 15 million shareholder clients. The company has two call centers, both of them located in New Jersey. The call centers operate from the beginning of the workday until early evening on weekdays. Agents in the ChaseMellon call centers help customers to accomplish such tasks as carrying out stock transactions, determining account balances, obtaining proceeds from stock sales, and even arranging for payment of dividends. The intensive use of the call center means that ChaseMellon is relying on the people, organizational structure, and technology of the call center to improve its service to customers.

An example of a call center providing technical services is the call center for Volvo customers requiring vehicle parts and other services, called Action Service [20]. Action Service supports Volvo’s trucks, buses, boats, and construction equipment. It helps customers order parts, but it also delivers technical support to customers and arranges for repairs. The company has established two call centers in Europe (both of which are in England) and two in North America (one in Mississauga, Ont., and the other in Greensboro, NC). Agents in the call centers are divided according to the need to assist customers who want to order parts, those who require technical support, and those who need emergency support to be dispatched. The agents are knowledgeable about many aspects of Volvo’s products and operation and are relied upon by customers to provide a customized, on-time response to their calls.

These examples suggest that the call-center concept is being used not only to increase the reliability and currency of information services provided to customers but also to help to manage the relationship between the organization and the client. Customers who are provided with relevant and accurate information when they require it are more likely to continue their relationship with the organization. Furthermore, there is a transition to more professionally oriented information services. Agents in the ChaseMellon call center, for example, are providing more than simple technical information to customers. They are providing professional services that customers rely upon in making important financial decisions.

## 3. Centralization and decentralization in call centers

IT occupations have always been ‘‘intimately coupled with large, corporate or state organizations’’ [23]. While early IT work provided for loosely defined tasks and outcomes, IT work has become increasingly circumscribed and directed towards organizational goals. Within this context of increasing control, the focus here is on centralization. Centralization concerns who will make decisions about customer service at the time that service is being delivered. A call center is concerned significantly with authority distribution because it moves the development of the corporate knowledge base from, in most instances, multiple locations in the organization towards a single location within the organization. Call centers are concerned in large part with automating and routinizing communications between agent and client, and centralization would be predicted to be associated with this automation. Yet the provision of customer service and support is often directed towards some kind of decentralization, because it relies on the capacity of workers to exercise some discretion and autonomy [9].

Studies of the degree to which the implementation of IT in organizations has been associated with centralization have varied in their conclusions. In the 1960s and 1970s, researchers looked at whether computers ‘‘caused’’ either centralization or decentralization. An early study, for example, predicted a reduction in the number of middle managers in organizations that computerized and, as a result, an increase in centralization [31]. This finding was contradicted not long afterwards [3]. Another researcher suggested that computers do not actually cause changes in organizational structure [26]. Instead, because of their flexibility, they may facilitate either centralization or decentralization. More recently, two researchers have concluded that the effects of IT on the ranks of middle management depended on where the technology was located, who controlled it, the existing extent of centralization or decentralization, and the roles of middle managers themselves. They emphasized that it was essential that ‘‘top managers become clear about the values they seek, that they convey these values when making major computing investments, and that they look to see whether these values are achieved’’ [24]. They argued that organizations adopting IT have available to them strategic options (having to do with such questions as centralization) that are not available to other organizations.

In the rest of this section, we report on the degree of centralization that can be expected to be associated with a call center and, by implication, the degree to which the capacity of the call center exists to assist in delivering excellent customer service and support. Respondents to a survey of managers who belong to a Canadian call-center industry association were asked about the extent to which agents have discretion and autonomy in serving customers. The responses suggested agents do not consider themselves to be selfcontained ‘‘islands of expertise’’ and that managers did not view them that way. Instead, agents commonly consulted one another on what the appropriate responses to customers should be. Furthermore, when faced with a nonroutine inquiry to which their databases did not appear to present a solution, agents attempted to resolve the inquiry immediately and to follow up with searches of the database afterwards. These consultations took place in spite of the many IT and OD elements intended to automate the communications between agent and client.

We surveyed 535 members of a Canadian callcenter industry association in June 2001. Appendix A provides details of the survey sample.

In the survey, we asked managers questions about how agents in the call center responded to inquiries from customers. To analyze the responses, we used the dimensions of standardization and differentiation. Examining these dimensions of the call-center’s organization provide a useful indication of centralization in the context of service delivery. They specify the distribution of authority based on the degree to which decision-making is either loosely coupled or tightly coupled with management influence [25].

Standardization refers to the degree to which information provided to customers through the call center may be regarded as consistent and uniformly structured. Differentiation refers to the extent to which agents exercise autonomy in customizing, personalizing, or departing from the routines associated with call-center protocols.<sup>2</sup>

IT use in call centers is in part intended to increase the reliability of information provided to customers. Some of the actions in the call center will increase the standardization of information provided to customers. Such actions will improve the likelihood that information is accurate, up-to-date, and endorsed by managers. A ‘‘mass production’’ approach to providing information through a call center would be associated with a centralized organizational structure [32]. Standardization of organizational procedures and technological automation is therefore an indicator of centralization. Standardized approaches to servicedelivery are centralized approaches, in that they rely on authority vested at or near the top of the organizational hierarchy.

On the other hand, some things that agents do will increase the differentiation of responses that agents provide to customers. Customized responses to customers help to provide a personal touch to interactions. Differentiation of responses will help to distinguish the organization’s services from those of other organizations. The resulting differentiation can be exercised whenever the agent receives an inquiry that is in some way unique and that is unavailable in the databases, call-center practices, or the general knowledge of the agent. Differentiation of customers will be associated with decentralization [14]. Differentiation is encouraged through drawing on the skills and judgement of the agents, rather than on routines, and it is therefore an indicator of decentralization. Differentiation suggests that automation and routines cannot provide agents with the full scale of responses they need in dealing with customers. Differentiation refers here to agents’ authority to deliver customized and variable responses to customers.

The first four questions on the survey asked managers about the relative autonomy agents enjoyed in their communications with customers. See Appendix B for a list of questions and responses. Agents who conferred with one another informally would be increasing differentiation. By departing from scripts and from authoritative information provided in databases, these agents would be increasing decentralization, because decisions would be made at the point of client inquiry, a step removed from distribution of authority at the level of the manager. The higher level of differentiation would involve group decision-making and consultation. Specifically, managers were asked whether agents

(1) asked for assistance from someone else in the call center when serving customers;

(2) were encouraged to ask for help when dealing with escalated calls;

(3) helped one another when serving ‘‘problem’’ customers;

(4) were encouraged to ask one another for help in serving ‘‘problem’’ customers.

A large majority of respondents chose ‘‘Yes’’ in response to all four of these questions, suggesting a preference for differentiation and an association, in turn, of the call center with decentralization. Agents discussed inquiries among themselves at least once a week in 88% of the cases. Only 10% responded that this occurred ‘‘hardly ever’’. This pattern of responses applied to customers that were easily served as well as those identified as ‘‘problem’’ customers. Not only were agents observed to be informally consulting with one another regarding how best to respond to customers, but an overwhelming percentage of managers encouraged them to do so. This suggests that differentiation of responses is something that both agents and managers saw as a desirable method for improving service delivery. It suggests a decentralized approach to service delivery, because it locates some significant decisions about service delivery at the point of contact between agent and client.

While the first four questions asked about the extent to which agents might be increasing decentralization in the organization by increasing the differentiation of their responses to customers, the next three questions asked about increases in standardization and, by extension, increases in centralization. Training for agents and the use of databases can be viewed as two important means by which agents are encouraged to increase standardization of information provided to customers. Training is a means by which a common understanding of procedures and processes in the call center can be achieved. Common responses to inquiries can be expected to follow. The use of databases represents a common source of information for agents, so it, too, is a contributor to increased standardization. Managers were therefore asked

(5) whether agents were provided with formal training for their work;

(6) the source of the training provided;

(7) whether agents used databases in responding to customers’ inquiries.

With respect to the two indications of increased standardization—formal training including its source and the use of databases—the responses were overwhelmingly positive and consistent, suggesting a concern for standardization and a corresponding association with centralization.

Next, we were interested to know how agents would be expected to respond to customers when it was not clear what information should be provided. An example would be an instance in which there would be a difference between information held in the call-center databases and the information or directions that had been provided by managers (verbally, for example, or through a written memo). We wanted to know how agents were asked to respond to customers in ambiguous situations of this kind, because in deciding on a course of action the agent would contribute to an increase in either differentiation or standardization. We asked managers in cases of ambiguity whether agents would be asked to

(8) resolve an inquiry immediately or take some other action.

A large majority of agents were reported to resolve the inquiry immediately, thereby suggesting a concern for differentiation. A disproportionately higher emphasis on resolving an inquiry immediately, as opposed to reporting it for clarification, indicated that the agent maintained the authority to make a significant decision regarding service delivery.

Finally, we wanted to know about the extent to which agents’ time and activities were monitored or tracked by managers. High levels of tracking by management would indicate a concern to increase standardization. Managers were asked

(9) whether agents’ time spent handling different kinds of inquiries could be tracked;

(10) which kinds of activities were linked to service standards;

(11) whether agents’ service levels could be monitored;

(12) whether management reports could be run on call-center activities.

Respondents indicated that in connection with all four of these areas there was a high level of tracking and monitoring of service levels taking place, suggesting a strong concern for standardization and, in turn, an association with centralization.

In summary, managers reporting on the operation of their call centers described service delivery characterized by both centralization and decentralization. The distribution of authority was directed to the front line of service delivery in the call center, as indicated by the autonomy agents experienced in deciding how to respond to customers in certain situations. Agents often asked for assistance from others, especially when customers’ inquiries were difficult to resolve. Agents also had the authority to resolve inquiries immediately with customers and to follow up on refining future responses afterwards. However, managers’ own authority was evident in several ways: training provided to agents, databases used to organize information for delivery to customers, efforts to set and measure service standards, and run management reports on agent activities. These techniques are likely to contribute to a common, centralized approach to decision-making.

## 4. Recommendations for putting the call center to work

We have suggested that the call center’s contribution to the competitive position of the organization is not a direct one. It is mediated by the varying extent to which decision-making authority regarding service provision is distributed to agents at the front line.

As the call center is established or expanded in service operations, centralization is a key organizational design consideration. The call center reflects both a technological innovation and a pattern of organizational structure; as such, it provides a means by which managers and executives may learn from the experience of other organizations.

Senior managers and executives who are responsible for establishing or managing call centers should consider the following guidelines:

1. A decentralized approach to decision-making and service delivery can be associated with the call center. Such an approach can and should be encouraged as a means of increasing efficiency and customer service. Customers will benefit by richer service that is delivered on-time if the organizational design recognizes the high-contact nature of service operations jobs. Assume that front-line staff will now make many decisions and judgements that might previously have been made centrally. Our study suggests that there is agreement between call-center managers and agents that informal consultations on service delivery issues is appropriate and desirable. Ensure that the call center will be supported as a decentralized unit and that top managers are familiar with procedures for monitoring and coaching front-line staff as needed.

2. Combining centralized control with decentralized decision-making is the ideal organizational design option in a service operation, and a call center can make that option feasible. The call center offers many opportunities for increasing the reliability of information provided to customers. This can be accomplished through automating and routinizing processes and practices, with the aid of IT. The call center should be regarded as an occasion to examine and, if appropriate, to redesign the organization’s knowledge base and the methods by which information to customers. A common approach to this issue can lead to increased efficiency, service differentiation, and, in the end, competitive advantage.

3. Finally, a call-center operation, like any IT application, must be part of a well-crafted organizational strategy that gives attention to people as well as information systems. ‘‘Reconfiguring’’ the organization can be a useful and effective way of conceiving of the changes needed to achieve harmony between technology and structure. Training for staff using the new technologies, as well as time for implementation, is crucial. People who know more about IT tend to view it positively—and thus to use it more effectively and more often— than people with less knowledge. As managers realize, service is not simply an IT problem. Advanced service technologies of the kind described here should be designed around business goals and must take account of how people at the point of contact with customers actually do their work. For that reason, all operational areas of the organization should be consulted as call centers are implemented, expanded, or developed. Senior managers should have a strong voice in developing dynamic knowledge bases, since these will influence customers’ primary connection to the organization and can contribute significantly to improvements in customer service, and thus, to the organization’s competitive advantage.

## Appendix A. Survey sample

Survey questionnaires were sent to 535 members of the Alberta Call Centre Association. About 60% of the membership actually operate call centers. Of those, there were some organizations with multiple memberships. With 68 completed surveys, we estimate that the response rate was 30% of the potential number of respondents.

The size of the organizations represented by respondents was smaller than the average organization in the US using one or more call centers of 15,000. About 43% of respondents said there were between 250 and 4999 employees in their organizations, while approximately 31% said that their organization was smaller than 250. The largest industry category chosen by respondents for their organization was ‘‘government, public administration, or military’’, which was chosen by 21% of respondents. The next largest category, that of ‘‘wholesaler, distributor, or retailer’’, was chosen by about 15% of respondents. About 13% of the managers surveyed said their organization was in ‘‘computer, data processing, or communications services’’, which was also the percentage of those choosing ‘‘other’’. The size of the call centers surveyed was, like the size of the organization, smaller than that surveyed in the US of 660 agents. About 57% of respondents said their organizations had between 10 and 99 agents. About 16% said ‘‘100–249’’, while 4% were in the category of ‘‘500 and up’’.
