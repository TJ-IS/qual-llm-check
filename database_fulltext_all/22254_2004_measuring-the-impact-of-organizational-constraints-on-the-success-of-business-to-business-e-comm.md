# Measuring the impact of organizational constraints on the success of business-to-business e-commerce efforts: a transactional focus

Frederick Kaefer<sup>a,\*</sup>, Elliot Bendoly<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Information Systems and Operations Management, Loyola University Chicago, 820 North Michigan Avenue, Chicago, IL 60611, USA <sup>b</sup>Decision and Information Analysis, Goizueta School of Business, Emory University, 1300 Clifton Road, Atlanta, GA 30322, USA

Received 27 March 2002; received in revised form 12 January 2003; accepted 28 June 2003

## Abstract

This study investigated the impact of two organizational constraints, technological compatibility and operational capacity, on the success of business-to-business (B2B) electronic commerce (e-commerce) efforts over a range of business settings. We focused specifically on the transactional efficiencies gained through the use of B2B e-commerce. To accomplish this, we considered an instrument originally developed to measure the benefits of intra-organizational information systems and applied it in two inter-organizational contexts: electronic data interchange (EDI) usage, and B2B technology usage by firms without EDI. Analysis was carried out on 86 firms in the consumer electronics industry, approximately half of which were product manufacturing firms and the other half service-providing firms. Our findings showed that the inter-organizational context had a significant bearing on which constraints have a greater impact on the success of B2B e-commerce efforts. <sup>#</sup> 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Business-to-business (B2B) e-commerce; Inter-organizational information systems; Transactional efficiency

## 1. Introduction

Electronic commerce (e-commerce) has captured the attention of the business world as both a great opportunity and as a source of competitive threat. Specifically, it is projected that business-to-business (B2B) e-commerce will greatly exceed business-toconsumer (B2C) e-commerce as a means for many organizations to simultaneously increase sales and reduce operational costs. One reason is that larger volumes of sales typically take place between business partners than between the organization and individual consumers. Another is the opportunity to gain efficiencies in inter-organizational communication by exploiting emerging information system (IS) technologies. Indeed, many organizations feel it necessary to engage in B2B e-commerce. If they do not, those competitors that do make use of such technologies threaten to outpace them in efficiency gains and hence jeopardize their market position. In order to make an effective decision, it is important to assess the benefits and understand what conditions are optimal when engaging in B2B activities.

A recent survey of business process issues in electronic commerce revealed that many senior managers are no longer able to discern clear choices for building B2B transaction-making infrastructures due to the rapid pace of technological innovation [16]. In a related examination of business-to-business electronic commerce issues, it has been suggested that the cost efficiencies from the automation of transactions might easily reduce costs by a factor of 5, 10 or more [18]. The implication of these statements is that even though the benefits of automating transactions are impressive, the constraints that decision makers face are complex and hard to measure. In an effort to obtain a better understanding of the impact of organizational constraints on the success of business-to-business ecommerce efforts, we consider possible distinctions between more established electronic data interchange (EDI) technologies and emerging non-EDI B2B systems, such as electronic marketplaces.

EDI has been in existence for many years and its use has been shown to have many potential benefits. In fact, EDI continues to be cited as a crucial resource for modern standardized communication between firms. There are a number of new technological approaches which threaten to replace traditional forms of EDI for communication between firms, particularly the extensible markup language (XML), enterprise application integration (EAI), and Internet-based EDI. Although XML holds great promise, supply chain analysts do not see XML supplanting EDI soon [32]. One of the reasons is that EDI is considered to be secure and a means to avoid risk, and so more than 80% of the very large organizations are still using EDI. EAI promises to extend the batch orientation of conventional EDI to support real-time transactions over the Internet; these are integrated with back-end systems and processes, with many application integration vendors finding ways to support EDI [2]. In fact, recent evidence supports the claim that EDI is guaranteed to be used for some time, since major manufacturers like General Motors have mandated EDI use for first-tier suppliers. Additionally, Wal-Mart has recently mandated that all of its suppliers begin to move all their EDI transactions over the Internet [14]. This is significant, since Wal-Mart has more than 14,000 suppliers who process more than US\$ 217 billion worth of transactions via EDI annually. An important aspect of this evolution from traditional EDI to Internet-based EDI is that a relatively small investment is required and this enables small- and medium-sized businesses to participate in the electronic marketplace [1,7].

Despite the evidence of benefits for both traditional and newer forms of EDI, surprisingly only a relatively small percentage of firms have adopted the technology [9,13,30]. To help understand why this remains the case, both empirical and mathematical modeling approaches have been applied. Two key issues identified through recent models of EDI adoption have been the capacity for a firm to accommodate a large number of buyers and the technological compatibility of those buyers with various forms of EDI [12]. The relevancy of capacity and compatibility limits emanating from that research echo several of the valuation and conversion barriers to e-commerce performance cited in other research [5]. Our investigation examines the effects that these constraining factors can have on transactional efficiency gains in both EDI and non-EDI B2B technology settings.

## 2. Background literature

The two categories of prior research that apply to this study focus on the measures of efficiency used to evaluate IS and the constraints on the potential efficiencies gained. The measures of efficiency have been previously developed and empirically validated in intra-organizational contexts by Mirani and Lederer [20]. Their study included a literature survey of major contributions on IS/information technology (IT) benefits, from which they tabulated 33 potential benefits of IS projects, across strategic, informational and transactional dimensions. These were based largely on a previous study by Weill [31], whose interpretation was, in turn, a formal derivative of Turner and Lucas’s categorization [29].

Whereas the strategic and informational benefits focus on issues such as competitiveness, quality, and access, the concept of transactional efficiency was developed with the often more tangible issue of cost reduction. Although only partially justifying the adoption of a technology, the analysis of anticipated cost savings tradeoffs with anticipated financial investments in an IT project can provide particularly powerful support for or against system adoption. In some cases, this support may point to the short- or mediumterm impracticality of a proposal, regardless of its long-term potential. In other cases, the obvious savings anticipated so outweigh anticipated expenditures that long-term objectives can be viewed merely as fortuitous byproducts of adoption. The availability of a metric that captures IT cost savings along various sub-dimensions is therefore critical for both validating the cost justification of projects and the capability of linking those benefits to the market and operational characteristics or constraints of a firm.

Due to its rapid evolution, there is a scarcity of research providing definitive evidence of the impact of such characteristics on B2B e-commerce. Fortunately, recent research has taken the view that cumulative research on EDI is also generally extendable to B2B e-commerce (see [27,28]). Furthermore, there are already numerous studies on factors that affect the benefits of EDI systems (e.g. [22]).

For transactional technologies, one characteristic repeatedly studied has been transaction volume and the impact it can have on anticipated benefits and adoption decisions. However, in order to understand the mechanisms at play, measures of current transactional volume may be insufficient; they do not represent the actual volume feasibly effected by IT adoption [4]. In contrast, issues such as relative physical capacity constraints may be more meaningful in assessing the total number of transactions for which cost savings would be anticipated.

Although not studied in great depth academically, there have been a few studies that have made specific efforts to focus on physical capacity constraints rather than existing transaction volume. Jain et al. [11] considered the impact that supply constraints have on innovation diffusion, where they focused on the adoption of telecommunication devices. In addition, Mukhopadhyay’s [21] operational concerns and network externality dimensions, when considered simultaneously, support such a constraint. The total volume of transactions faced over the planning horizon may be dependent upon the total physical volume of goods and services provided to contracted business partners, firms necessarily gauge (directly or indirectly) their adoption decision by the limitations of their own productivity and the availability of trading partners [15].

Another issue often considered when discussing inter-organizational technology is that of compatibility among business partners. In one such study, Premkumar et al. examined the relationship between cost and compatibility measures and its impact on internal and external EDI diffusion [24]. While their findings supported the idea that greater internal compatibility is associated with better adaptation to and diffusion of EDI, the issue of inter-firm compatibility was not considered in depth. Arunchalam’s survey of IS managers revealed that 36.3% of the respondents considered non-automation and a lack of technical-sophistication of customers to be serious barriers to EDI adoption [3]. However, the specific form of such a barrier was not suggested in that study. Yet another study claimed that the adoption decisions made by firms can be somewhat paradoxical when trade volume alone is used as a predictor of EDI suitability [8].

Technological compatibility as a constraint to adoption of a communications technology has also been considered within Mukhopadhyay’s framework, which incorporated issues of IT context and network externality, and within Massetti and Zmud’s [19] concept of diversity. Similar strategic concepts have already been well established in the mass customization and niche targeting literature of the operations management and marketing disciplines [17,25]. Within such literature, it is generally accepted that firms dealing with buyers that have diverse and conflicting needs may be forced into the adoption of a broader set of communication technologies than firms dealing with more homogenous buyers. At the same time, when prohibitive diversity is the result of only a limited number of buyers, firms may actively seek out more homogenous subsets of buyers to avoid additional technology setup costs.

As with these studies, the constraints to efficiency considered here include operational and technological characteristics of associated business settings. We propose that these constraints play a critical role in justifying the adoption and use of the B2B technology. Furthermore, whereas past studies have focused on the consideration of these constraints in isolation, we view their simultaneous consideration and potential interaction as critical to a more complete understanding of their impact. These issues have been studied simultaneously only from mathematical and theoretical techniques in the past.

## 3. Research factors and hypotheses

The objective of this research is to show that the 10 items that make up the transactional efficiency construct (see Table 1) can be used to illustrate the impact that technological compatibility and operational capacity can have on various facets of effectiveness. Recent research has already suggested that firms capable of catering to a large number of diverse buyers will be driven to adopt a larger variety of communication technologies than those dealing with smaller or less diverse sets of buyers. Various factors influence this capability, including the number of buyers in the market, the operational capacity of a supplier to engage in business contracts with a large number of these buyers, and the basic willingness of such buyers to use a variety of communication technologies. Similarly, these drivers of adoption should be expected to provide insight into the actual effectiveness of the technologies, once adopted.

Two separate views of technological compatibility involving modes of communication are required in illustrating potential issues. First, greater technological compatibility with EDI technologies among partner firms increases the feasibility and benefits accrued from the adoption of the technology. Second, the less compatible partners are with non-EDI technology, the greater the benefits from EDI usage and the more critical the adoption of EDI in general.

The hypotheses designed to measure the impacts of these factors using the transactional efficiency construct are as follows:

H1. The higher the compatibility with EDI technologies among business partners, the greater the transactional efficiencies observed for EDI technologies.

H2. The higher the compatibility with non-EDI B2B technologies among business partners, the greater the transactional efficiencies observed for non-EDI B2B technologies.

The second issue of focus here relates to a firm’s physical capacity to deal with a large number of business partners, relative to the existing market. A relative view is taken here to emphasize that although certain markets may only have a few buyers, the number of transactions that business with such buyers generates may be sufficient to encourage variable cost reduction efforts from EDI adoption. Furthermore, relative capacity provides a measure of potential market influence, or power, an issue cited in past research as having a significant role in the adoption decision of business partners [10]. Firms with greater relative levels of capacity may therefore also represent those more capable of ensuring adoption by their buyers.

Similar to the earlier hypotheses, the relationship between operational capacity and transactional efficiency is hypothesized as follows:

H3. The greater the relative capacity of a firm to accommodate market demand, the greater the transactional efficiencies observed through the use of EDI and non-EDI B2B technologies.

In quantitatively operationalizing compatibility and relative capacity, we follow the recommendation of Kaefer and Bendoly in their analytical study of EDI users.

The survey consisted of the 10 items as well as questions regarding the number of available communication technologies, the number of products/services delivered annually, and the average buyer demand. A closer examination of the interpretability of these results showed that two of the resulting factors appeared to genuinely reflect the communication efficiency and system development efficiency sub-dimensions originally observed by Mirani and Lederer, with the other two representing an apparent split within their business efficiency sub-dimension. Furthermore, this split appeared to be logical, in that it divided human resource benefits from those gained through other operational avenues. The logical sub-groupings and otherwise strong agreement with Mirani and Lederer’s categorizations provided face validity in accordance with the recommendations of Sethi and King [26]. Here, we refer to these two sub-dimensions as personnel and capital efficiency, respectively. In order to develop the specific quantitative representations of each of these four factors, the items for which loadings on a given factor were above the 0.5 criteria were summed and the resulting index normalized (i.e. mean-centered and divided by the standard deviation of the summed index). This transformation was designed specifically to facilitate comparability in effect size for subsequent analysis, while at the same time avoiding any adverse effects on statistical results.

Measurement of the situational factors regarding compatibility and capacity followed the conceptual framework of Kaefer and Bendoly’s analytical study. Along these lines, we refer to the ratio of expected usable to available EDI technologies cited by respondents as EDI technological, or modal, compatibility $( \mathbf { M C } _ { \mathrm { E } } )$ , and the ratio of usable to available non-EDI technologies as non-EDI compatibility $( \mathbf { M C _ { N } } )$ As for capacity considerations, the availability of demand information on all clientele in a market would be ideal, however, given a potentially large number of such buyers the request for data on each from every supplier in a target survey population would have proved impractical. The assessment of average demand per buyer was a much more manageable task in the context of supplier targeted survey work. Operational capacity (CA) was therefore calculated as the ratio of current maximal operating capacity to the product of average demand per buyer times the number of potential buyers in the market as a whole.

Given these definitions, we were able to test the adequacy of the transactional efficiency sub-dimensions in determining the impacts of technological compatibility and operational capacity effects. In order to begin such an investigation, we considered the nature of the sample population available. Based on the descriptions provided by these firms, the sample was split into two separate populations:

one consisting of firms whose primary source of income reported was production oriented (44 complete responses) and one consisting of firms whose primary focus was manufacturing service oriented (42 complete responses). For the remainder of our discussion, these two types of firms are referred to as product and service firms, respectively. Such separate consideration is well in line with prior research that emphasized the role that exogenous factors can have on mechanisms of EDI adoption and use. The analysis proposed was conducted for both populations and, as would be anticipated, with distinct findings identified for each.

## 5. Conclusion

The goal of this research was to obtain a better understanding of the circumstances under which specific types of B2B technology generated transactional efficiencies, and the form of these efficiencies. To achieve this, we utilized a multi-item survey in an attempt to capture the effects of technological compatibility and operational capacity constraints within various environments. Based on an analysis of survey data collected across 86 firms, our findings revealed that the multi-dimensional nature of transactional efficiency appeared to be capable of distinguishing idiosyncrasies associated with alternate technologies (EDI versus non-EDI B2B technologies) as well as performance differences associated with alternate firm orientations (product versus service).

The distinctions were investigated across each of the four transactional efficiency sub-dimensions observed (communication, system development, personnel and capital). Various levels of support for the theoretically hypothesized effects have been summarized in Table 10. For example, H2 was fully supported, while H3 found support along all but one performance sub-dimension in EDI settings. It was consistently supported along the system development sub-dimension in all settings. Overall, support for H1 remained relatively weak.

While the system development sub-dimension did not show significant differences between product and service firm perceptions of efficiency gains for either technology case, the other sub-dimensions investigated did. Particularly interesting was the fact that product firms showed significantly fewer gains, on average, than did service firms in the EDI case. In contrast, in the non-EDI B2B case, product firms showed significantly greater gains in the personnel and capital efficiency sub-dimensions. This was not entirely surprising given the potentially divergent purposes of the two technologies. Specifically, while traditional EDI technologies have been geared towards usage in established long-term partnerships, where volume economies are critical, this is not the case for all contemporary non-EDI B2B technologies. Instead, such applications are often associated with a much larger community of potential short-term partnerships and smaller spot purchases aimed at reducing impending surpluses and shortages. Therefore, while price remained an issue, availability and an ability to rapidly meet spot specifications may have provided a more critical issue than volume.

For the non-EDI B2B case, however, technological compatibility with non-EDI technologies seemed to play a more common role. As expected, in neither case was compatibility with alternate technologies associated with significant effects on efficiency gains.

Therefore, along with the implication that existing metrics can be used in distinguishing alternative dynamics in e-commerce contexts, the nature of these distinctions is considerably intriguing. The observation that relative capacity issues seem to be more important in efficiency gains through EDI use is most likely linked to the relatively volume focused nature of this technology. Flexibility, on the other hand, has generally been a more predominant issue in the use of more recent non-EDI B2B technologies, such as online auctions and marketplace environments. Shorterterm buyer–supplier relationships have been typical of the characterizations of these environments in the press, as have alternate issues, such as quality assurance and spot availability. For these reasons, the relatively greater importance of technological compatibility may be more an artifact of the relative decrease in the importance of volume than anything else. The fact that product firms seem to fare better with respect to certain efficiency gains through non-EDI B2B usage, relative to service firms, may stem from this as well.
