# Research

# The use of spreadsheets in organizations: Determinants and consequences

Yolande E. Chan $^{a}$ , Veda C. Storey $^{b,*}$

$^{a}$ School of Business, Queen's University, Kingston, ONT, Canada K7L 3N6 $^{b}$ College of Business Administration, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303, USA

## Abstract

Spreadsheets have long been recognized as important tools for end-user computing. This research explores their use within business organizations. A survey was carried out to investigate the relationships among tasks, spreadsheet proficiency, usage, and satisfaction. The results suggested that the spreadsheet proficiency can have a greater impact on the tasks than the task can have on the spreadsheet proficiency. It was also found that spreadsheet users often do not use many of the commonly available spreadsheet features, and they do not appear inclined to use other software packages for their tasks, even if these packages might be more suitable. The proficiency of the spreadsheet users was not found to be related to the importance of the decisions being taken as a result of the spreadsheet analyses.

Keywords: End-user computing; spreadsheets; Software packages

## 1. Introduction

End-user computing, the autonomous use of information technology by knowledge workers outside the IS department, is an important part of organizational computing today $[4, 13, 18]$ . The rapid growth in end-user computing has resulted in people ranging from sophisticated to novice, motivated to resistant, and frequent to intermittent $[21]$ . End-user developers have reported that they are responsible for all the phases of systems development (design, construction and on-going maintenance) $[11]$ . Users are becoming programmers, analysts, and designers, with the development of user-friendly softwares and tools being one of the main reasons for the proliferation of end-user computing $[2, 19]$ .

Researchers have been exploring a number of issues related to end-user computing, including: the adoption of spreadsheet software; the application of role theory to the end-user development; the training of the end-users $[15, 16]$ ; the measurement of end-user computing $[9, 10]$ ; user information satisfaction $[12]$ ; and measures for software acceptance and use $[7, 14]$ . Furthermore, since its emergence, a considerable amount of attention has been focused on the potential problems that can arise due to end-user computing and the organizational impact of the resulting errors (e.g. $[1, 5, 6, 8, 17]$ ).

## 1.1. Spreadsheet applications

A particularly noticeable and important area of end-user computing is spreadsheet applications. Financial analysts, among others, tend to spend a great deal of time performing detailed analyses using spreadsheets. In fact, the adoption of the spreadsheet softwares in finance and accounting seems to be led primarily by the end-users [4]. Many of the models are both complicated and time-consuming to build. These may be linked together, leading to larger models that are very difficult to test, apply, modify, and debug. Many analysts complain about the time they spend transferring figures from mainframe computer printouts into spreadsheets before they can carry out analyses. It also appears that analysts can become quite proficient in the use of their chosen spreadsheets and, as a result, can be reluctant to adopt other software packages that might be more suitable for their applications.

Brancheau and Brown [3], in their review and analysis of end-user computing research, propose a model for the management of end-user computing. The component that deals with an individual end-user computing management, identifies four important factors (the end-user, tool, task, and end-user action) as being crucial to the management of end-user computing. This research examines the first three of these factors for the spreadsheet and other business software usage. They also recommend the future end-user computing research questions be better defined and narrower in scope.

## 1.2. Research Objective

The objective of this study is to explore the recent trends in end-user computing as they are related to the usage patterns of spreadsheets and other business modeling software. Specifically, the issues investigated are the relationships between the tasks performed and tools used by the spreadsheet users. Also investigated are the relationships between user proficiency levels and the use of, and satisfaction with, the spreadsheet packages.

## 2. Research methodology

## 2.1. Research hypothesis and model

Spreadsheet packages are probably the most commonly used decision support software. Some of the frequent users of these tools are financial analysts, market forecasters, and strategic planners. With the increasing sophistication of the end-users and end-user computing, one would expect that those who are most proficient in the use of spreadsheets might also be willing to adopt other packages, such as database management systems.

Five hypotheses are tested:

H1. A business analyst's $^{1}$ spreadsheet proficiency level is related to the tasks he or she perform.

H2. A business analyst's use of the specific spreadsheet features is related to the tasks he or she perform.

H3. A business analyst's spreadsheet proficiency level is related to his or her use of the software packages.

H4. A business analyst's spreadsheet proficiency level is related specifically to his or her use of the spreadsheet features.

H5. A business analyst's spreadsheet proficiency level is related to his or her satisfaction with software packages in general, and the spreadsheet packages in particular.

The model implies that the proficiency has an impact on the tasks that the analysts are asked to perform, and that the tasks performed also have an impact on the proficiency levels. It also suggests similar relationships between the tasks performed and the use of spreadsheet features; spreadsheet proficiency and the frequency of the use of software packages, particularly spreadsheet packages; spreadsheet proficiency and the frequency of the use of spreadsheet features; and finally, spreadsheet proficiency and user satisfaction levels with the spreadsheet packages used.

## 2.2. Survey procedures

To conduct this research, a field study was carried out. An initial questionnaire was first developed, and there were several rounds of pre-testing done by the business analysts and survey designers; this resulted in revisions of the composition and format of the questions. The survey instrument was sent to 1000 business analysts whose names were obtained from a mailing list provided by Lotus Magazine. It was mailed during February and March, 1992, with follow-up correspondence sent in May, 1992. 256 business analysts completed the survey, representing a 25% response rate. ## 3.1.2. Usage

The questionnaire also sought to capture the information pertaining to the respondent's current position, including the respondent's tasks and tools used to carry out them. The most frequently performed analyses are presented in Table 6. The questionnaire also gathered data on the time spent by the respondent on the analyses versus simple data collection. There appears to be an even split between data collection and analyses: on an average, $47\%$ of the respondent's time was allocated to data collection, and $45\%$ to the analyses. However, $12.7\%$ of the respondents reported an allocation of $80\%$ of their time to data collection (subsequently the mode). As shown in Table 7, manual input was the most common source of data. These results suggest that the analysts spend considerable time on data collection; and second, the users were either unaware of, or unwilling or unable to use, the import/export features of their software tools.

The respondents were also asked to describe the type of computer systems they used to carry out their analyses. Many organizations were dominated by personal computers. While this may be consistent with the fact that many in the sample are small, it is also consistent with the trend towards end-user and client-server computing, and the rising importance of personal computers. A list of the systems used is given in Table 8.

The degree of computer networking within the organization was found as moderate: 55% reported that their computer systems were not networked. This indicates that there was a substantial amount of isolated computing. It is consistent with an earlier finding that manual input is the most common source of data. Finally, this may be related to the small size of many of the organizations.

## 3.2.2. Measurement of the relationships between constructs

H1 - Tasks performed and spreadsheet proficiency. To test the relationship between the tasks performed (interval data) and spreadsheet proficiency (ordinal data) the eta coefficient was calculated $^{3}$ . When squared, eta can be interpreted as the proportion of the total variability in the dependent variable that can be accounted for by knowing the values of the independent variable. The squared eta scores between each of the variables are presented in Table 15. The time allocated for the most commonly performed analyses was the dependent variable, whereas the Likert score for spreadsheet proficiency was the independent variable. Obviously, the two constructs are related. The spreadsheet proficiency appeared to have an impact on the types of analyses performed, most notably, asset valuation, capital budgeting analyses, and statistical analyses. This is probably because, only proficient users are likely to be able to use those functions in these analyses.

Are those who are better at spreadsheet analysis, less likely to elect, or be asked, to carry out tasks that involve word processing? For single user stand-alone systems, is it really more attractive for the potential buyer to bundle spreadsheet and word processing packages together as many of the software vendors do today? Do spreadsheet users really value word processing packages highly?"

H5 - Spreadsheet proficiency and satisfaction with software packages. ## 3.3. Expanded research model

In an attempt to understand more about the factors involved in spreadsheet usage, an expanded version of the main research model was developed: it is shown in Figure 3. First, it seemed that the user's functional area would affect the number and types of tasks performed; e.g. more spreadsheet use would be expected in finance and accounting, than in personnel. Next, it was thought that computer training would affect a user's level of spreadsheet proficiency. It was also hypothesized that computer training would have an impact on the source of data used for analysis: those with computer training were expected to be more inclined to automate the data input. The training was also expected to have an impact on the most frequently performed analyses or tasks; those with more computer training would be expected to perform more sophisticated analyses. Finally, it seemed that the users who were more proficient with spreadsheets might report a greater importance of their analyses in companys' decision making and operations.

Functional area. The respondent was asked to indicate his or her department within the organization.

Computer training and tasks performed. To test the relationship between computer training and the respondent's tasks, the chi-square statistic was calculated, and the two variables were tested for independence. The statistic found was 18.2, with 10 degrees of freedom, significant at the 0.05 level. This was enough evidence to reject the independence hypothesis in favour of a dependence between the variables. That is, there was a link between computer training and the respondent's tasks.

Computer training and spreadsheet proficiency. To test the relationship between computer training and spreadsheet proficiency, the chi-square statistic was calculated, and the variables were tested for independence. The statistic found was 1.02, with 4 degrees of freedom, and was not significant at the 0.91 level. Hence, the independence hypothesis could not be rejected. That is, computer training and spreadsheet proficiency were not found to be related. It appears, therefore, that a large number of spreadsheet users are self-taught and do not receive formal computer training in the use of the spreadsheets. This is somewhat contrary to the finding of Nelson and Cheney [16] who concluded that there is generally a positive relationship between computer-related training that a user receives and his or her ability to use the computer resource. In our study, however, the most proficient spreadsheet users were not always the ones who had received the most formal computer training.

Spreadsheet proficiency and importance of decision making. To test the relationship between the spreadsheet proficiency and the importance of the decisions being made, the chi-square statistic was calculated, and the variables were tested for independence. The statistic found was 22.3, with 24 degrees of freedom, and significant at the 0.561 level. Again, the independence hypothesis could not be rejected. That is, the spreadsheet proficiency and the importance of the decisions being taken were not found to be related.

These findings suggested that as spreadsheet usage was influenced by the tasks performed, and the tasks performed by the functional area, different functional areas may well have had quite different expectations and needs of spreadsheet packages. General computer training (not courses and seminars dedicated to spreadsheet usage) appeared to improve slightly the user's ability to work with spreadsheet data; e.g. to download the data into spreadsheets. However, this training did not appear to be related to the overall spreadsheet proficiency, but it was related to the tasks that the respondents carried out. Certain tasks appeared to be associated with more formal computer skills.

Perhaps, the most interesting finding of all was that spreadsheet proficiency was not linked to the importance or significance of the decisions taken as a result of the analyses. The proficiency of an analyst involved in computations that could influence the expenditure of tens of thousands of dollars might be lower than that of another analyst working on a spreadsheet that would affect only a few hundred dollars.

## 3.4. Case study: Ardmore Farms

The following example describes a real-life scenario involving a senior analyst and the determinants and consequences of his spreadsheet usage. It is intended to illustrate the positive results that can be obtained from an appropriate spreadsheet use. Ardmore Farms, a subsidiary of Quaker Oats, employs approximately 400 people in the food services industry. It is a relatively small, having nine levels of management and approximately \$55 million in annual sales. The Assistant Product Manager in the marketing department holds an MBA degree in finance and marketing.

Before August 1992, a paper-based system was placed for keeping information on certain bids and contracts. The data included such items as code-number, price, and volume. To overcome inefficiencies associated with the manual system, the manager who had received formal university-level computer training developed a pricing system in an Excel $^{TM}$ spreadsheet using capabilities, such as look-up tables, etc. The manager quickly realized, however, that his application, if it were to be developed properly, needed to include database capabilities in addition to the flexibility and analytical tools of Excel $^{TM}$ .

Instead of inappropriately trying to use Excel as a spreadsheet-and-database, he developed a database component in $Q+E^{TM}$ which stored the data and interfaced it with the $Excel^{TM}$ spreadsheet. The system, called PRICE\_IT, is a pricing analysis system that is still used today. It computes a weighted average variable profit that is used to examine a given pricing scenario. This information is stored and compiled on a per customer basis. A series of reports is run that compute information by territory, region, and zone. This information is totally created electronically; it can immediately be faxed to any appropriate destination or shared over a PC-based LAN. Because the reports are so widely distributed and relied on, the system was explicitly reviewed and tested by the manager and colleagues before it was used as a production system. About 20 hours per month of the manager's time is now spent on pricing or profitability analysis. Only 20% of this time is allocated for data collection and entry; 80% is spent on the analysis of the data. The manager avoids manual re-entry of the data as far as possible. Most data are electronically received and distributed.

The manager's knowledge of spreadsheets was obtained in university and employer-sponsored courses. He reports a high level of spreadsheet proficiency. Today, he spends approximately 30 hours per week performing mid-range to long-term planning, some of which is largely based on spreadsheet analyses. The results are reported directly to the CEO. He estimates that if a major spreadsheet error went undetected it could conceivably cost the company up to \$1 million. The spreadsheet features he uses most often are import/export, mathematical, and what-if functions.

The manager rates his spreadsheet and database packages high. He says that his greatest satisfaction while using the spreadsheet package results from his ability to import data from other sources, to work with models developed by himself and others, and to exploit the wide range of analytical features and graphical capabilities provided by the package.

This minicase illustrates several features of our study. The company was a representative of the type of firm surveyed. The manager, however, was an expert spreadsheet user. His main tasks involved pricing analyses. Despite his proficiency with spreadsheets and his avoidance of manual data re-entry, a considerable amount of time was still spent on data collection and entry (20% as opposed to the average respondent data collection and entry time of 47%, however). The major applications, for which the spreadsheets were used, had become more complex than the spreadsheet's capabilities. The manager, unlike many of the respondents in our study, recognized the need for accuracy and additional software support—in the form of a well-tested interface—to the capabilities of a database management system. The scenario highlights the common circumstances and limitations faced by many spreadsheet users. It also reveals that spreadsheet usage, when the user is proficient and alert to the importance of the decisions being made as a result of the spreadsheet analyses, can be very successful.

## 4. Conclusion

The empirical evidence, gathered in this study, provides some support to our model, and the five main hypotheses that relate the tasks performed, spreadsheet proficiency, use of spreadsheet features, use of various software packages, and satisfaction with these packages. The evidence provides limited support to the expanded model, and the relationships among the functional areas, tasks performed, computer training, spreadsheet proficiency, source of data, and importance of the decisions made.
