# User participation in knowledge update of expert systems

Brenda Mak $^{a,*}$ , Bernd H. Schmitt $^{b}$ , Kalle Lyytinen $^{c}$

$^{a}$ Department of Computer and Information Science, UC 482, Cleveland State University, Cleveland, OH 44115, USA $^{b}$ Marketing Department, Columbia Business School, New York, USA

$^{c}$ Department of Computer Science and Information Systems, University of Jyvaskyla, Jyvaskyla, Finland

## Abstract

Applying the Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM) of persuasion we conducted an experiment to examine the effect of participation of users in the design process on their acceptance of system recommendations and revision of their original decisions. Results of our study indicate that two different types of information processing occur when subjects are reviewing the expert system recommendations.

(i) For users who have a high perceived level of participation in updating the knowledge of the ES, ambiguity of the decision setting is the primary determinant affecting acceptance of the recommendation from the ES.

(ii) For users who have a low perceived level of participation in updating the knowledge of the ES, acceptance of system recommendation to revise their original judgment is dependent on the perceived credibility of the experts building the system.

We discuss the implications of these findings for the design of expert systems. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Expert system; Elaboration Likelihood Model; User participation; Expert system use; Ambiguity

## 1. Introduction

Many studies of information systems (IS) use have addressed the relationship between user participation and system success (for a review see [20, 24]). Some tests have shown that user participation has been associated with a wide array of favorable outcomes [28]. Apparently, it leads to increased satisfaction [3, 19], better user understanding, greater system use, and improved user acceptance. The impact of user participation, however, is not always positive [15, 37]. To explain confounding findings, researchers have proposed a contingency approach which examines the role of contextual variables that mediate the relationship between user participation and system success. Investigated contingencies include user [12] and system characteristics [13, 14], as well as cognitive [36] and motivational factors.

Doll and Torkzadeh [11] analyzed the role of 'motivation to participate' in affecting the relationship between user participation and end-user satisfaction. They extended the research to the context of end-user computing, noting the change of user and analyst relationship over the years. Instead of relying solely on the analyst, end-users are becoming more knowledgeable, sharing in decision making, and engaging in activities performed by the analysts [27]. From a designer's perspective, it is necessary to analyze the effects of the participation of knowledgeable users on system refinement.

This type of knowledge work issue is a crucial element in many types of IS. Notable examples are collaborative work [18], transaction oriented [38], decision support [8], group decision support [32], executive information [39], and ES [26] – which are particularly interesting as they attempt to accommodate the decision maker's expertise. Understanding the effects of user participation in the knowledge update of an ES is critical to improving the expert system and its knowledge base. For highly knowledgeable users, the cognitive effect of their participation in knowledge update of the system could be particularly important in affecting their use of it. None of the studies, however, has attempted to address how user participation in knowledge update affects his or her willingness to accept the results of the ES, and thus whether these strategies increase the effectiveness of the user. Yet this question is obviously highly relevant. Therefore it is appropriate and necessary to ask questions like: What types of information do knowledgeable users consider when they interact with the system? Do users with a high participation level in design (who believe that their views and ideas have been incorporated into the system) utilize information differently from those with a low participation level? Here we adopt a cognitive approach to explore whether user participation and its related information examination processes can be used, indeed, as a good predictor of system effectiveness. In particular, we analyze the effects of user participation in updating ES knowledge on the user's comfort level in accepting an ES recommendation that is counter to the user's intuitive guess. We adopt the Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM) [30, 31] to predict and examine the impact of user participation on cognitive processes and decisions.

## 2. Review of the pertinent ELM research

The ELM was developed to examine processes of persuasion. It posits that there are two distinct mechanisms of processing a communication that is intended to persuade a person to change his or her attitude. What is termed a central processing route is adopted by subjects with the ability and motivation to understand the message, while a peripheral route is used by those without these attributes. In ELM studies, motivation has frequently been increased by involvement: if individuals are highly involved, they are more motivated to pay attention to and have the ability to understand the central arguments of a persuasive message. However, if they are less involved, they process and are persuaded by other, sometimes peripheral, cues (e.g. their belief in the credibility of the message). Predictions of the ELM have been supported in numerous studies on persuasion as well as in studies on advertising and health programs.

All these studies are based on nearly the same type of research design. This can be exemplified by Andrews and Shimp's study [4] which tested ELM's central and peripheral route predictions in advertising; it attempted to test ELM's predictions on how subjects' involvement affects their processing of a beer advertisement. Results showed highly involved subjects paid more attention to argument strength. Their cognitive activity increased and their attitude changes were greater when exposed to a strong message. In contrast, subjects who had low involvement paid more attention to peripheral source characteristics (on the message source rather than on the message information) and exhibited a greater attitude change when exposed to favorable sources.

The ELM has not previously been applied to study interactions between a user and an ES. Such an application, however, seems to be a promising way to examine the users' information processing during an ES use because the ELM was originally developed to aid in understanding counseling processes $[16, 35]$ and many ES deal with counseling functions $[21]$ . However, a consultative ES can be seen as similar to a human counselor. Therefore, the user's decision to accept advice from an ES may be influenced by similar factors to those that occur when a client accepts human counselor advice. The ELM predictions on relations between the involvement and the acceptance of counselor advice should therefore also hold for the interactions between user participation in the knowledge update of an ES and user acceptance of system recommendation.

We posit that user participation in updating an ES knowledge base will affect the way of processing information and improve user acceptance of the ES outputs. For users with high participation in the update, the user's decision to accept the ES advice is affected by factors central to the decision, such as argument strength of the message, or ambiguity of the decision setting. On the other hand, for users with low participation, the user's decision to accept advice is affected by external cues, such as perceived credibility of the experts building the system. The hypotheses for our study can be stated as:

H1: For users who have a high perceived level of participation in updating the knowledge of the ES, ambiguity of the decision setting is the primary determinant affecting acceptance of the recommendation from the ES.

H2: For users who have a low perceived level of participation in updating the knowledge of the ES, acceptance of system recommendation to revise their original judgment is dependent on the perceived credibility of the experts building the system.

## 3. ELM constructs in the context of user-system interactions

What exactly do the ELM concepts – involvement, ways of processing information, and message acceptance – mean in the context of user interactions with an expert system? Clearly, the ELM construct of involvement is closely related to the user participation in ES design which asks whether a user has been given an opportunity to update and refine the knowledge base of the expert system. Our observation is supported by previous research which has shown that participation is related to the motivational factor of involvement [5, 29].

According to Barki and Hartwick [6], the key to the success of user participation is the subjective psychological state of user involvement in which users' personal relevance is attached to the system. Thus the research suggests that the perceived level of participation is a crucial factor in determining its effectiveness [22]. Participative design leads to higher user enthusiasm [17], and the users must believe that their efforts are taken seriously [2]. We therefore analyze the effects of 'perceived participation in knowledge update' in this study.

In most ELM studies, the strength of the argument has been the factor studied to find out whether subjects have paid attention to the central message. Some researchers, however, have questioned its appropriateness [1]. In the context of ES interactions, when arguments are not specified for the ES recommendations, the ambiguity in the problem addressed by the system would be a key decision factor in determining the user's acceptance of the ES advice. Its impact would serve as a critical trait indicating whether the user has been studying the problem carefully.

Ambiguity can be defined mathematically as ‘a disjunction of risky prospects,’ or the uncertainty over the possible probabilities of a certain event happening [9]. From a problem-solving perspective, Schrader et al. [33] defined ambiguity as ‘a lack of understanding of the algorithm and the functional relationships among the decision factors.’ Using Schrader’s definition, ambiguity refers to the lack of understanding the user has of the functional relationships among the variables affecting the decision analyzed by the expert system. Suppose an expert system is used for making market entry decisions and the user (e.g. a marketing manager) is asked to make such a decision. Ambiguity is related to whether a specific clear-cut strategy solution exists. If a unique solution exists, the user may see no need to rely on the expert system. If the decision setting, however, is ambiguous, that is, if more than one strategy could be applied, the user will seek to reduce ambiguity [10] by relying on the advice of the ES. Consequently, to determine whether or not to follow the recommendation, a user must closely inspect the decision setting of the case and carefully, using reasoned arguments, arrive at his or her final solution. Thus the tendency to reduce ambiguity of the decision setting would determine the user’s reliance on the expert system in making decisions.

In ELM studies, one of the major peripheral cues has been the perceived credibility of the message source. In the context of ES interactions, the 'perceived credibility of the experts building the system' is thus likely to be a major peripheral concern. Finally, in studying the users' interactions with the system, their level of message acceptance and the associated change in their decisions form the dependent variable, as in most ELM research. This behavioral impact on users' decisions is important in assessing the influence of ES on managerial decision-making behavior. Thus, rather than measuring attitudinal outcomes, in line with ELM studies, we observe behavioral change directly in the form of user level of acceptance. The dependent variable is thus the user decision to accept the system's advice to revise the original judgment. The factors are 'user perceived participation in knowledge update,' ambiguity of the decision setting, and 'perceived credibility of the experts building the system.'

We suggest that two mechanisms will exist to process system advice. For users with a high perceived level of participation in updating the knowledge in the ES, the ambiguity of the decision setting is the major determinant affecting their reliance on the system for making decisions. In a more uncertain situation, they tend to reduce ambiguity by accepting the system's recommendation. By contrast, for users with a low perceived level of participation in updating the knowledge in the ES, user decision to accept the system's advice is determined by the perceived credibility of the experts.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Development of the system

A laboratory experiment was conducted to test the stated hypotheses. As suggested by McGrath [25], this method allows the researcher to study the behavior of interest with considerable precision, thus bringing substantial insights to our pioneer research of the ELM in IS. Once the role of the theory in explaining user behavior has been well-understood, its wider ramifications can be further established with follow-up studies. Such a viewpoint is also supported by Barnard [7], who commented:

Exploratory data of this type can fulfill both practical and research functions. They can, of course, be used to provide feedback to designers concerning usability. They can also provide a rich hunting ground for ideas and insight concerning the more basic causes of difficulties.... In short, such studies help us to understand the phenomenon of system use....

A second reason for choosing the laboratory method was due to practical difficulties. Tremendous resources in time and labor are required in order to observe how managers update the expert system in a field experiment. It is extremely costly to gain access to appropriate field subjects knowledgeable enough to participate in the refinement of the ES. Due to these practical concerns and because this study focuses on exploring the ELM in the context of ES-human interactions, we therefore conducted a laboratory experiment to enrich our understanding of the ELM and its IS implications.

A decision was made to develop an ES dealing with 'entry timing,' which is 'determining when to introduce a new product into the marketplace.' Ten senior MBA students, with extensive experience in strategic planning, were recruited from the Kellogg Graduate School of Management (KGSM) at Northwestern University in the US to participate in the protocol analysis; subjects were asked to think aloud about a hypothetical case and their thinking processes were traced to ascertain the variables affecting their decisions. Seven decision factors were identified. These were: position of firm, financial strength, expected demand growth, product life cycle, diffusion across competitors, cannibalization, and market development cost.

After the protocol analysis, the ES was developed to support new product entry timing decisions. Cases were generated by the system; these were then used in the experiment to test hypotheses on user participation and system acceptance. The subjects were 36 senior MBA students in KGSM at Northwestern University in the US. As interactions with the ES are typically long-term, involving a large number of situations, subjects judged a total of fifteen cases based on ten new product entries (a TV phone, a beeper watch, a decision-support system teaching piano skills, a notebook computer, a VCR with compact discs, a car with automatic tune-up system, a voice-driven computer, a hi-fi-television, a computerized diary reminding users of daily appointments, and a scanner). Magazines on high-tech development were used to identify these ten hypothetical new products.

Each case consisted of a product script and the seven decision variables identified in the protocol analysis. The variables and their values were: the position of the firm (dominant versus small), the financial strength of the firm (strong versus weak), the expected demand growth (high versus low), the product's life cycle (long versus short), diffusion across competitors (fast versus slow), drop in sales of the firm's existing products due to competition from the new product (high versus low), and the cost of market development (high versus low). With the ten products and the characteristics of the seven determining factors, the system generated a case by selecting a new product, and selecting a value for each of the seven variables.

## 4.2. Experimental procedures

All the subjects had prior work experience and were well-trained in marketing and strategic analysis. Thus they can be considered as a group of knowledgeable users for the ES. Each subject attended two sessions involving ten cases in the first session and five in the second session. For each case, subjects evaluated the seven factors while making a decision regarding entry timing. Recommendations were given by the ES only in the second session, and the subjects, after reviewing the ES advice, could choose to revise their initial guess for the case solution.

In between the two sessions, there was a short break to administer the experimental conditions. Overall, a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ design was used in the study. The between-subjects factors were ‘perceived participation in knowledge update’ and ‘perceived expert credibility – case ambiguity’ was a within-subjects factor. The subjects were randomly assigned to one of the four experimental groups that received different levels of ‘perceived participation’ (high/low) and ‘perceived credibility’ (high/low). To vary perceived participation before solving the second set of cases, the ‘high perceived participation’ groups were led to believe that their judgments in the first session were incorporated into the ES knowledge base, before they reviewed its recommendation in the second session. To vary credibility, the ‘high (low) perceived credibility’ groups were led to believe that the experts building the knowledge base were marketing managers with comparatively extensive (limited) marketing experience. The subjects in each group solved problems with different ambiguity levels. The ambiguity of the case recommendation was constructed based on entry-timing findings in the strategic-timing literature [23], and each case differs in its ambiguity level of decision setting. These cases were coded as ambiguous or unambiguous, based on literature findings.

The dependent variable - users' decision to accept the system's recommendation to revise their original judgment - was operationalized as the probability (on a scale of 0% to 100%) that the subjects revised their initial judgments in the direction of the system's recommendation for the five cases presented in the second session. Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was used to analyze the relationships among perceived user participation in knowledge update, perceived credibility, ambiguity, and user decision to accept system's recommendation to revise the original judgment.

We performed checks to find out whether the groups varied in the desired levels of participation and credibility. First, questions concerning various aspects of the use of the knowledge-based system were asked to analyze the overall effect of user participation in knowledge update. Second, subjects in the two credibility groups were asked to rate the perceived level of difference between the system's recommendation and their own judgments on a 5-point scale (1 = not at all different; 5 = very different). Third, eight other MBA students with experience in marketing were asked to evaluate the ambiguity levels of the cases on a 5-point scale (1 = very unambiguous; 5 = very ambiguous). Based on the case descriptions, they analyzed whether it is difficult to decide on a clear-cut strategy solution and assessed the ambiguity level of the case.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Checks on the factors

The results of the checks showed that the groups varied in the levels of participation and credibility. The result of a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed that user participation in knowledge update resulted in significantly higher ratings in confidence in decision (mean = 4.19 vs. mean = 3.40, p < 0.01), quality of decision improved (mean = 4.06 vs. mean = 3.33, p < 0.05), and trust in the system's knowledge base (mean = 3.25 vs. mean = 2.67, p < 0.05). Subjects in the extensive marketing-experience group (mean = 2.36) scored significantly higher on perceived levels of difference than the limited experience group (mean = 1.75, p < 0.01). In addition, the result of the ambiguity checks showed that the cases had been coded in the appropriate levels of ambiguity. A rate of 69.7% agreement was attained between the ambiguity coding assigned to the cases and the average of the raters' evaluation of the case ambiguity level (for reference on the method adopted for computation of inter-rater agreement, see [34]).

## 5.2. User acceptance of system recommendation

A three-way MANOVA with user participation in knowledge update, source credibility, and ambiguity as factors was performed on the users' decision to accept system recommendation to revise their original judgment. As shown in Table 1, two main effects were significant (for source credibility and ambiguity) and, most importantly, the three-way interaction approached significance (at p = 0.07). The main effects indicated that: (i) subjects in the high credibility group on average made less than half as many revisions than those in the low credibility group (mean = 14% vs. mean = 32%); that is, subjects were much less likely to revise their own judgments when the ES was perceived to be constructed by managers with limited rather than extensive experience. (ii) Subjects were more likely to revise their judgments when the case scenarios were ambiguous in contrast to unambiguous (mean = 52% vs. mean = 6%).

We had postulated that users with a high perceived level of participation would be affected by ambiguity whereas users with low participation would be affected by source credibility. This should have resulted in two two-way interactions (an interaction of participation and ambiguity and an interaction of participation and source credibility). As shown, a three-way interaction effect was identified instead (see Table 2 for the means). Before examining the means in detail, it should be noted that user acceptance of system recommendations varied widely, from no revisions (0%) to revisions in more than half of the cases (up to 80%). In the case of high participation, as predicted, subjects were significantly more likely to revise their judgments and to accept the system's recommendations when ambiguity was high rather than low, both when the source credibility was low (mean = 50% vs. mean = 6.7%, $t(21) = -2.65$ , $p < 0.05$ ) and when it was high (mean = 57% vs. mean = 18%, $t(16) = -1.76$ , $p = 0.09$ ). In the case of low participation, user acceptance of system's recommendation and decision change were generally low, but source credibility interacted with ambiguity. When the ambiguity was low, the source credibility was not taken into account. However, when ambiguity was high, as predicted, subjects were more likely to accept the system's recommendations to revise their original judgments when the source was highly credible (mean = 80% vs. 20%, $t(8) = -2.12$ , p = 0.06). In sum, the predictions derived from the ELM model were confirmed for high participation, but only partially supported for the low participation level.

## 6. Discussions and implications

The results of our study illustrate the importance of cognitive mechanisms in mediating the relationship between user participation and the level and type of system use. Two different ways of processing ES recommendation are adopted by users at different perceived levels of participation. The first one, focusing on key characteristics of the ES advice, such as case ambiguity or adequacy of explanations, is employed by users who thought that they had participated in the knowledge update. In contrast, users who believed that they had not participated in the knowledge update accepted the system's advice to revise their judgments based jointly on the credibility of the experts and the ambiguity of the case. Moreover, source credibility affected user acceptance and decision change in the case of high ambiguity but not in the case of low ambiguity. Our results thus show that the ELM is a useful theoretical framework for predicting user information processing in user-system interactions.

The implications of these findings for system design are twofold. First, for users who are not likely to update the knowledge base of the ES, the emphasis on peripheral characteristics of the ES, such as endorsements of the overall system by well-known companies and managers, seems to be particularly important. Second, for users who update the knowledge base, they pay attention to the soundness of the advice suggested by the ES to decide on their acceptance of its advice. Here ambiguity of the case and supporting explanations of systems recommendations are critical. The design of appropriate features for the two user groups helps to allocate and reserve system resources.

Moreover, the results also suggest that different types of problems may be assigned to be solved by users with different participation patterns. Routine decisions (e.g. tactical decisions that are more structured) can be solved by users without knowledge update: they will solve the problem by relying on the information from peripheral cues, such as the perceived credibility of the expertise captured in the ES. On the other hand, for more novel problems (e.g. the more unstructured strategic problems), updating of the knowledge base may be desired, since users pay attention to the central arguments of the system messages. Therefore, the system developed for solving more routinized decisions requires fewer sophisticated explanations for backing up the system recommendation. For instance, in training managers to use a new system, if attention to explanations is desired, then training material with more novel cases is required to motivate the user to focus on the system's discussion and reasoning.

Dr. Mak has published in IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man and Cybernetics, Expert Systems With Appli-

cations, and others. Her research and consulting experience are in the areas of statistical diagnostic systems, decision support systems, human and computer interactions, and neural networks.

Bernd Schmitt (Ph.D., 1988, Cornell University) is associate professor of Business at Columbia Business School in New York and the BAT Chair of Marketing at the China-Europe International Business School in Shanghai. He has been a visiting professor at M.I.T, in Poland, Germany, Hong Kong and China. A specialist in international market

ing strategy, corporate identity and consumer behavior, his research has been published in academic and management journals, including the Journal of Marketing Research, the Journal of Consumer Research, the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, California Management Review, and Long Range Planning.

Kalle Lyytinen is professor in Information Systems at the Univeristy of Jyvaskyla, Finland. He currently serves on the editorial boards of Information Systems Journal, European Journal of Information Systems, Accounting, Management and Information Technology, MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Information Technology & People, Requirements Engineering Jour

nal and Journal of Strategic Information Systems. He has published over 70 articles and edited or written six books. His research interests include information system theories, system design methods and tools, electronic commerce, computer-supported cooperative work, decision making theories, and diffusion of complex standardized technologies.
