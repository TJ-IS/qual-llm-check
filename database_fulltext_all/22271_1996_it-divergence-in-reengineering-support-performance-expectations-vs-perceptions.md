# IT divergence in reengineering support: Performance expectations vs. perceptions

Michael E. Whitman $^{1}$

Department of Management, College of Business and Economics, University of Nevada, Las Vegas, 4505 Maryland Parkway, Box 456009, Las Vegas, Nevada, 89154-6009

## Abstract

Current literature is replete with reports highlighting the successes and failures of business process reengineering (BPR) efforts. Organizations have begun to realize the key enabling role of information technology (IT) in support of these efforts. However, expectations of the influence of IT in BPR as well as aspects of the organization which influence IT, may far outweigh the actual results. A panel of experts was questioned and 43 businesses currently undergoing reengineering were examined in order to determine a ‘divergence’ between expectations and observations on the role of IT in BPR. The organizational groups within the businesses consisted of business managers, executives, administrative/staff personnel, IT personnel, and consultants actually involved in reengineering projects. The study sought answers to three research questions: Are there significant differences in IT in BPR expectations and observations?, Do they vary by constituency group?, and Do they vary according to the level of organizational IT effectiveness? The study identified three factors that represent significant differences in these shortfalls. These are labeled as Strategic Planning Empowerment, IT Support for Process Improvement, and IT/Business Politics. The study also determined differences between groups, as well as between varying levels of IT effectiveness.

Keywords: Business Process Redesign; Business Process Reengineering; Business Process Transformation; Change; Change Management; Information Technology; Information Technology Expectations; Information Technology Performance; Organizational Change; Process Innovation

## 1. Introduction

Amidst a voluminous series of reports of successes and failures of business process reengineering (BPR) efforts, lies an issue of critical importance. Information technology (IT), as an acknowledged contributor to BPR, plays a more important role than most people currently assume. A recent study of 43 companies involved in BPR projects revealed a significant discrepancy in the influence of IT during BPR. This, termed a 'divergence gap', suggests that the role of IT in BPR, as well as the influence an organization has over this role, is not given enough consideration to improve the likelihood of success. The term 'divergence' is used to describe differences in perceived and expected influences of IT in BPR. Specifically this represents the deviation between the extent the respondent observed the items having an influence and the expectation of influence the respondent felt should have occurred.

IT is recognized as a fundamental enabler of process oriented change [7, 10, 12, 19, 23, 27, 30, 31]. IT is purported to affect multiple phases of the BPR effort; in preparation, conduct, and implementation of needed changes [6]. Unfortunately, IT's impact as an enabler in BPR is not well understood. This study sought to address this impact by interviewing individuals in a number of companies that have recently undergone reengineering efforts and determining what factors they identified as critical in the process. The interview structures were based on information derived from a panel of expert BPR consultants and researchers, focusing on the influences on BPR from organizational IT. The individuals within the companies consisted of business managers, executives, administrative/staff personnel, IT personnel, and consultants actually involved in BPR projects. These individuals identified the suspected 'divergence gap' by identifying the extent to which a list of items should and do affect the use of IT in BPR.

## 2. Related studies

While large-scale organizational change is certainly not a new problem, the focus of BPR is definitely a fresh perspective on organizational operational inefficiencies. As such, there are virtually no empirical studies focusing on reengineering; the focus of the dominant literature rests on case presentations or conceptual projections. Grover et al. [9] suggested “three research streams can provide insight into the phenomenon [of BPR]. These are (1) studies that investigate the relationship between IT and the organization, (2) literature on strategic planning for IT, and (3) research on organizational innovation.” Krovi [16] points out “investigating the correlation between organizational change and IT implementation will serve as a basis for finding out why resistance always comes up to such changes.”

As BPR involves radical change on a large scale, one must understand the characteristics of change. Organizational development specialists require a clear distinction between incremental or evolutionary change, and radical, large scale, or revolutionary change [1, 20, 21]. Barczak et al. [2] note: “over time, incremental changes can increase inefficiencies, large scale change can counter the movement toward inefficiency and involves an overall reorganization of parts into a harmonious or symmetrical relationship.” They also identified key elements that focused on pattern structure or process dissolving and recreation.

Most models of change (and their related intervention strategies) have roots in the classic Lewin [18] model of unfreezing, moving and refreezing. Damanpour [5] pointed out that radical innovations “produce fundamental changes in the activities of an organization and represent clear departures from existing practices, as opposed to incremental changes which result in little departure from existing practices.”

Tranfield and Smith [28] stressed the use of a methodology as the cornerstones of the transformation process in managing rapid change, stating that it “... should be business driven; should emphasize a ‘back to basics’ focus; should be top management driven; should drive from front end to back end; should integrate change strategies; should focus on an investment in people as well as technology; must get everybody ‘on board’ as participants in the effort; should have clear targets; and clearly defined time scales.” This strategy is evident in many popular BPR methodologies.

## Zajac and Kraatz [32] stated that

“... organizations do undergo restructuring in significant numbers in response to changing environmental conditions... the impact of these changing environmental conditions is to direct organizations’ restructuring efforts in a patterned, theoretically predictable (adaptive) way... and organizations undergoing forms of restructuring that can be defined as a priori desirable are more likely to experience performance improvements and enhanced likelihood for survival...”

In any event, an improved understanding of change factors can improve overall performance. Traditional change strategies contain many key elements that may prove beneficial to a reengineering effort. Understanding influences can directly improve the probabilities of BPR success. As a recognized enabler, it is important to better understand IT's role in the BPR process, and to understand the facets of the organization that may be employed to support that role.

## 3. Study objectives

The research sought to determine the scope and domain of the factors that influence the use of IT as it supports BPR. It focused on three questions:

1. Are there differences in expectations and observations of influences of IT as a primary enabler of BPR?

2. Do these differences vary between constituency groups involved in BPR?

3. Do these differences vary based upon reported levels of IT effectiveness with the organization?

While the research questions do not pose a null and alternative hypothesis at each stage, the study expected to find differences between; expectations and observations, constituency groups and reported levels of IT effectiveness.

The study began with a series of interviews with individuals who were deemed knowledgeable in BPR, based upon expertise demonstrated in their prior work. These individuals were asked to explain the importance of IT in BPR, and to identify those items that could influence the use of IT as an enabler of BPR. The resulting interviews were transcribed and subjected to a content analysis by two independent researchers. They searched the transcripts for key items that reflected the interaction of IT as an enabler of BPR and for items that influenced this role. The resulting lists from the researchers were compared and items that both had selected were retained. The items were then used in the survey questionnaire.

A questionnaire incorporating these items was developed and subjected to two independent reviews for item clarity and reliability. First, it was examined by a panel of qualified researchers for item clarity and relevance, and then refined based upon their qualitative comments. The instrument was then pilot tested at a major American fleet leasing company, which was determined to be representative of the target sample. The resulting qualitative and quantitative comments were incorporated in the final instrument, which was subsequently administered to respondents.

The study focused on companies that reported completed or ongoing BPR projects. These projects addressed BPR in a number of functional areas. This functional differentiation, in addition to a process-orientation and a wide-scale, radical change focus was important in classifying the projects as BPR. The individual respondents within these companies consisted of persons directly involved in the reengineering process, in roles varying from BPR team members, consultants, steering committee members, IT support personnel, and business process owners. The respondents were asked, based upon their direct experience, to identify the degree to which the item actually (perceived) affected and should (expected) affect the use of IT in BPR. The potential responses were presented in a 6-point scale ranging from not at all (1) to to a very great extent (6). A differential score was then calculated from the perceived and expected scales. The resulting differential scale was then subjected to a series of analyses.

A total of 43 companies from a variety of service and manufacturing industries agreed to participate in the study. Every company had conducted or was conducting a reengineering project at a strategic level, with the employees involved in various reengineering projects from their conception or for at least six months.

A total of 375 surveys were sent to the contact persons in the 43 companies. A total of 24 companies returned 130 completed surveys (a response rate of 34.7%). The largest number of surveys received from any one company was 15. Most were from the communications industry, followed by manufacturing. Table 1 shows the response rates based upon the primary industries. The largest category for duration of involvement for employee respondents was 1 to 3 years, followed by the one for more than 3 years. Companies averaged involvement in 7.08 reengineering projects (standard deviation of 0.678). 71.4% of companies reported using the services of an external consultant to support their reengineering efforts.

## 3.2. DIFFER Scale Factors

Analysis of the differential scale identified three factors that differ between items the respondents expect to impact the use of IT in strategic BPR and those respondents perceive to impact that use. These were identified through an exploratory factor analysis of the 62 individual item differences reported by study respondents. The total number of variables with respect to the total number of usable instruments was determined to be acceptable based upon a 2:1 ratio as reported by Cattell [3] and Gorsuch [8]. In fact, Cattell indicated that the ratio can drop as low as 1.1:1, as long as there are at least one hundred more subjects than variables. The 130 collected surveys therefore provided a satisfactory sample size.

The exploratory factor analysis method was selected as applicable due to a lack of sufficient knowledge about the impact that IT can have on BPR, and especially about the various organizational influences. According to Kim and Mueller [15] "factor analysis may be used as an expedient way of ascertaining the minimum number of hypothetical factors that can account for the observed covariation, and as a means of exploring the data for possible data reduction." Churchill [4] also says that factor analysis can indeed be used to suggest dimensions.

The exploratory analysis began with an oblique rotation factor analysis of the individual differences, as a degree of correlation between factors was suspected. The oblique rotation can provide “a simpler and/or more easily interpretable solution” [15]. Items were assigned to a factor based on the value of its factor loading. The analysis used a minimal acceptable factor loading of 0.45, based upon prior heuristic values by Straub and Carlson [26], Sethi and King [25], and Lederer and Sethi [17], and eliminated items which cross-loaded.

Initial analysis resulted in the identification of 9 factors. Due to an inordinate number of single- and dual-item factors, it was suspected that the number of actual factors was substantially lower. A Velicer's test was conducted comparing the root mean square off-diagonal partials to obtain the minimal average partial [29]. According to this test the true number of significant factors would occur when the value is minimized. After examining each possible iteration, beginning at 9 potential factors and moving down, the root mean square off-diagonal partials finally minimized at three factors. The items comprising these factors were then studied to determine a nominal correlation.

Once the factors were named, they were subjected to a Cronbach's coefficient alpha to evaluate internal reliability [14], using a heuristic value of 0.6 for alphas based upon prior research; e.g., [22]. Two of the three original factors tested well; the third required a reduction in items to bring the coefficient alpha to an acceptable level. Once this reduction had been completed, the factor analysis and Velicer's test were repeated to ensure that the factor item loadings were not affected by the reduction in items. The second iteration resulted in factors identical to the initial loadings. Table 3 presents the resulting factors, their coefficient alphas and factor loading ranges.

Once the individual factors had been identified and subjected to measures of internal consistency, a MANOVA was conducted to determine the independence of each item. Both Hotelling's $T^{2}$ and the Wilk's lambda indicated that the factors appear to be significantly different (p<0.000). "Small values (of the Wilk's lambda) indicate that group means do appear to be different" [24]. Similarly, the low observed significance level of the Hotelling's $T^{2}$ would lead to the rejection of a null hypothesis that the items are equal.

## 4. Interpretations of findings

The findings, to some degree, support the contentions of the research questions, particularly with regard to the identification of differential factors with respect to IT's involvement in BPR.

## 4.1. Identification of differential factors

The study identified three factors representing differential views of the expected versus the perceived influences of IT in BPR. These influences typically serve to strengthen the use and support of IT as an enabler during reengineering efforts. The three factors discovered were:

Factor 1: Strategic Planning Empowerment, deals with 12 items that focus on the strategic application and attention required of a BPR effort. This factor emphasizes the integration of strategy and business processes, and the need to address organizational strategies in business processes through reengineering. Of particular importance, is the need to recognize BPR as a strategic level effort, demanding a multifunctional mix of applied resources and executive attention.

Factor 2: IT Support for Process Improvement, contains eight items that reinforce the IT focus on business processes as a central component of BPR. These items indicate a direct concentration of IT resources in support of the business processes and subsequent support for redesigned processes by developed IT.

Factor 3: IT/Business Politics, contains three items that highlight the potentially negative effect of poor relationships between IT and business segments, and the influence of IT departmental politics. This factor also illustrates the need to incorporate IT in the project, but not allow the effort to serve as an IT technology update, or require IT to host the effort.

## 4.2. Difference in scale items

In order to evaluate a BPR divergence with respect to scale factors, each item was ranked according to its individual mean. This represents the difference between the expected and observed influence with respect to IT in BPR and it should provide additional insight into the degree of importance of an item. The individual items associated with Strategic Planning Empowerment are presented in Table 4.

The items at the top of the list indicated those with the least difference between expected and perceived influence. Apparently, individuals with the closest degree of involvement have the most realistic expectations of IT's role in BPR, as well as understanding IT's influence. The items toward the bottom of the list focus on IT modeling and methodologies: the key planning areas of a BPR effort. Recognition of the relationships can assist the organization in applying its critical resources to the effort of supporting the planning and execution of the BPR process.

The items comprising IT Support for Process Improvement are presented in Table 5. As is evident, the items at the top represent process-oriented problems: individuals involved in BPR possess a functional understanding of the expected IT support for processes. The lower end of the scale concentrates on the IT department's role: the business personnel's expectations of the IT personnel capability may be unrealistic.

## 4.3. Differences between constituencies

Once the factors were identified, the next phase involved determining whether or not the factors varied between the constituency groups: Business Managers, Administrative/Staff Personnel, Business Executives, IT Personnel, and External Consultants. Table 7 presents the means of the divergence gap for the various constituencies by each factor.

The analysis began with an ANOVA of the individual factors by constituent group. This consisted of a one-way ANOVA, with a Scheffe's pairwise comparison to determine individual differences. The respondent constituency groups consisted of 46 managers, 17 administrative/staff personnel, 19 executives, 16 IS personnel, 5 external consultants and 6 others. In each instance a probability of significance was reported. An ANOVA with Scheffe's tests of pairwise comparison of IS Personnel with Administrative/Staff Personnel, and of IS personnel with Consultants were the only two significant associations, and then only at levels of less than 0.1 but greater than 0.05. Note that the Scheffe's test was used in lieu of the more rigorous Tukey's test as the former allows selection of significance levels ranging from 0 to 1.

As the relatively small sample size for all but one of the groups could make generalization of the parametric outcomes statistically weak, a non-parametric equivalent was also conducted to allow more robust interpretation of the results. Table 8 presents the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis pairwise.

From this table the data for Strategic Planning Empowerment suggests distinction among reported differences in associations between IS personnel and all other functional groups except executives, as well as in associations between managers and consultants, and between executives and both administrative/staff personnel and consultants. When the means for these groups are examined it appears that, for this factor, IS personnel possess a lower level of expectation (as derived from the difference between observations and expectations) than most of their counterparts. IS personnel may better understand the capabilities, weaknesses, and influences that could affect it, as well as the strategic planning process that accompanies it.

The difference in perceived ideals of IT, influence for administrative/staff personnel and both executives and IT personnel, could stem from the traditional separation of these personnel in the planning process for IT in BPR. In a typical organization, administrative/staff personnel are separated from executives, usually by one or more levels of an organizational chart. They are excluded from most briefings and discussions and, subsequently, the applications and influence of IT in support of BPR. Traditionally, administrative/staff personnel are also distanced from IT personnel, although this depends on the roles and the way the IS versus end-user support is structured.

The difference in perceived ideals between consultants and IT personnel and executives presents a more challenging analysis. Ideally, consultants work closely with executives in the planning and execution of BPR efforts. A difference in opinion between external and internal participants of BPR could indicate a failure to communicate the overall integration of IT into the BPR process, or simply a failure to follow a recommended involvement. Due to the expensive nature of IT-based solutions and support, IT may be the subject of cost-cutting measures when the projected costs of IT-supported BPR are evaluated economically. Whatever the difference, the potential for a reduced payoff and increased risk could result from a dissimilar view.

Differences with regard to IT Support for Process Improvement only appear at any relevant level of significance between Executives and Consultants; this could again result from expectancies being misrepresented in communications at the executive advisory level. Differences in IT/Business Politics may also be closely linked to the IT perspective possessed by IS personnel. In the third factor, significant differences occur between IS personnel and all other categories at varying levels of significance. This could also represent an internal/external political battle between IS and the rest of the BPR collective as increased demands are placed on IS to support all phases of the BPR effort, in addition to supporting the business as a whole.

In general, the most significant differences appeared in the opinions of IS personnel which vary from all other groups associated with the BPR effort. It is imperative that knowledge of the capabilities of the IT supporting the effort be conveyed to all involved in its application during BPR. It is also important to ensure that IS personnel are involved and associated in a manner that reduces the political turmoil which could negate any positive changes resulting from the effort.

## 5. Limitations of the study

The most important limitations are: (1) the exploratory nature of the study; (2) the restricted survey population; and (3) the level of operational knowledge about the subject.

As this is the first empirical study to be reported in this area, no established theories, hypotheses, or previous studies exist to provide a strong theoretical foundation. In addition, some companies had more experience with BPR and, naturally, a more detailed understanding of the subject matter.

The study also purposefully restricted the selection of the survey respondents. The initial contact with the organizational representatives resulted from perusal of current literature in which the companies' BPR efforts were reported. If an organization did not have its name reported, it was virtually impossible for it to be identified as a potential subject. While the experts were asked to suggest additional potential subjects, most declined under claims of proprietary knowledge and professional confidentiality. Thus, the subject group was predetermined by its willingness to reveal its efforts in the published literature. There is also a problem in interpretation of the results of statistical studies due to insufficient sample sizes within one or more categories, which can inhibit generalization of the results.

The third and predominant limitation is the level of operational knowledge about BPR. While discussing the study with the experts, most indicated that the definition of the term BPR depends on who defines it. Thus the study first had to develop a set of definitions.

## 6. Conclusions

In general, the significance of differences in factor groups indicates a poignant need for organizations to devote strategic attention to BPR efforts. Only by providing the BPR effort with the necessary attention can it be expected to provide the desired outcomes. This is not necessarily a pure IT perspective: indeed IT should play an important role in BPR projects, but not the lead role.

Dramatic differences in the means indicated that IT is not being used as much as it could be. There may be perceptions of a more vital role, however, than is actually occurring. Experts indicated that BPR projects are often ‘handed-off’ to IT departments. Once the IT departments begin their work, organization members frequently categorize the effort as just another IT project and fail to give the effort the strategic attention it deserves. Similar differences occur in the area of strategic considerations for BPR. With the degree of change involved, BPR demands a concerted effort from all levels of the organization, in addition to a priority of organizational resources.

BPR can be costly, but if managed properly it can also be profitable. As a key component of business as well as the reengineering of business, IT can be a significant facilitator. But, like any system, if improperly applied, it can fail to provide the expected outcomes.
