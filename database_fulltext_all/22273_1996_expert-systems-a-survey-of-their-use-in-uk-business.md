# Expert systems: A survey of their use in UK business

Elayne Coakes $^{1,*}$ , Kim Merchant $^{2}$

Department of Business Systems, University of Luton, Park Square, Luton, LU1 35U, UK

## Abstract

A number of surveys have been carried out in the past ten years on the use of expert systems in business. This paper compares previous survey findings with a new one undertaken in the Spring of 1994; it finds significant differences in the level of use of expert systems. One thousand companies across the UK were surveyed regarding their use of information systems in general and expert systems in particular, and the results are discussed in this paper. A response rate of 22.5% showed that 50 out of 214 organisations, use expert systems for a variety of applications, ranging from strategic to operational. The analysis identifies users of expert systems by organisation size and industry sector. It also highlights their reasons for use. The paper concludes by identifying the perceived business benefits and the relative costs involved with the purchase or development of an expert system, as identified by the organisations.

Keywords: Expert systems; Organisation system maturity; Survey of expert system use in UK business

## 1. Introduction

Organisations are faced with the problem of how to retain experts and their expert knowledge, in a society of competition and change. An expert system (ES) is a software system that retains the knowledge of these experts for the organisations for later use. It represents the knowledge of a specialist subject with an aim of solving problems or giving advice. It may replace a human expert or assist that expert in performing his(her)role. ES is derived from work performed in Artificial Intelligence research. Typical tasks for such systems include diagnosis of faults or diseases, configurations of computers or other complex objects, structural analysis of chemical compounds and planning sequential actions [3].

## 2. Survey aims

The survey attempted to discover whether the UK companies, across a range of sectors and locations, were utilising the potential benefits of ES. Previous surveys of American companies $[1]$ and quantitative modelling techniques $[6]$ of the UK companies found very few users of ES among their respondents. It was hoped that by targeting a broad range of organisations, a true picture of the situation across organisations in the UK would be achieved. Survey data would provide answers to questions about those sectors of the UK industry that would most likely to use ES and whether the size of an organisation affected the take-up rate, both within and outside the industry sector.

The type of hardware and software used in the organisations and their most common applications was requested. We expected that organisations showing the greatest variety of software applications and purposes would be the one's most likely to use ES. This might indicate that they were more sophisticated in their computer usage. This was based on Nolan's Maturity Stage in the development, management and use of technology as indicated by the application portfolio [3, 8] and such organisations would have at the minimum, reached Porter's [9] Optimisation or Enhancement Stage.

The survey was also intended to determine reasons why organisations had chosen to implement ES and the perceived benefits to the organisation. We also wanted to know what factors would prompt non-users to purchase ES and whether they had plans to do so in the near future.

It has been suggested that ES have been significantly oversold, that claims for their values and capabilities are often excessive, and in fact that they are only effective in relatively simple problem domains. In the survey, we tried to discover whether the 1980's were the peak time for purchase and also whether usage of the systems were concentrated in relatively simple problem domains.

## 3. Survey background

Since they first emerged from the University laboratories in the 1960's and 1970's, expert systems have been hailed as 'revolutionary solutions to problems in almost every area of human activity' [10]. Yet as Simons continues, such 'hype' produces an inevitable backlash. Artificial Intelligence was perceived as a direct threat to human (and expert) status. In 1984, Martins declared 'For the most part, current off-the-shelf expert system tools... cost too much, are poorly supported, lack adequate documentation, are hard to use, yield very inefficient programs and seem to have sharply limited applicability to complex real-world problems' [7]. This indicated that, at least then, writers felt that ES had been oversold and were not contributing value.

In 1988, a survey of data managers of 126 randomly selected companies was carried out [1] in the US. Of the 47 valid responses only two managers claimed to be using ES in their organisations. Some 22 respondents felt that the systems would not be cost effective; but $36\%$ were of the opinion that if there were a perceived business need, the cost to develop such a system could be justified.

The low number of respondents claiming to use ES was confirmed in the UK by a survey of quantitative modelling techniques, carried out in 1993 [6]. They discovered that, of 80 respondents, 58% had never heard of expert systems and only 4% used them frequently, while 7% used them occasionally. Whilst the survey concerned itself mainly with quantitative modelling, ES had been included as it was suggested that the area of modelling provided a high potential return on investment. In 1984, Johnson [5] identified four such ‘promising islands’ for ES development:

## 4. Survey design

Despite all claims to the contrary, ES applications are relatively expensive to develop and require significant expertise in the techniques of knowledge elicitation and translation from the domain expert to the software system. It therefore seemed likely that such systems would not be developed by the smaller company and a sample of 1000 companies was identified by turnover, sector, and location. Although most large organisations tend to be clustered around Southeastern England, we tried to cover a more representative sample of organisations in UK and thus added more companies from Leeds, Manchester and Birmingham conurbations.

Initially, where multiple addresses for one organisation were identified, each location was targeted, as it seemed that since ES can be run on microcomputers, some local developments might have occurred. This spread the range of locations beyond the conurbations into Scotland and Wales, and throughout the UK. Further companies were identified, particularly in the southeast by listings of those that employed graduates on a regular basis, as this was felt to be an indicator of size. The final group of organisations was selected from a university supplied list of large local employers.

The survey was addressed to the Managing Director, with a letter requesting that he/she pass it on, if necessary, to a more suitable candidate. In most cases it was passed to the Information Technology Director or equivalent.

The questionnaire was designed to appear attractive and appealing, as well as relatively quick to answer. It was piloted and amended according to the suggestions received. A copy of the survey results was offered to participating companies to encourage responses, and this was proved popular. The response rate for the survey was 22.5% (225), which exceeded expectations and 68% (154) of the respondents asked for the survey report. This is despite the comment of Hawkins and Tull [4] that promising a copy of the results does ‘not increase responses even among industrial respondents’.

After about eight weeks, a reminder letter was sent to selected main offices of organisations that had not responded. However, the numbers on the questionnaires were utilised to track only the responses, and the anonymity of respondents has been preserved.

A definition of an ES was not included in our questionnaire: we wished to ascertain respondents' knowledge and understanding of the term, even amongst non-users. This did result in our receiving several faxes and telephone calls for explanation. It also resulted in a small number of non-valid responses (11), where respondents claimed systems to be expert but they did not fall within the definition.

The results were analysed using SPSS, Excel, and Minitab to provide statistical significances, frequencies, cross-tabulations and percentages of responses to questions.

## 5. Survey results

Of the 225 responses, 172 were received within the first 3 weeks and a further 53 were received after the reminder. Only 11 (5%) of responses were considered invalid. Of the valid responses 23% claimed to use ES - an interesting contrast to the 4% claimed in previous surveys.

Not all respondents completed the questions indicated in Tables 8 and 9, resulting in a base of less than 164 respondents. Again, two columns of percentages have been calculated.

Interestingly, it can be seen from Table 1 that the highest number (about 24%) of respondents who claimed to use expert systems are in the financial services sector. The next most frequent usage occurring in consultancy/law, a category that included solicitors and management consultants.

Of those who did not use expert systems the highest number of respondents were in manufacturing and sales/marketing retail; most of them expressed an interest in obtaining the report and in participating in an in-depth study in future.

It would appear that the use of ES is not confined to large organisations, as 28% of respondents that use them have less than 200 employees (see Table 2).

An analysis of the length of time that ES have been in use by the respondents (see Table 3) indicates that interest in expert systems has declined recently.

Most organisations have purchased or developed ES either as part of a strategic plan or to replace human experts (see Table 4). Many such systems were implemented because competitors used them, perhaps indicating an awareness of the potential for competitive advantage that is offered by ES.

## 6. Survey's findings compared to aims

Of the 50 organisations responding to the survey who do use expert systems, 12 were in the financial services sector and eight into the consultancy/law sector.

Contrary to original expectations, organisational size did not prove to be a significant factor in the use of ES, as 21 users employed less than 1000 employees and of these 14 employed less than 200 people.

A factor that did affect usage was the maturity of computer usage within the organisation. This was indicated by the comparisons (maturity would be indicated here by frequency of use of software, and number and variety of functions performed) as shown in Table 10.

A statistical analysis of these results was carried out using a two-tailed t-test. It showed a probability of less than 0.5 in 10,000 that they were chance related. As a result of the high level of respondents and the significance of the original test a Chi-Square test was also conducted. The results showed a 0.0002 level of significance. We are therefore highly confident that organisations using ES have a significant difference in their maturity and utilisation of computer systems as compared to organisations that do not use them.

## 7. Conclusions

Not surprisingly, the majority of firms using ES are in the financial services sector. These companies mainly use them for routine decision-making (mort-

\- Are expensive to maintain, especially in an environment where technology is always changing.

\- Are only as good as the people that develop them. Problems can arise if the expert leaves the company.

\- ‘De-skill’ people and lack the human element of decision-making. If they are used to replace humans there can be ‘technology resistance’. They are thus a direct threat to human status. Organisations stated that users did not understand their system; that the system was too rigid and that they removed ‘individual flair’.

\- 'Are very difficult to implement-I suspect they were oversold in the mid-80s and did not bring the benefits proclaimed', according to one respondent.

It seems that IS-mature companies have taken up the challenge of ES in the past, but without any enthusiasm today.
