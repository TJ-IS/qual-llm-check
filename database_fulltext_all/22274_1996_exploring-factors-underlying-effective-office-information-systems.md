## Abstract

Office Automation (OA) traditionally emphasized productivity of the clerical workforce, ignoring higher-level office tasks. Office Information Systems (OIS) are meant to support office tasks done by managers and professionals within a company. By surveying local firms through telephone interviews, we investigate the factors responsible for effective OIS. We analyzed the results by partitioning responses by type of firm, firm size, degree of integration of OIS tools, top management support, and usage of OIS tools. Some findings are: professional service firms reported a greater improvement of productivity due to the introduction of OIS than non-professional service firms. A higher degree of system integration led to higher success rates of OIS in the perspective organization.

Keywords: Effectiveness; Integration of office systems; Office automation; Office information systems; Office productivity

## 1. Introduction

"Office technology has been tool-obsessed and product obsessed... Office technology does not have a concept of architectural integration" – Peter Keen [7]. Limited technological support has been provided for higher-level office tasks, though managerial applications are the best way to rationalize the investment in OA. Sheng et al. [9] introduced the concept of an Integrated Office Information System (IOIS) which consists of [integrated] multiple disparate office systems working in heterogeneous environments to support higher-level office tasks. The higher-level office tasks IOIS supports are the semi structured and unstructured tasks performed by managers and professionals. This is opposed to the concept of OA, the automation of lower-level, structured office tasks.

The present research attempts to determine the underlying factors that make OIS effective. Effectiveness is measured in three dimensions: office productivity of knowledge workers, satisfaction with OIS, and success of OIS. This research attempts to determine when OIS is effective, and thus to find areas where investment into OIS is worthwhile.

## 2. Previous research

Poppel [8] categorized OIS tools by function: personal processing, information transfer, information retrieval, conferencing, and activity management; he found that top management support was necessary for office systems to thrive. McLeod and Jones [7] believed that top management would be most interested in using voicemail and teleconferencing, since these methods would rely on oral communication, which had been shown (in other studies) to be the favored means of information gathering by executives; they hypothesize that top management would not use all OIS capabilities, but would delegate tasks involving information retrieval and facsimile transmission to clerical or staff employees to reduce time spent analyzing bulk data. Daft and Lengel [2] argued that managers would want to reduce equivocality or ambiguity using rich media, which provides quick feedback, use many channels and cues, and allow personalization and language variety. The technology for information transfer and conferencing provides richer communication for managers and also lets managers span boundaries of internal and geographical domains.

There are two kinds of integration: front-end and back-end. Front-end integration is ensured by providing a good interface for the user. A single menu system which encompasses all OIS subsystems would be an example. Back-end integration arises when there is integration and sharing of data and knowledge structures. A system in which data are easily transferred from program to program would exemplify a program with high back-end integration. Systems that can span an entire organization and access external databases will be more powerful tools to generate rich information and reduce ambiguity, as in the integrated databases discussed since the late 60s and early 70s.

Many people since then have stated that success of white-collar productivity tools is directly related to the degree of their integration into everyday business tasks. Igbaria and Nachman [5] found that the information product, including data output reliability and relevancy was important in user satisfaction with end user systems. Jih [6] maintains that a user friendly interface would result in more user satisfaction and hence more use.

## 3. Research method

A copy of the survey, including survey instructions, is given in Appendix A. The questionnaire was developed using the material of previous research tools. The survey covers six areas of OIS: software tools used, user profile, integration of OIS tools, productivity of knowledge workers, success measure of OIS, and satisfaction with OIS. Questions are most commonly answered with a yes/no choice or a choice of one to five on a Likert scale, with one meaning large decrease/very dissatisfied and five meaning large increase/very much satisfied. The advantages of the Likert scale are that it is easy to construct and respondents readily understand how to use the scale. A major portion of the survey was based on an instrument used by other researchers to examine user satisfaction with OA. This instrument was a modification of the Pearson and Bailey user satisfaction instrument. “This instrument was proved to be both reliable and valid by a number of researchers” [11]. Content validity of an instrument is a subjective evaluation of how well the content of a scale represents the measurement task at hand. The content validity is claimed due to the credence in the Pearson and Bailey instrument. Additionally, the content validity of the instrument was examined via testing with a graduate student volunteer, and the survey was edited for understandability prior to use.

## 3.1. The sample

Twenty company names were drawn from the Harris 1988 Ohio Services Directory and another twenty from the Harris 1991 Ohio Industrial Directory [3, 4]. Using a random selection of firms within the Cleveland area, the first five firms with 100 or more employees were picked. Then another random selection was made and another five firms picked, etc. Due to difficulties in contacting sample firms and getting usable results, an additional ten companies with more than 500 employees, were drawn from both Harris Directories to expand the sample base and make it more likely that the companies had a data processing (DP) function. Appendix B lists the final 60 firms.

Out of 32 firms contacted, 16 responses were usable instead of the 20 initially desired. Reasons for nonproductive contacts included a lack of time of the potential respondent (which averaged about 20 minutes to administer), company policy against allowing surveys, and our inability to contact a responsible party. The initial plan called for surveying a DP manager and a manager or professional in each firm. This plan was discarded due to time constraints. Out of the final 16 respondents, only two were non-DP managers or professionals.

## 3.2. Measurement

The initial plan was to survey an equal number of service and industrial firms. However, the distribution of final responses did not accommodate this plan. However, when analyzed by SIC code, the survey respondents fall into two groups of eight firms each: those with SIC codes in the 6000's and 8000's (offering professional services, such as law, accounting, banking, or real estate) and those firms with other SIC codes. Each respondent is coded as either belonging to the first group or not, since professionals outside of an industrial environment could have an entirely different need for OIS.

The calculated variables include the total number of OIS components used by the company (SWTOT), the percentage of all professional knowledge workers at that location (PERPRO), the product of the percentage of knowledge workers who have access to OIS and the percentage of OIS capacity used by those who have access (PERUSE), the average response to question four about productivity (AVPROD), the average response to question 5.1 about system success (AVSUC), and the average response to question 5.2 about user satisfaction (AVSAT). Another calculated measure is a total measure of integration (DEGINT) calculated by summing each respondent's scores for questions 3.1, 3.1.3, 3.2, and 3.4. The result is a whole number ranging from one to four which combines data about both front and back-end integration, intra-organizational integration, and connections with external systems.

He hypothesized that, after a learning curve effect, the first group would report a gain in productivity, success, and satisfaction, while the second group would report decreases in almost all measures. Others have found that executives use computer-based systems as only one means of gathering and disseminating information; verbal information and written information are very important information exchange mechanisms [12].

## 5.1. Partition by type of organization

The professional service firms in this sample used significantly more OIS components than the non-professional service firms and they also reported a higher average productivity rating.

This corresponds with the anecdotal observation of one industrial respondent who believes that OIS configuration is related to the configuration of the firm's production activities. He mentioned that his firm did not really need as much OIS as a law or accounting firm would, since its manufacturing and managerial organization was simple. Apparently, the professional service firms require a more intense OIS environment to increase productivity.

## 5.2. Partition by size of organization

Smaller firms in the sample tend to find significantly more productivity and success using OIS than larger firms. This may be due to a higher integration of the system as well as higher satisfaction with training (4.5 for small vs. 3.4 for large). Smaller organizations are also associated with higher percentage of use by top management and higher top management support compared to large organizations. Another implication is that in smaller organizations, purchases of OIS tools are made more carefully and employees make better use of them. In large organizations, there is less control of purchasing and the tools may be used only locally and ineffectively. However, the type of organization should also be taken into account in making conclusions from these results.

## 5.3. Partition by number of OIS components

The initial idea behind the split was to see if firms who kept their OIS applications simple were able to achieve efficiencies in productivity, success, and satisfaction. This is not borne out by the statistical tests. Thus the number of OIS components has no bearing on the effectiveness of OIS.

## 5.4. Partition by amount of OIS usage

The only significant result of partition based on the percentage of OIS actually used by managers and professionals is that the group of firms with high overall usage also had high top management usage.

## 5.5. Partition by degree of integration

Firms with high OIS integration reported significantly higher success, satisfaction with the OIS, and satisfaction with top management support. The higher success score for firms with high OIS integration agrees with previous studies $[1, 11]$ . Office productivity is higher in firms with good data integration. Thus OIS are effectively utilized when the tools are well-integrated within themselves and when the OIS subsystems are properly integrated with the physical organizational units. Then, top management can realize the benefits better and support ongoing use of the tools.

## 5.6. Partition by amount of top management support

Firms in which knowledge workers are more satisfied with top management support for OIS show a higher average productivity, success, and satisfaction. This lends considerable support to the notion that OIS success is a matter of top management support. There is no significant difference in software tools that are available in firms which have high and low support for OIS. The firms with less support generally use their OIS tools for low level tasks. Thus, the top management support for OIS, as for most other systems, is a key factor.

## 6. Correlation and regression analyses

Correlation analysis was performed on the data between independent, such as OIS tools and degree of integration, and dependent variables, such as office productivity, satisfaction, and success with OIS. There is a 0.488 correlation coefficient in the use of modem-based fax with the measure of more effective communications. This is the only software in the information transfer category significantly correlated with any productivity measure and provides support for Ugbah and Dewine's finding that the use of new communication technologies increases the perceived task-related use of data [10]. However, we found an overall weak correlation of information transfer and conferencing tools with productivity measures. Also, the correlation of the percentage of total use of OIS capacity with the displayed variables is not significant.

The average satisfaction score is significantly correlated with the percentage of top management who use the OIS in place. Satisfaction with top management support for OIS is very highly correlated with the average productivity, success, and overall satisfaction measures. Thus top management involvement is a strong factor for OIS success. Also notable is the nonsignificant correlation of top management support with the percentage of top management who uses OIS. This relationship supports the view that there is no need for managers to use OIS to support its use by others, although both measures tend to move in the same direction.

Both average productivity rating (AVPROD) and average success rating (AVSUC) were regressed against the total software used (SWTOT), degree of integration (DEGINT), average satisfaction (AVSAT), percentage of top management who use OIS (USE6), whether the firm is a professional service firm (SERV), the percentage of knowledge workers who are professional (PERPRO), and a dummy variable indicating satisfaction with top management support for OIS (1 of SAT6 is $\geq4$ ). The equation for average productivity was not significant (p value of 0.1589) but the equation for average success was significant (p value of 0.0428). Its adjusted $R^{2}$ was 55.9% and the only coefficients with any significance were SERV (p value of 0.1024) and the dummy for top management support (p value of 0.0514). The idea that professional service firms might have a differential utility for OIS appears to have more support here.

## 7. Conclusion

Sixteen companies with locations in the Cleveland area were surveyed about their use of OIS. The survey gathered information about the software and hardware tools used, OIS user census, degree of integration, effect on productivity of knowledge workers, user evaluation of success, and user satisfaction with OIS. The sample showed that (a) 35% of top management in the surveyed companies used OIS (b) the average respondent saw an increase in productivity due to OIS use (c) on the average, knowledge workers are satisfied with support and success of OIS.

Professional service firms indicated a greater increase in productivity due to OIS use, possibly due to differences in the type of activities in the two groups. Professional firms used less OIS for management applications, indicating that they are still using the traditional OA approach and there is need to reorient them. Smaller firms reported greater productivity and effectiveness gains than larger (national or worldwide) firms, possibly due to higher system integration and/or satisfaction with OIS training. Firms with a higher degree of integration in their OIS reported significantly higher success and satisfaction. Firms that experienced higher satisfaction with top management support also were characterized by significantly greater OIS productivity, success, and average satisfaction measures.
