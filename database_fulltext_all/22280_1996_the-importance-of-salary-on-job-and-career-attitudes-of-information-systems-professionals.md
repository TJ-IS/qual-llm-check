# The importance of salary on job and career attitudes of information systems professionals $^{1}$

Ephraim R. McLean $^{a,*}$ , Stanley J. Smits $^{b}$ , John R. Tanner $^{c}$

$^{a}$ Department of Computer Information systems, College of Business Administration, Georgia State University, P.O. Box 4015, Atlanta, Georgia 30302-4015, USA

$^{b}$ Department of Management, College of Business Administration, Georgia State University, P.O. Box 4014, Atlanta, Georgia 30302-4014, USA

## Abstract

In labeling pay or money as a “mere” hygiene factor, Frederick Herzberg set off a controversy that has continued up to the present by asking: Does money matter? Our study, involving several hundred I/S graduates over a four-year period, measures the importance of “above-average salaries” to these graduates as they progress through the early months of their I/S careers. Initially, salary is ranked highly; but as time passes, other factors tend to crowd out salary in importance. It loses its role as a motivator; and, as Herzberg suggests, becomes a hygiene factor.

Keywords: Careers; Money; Motivation; Pay; Salary

## 1. Introduction

Most people shape their behavior through an iterative process of learning, involving mental rehearsal, action, and feedback. For people entering a new career or occupation, this process involves attempting to perform according to certain standards, observing the consequences, and making the necessary adjustments. These observations may lead to an increased effort (“I need to improve”), settling into a routine (“I’m doing okay”), or quitting (“I don’t fit here"). The feedback underlying these observations may take many forms, such a performance monitoring and coaching by supervisors, self-comparisons with peers, and formal performance appraisals and rewards. A common metric in our society for assessing one's career progress is money. Questions such as "How well does my chosen career pay?", "How am I compensated in comparison to my peers?", and "Is my salary stable or changing?" are typical.

Herzberg et al. [12] started an enduring controversy in 1959 with the publication of their research labeling money as a “hygiene” variable having, at best, a small effect on the motivation to work. Their research, criticized for overgeneralizing beyond its limited sample of occupational groups (accountants and engineers), was rejected as “academic nonsense”

by most business students and employers [5]. Others contend that the intentions reported by individuals are not always consistent with their actions. They may say “Money doesn’t matter as much as other things”; but their subsequent actions belie their statements. Not surprisingly, the controversy continues unabated till the present time.

While popular opinion comes down clearly on the side of money as a motivator, a more in-depth look results in a more complex picture. Even such sacrosanct notions as pay for performance are called into question: “Tying a cash incentive to a task diminishes the inherent attractiveness of that task” [22]. Citing the “age-old concern for equity,” Cumming [3] contends that “the typical pay system does not provide enough leeway to reward really good performers without unfairly penalizing satisfactory but unexceptional workers who also must be retained and kept motivated.”

Deci [6] contended that money does not always have the impact those administering it intend it to have. He hypothesized that contingent (i.e., monetary) reward decreases the intrinsic interest in a task. A study by McNeill and Kimmel [17] examined how individuals who are intrinsically motivated to perform a cognitive problem-solving task react when they are offered a monetary incentive for doing it. Their results suggested that the offer of money dramatically reduces an individual's intrinsic motivation and actually impairs performance. More recently Alfie Kohn argues that:

“Pay does not motivate people. People need enough money to live, but they need more than money to thrive. When polled on what matters most in a job, employees consistently cite half-a-dozen priorities, such as interesting work, before they mention pay. In contrast, when asked what employees care about, employers consistently rank money first” [2].

## 2. A synopsis of theory and earlier findings

Theories of motivation, such as Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs [13], Alderfer's ERG Theory [1], and Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory [12], treat money as a lower-order need; that is, one that must be met for the purpose of survival. Maslow considers a need to have “drive potential”; that is, an unmet need causes behaviors that are perceived to be a way of satisfying it. When it is perceived as having been met, it no longer results in the need-satisfying behavior.

In Herzberg's schema, money is considered a hygiene factor. In his terms, hygiene factors define the environment or context within which work occurs. When these factors (e.g., salary, job security, working conditions, status, company procedures, quality of supervision, quality of interpersonal relationships) are arrayed on a continuum, they account for a work environment ranging from inadequate (where dissatisfaction is present) to adequate (where no dissatisfaction is present). Motivational factors, on the other hand, are a set of intrinsic factors (e.g., achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement, the possibility for growth) associated with the work itself. When they are absent, the work provides little or no satisfaction (and thus is not motivating); but when they are present, the work itself is a source of satisfaction (see Figure 1).

Herzberg's theory has been labeled as the “most criticized” of the motivation theories [7]. Money – pay, salary – is a major source of confusion and contention in Herzberg's theory, because it provides the means for meeting lower-order needs (the capacity to buy food, clothing, and shelter) and it provides a quantitative index that can be used to measure achievement, recognition, and advancement.

## 2.1. Previous findings

Gauging one's achievement, recognition, and advancement is part of the process variously labeled as “organizational socialization” [23], “vocational development” [11], or “career maturation.” McLean et al. [15] reported the presence of income as a strong hygiene factor on their Job Preference Inventory scale by college students preparing to enter the I/S field. An I/S job that “provides above-average income” was one of the six variables that loaded on the hygiene factor.

The characteristics preferred in an I/S job are moderated initially by one's need for achievement [26], career certainty, work self-concept, and selected characteristics of the academic programs conferring the I/S degree [14]. After a period of I/S employment, the perceived discrepancies between the characteristics preferred in an I/S job and the realities of the job are moderated by the length of employment and the salary level attained. These preference-reality discrepancy scores, in turn, influence job and career attitudes [16].

## 3. The present study

Beginning in 1987, more than 1,000 graduating I/S majors from 38 colleges and universities volunteered to participate in a longitudinal study of I/S career progression and maturation [16]. An initial survey and two post-employment follow-ups made it possible to link pre-graduation job characteristic preferences with the realities of I/S employment and to assess the impact of met and unmet preferences on job and career attitudes.

## 3.1. Research methodology

## 3.1.1. Instrumentation

The instruments used to develop the pre-graduation database have been described extensively in earlier publications $[14, 15, 16, 26]$ . Only one Likert-scale item from the Job Preference Inventory was used in this study: “How important is it for you to be in an organization where work provides above-average salaries?”

The first follow-up survey contained 20 job and career attitude statements about the participants' present job and future career plans. Five-point Likert scales ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree were used by the respondents to rate each item. The items came from two sources: (a) the Job Satisfaction Index developed by Miller and Muthard [18] and validated in a national study of employment retention and turnover by Smits [25]; and (b) the general job satisfaction, autonomy, work overload, job value, job involvement, and group effectiveness subscales of the Organizational Climate Scale [24]. The various sections of the Job Satisfaction Index have split-half reliability coefficients ranking from 0.47 to 0.89 with the mean, using the Fisher Z transformation, being 0.80. The subscales of the Organizational Climate Scale from which the items were selected for the first follow-up study had Cronbach alphas ranging from 0.62 to 0.90.

The second follow-up study focused on organizational commitment, vocational development, and career progression. The instrumentation consisted of the 15-item Organizational Commitment Scale, developed by Mowday et al. [20]; the Vocational Development Scales [11], consisting of eight single-item scales; and the Career Progression Scale developed by Thompson et al. [27] to assess their Four-Stage Career Model [4].

## 3.1.2. Subjects

At the time the first follow-up survey was conducted, our database contained 920 former I/S students who had been graduates for six months or longer. Of these, 35 had missing or incomplete addresses and could not be mailed. Of the 885 survey forms mailed out, 109 were returned undeliverable. It is not possible to determine how many of the remaining 776 former I/S students actually received the instrument because it is easier to discard unwanted mail than to return it to the sender marked as “Not known at this address.” Therefore, the 326 responses probably represent far more than the known return of 42% (326 ÷ 776).

The second follow-up was mailed to the 326 respondents of the first follow-up. Of these, 53 were returned undeliverable along with 149 completed survey forms (54%). Of the 148 respondents, 136 were employed in I/S jobs. Twenty-five of the respondents to the second follow-up were from two schools that had entered the database too late to be included in the first follow-up. Therefore, there are 111 former I/S students with complete data. Depending upon the analysis, the sample size therefore ranges from 111 to 136.

As always, the issue of non-respondant bias was a source of concern. Profiles of the final sample were compared to the original database of subjects and no statistically significant differences were found. Therefore, although this is not a proof that there are no differences between the opinions of the respondents and the non-respondants, it provides at least partial support for the notion.

The variables and factor loadings for these dependent variables are presented in Table 1. Job Satisfaction was the response to the Likert-scale item “In general, I am satisfied with my job” using a five-point scale, where “5” indicated strong agreement; and Career Certainty was the response to the five-point Likert-scale item “Now that you have had an opportunity to gain work experience in I/S, how certain are you about having chosen it as your career?" where "5" indicated high certainty.

The research models presented in Figure 2 and Figure 3 enabled us to address these three research questions:

1. What is the relationship between present salary level and the following contextual variables: (a) time since graduation, (b) degree earned, (c) number of I/S jobs held, and (d) job tenure?

2. To what degree does the importance associated with salary prior to graduation and present salary level moderate (a) present career outlook, (b) long-term career outlook, (c) job satisfaction, and (d) career certainty?

3. To what degree does the importance associated with salary prior to graduation and present salary level moderate scores on (a) the Organizational Commitment Scale, and (b) the Vocational Development Scales?

## 3.1.4. Statistical analyses

Pre-graduation ratings assigned to the importance of above-average salary were combined with the post-employment salary levels attained to classify the former I/S students responding to the second follow-up in four groups. These groups are defined in Table 2. The definitions serve as the four experimental conditions under which job and career attitudes are shaped during the two periods of career advancement (first follow-up =22 months and second follow-up =46 months). The two levels of the importance of income and the two levels of attained income form the basis for a $2 \times 2$ factorial design with job and career attitudes as the dependent variables.

## 3.2. Results

With respect to the first research question, the relationships between salary level at the first follow-up and the selected contextual variables are as follows:

1. Salary level and time (in months) since graduation: r = 0.32; p < 0.01

2. Salary level and degree (undergraduate vs. graduate): $F(1, 131 \text{ df}) = 15.23; p < 0.001$

3. Salary level and number of full-time I/S jobs held since graduation: r = 0.14; not significant at p = 0.05

4. Salary level and time (in months) with current employer: r = 0.20; p < 0.05

As can be seen, the most significant contextual variable influencing salary level is the level of degree earned.

The results relevant to the second research question are presented in Table 2. The various multivariate tests of significance [8] indicate that salary importance has a significant impact (p < 0.0001) on the four dependent variables. Furthermore, the univariate F-tests indicate that the most significant among these variables is Present Career Outlook (p < 0.0001).

Returning to the research questions in the light of these results, the following results are notable:

1. The present salary level is related significantly to the level of degree, time since graduation, and time with current employer.

2. At the time of the first follow-up study, shortly after graduation, there is a significant relationship between salary importance and career outlook.

## 4. Discussion

Undoubtedly, salary is a more complex and subtle variable than suggested by our research design. First, salary accounts for only approximately 72% of the total compensation paid to employees [21, 28]. Second, satisfaction with salary involves four facets – level, benefits, raises, and structure and administration [10]. As Deci [6] pointed out two decades ago “... it is not the money per se which motivates performance but rather the way that it is administered....” In our study, there was no information regarding non-salary forms of compensation. It treated salary as a unidimensional variable and asked nothing about the way in which salary was administered. The study did, however, track salary importance over a period of approximately four years.

The analyses furnish us with some interesting results. As expected, salary levels differ significantly among recent graduates with undergraduate versus graduate degrees; and salary levels are positively related to how long one has been out of school and on the job. On the other hand, people who see salary increases as a reliable means of improving job satisfaction and organizational commitment may find the lack of a relationship between them in this study disconcerting.

Two observations seem most relevant. First, salary seems to provide a benchmark or measure used by recent I/S graduates to gauge their present career status and the degree to which their choice of an I/S career fits their personality and ability. The salary level they have attained helps them evaluate their decision to enter the I/S field. They use the tangible nature of salary to evaluate such intangibles as competence, meaning, success, and interest; that is, the four variables we defined as Present Career Outlook. Second, the difference between salary importance and job satisfaction at the first follow-up and organizational commitment at the second follow-up supports Herzberg's contention that salary is a hygiene factor.

Does this mean that I/S employers need not concern themselves with the pay scales of employees? No. Herzberg's theory of motivation and others regarding employment turnover (e.g., [9, 19, 29]) state that if salaries are seen as inadequate, dissatisfaction, turnover intentions, and ultimate employment turnover follow. This study suggests two implications of importance to the management: (a) I/S employers should prevent dissatisfaction by paying salaries that are perceived to be equitable, and (b) salaries alone will not result in positive long-term career outlooks, job satisfaction, or organizational commitment.

Thus Herzberg's theory supports commendably the importance of job restructuring as a way to improve employee's motivation and satisfaction. To accomplish this, managers should re-engineer I/S jobs to add a sense of responsibility; increase authority and autonomy to give ownership to decision making; provide more frequent and more in-depth feedback on performance; hold I/S professionals accountable for meeting performance goals and then reward them accordingly; structure their careers to provide opportunities for personal growth and development; and increase their sense of achievement through increasing their responsibility, autonomy, and accountability.

## 5. Conclusions

As discussed above, our study provides support for the notion that salary is not a long-term source of work motivation. While an adequate salary is necessary in order to prevent dissatisfaction and employment turnover, particularly initially, it does not necessarily lead to long-term job satisfaction and organizational commitment.

The challenge of I/S managers was appropriately stated by Herzberg nearly forty years ago: “If you want people motivated to do a good job, give them a good job to do.”
