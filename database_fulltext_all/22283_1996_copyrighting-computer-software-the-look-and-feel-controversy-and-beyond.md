# Copyrighting computer software: The “look and feel” controversy and beyond

Bijoy Bordoloi $^{a,*}$ , Paul Ilami $^{a}$ , Peter P. Mykytyn Jr. $^{a}$ and Kathleen Mykytyn $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Information Systems and Management Sciences University of Texas at Arlington, Arlington, TX 76019, USA $^{b}$ 1830 Carriage House Circle, #1603, Arlington, TX 76011, USA

## Abstract

In today's society, the development of new technologies generally outpaces the laws and regulations created to protect them. This is precisely what has happened in copyright cases involving the copyrightability of the “look and feel” of a program (the visual displays and specific command keystrokes that make a computer program unique). Currently, regulatory agencies and judicial interpretation of copyright law is not precise, leading to uncertainty and a lack of uniformity. Until the courts and/or agencies reach some consistency in their rulings, software developers and individual programmers are left in a quandary as to when charges of copyright infringement might be served. Although it may be difficult to achieve consistent interpretation, US case law does provide some guidance that can aid US developers in not only protecting their work, but also avoiding charges of copyright infringement. This paper examines the current US copyright laws and limitations with special attention to “look and feel.” International copyright aspects, as outlined in the Berne Convention, the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), and the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) are also addressed. It also reviews several cases that deal with (or have somehow impacted) questions surrounding “look and feel,” along with commentaries on their individual impacts, evaluates where copyright laws stand today, and discusses some possible alternatives for software developers based on the results of recent cases.

Keywords: Computer industry legal issues; Copyright infringement; Information systems legal issues; International aspects of computer software; Lawsuits and litigation; “Look and feel” of computer software; Software copyright

## 1. Introduction

Driven by reduced costs and improvements, computers are becoming increasingly useful. Speed and transfer rates continue to increase as competitors struggle for industry footholds. And like computers, software is evolving in a similar manner.

William F. Ogburn first coined “cultural lag” to mean the characteristic gap created when the faster rate of technological change in a society outpaces the slower moving non-material culture; this includes the law. For purposes of this paper, it especially refers to copyright law. This syndrome has resulted in the application of traditional copyright rules to new computer technology in US courts as well as the criticism of more progressive approaches $[15]$ . One of the more representative examples of this concerns the so-called “look and feel” controversy. From a legal perspective, the technical nature of the disputes and the terminology used makes trial proceedings difficult for litigators, who often are essentially lay persons. It can also be confusing for software developers who may be unsure of what their positions are in terms of copyright laws and regulations, especially with respect to the international community.

Although there are other forms of legal protection for computer software (patents, trade secrets, and trade dress), here the primary concern is issues surrounding the application of copyright protection, where much of this particular controversy rests. Oftentimes, software companies in the US have found themselves in competition with unauthorized, pirated copies of their own products. This was brought to light in early 1995 with respect to the Peoples Republic of China (PRC) and its reluctance to deal with software pirates in its country. More specifically, software piracy in Taiwan resulted in an estimated loss of over \$700 million to US software companies in 1990, with losses of \$400 million due to piracy in the PRC. The difficulties with the PRC have continued into 1996 as well.

These issues become more pronounced with the advent of international trade agreements such as the Geneva-based General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), the Berne Convention, and the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA). Large multi-million dollar corporations and small turnkey operations both have large stakes in the formulation of new laws and copyright protections included in international trade agreements. To date, many software companies have been embroiled in copyright litigation and related controversies that have involved, among other things, the “look and feel” of a program. In fact, some of the recent cases in the US involve some very well known companies (Broderbund, Lotus, Apple, and Microsoft, for example). Most decisions are crucial to these companies’ profits. Therefore, this controversy should be of interest to all software developers.

## 2. "Look and feel" - a general definition

In general, one could define “look and feel” as the visual setup of screens used in the interface between the person and the program. As a user becomes familiar with a particular program, he/she grows accustomed to its interface. This is a very broad definition and it is easier to describe what it does not encompass.

In terms of software, if a user cannot see an element of a computer program, then it is not part of its “look and feel.” This naturally excludes the source code, object code, or any internal structure or organization. Similarly, micro code, disk operating systems, and any other programs executed “behind the screens” are not covered. Additionally, any user support (such as textual documents and training) is not included. What is left are any elements of the program that a user can perceive (see, hear, etc.) and use for interaction with the computer, such as the user interface [16].

In recent years, the role of user-friendly software has become more important. In fact, a program's “look and feel” could be its most important, marketable factor today. Thus, it is necessary to protect it as an investment. Numerous cases have been brought to US courts during the past 10–15 years dealing with this perspective of computer software, illustrating the importance that it plays in the total spectrum of copyright of computer software.

## 3. Computer software and copyright laws

## 3.1. History

In the past, opponents of software copyright protection argued that a patent offered greater protection for a computer program than a copyright. In fact, the US Copyright Office began accepting computer programs for registration as “books” in 1964. Although registration did not automatically mean that software was copyrightable, it did reflect the view of many authorities both in and outside of the US Copyright Office that computer programs possess ample innovation and authorship to be copyrightable; further, they do not bear the fatal defects of the utility-nonutility or idea-expression dichotomies discussed later [10]. The US Supreme Court in 1972 settled the issue (temporarily) when it stated that copyright protection, rather than patent protection, would be the avenue available for computer programs.

In the US, the initial responsibility of copyright protection rests with the US Congress. Pursuant to this power, the legislative body has enacted federal copyright laws: a recent example is the Copyright Act of 1976, which provides copyright protection for original works of authorship fixed in any tangible medium of expression, including books, articles, records, and films. In order to help the computer software industry, Congress chartered the National Commission on New Technological Uses of Copyrighted Works (“CONTU”) in 1980. This body made recommendations that led to the adoption of the Computer Software Copyright Act of 1980, which amended Section 101 of Title 17 of the US Code (U.S.C.) to include the definition of “computer program.” This definition assured that software was no longer simply a literary work but had its own category. In fact, copyright has been the dominant method of legal software protection in the US because it is relatively easy to claim, inexpensive to register, and provides broad, though not complete, legal protection.

## 3.2. US copyright limitations and concepts

Computer software, however, has been difficult to protect under copyright law in the US. The problem is compounded as the number of computer software programs continues to increase. It has been estimated that approximately 15 000 computer programs are written in the US each day and the total value of this software reaches tens of billions of dollars. However, some programs may even be ineligible for copyright, not because they are computer programs, but because they may either lack minimal originality or constitute the only way of achieving some particular result, so that the program itself may be characterized as manifesting an idea rather than one among many possible ways of expressing that idea.

There are other problems with copyright protection. A US court summarized the limitations/concepts in a 1988 ruling: "In no case does copyright protection for an original work of authorship extend to any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle, or discovery, regardless of the form in which it is described, explained, illustrated, or embodied in such work” [1]. This is the result of several “judge-made” (case) laws, (1) the idea-expression dichotomy, (2) the limitation against functional (utilitarian) items, and (3) the literal/nonliteral distinction. Each is now briefly discussed.

The idea-expression dichotomy limits copyright protection to the expression of ideas, but not the ideas themselves. There are sometimes a few ways to express an idea, or the expression may be bound up in the idea; thus, the idea and the expression merge to making the expression uncopyrightable. In a merger situation, protecting the expression would be to grant sole use of the idea to the first person to express it, creating a monopoly. This rationale, however, does not apply if the situation falls under patent law. Under patent law, the idea itself may be patented. Additional patent aspects are discussed in the next paragraph. A computer spreadsheet is an example of an idea because it is necessary to arrange the screen in a two-dimensional form to allow for mathematical calculations. The expression of the spreadsheet idea comes in the form of many different styles of spreadsheets available from many vendors. Each may copyright its expression of the spreadsheet idea, but not the idea of the spreadsheet itself. Perhaps one of the earliest judicial opinions of the concept of idea vs. expression was presented by Judge Learned Hand, a US judge appointed to the US Court of Appeals in 1924. His opinion pertaining to literary creations was based not on infringement occurring at a particular point, but rather along a continuum based on the development of characters and sequence.

A second copyright limitation involves utilitarian aspects of computers. If an aspect of a program is used as a process, system or operation, it cannot be protected by copyright: it is covered by patent law. A widely followed doctrine governing the patent protection of software is promoted by the European Patent Office. This position generally holds that patent protection is applicable to software if its application has a ‘technical effect.’ For example, software that controls electronic engine timing is patentable under this proposition. How the user interface of a computer program functions has little utility and is thus copyrightable. As another example, the “look and feel” of a word processor is irrelevant to the courts as long as it has the ability to create documents and edit others. However, the functional and nonfunctional aspects of a program must be separable. If they are, the nonfunctional aspects are protectable under copyright law.

The third important concept is the literal/nonliteral distinction. The literal components include the source, object, and micro-code whereas nonliteral parts relate to the sequence, structure, and organization of the program, as well as the screen output, which is usually called the user interface today. The “look and feel” issues relate to the nonliteral components. The literal elements generally are protectable by copyright, and some nonliteral elements of expression are also subject to copyright protection. However, US courts have not been unanimous regarding precise definitions of nonliteral components.

## 3.3. Copyrights and "look and feel"

In 1988, the US Copyright Office announced that it would only accept a single registration of a program and that this would protect code and visual displays. The Office concluded that code and screen displays are integrally related and ordinarily form a single work.

The final report of CONTU did not make any recommendations on the copyrightability of the “look and feel” of a program, but there are two opinions on this. On the one hand, a majority of people commenting to CONTU, including software industry representatives, feared that strong protection will lead to software monopolization and increased costs. They also felt that the length of copyrights (the life of the author plus 50 years after his/her death or 75 years from publication in the case of a corporation) is too long. The other view expressed by the rest supports strong protection to help small developers, innovation in general, and research investments. Each side agrees that copyright protection is required, but they fail to agree on the optimal type.

## 4. Computer software and the international community

In the international arena, protection of intellectual property presents increasing challenges for software developers: US copyright holders can utilize the services of the US Customs Service to block infringing software from entering the US and international trade agreements have generally addressed issues surrounding copyrights.

## 4.1. US Customs Service

When the Customs Service suspects that some imported work infringes a copyright, the work can be detained pending the outcome of an investigation. If it determines that there is an attempt to import piratic copies of a recorded work (i.e., one made under some circumstances which would violate the copyright law [7]), the copies can be seized and forfeiture proceedings instituted. If the importer challenges the seizure, any defense permitted in a US federal court proceeding under the Copyright Act would be allowed. This process takes place under the auspices of the Customs Service district director and, even though the burden of proving infringement falls upon the copyright holder, no judicial authority is invoked.

Unfortunately, the Customs Service has not issued rulings dealing with the “look and feel” aspects of software. Generally, Customs applies the “ordinary observer” test to determine infringement issues. Under this test, the two works would be compared to determine if their appeal to an ordinary observer would be the same. Still, the Customs Service offers effective methods to US software developers for dealing with infringing imports by providing the personnel and means to block the entry of such imports at the border of the US. Software developers should therefore register copyrighted material with the Customs Service to facilitate pursuing seizures and forfeitures when piratic copies are imported.

## 4.2. Berne Convention

The Berne Convention, created in 1886, was established to provide protection for intellectual property in Europe. Since that time, it has undergone revisions to meet technological developments and changing conditions relating to international uniformity in copyright protection. In 1989, the US became a signatory to the Berne Convention through President Reagan's signing of the Berne Convention Implementation Act. The Act is stated to be the foremost treaty for the protection of intellectual property [9].

Twenty-four countries are members of Berne, which is administered by the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) in Geneva, Switzerland. Intellectual property is protected if it is made available or published in a country that is a member of the Berne Convention, regardless of the citizenship of the author or creator. The impact of this document has been felt in most international agreements bearing on the intellectual property rights of artists and authors. Essentially, it appears to be the standard for protection against copyright infringement and is used extensively as a guide for other international trade agreements such as NAFTA and GATT. The Berne Convention's importance is also indicated by the fact that the European Community's Directive for Legal protection of Computer Programs, adopted in May 1991 by the European Economic Community, provides protection to computer programs within the ambit of the Berne Convention.

One aspect of the Berne Convention deals with the recognition of authors' moral rights. These rights refer to those inherent rights of authorship that exist in persons who create intellectual works, separate and apart from rights created by copyright law. They are intended to control the integrity of artists' creations and protect their interests in the quality of their work and their reputation. Authors can also object to a use or distortion of their works, even if they do not own or never owned the copyright, if it would injure their reputation or honor. Distinguished from economic or pecuniary rights, which are protected in the US by US copyright laws, moral rights, also termed personal rights, give the author rights to withhold publication, claim authorship, prevent destruction or mutilation of the work, and be free of excessive criticism.

France has emerged as the leader in recognizing moral rights with Germany and Italy following closely behind. One provision of the French statute provides that authors shall enjoy the right of respect for their name, authorship and work that shall be perpetual, inalienable, and imprescriptible. The right to integrity in the German statute states that authors have the right to prohibit any distortion or any other mutilation which would prejudice lawful intellectual or personal interest in their works. In Italy, artists can oppose any distortion, mutilation, or other modification capable of prejudicing their honor or reputation.

Typically, moral rights have not been accepted in the US, but based on various common law doctrines, such as defamation and misrepresentation, courts have frequently upheld what amounts to claims of moral right. The legislation by which the US acceded to the Berne Convention specifically states that the Convention's terms are not binding without legislation implementing its specific provisions, including moral rights. Thus, it remains to be seen whether further federal or state legislation will adopt specific moral rights provisions or whether federal and state courts will use the Berne provisions to broadly protect moral rights. In the sense that global competition is integral to many activities today, US software developers competing internationally must recognize some of the peculiarities existing today as a result of international agreements.

## 4.3. NAFTA

Foster and Alexander [6] note that NAFTA offers greater protection for companies' intellectual property rights than any other international agreement. It establishes minimum standards for the protection and enforcement of intellectual property rights in North America and requires each country to offer the citizens of another country no less favorable treatment than it offers its own citizens in the protection and enforcement of such rights.

The copyright provisions under NAFTA extend to all works embodying original expression, as well as those covered by the Berne Convention. Protection is accorded to computer programs as literary works and permits their creators to: (1) prohibit commercial rental of copies of software; (2) authorize or prohibit reproductions, distributions, performances or displays of the work to the public; and (3) prohibit the unauthorized importation of copies of a copyrighted work.

Since NAFTA designates software as literary works as defined by the Berne Convention, software developers will be offered more comprehensive copyright protection than is currently available in the US. Unfortunately, the escape clauses in NAFTA could be used by a company to confine or limit its copyright protection obligations. Further, copyright protection under NAFTA is weakened because the moral rights provision of the Berne Convention is excluded. On the whole, however, NAFTA does provide significant protection for software developers.

## 4.4. GATT

Under the Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights, Annexure 1C of GATT, both computer programs and data compilations are treated as copyrightable literary works. Basically, GATT contains three components: standards, enforcement, and basic principles.

The Agreement requires participating nations to protect software as literary works and mandates compliance with the international minimum standards contained in the Berne Convention. Enforcement of copyright standards requires that the participating nations make available remedies both in their courts and at their borders [14].

With respect to software, GATT extends copyright protections to expressions and not to ideas, procedures, and methods of operation, etc. It is thus possible for a competitor to decompile object code in copyrighted software through reverse engineering to obtain the source code. As long as an “expression” is not incorporated in any new software, there is no copyright infringement. Legal scholars note that the same difficulties that exist in the US, e.g., “look and feel” infringement issues, are still present under GATT [11].

## 4.5. Some current international issues

Reichman has also suggested that few copyright problems will arise in the international community as long as the main issue deals with a high level, surface expression, such as program code, or unless wholesale duplication has occurred. Beyond that, however, appropriate bodies in developing countries, such as courts and commentators, disagree on how to distinguish between the expression of a program, which is copyrightable, and its unprotectable ideas; further, no consensus has emerged concerning the proper scope of protection in cases that relate to nonliteral elements, especially those pertaining to structure, sequence, and organization.

Reichman also raises some concern relevant to recent US court decisions (e.g., Sega Enters., Ltd. vs. Accolade, Inc. [12]) regarding reverse engineering, wherein so-called second comers can pursue their interests to reverse engineer a copyrighted computer program. In a sense, then, noncopyrightable components have increased in number. If the same matter is extrapolated to developing countries, it suggests that software developers might feel free to pursue similar courses of action, such as reverse engineering.

## 5. A review of the US legal environment surrounding “look and feel”

There have been a number of cases in the US and other countries that document the progression of software copyright issues. We review a number of them to highlight the uncertainty facing software developers in the US and elsewhere. The matter is uncertain because the US courts have not been unanimous about the nonliteral elements of software.

## 5.1. Whelan Associates, Inc. vs. Jaslow Dental Laboratory, Inc.

In 1986 Jaslow Dental Laboratory, Inc. hired Whelan Associates to develop a computer program to computerize Jaslow; the program was called Dentalab. The agreement between Jaslow and Whelan also specified that Whelan could sell the program to others and that Jaslow would receive a ten percent commission on such sales. In the interim, Jaslow had also been developing its own version of Dentalab, called Dentcom, which would run on smaller computers and which was expected to have a larger potential market. The agreement between Jaslow and Whelan was eventually terminated, with each party considering itself the owner of Dentalab. Jaslow then marketed both programs, and Whelan continued to market the Dentalab program.

The resulting case [17] is considered a landmark one, because the US district and appellate courts struggled with the copyrightability of the underlying structure of the program. Copying of the source or object code was not at issue; in fact, the Dentcom program was written in a different language for a different computer. The appeals court upheld the district court's ruling of substantial similarity and announced a new rule that extended copyright protection beyond the source and object code to the program's nonliteral elements. In this case, these elements included virtually identical file structures and screen outputs as well as several key subroutines that performed virtually identically and contained overall structural similarities. In essence, copyright protection thus encompassed the structure, sequence, and organization of computer programs.

The court concluded that the CONTU Final Report, in its reference to the copyrightability of flow charts, supported its approach. However, this integrative approach addressed the idea-expression dichotomy in a simplistic manner in that it assumed that only one idea underlies any computer program. Such a conclusion is unrealistic, given the nature of computer programs and their complex structure of subroutines. The court has been criticized for its ruling, in that it was providing too much protection: whatever is not an idea is considered an expression and thus copyrightable. It has been argued that the court should have provided a much more definitive meaning of idea, thus limiting the impact of its ruling. In effect, it was felt that programmers would be at a disadvantage in trying to create competing programs that would be too similar to those in existence at the time. Critics also believed that protecting the program's structure would hinder progress in developing software.

## 5.2. Lotus Development Corp. vs. Paperback Software International

In 1990 Lotus Development Corp. charged Paperback Software International with copyright infringement of the user interface of Lotus' spreadsheet program, Lotus 1-2-3 [8]. The case was one of the most watched, because it dealt with one of the industry's most popular and well known programs and because it brought "look and feel" to the forefront.

At about the time Lotus developed 1-2-3, another programmer had been working on his own spreadsheet program. The menu hierarchy of the user interface of his program, among other program aspects, was completed even before he had seen 1-2-3 in operation. He believed, however, that to make his program marketable and successful, it needed to be compatible with 1-2-3. To accomplish this, he changed the menu hierarchy to conform to that of 1-2-3 and then marketed the program, called VP-Planner, as a 1-2-3 “workalike.”

Similar to the decision in the Jaslow-Whelan case, the district court found that copyright infringement of computer software can occur without any literal copying. Rejecting Paperback's contention that copyrightability should be based on a literal-nonliteral distinction, the court believed that both the CONTU Final Report and US congressional intent supported an analysis on the idea-expression distinction. It proceeded to develop a three-part test to determine copyrightability.

First, the court noted that even as the idea of an electronic spreadsheet is not copyrightable, a particular expression of such an idea may be. Second, the court addressed the issue of functionality, holding that the expressive nature of the menu structure was separable from the utilitarian nature, e.g., the use of the “/” key to enter the menu command system. Finally, the court addressed whether there was substantial similarity between 1-2-3 and VP-Planner. The court listened to expert testimony and attempted to place itself in the position of a lay observer, concluding that the similarities overwhelmed the differences from the perspective of both an expert and an ordinary viewer. Thus, infringement was found, even though VP-Planner’s user interface was slightly different in that it included additional elements.

Several criticisms resulted from the court's findings. First, affording protection to the user interface inhibits the constitutional goal of promoting progress. Another criticism is that copying the user interface of what is considered a standard product is necessary to achieve standardization and compatibility in the software industry. The court rejected this argument, however, stating that compatibility and standardization cannot prevail over the rights of authors. A final criticism against the court's finding is that programmers should be free to borrow and improve upon the ideas of other programmers, since innovation occurs by building on previous work. Again, the court rejected this argument, finding instead that adequate room for innovation remains even though other software developers cannot copy expressions of ideas from previous developers.

## 5.3. Brown Bag Software vs. Symantec Corp.

An independent programmer, John Friend, developed an outlining program called PC-Outline which he subsequently sold to Brown Bag Software; he also retained a non-exclusive right to part of the source code. Subsequently, he developed another outlining program called Grandview. In turn, he sold this program to Symantec, which marketed the program as an update to its own outlining program. Brown Bag sued Symantec and Friend alleging that the Grandview user interface was substantially similar to that of PC-Outline and that it had been copied from it. The district court ruled in favor of Symantec, stating that no infringement had occurred. On appeal by Brown Bag, the US Ninth Circuit agreed with the district ruling.

The case [2] is important for software developers because of the way that the court examined the Grandview program. The trial court grouped similar features of the program in different categories. One group dealt with concepts fundamental to programs of this sort, such as the need to access files, edit data, and print. A second group related to similarities in function, i.e., the two programs performed similar functions or performed them in similar ways. A third group of similarities, such as the use of pull-down menus, was considered standard or commonplace by the court. The use of certain colors formed the basis for the fourth group. However, the court found these to be functional, because human-factors analysis recommended the use of certain colors against others. Finally, the court ruled that a fifth group of similarities were covered by a license agreement between Brown Bag and Friend.

The approach taken by the trial court, i.e., analytic dissection of the user interface, was deemed proper by the appeals court. Although Brown Bag believed that the court had failed to see that a jury might find expressiveness in the combination of user interface elements, the appellate court said such an approach was necessary in order to determine what was within or beyond the scope of protection afforded to a particular work.

The analytic dissection approach, part of what the courts defined as the extrinsic-intrinsic test, in determining copyright infringement has been criticized because of the inadequacy of definition in its test. Dissection is a highly complex process, yet there has been little guidance as to what it entails other than incidentally by example. Thus, it only tends to add confusion to software developers as they attempt to safeguard their approach to development and distribution.

## 5.4. Computer Associates International, Inc. vs. Altai, Inc.

This case [3] represents the fourth test in US courts for determining whether the nonliteral elements of computer programs are protected by copyright. Computer Associates developed ADAPTER, an operating system compatibility program that allows the main program to be compatible with various operating systems. Altai hired one of the programmers who had worked for Computer Associates and who had worked on the ADAPTER project. In turn, the programmer developed a program similar to ADAPTER; it was called OSCAR 3.4. Although unknown to Altai, 30 percent of OSCAR 3.4's code was copied from ADAPTER. After Computer Associates sued Altai, new programmers who had not been involved in the development of OSCAR 3.4 rewrote the program, calling the new version OSCAR 3.5. The district court ruled that Altai had copied ADAPTER code, however Altai was found not guilty of copyright infringement. Computer Associates appealed, but the district court's ruling was upheld.

The court rejected tests established by other courts to determine copyright infringement of a program's nonliteral elements, including the test established in Whelen vs. Jaslow. That test was considered too simplistic and broad. Instead, it announced its own test, the Abstraction–Filtration–Comparison test. This was a three-part test that dealt, first, with the program's levels of abstraction; these included object code, source code, parameter lists, i.e., the passing of information between subroutines, and the program's general outline. The second step filters out those elements that are not copyrightable. The third step examines the “substantial similarity” of the remaining elements, a determination of whether any copying constitutes copyright infringement.

The court again took an anti-protectionist viewpoint in that the three-part test would naturally eliminate many elements of computer programs from protection, providing for a bias toward the defendant wherever the test was used. The effect was to decrease the extent of copyright protection on the “look and feel” of software. It has even been suggested that application of the three-part test might even discourage any innovation in programming techniques and leave a program’s nonliteral elements underprotected.

## 5.5. Non-US copyright infringement perspectives

If it seems that US copyright law pertaining to computer software is somewhat equivocal, Cook [4] indicates that the same is true in the United Kingdom (UK). He emphasizes this point by stating that, in his opinion, excesses and confused thinking have prevailed in earlier UK software copyright cases and that recently a single case “effectively redress[ed] the balance in an area of law previously ill-served by case law”, the area being UK software copyright law. On the other hand, several countries in South America have embarked on a program of changes in intellectual copyright protection, to include computer software. Some of these changes have resulted from new laws as well as a change in attitude of the courts. In 1993 Venezuela amended its copyright laws to include software, and in 1992 an Argentinean court ordered the seizure of assets of a software pirate. Also in 1992, Paraguay became a member of the Berne Convention with Bolivia following in 1993. And in 1993, French courts have shown that they are much more at ease in the field of software copyright and that they are treating disputes with a level of sophistication far beyond that applied earlier.

## 6. Implications for software developers and some suggested actions

It should be evident to software developers that one opinion rendered by a US district court does not necessarily carry over to other US courts. Our brief review of “look and feel” cases provides support for that diversity of opinions. What is clear, however, is that reviews of current literature dealing with copyright of computer software, especially user interface perspectives, reveal that a close study by qualified copyright attorneys of the four cases cited is prudent. This is so because these four cases present distinct legal tests to be used by US courts to determine the copyrightability of nonliteral elements in computer software.

In an ideal situation, a modified version of the test in the Altai case might be the best approach to adopt from the perspective of copyright principles. Such an approach may disregard policy perspectives, however. Debate over approaches and tests must consider just how progress is promoted, i.e., the necessity of providing an adequate level of copyright protection without hindering creativity. In the US, the only body that should make such a policy determination is Congress. Velasco has indicated that Congress took some measures in this respect in the 1970s with the establishment of CONTU. However, since Congress has not provided any further type of policy direction regarding this issue, the result is a proliferation of several incompatible standards. Ideally, Congress should act to minimize the uncertainty, if not eliminate it. In the meantime, software developers and the courts themselves must deal with the problem.

According to Cook [5], the matter of the US' approach to intellectual property in the international community is out of step, notwithstanding Berne, GATT, and NAFTA. He would argue that the US approach is not necessarily good or bad in comparison with the rest of the world, just that it is different. In turn, this could affect US companies' competitiveness within the international community in the sense that US organizations are likely to apply thinking based on US copyright law to international matters.

Software developers should examine proceedings outside the US also. For example, Shoesmith [13] reports that the Canadian Alliance Against Software Theft (CAAST) announced three lawsuits in early 1995, one against a retail software dealer and two against computer hardware dealers – all for software copyright infringement. One of the cases deals with alleged unlawful renting of software distributed by Lotus, Microsoft, Novell, and the Wordperfect Applications Group. The others deal with the alleged unlawful loading of unlicensed Microsoft products on computer hard drives.

In view of much of the uncertainty, what actions can software developers take? First, developers must take every opportunity to prosecute when infringement is suspected. This action would be so expensive for the infringer that continuing infringement would not be worth the cost. Further, the courts may be able to arrive at a consensus of opinion that would clarify the “look and feel” issues. Second, with regard to possible copyright infringement in the US, developers should be vigilant with the US Customs Service. Developers should record registered copyrights with them and also vigorously pursue, through administrative and/or judicial channels, infringements of their copyrights with the realization that seizures of software that infringes on a developer’s copyright is lost revenue to the infringer. Eventually, it may be more costly for the infringer to continue. Third, whether the developer markets in the US and internationally, or in the US alone, the accent on high quality, aggressive customer service should be the rule. This means continuously investigating better methods, approaches to marketing and promotion, distribution, and pricing strategies. These actions would make it much more difficult for a copyright infringer to copy the material, since service and support are integral to satisfying consumer and business needs. In a sense, developers must become more innovative – thereby making it much less attractive for others to develop applications that could border on possible copyright infringement.

Developers should also become much more vigilant in their investigation and understanding of US court decisions. Often, many organizations employ corporate attorneys to manage legal affairs; unfortunately, however, many corporate attorneys lack the specific training and experience necessary to manage software copyright problems. This means that the use of consultants who specialize in these matters should be considered. This becomes a necessity when the organization competes in the international community.
