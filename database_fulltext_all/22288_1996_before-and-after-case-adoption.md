# Before and after CASE adoption

Anthony C. Nelson $^{a,*}$ , Joe Rottman $^{b}$

$^{a}$ University of South Florida, 140 Seventh Avenue South, St. Petersburg, FL 33701-5016, USA $^{b}$ University of Missouri, 8001 Natural Bridge Road, St. Louis, MO 63121, USA

## Abstract

Various authors and companies claim that the CASE technology increases both the quality and productivity of systems development. However, although the literature is replete with reports of successful adoption, many companies have not adopted it. This paper addresses the major reasons for companies' adoption of CASE and the major obstacles preventing the adoption. Furthermore, the authors investigated the changes in systems development practices in the light of the CASE technology. Finally, we determined the extent to which companies use and appreciate CASE. The authors analyzed 253 returned surveys to shed light on the questions: How do CASE users differ from non-users, and what are the differences between systems development practices before and after CASE adoption? They found that the CASE users spend more time during the front-end SDLC phases, use standard systems development techniques more often, and experience less difficulty during systems development.

## 1. Introduction

Many have recommended CASE tools as a solution for the major problems inherent in traditional systems development, such as delays, inaccuracies, difficult revisions, and inadequate documentation. Reports of a 50 percent increase in the productivity and development of more accurate systems in less time have led IS professionals to alter – and sometimes completely restructure – their systems development methodology in order to incorporate these tools [10]. Nevertheless, despite the tangible benefits offered by the CASE tools, many companies cannot afford to invest the time, effort, and resources, necessary to adopt and implement them [4].

The adoption of the CASE technology has an impact on development efforts. Various researchers have analyzed this impact (e.g. [12]). For example, Orlikowski [11] addresses the manner in which enforced standardization in software development methodology and characteristics of various firms affect the adoption and success of the CASE tools. Furthermore, several researchers investigated the extent to which CASE has penetrated the industry, and the manner in which companies are using it [5].

The crucial analysis has, however, not been made: what specific changes in systems development can the IS professionals expect to occur during CASE adoption and usage? Past research has not provided a detailed before and after as well as a comparison between the CASE users and non-users; for example, one article gives a picture of the process of implementing CASE at Amtrak, yet both sides of the process are not explored [7]. Others have looked at the reasons that lead firms to adopt CASE, but not the manner in which CASE has affected their software development practices [9].

This paper addresses these shortcomings by focusing on four major areas: the motivations and obstacles to CASE adoption; the SD practices before and after CASE adoption; a comparison of the users of CASE with non-users; and the extent to which CASE tools are used and appreciated. It provides a descriptive study that illustrates the extent to which events are taking place within the systems development departments. Certain characteristics and comparisons are described. However, this is not a causal study.

## 2. Survey questionnaire design

To explore the issues, the authors developed a survey instrument to record the motivations and obstacles to CASE adoption and to shed light on the benefits/risks of CASE usage. Furthermore, the changes in development practices were documented using the instrument. We used several sources to derive the questionnaire items (see Appendix A), and used the benefits and problems reported in various studies $[3, 7]$ . The common practices found in systems development textbooks and those used by several large organizations in the area local to us, were also used. The features and functions of the CASE products used in several studies $[1, 2]$ were also considered. After pilot tests with five systems development managers, the authors revised the questionnaire to improve the clarity and contents.

To capture the full spectrum of CASE adoption behavior, we needed firms currently using CASE, those who had not yet considered CASE, and others who were actively evaluating it. Thus, the survey sample was drawn from both CASE users and the general population of the IS development groups.

Four CASE vendors agreed to participate by furnishing their customer lists. In addition, we took random samples from firms listed in the Compact

Disclosure database based on filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) by publicly listed companies. Of a total mail of 1054 questionnaires, 253 usable responses were received, representing a response rate of 24 percent.

## 3. Motivations and obstacles to CASE adoption

IS professionals are motivated to use CASE due to many general factors such as incomplete/inaccurate systems, slow development turn around time, difficulty in modifying deliverables, and inadequate documentation. We sought to discover the three most important and specific factors that initially motivated organizations to adopt CASE. Table 1 shows the number and percentage of times the respondents listed each problem area as a leading motivating factor. The problem areas include the SDLC phases and project management tasks. Problems with conceptual analysis, maintenance, and documentation are the most important motivating factors. Productivity measurement and project tracking do not present significant problems for the users, and the remaining participants' problem areas are evenly cited. Table 2 lists the importance that the participants placed on each type of CASE feature during the selection process. The most important CASE feature is the generation of graphical representations. The numbers are the average for all the samples (141) with the standard deviation (s.d.). These categories represent a classification of the technical features of the CASE tools, such as generating representations (e.g. data-flow diagrams); analyzing inconsistencies within the graphical representations; generating codes automatically from design specifications; project management/control; tools to facilitate communication and coordination among the project team members; and on-line or manual instructional material.

## 4. Level of difficulty in systems development

Table 4 compares the difficulty in systems development before and after CASE adoption. The areas of difficulty are the same areas that motivated CASE adoption. Generally, systems development tasks are easier after CASE adoption. Developers achieve better systems quality, make easier design changes, and find it easier to communicate with users after CASE adoption. The CASE users experience the greatest change in systems documentation, which is also a primary motivating factor in adopting CASE. Difficulty decreases by a statistically significant amount in each area.

This table also compares the difficulty in using CASE tools with that in not using them. As a whole, the CASE users experience fewer problems. The greatest difference between the users and non-users is in the area of documentation. CASE facilitates documentation, which is a catalyst that leads to benefits in modeling, measurement, and management [13].

## 5. Percentage of time spent in SDLC phases

Table 5 compares the percentage of time spent in each SDLC phase before and after CASE adoption. Total time spent on feasibility study, conceptual analysis and detailed design increases. On the other hand, the percentage of time spent on coding, testing and conversion, and maintenance decreases. The percentage of time spent in the maintenance phase decreases most.

Table 7 compares the usage of various techniques before and after CASE adoption. In all instances, there is a statistically significant increase in usage after adoption. CASE tools facilitate and/or enforce the use of various techniques. It is therefore not surprising that the technique usage increased.

Furthermore, the CASE users follow systems development techniques to a greater extent than non-users. However, there is only a significant difference in five of the twelve techniques.

## 7. Expected vs. realized benefits.

We asked non-users to rate the benefits they would expect from the use of CASE. We also asked CASE users to rate the benefits that they expected before its adoption, and the benefits realized after the adoption. Table 8 shows the comparison of these expectations. In general, CASE users expected less before CASE adoption than non-users. Furthermore, CASE does not live up to users' expectations. There is a statistically significant difference between the expectations and realizations for all the four benefits. The realized benefits are less than the expected benefits. We questioned whether the users had answered in a self-justifying manner. Since non-users expected more than the users, users may have deflated their answers. If this is true, there would be a greater difference between the expectations and realizations.

Table 9 shows the extent to which the CASE tools are used and how much the users are satisfied with the various features. The levels of satisfaction and usage reflect each other: both are fairly low. Apparently, many users are either not taking advantage of the CASE features or the tools do not support their methods [6]. Front-end tools (e.g. DFD, data modeling) are used most often and are found most satisfying.

## 8. Conclusions

The major conclusions are as follows. The new development approach associated with the CASE tools, and the high purchase and implementation costs, are two primary obstacles to CASE adoption. Companies adopt CASE primarily because of problems with conceptual analysis, maintenance, and documentation. Although the CASE products do not appear to live up to the sales hype, they do provide many benefits. After CASE adoption, systems developers experience fewer problems in analysis, maintenance, documentation, and other areas of systems development. CASE users spend more time during the front-end SDLC phases (i.e. analysis and design) and less during the back-end phases. Thus, they probably improve their chances of successful systems developments [8]. They also spend more time using standard systems development techniques.

Many companies, however, do not use CASE products or standard techniques. Companies that use CASE do not take full advantage of the various features. Furthermore, they appear only moderately satisfied with the features and products.
