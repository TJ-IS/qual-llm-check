# Key issues in information systems management: a Delphi study in Slovenia

Sasa Dekleva $^{a,*}$ , Jože Zupančič $^{b}$

$^{a}$ DePaul University School of Accountancy, 1 E. Jackson Blvd., Chicago, IL 60604-2287, USA $^{b}$ University of Maribor, School of Organizational Sciences, Pressernova 11, 64000 Kranj, Slovenia

## Abstract

Formerly one of the Yugoslav republics, Slovenia declared its independence in June 1991. Together with Hungary, the Czech Republic and Poland, Slovenia belongs to the group of leading IS technology markets among the new democracies in Central and Eastern Europe. The Delphi technique was used by the authors to compile a list of the key issues in information systems (IS) management in Slovenia, to quantify them, and, through iterations, to strengthen consensus about their importance. The results are presented and compared with those obtained in similar studies in the USA. The findings indicate profound differences in IS management issues between Slovenia and the USA and these differences are believed to be largely applicable to other new Central and Eastern European democracies.

Keywords: Delphi study; Information systems management; key issues; Questionnaire analysis

## 1. Introduction

Major issues in IS management have been investigated in the USA every three years during the 1980s [2, 3, 6]. Similar investigations were carried out in several other countries, for example Australia [20], Taiwan [9], Germany [13], and Slovenia [21]. Watson and Brancheau[19] who compared the results of some of these studies found that important IS management issues depended on the level of development and cultural environment, and issues also evolved over time. Deans et al. [5] reported that IS issues depended on the political, legal, economic, cultural, and technological environments of the foreign countries studied. In other words, the evidence suggests that the nature of a particular information technology market varies significantly.

Developments in Central and East European countries seem to be of particular interest to the IT community. According to Moeller [15], German unification and the opening up of Eastern Europe are the major factors that will determine the development of the IT market in the 1990s. Restrictions on the export of high technology from the West were lifted at the end of the Cold War. Dyson [8] proposed that computing had a future in East European countries, while Kempfer [12] predicted that the fastest growth of the value-added retail business will be there: business is expected to expand at an annual rate of approximately 20% during the next five years. Several observers (e.g., [1, 10, 17]) reported burgeoning IT sales in Eastern Europe, where the collapse of the centralized economies had produced a huge demand for computer power probably because the technological infrastructure had been severely neglected. All of Europe is apparently important for high technology vendors from the West and for the IT industry in general. However, past studies suggested that IS practices of the USA and other developed countries cannot be transferred blindly

Dyson identified two groups of Central and East European countries. She categorized Slovenia, Hungary, the Czech Republic, and Poland in the group of leading countries and found that the situation in other countries, such as the Baltic Republics, Romania, Ukraine, Russia, and the other nations of the Commonwealth of Independent States, was not as promising. Thus the study may show how these leading countries are evolving.

Although the key issues in IS management have been investigated in many countries, including Slovenia, many research methodologies were used, making the comparisons of the findings difficult. A previous study in Slovenia showed that the investigation of key issues in IS management was only one of several topics, the sample was small (29 responses for a response rate of 28%), and the participants were asked to rate a predetermined set of issues. The Delphi technique, used in a sequence of the studies in the USA appeared particularly appropriate. Watson and Brancheau recommended the adoption of the Delphi format for future national studies. This way the accumulation of comparable information and knowledge about IS practices and regional differences can begin. This study is essentially a repetition of the American studies, but conducted in Slovenia.

Slovenia was the most economically advanced republic in the former Yugoslavia. While having less than 8 percent of the Yugoslav population, Slovenia created 20 percent of its GDP. Productivity on an average, was twice as high as in Yugoslavia as whole, and 40 percent of its computers were installed in Slovenia. Despite central control and a failed ideology, Slovenia has nonetheless managed to develop a respectable level of IS culture. One reason for this was that the USA did not impose an embargo on the sales of computer technology to the former Yugoslavia because it was not a member of the Warsaw Pact. Citizens of the former Yugoslavia were also free to travel abroad and to subscribe to western professional, technical, and academic journals. Computerization started in the late 1960s when (mostly IBM) computers were purchased by larger companies and the government. Thousands of employees with various professional and educational backgrounds were trained by vendors, most of them in IBM's proprietary educational center. A few years later, the two Slovenian universities established several IS and computer science programs.

Following Slovenia's separation from Yugoslavia in 1991, the Yugoslav army withdrew after a brief war. A small country of about the same size as Israel and a population of 2 million, Slovenia has been recognized by the world community as a separate country. In 1994, Slovenia's GDP per capita was just over \$7,000 with an estimated three percent annual growth rate. However, in the transition to a market economy and private ownership, most larger companies disintegrated. Mainframe computers were abandoned and PCs, often networked, became the predominant IT platform. The number of PCs in homes has also grown rapidly.

Smaller companies usually do not have their own IS staff and PC-based systems are implemented with inexpensive software packages. Most larger companies have their own IS organizations, developing advanced information systems and often incorporating technologies such as EIS, EDI and office automation [11]. However, many such projects are hindered by a lack of expertise and limited financial resources. Government institutions have their IS activities coordinated by the Governmental Center for Informatics which is responsible for IT infrastructure and IS development.

Through a unique partnership of government, private enterprise and academe, the country made considerable progress in introducing EDI [14]. Although much remained to be done in improving Slovenia's communications infrastructure, many companies have successfully implemented EDI.

## 2. Methodology

## 2.1. Delphi technique

The Delphi technique, a methodology for organizing and prioritizing the collective judgement of a group, involves iterative surveying of the same group $[4, 7, 18]$ . The initial procedure is to prepare, distribute, and synthesize a series of problem statements for evaluation. Participants receive feedback in the form of their own previous responses and any statistical or other data describing the entire group with each succeeding round. This enables the participants to reaffirm original opinions, modify some, and “collectively brainstorm” by adding new items to the list. It is believed that the technique leads to consensus on major points, but also uncovers minority opinions.

## 2.2. Selection of participants

The Slovenian Governmental Office for Statistics provided a mailing list of 360 Slovenian industrial, commercial and service organizations with more than 300 employees. Major political and economic changes, initiated in Slovenia after its separation, caused dynamic restructuring and adjustments of businesses to the new situation. Because the major market-the rest of former Yugoslavia-remained unstable and virtually closed for Slovenian exporters, many organizations were forced to downsize, close down, or restructure to compete in the new markets. Although outdated, the mailing list was the one best available at that time.

Table 1 shows some demographics of organizations participating in the fourth and last round. Only three participating organizations have less than 100 employees and another nine have less than 300; the vast majority of organizations are medium or large by our count.

Round one: The study began in October 1991. Addresses of 330 larger Slovenian organizations were used for mailing the first-round questionnaire to their unidentified IS managers. Managers were requested to specify five to ten issues which they thought would be the most important three to five years into the future, and to provide reasons for their choice. One hundred and five respondents provided 634 issues; i.e., about six statements per respondent. Organizations were under great stress at that time and many were closing down, changing ownership, merging, disintegrating, or restructuring. The achieved response rate of 32 percent was therefore considered good.

Round two: The responses from the first round were analyzed, and proposed issues were classified according to the scheme used by Niederman et al. [16]. This scheme made the processing of statements much easier. All issues suggested by at least five participants were included, resulting in a list of 26 distinct IS management issues and corresponding rationales. The analysis was performed independently by the two authors. The two results needed only slight adjustments.

The list of major issues in IS management in Slovenia was presented in a random sequence on the second-round questionnaire. Space allowed the participants to add new concerns at that time. Each respondent was asked to rate the listed (and any added) issues using a rating scale from 1 (unimportant) to 10 (most important) and to suggest changes in wording, etc. The second-round questionnaires were mailed to the IS managers from the initial 330 organizations in February 1992. One hundred and sixty-three responded, yielding a response rate of 49 percent.

Round three: One hundred and forty-four organizations that did not respond to either of the first two rounds were dropped from the study. The second-round responses were then analyzed in two ways. First, rates were processed statistically and means were provided as a feedback to the remaining participants, along with their individual second-round ratings. Second, suggestions for new issues and modifications were studied. All new issues were either suggested by only one respondent or overlapped existing issues. Only the wording of two descriptions and seven rationales was slightly improved.

The third-round questionnaires were mailed in June 1992 to the remaining 186 participants. They were again asked to rate the problems and provide rationales for low and high rates when their new ones differed by three or more points (on a ten point scale) from second round group means. One hundred and twenty-nine (69%) Slovenian IS managers responded in this round, providing 146 explanations for significant disagreement with the group means.

Round four: A compilation of explanations for disagreements with group means was attached to the fourth-round questionnaires, which were mailed to the remaining 186 participants in September 1992, asking them to read the reasons for disagreements and rate the issues once again; 148 participants responded, yielding a response rate of 80 percent.

## 3. Findings

The anticipated critical issues for Slovenian IS managers for the period of 1995 through 1997 are shown in Table 5. The importance of all 26 issues was rated higher than six on a ten point scale; thus all were considered important. The seven top rated issues did not change position during the last round and rated somewhat higher than the others. Rationales for key issues, etc. can be obtained from the authors.

## 3.1. Top seven issues

3.1.1. Inadequate appreciation of IS by executives and other users and their lack of involvement in IS development

Respondents reported a general lack of understanding of the strategic value of information for managerial control, strategic planning, and company growth. They feel that executives should play a much more active role in IS planning and that users' participation in requirements definition is inadequate. Many respondents feel that this is due to lack of related education and knowledge; e.g., see the sixth ranked issue. One participant explained that this lack of appreciation of information as a competitive weapon has its roots in the previous economic system which ignored marketplace competition. Such conditions did not stimulate executives and managers to search actively for improved performance.

## 3.1.2. Education of IS professionals

Respondents were convinced that the future of IS as a discipline depends on the existence of highly qualified “professionals.” They stressed the importance of universities and research institutions as well as that of hiring practices in industry. While many participants were convinced that most of the abundantly offered seminars are ineffective, a few argued that educational opportunities exist but are not pursued. A minority opinion related this issue not so much to the inadequate education of IS professionals but to a lack of sophistication in IS users.

## 3.1.3. Lack of IS strategic planning

Participants said that information is an important managerial asset and thus must be identified and planned for in the long term. They associated IS planning with general corporate strategic planning. Several respondents believe that there often is no general business strategic planning and associate this situation with the lack of a national long-term strategy. It should be noted that the economic situation in Slovenia was very volatile at the time of the survey, as breathtaking political, social and economic changes were in progress.

## 3.1.4. Management of IS function

Respondents suggested that the IS function should be managed and organized like other business functions. This includes short-term planning, working in teams, staff motivation, project management, methodology implementation, quality control, scheduling, user support, etc. Responses suggest that management education and practices of IS professionals were nowhere near their desired level.

## 3.1.5. Organizational problems

Respondents stated that proper structure, organization, and management of business processes and functions and their stability are essential foundations for the development of effective IS. These, in turn, can function as catalysts to improve business processes. This opinion reflected both the historic neglect of such issues as organizational informatics and management, and the state of transition and instability. They further reported that IS is frequently seen as something independent and parallel to other business systems instead of as an integral part of them. Respondents suggested that IS, a key business function, should be appropriately positioned in the organizational structure.

## 3.1.6. Education of IS users

Educated executives and other users should better understand the role of information services. They can make proper decisions about strategic objectives and investments, better determine IS requirements, follow world trends, and can use information technology more effectively. Such executives also learn how management practices can be improved because of the power of information technology. The opinion that IS users generally are unsophisticated was unanimously and loudly expressed. A few respondents felt that users should not only be information literate but also trained in end-user computing.

## 3.1.7. Integration of subsystems into comprehensive information architecture

Participants explained that computer applications have normally satisfied only one business function. They believe that increased organizational synergy, an important potential contribution of IS, can be achieved only with centralized planning. Centralized data dictionaries, integrated databases (although possibly physically distributed), and integration of process control and business computing provide for integration of information subsystems, enabling continuous and effective control of production and other business processes.

## 3.2. Movement toward consensus

In theory, the Delphi technique gradually increases consensus over the several rounds of surveying. The table in Appendix A shows that consensus increased from the second through the fourth round. Standard deviation, a measure of the spread of opinion, increased from the second to the third round for only three out of 26 issues, and from the third to the fourth round for only four (different) issues. Standard deviations in the fourth round are lower than corresponding values from the second round with no exception.

## 3.3. Analysis by industry, number of employees, and respondent's position

Analysis of issues ratings by industry category did not reveal any interesting associations, except for a few regarding some of the lower-ranking cases. Pearson correlation analysis between the total number of employees in responding organizations and issues ratings also did not show any significant correlation. The number of employees in the IS departments, however, revealed a number of correlations with the ratings, as shown in Table 6. All but two of these significant correlations are negative, suggesting that larger groups address most of the issues with more success than smaller ones. However, the differences in means between the group of 63 companies with five or less employees in the IS department and 27 companies with an IS staff of at least 15 are less than 0.5 (on a ten point scale) for almost all issues. Users' appreciation and development productivity show significant positive correlations; these issues worry respondents from larger units more than those from smaller ones. The differences in mean ratings between the same two groups are 0.8 and 0.6, respectively.

## 3.4. Comparison with results from the USA

To compare the results with those in similar studies in the USA, the issues were classified using the scheme of Niederman et al. The classification scheme and its application is presented in Table 7. A comparison of the frequencies of different classifications and groupings between this and the latest American study does not reveal any real difference between the two countries, because the frequencies are comparable. Management issues dominate the top of the Slovenian list more than the American equivalent. Twelve Slovenian issues are internal to the IS organization compared to only nine on the American list. Also, the Slovenian list contains ten issues from the internal effectiveness category and only five from business relationship, compared with six internal effectiveness and eight business relationship issues on the American list. It appears that management of internal processes in the IS organization preoccupies Slovenian respondents more than the American ones, where a higher proportion of issues is external and connected with business relationships.

## 4. Conclusions

The results of this study are not surprising. Key issues in IS management in Slovenia differ from those in the USA. These findings are believed to be largely applicable to other new Central and East European democracies, and particularly to Hungary, the Czech Republic, and Poland, which are at a comparable stage of development.

Classifying issues into categories used in the American studies and simply comparing such categories actually hides the real scope of differences. However, it reveals a higher presence of management-related problems and particularly management issues internal to the IS organization in Slovenia as opposed to a somewhat greater number of external issues associated with business relationships in the USA.

IS-related services, such as educational, legal, and professional-abundant in the USA-are still scarce or inefficient in Slovenia. The same is the case with national information infrastructure, such as communication facilities, product supply, and external databases. Management of the IS function is an important issue in Slovenia because management education was not available before the change of political and economic systems. This lack of management skills is demonstrated by the importance of issues such as product selection, productivity, and change and project management. In other words, some of the Slovenian issues reflect past neglect. One of the consequences is a lack of knowledge and skills due to limited education. Missing among the Slovenian issues and very much present on the American list are the mission-critical and strategic issues, such as data and systems as corporate resources and assessment of their value, technology islands and infrastructure, IS portfolio and organizational structure, global perspective, and security and recovery.

Understanding the major issues in IS management in Slovenia should help Slovenian and other Central and Eastern European executives focus and direct their attention. Domestic vendors of IS technology and services can benefit because the study showed what products and skills are in short supply. Perhaps the greatest mistake a vendor of products, services, or education could make would be to presume that US issues are Slovenian (or, worse, global) issues; such is not the case.
