# Research

# A framework for studying computer support of organizational infrastructure $^{1}$

Clyde W. Holsapple $^{a,*}$ , Wenhong Luo $^{b}$

$^{a}$ School of Management, Carol Martin Gatton College of Business and Economics, University of Kentucky, Lexington, KY 40506-003, USA $^{b}$ Department of Management, Worcester Polytechnic Institute 100 Institute Road, Worcester, MA 01609-2280, USA

## Abstract

This paper introduces a framework for studying organizational computing. Its pivotal construct relates technological infrastructure to the purposes for which organizational infrastructure exists and the activities that it enables. Drawing on a diverse body of organization science literature, the framework identifies five purposes. It also involves a progression of five types of activities. From this, we derive a taxonomy for computer-based support of organizations. We also derive research models for guiding empirical research concerned with studying the fit between organizational and technological infrastructure.

Keywords: Framework; Infrastructure; Organizational computing; Research models; Taxonomy

## 1. Introduction

The study of computer support for multiparticipant work has been termed organizational computing (OC) [1]. The OC field is concerned with development, operation, and evaluation of computing systems. To explore its possibilities, it is necessary to understand the relationship between organization needs and Technological Infrastructure (TI). There are two distinct and complementary perspectives for this relationship: the technological and organizational perspectives [18]. While the first emphasizes the application of existing technologies for the satisfaction of organization needs, the second seeks to identify the needs as a basis for inventing, applying, and evaluating various technologies.

Both perspectives play an important role in determining computer support possibilities for organizations. To date, research frameworks tend to adopt the technological perspective $[10, 14, 23, 28]$ . Each classifies computer technologies in categories based on the activities being supported. Here, we introduce a complementary framework that uses an organizational perspective for devising (1) a taxonomy for organizing past findings and stimulating further investigation, and (2) models for assessing and guiding research.

## 2. Background

A large body of literature on the design of organizations is based on the role theory perspective [17, 21]. From this perspective, an organization is a social system of interactions among entities constrained by shared norms and expectations [3].

Entities in an organization can occupy a number of positions and play the roles associated with these positions [35]. Interactions are determined by the relationships among the roles that the entities occupy. These constitute the structural aspect of a social system; it also encompasses norms and rules designed to regulate entity behaviors so that desired objectives and goals can be achieved. Analysis and design of organizations should therefore focus on three building blocks of a social system: roles, relationships, and regulations.

Roles. Role prescription has been an important part of organizational theories. For instance, it has been suggested that a job position consists of three aspects [27]: tasks contained in a job; behavior formalization, which standardizes work processes and controls the behaviors of role occupants; and specification of requirements (e.g., knowledge, skills, experience) for filling a position.

The information processing model argues that the degree of task uncertainty determines the nature of a role $[12]$ . When there is little task uncertainty, a role can be defined in terms of programs and procedures. As task uncertainty increases, roles based on programs and procedures are no longer sufficient for handling all tasks: managerial roles are therefore created to gather information, coordinate task execution, and deal with contingencies.

Relationships. One way to describe relationships among roles involves interdependencies. With pooled interdependence, each role renders support to and is supported by all other roles. Sequential interdependence denotes a relationship where one role's output is another role's input. With reciprocal interdependence, the role relationship is a two-way one. Another characterization of role relationships allows them to be tightly or loosely coupled [31, 39].

Relationships. Regulations, such as those embodied in various coordination mechanisms, govern the use of an organizational structure. Coordination can be achieved by standardization, by plan, or by mutual adjustment [36]. With the first of these, routines are created for role occupants to follow. This is often used to regulate activities in relatively simple and repetitive task situations. Rather than focusing on the actions of each role occupant, coordination by plan focuses on results through an overall schedule of tasks. Coordination by mutual adjustment involves exchanging information during the execution of a task, with role occupants adapting their actions based on new information.

Given a structure, an organization achieves its objectives by appropriately selecting and designing regulations to control and coordinate activities of interrelated roles. Together, structure and regulations constitute the underlying foundation or skeleton of an organization, forming what we call the organizational infrastructure: it both constrains and makes possible what participants in an organization can accomplish. It is susceptible to computer-based support.

## 3. A research framework

The foregoing view of organizational infrastructure leads to a framework for studying computer support of organizations. Assuming that computer technologies employed by organizations should enable or facilitate achievement of organizational objectives, this framework is advanced from the organizational (rather than technological) perspective. The intent is to foster OC research by furnishing a common approach for studying the nature of OC systems. In particular, the framework identifies and outlines variables critical to investigation of computer support possibilities.

At a high level, the framework consists of four components illustrated in Figure 1. Comprising roles, relationships, and regulations, organizational infrastructure (OI) is the backbone and is the basis upon which an organization is able to realize its objectives. When a specific set of these is adopted, we have an instance of OI.

Technological infrastructure refers to the set of computer-related technologies that have been deployed to complement, permit, or otherwise support an OI instance. This notion is consistent with so-called hard infrastructure as described by Benjamin and Scott Morton [2]. They define this as computers, telecommunication networks, databases, and other technological artifacts that provide a common base for business processes. In contrast, they define soft infrastructure as visions, goals, measures, and rewards (i.e., regulations) that guide the use of hard infrastructure.

An organization's TI should be aligned with its OI to support functions assumed by the latter. Such support may include: 1) reinforcing aspects of OI currently in place, and 2) enabling elements of OI not possible without the support of computer technology.

TI can support organization participants in carrying out their tasks, thereby empowering participants with ways to gather, analyze, and disseminate knowledge relevant to the role. TI can also strengthen or better exploit relationships among roles by facilitating participant interactions. Finally, TI can be used to help enforce regulations (e.g., monitor participants' activities, detect regulation violators, or channel work flows).

By enabling new OI, TI permits the adoption of role arrangements and regulations that would not otherwise be possible. For instance, computer technology allows integration of information from many sources and dissemination to different locations on a real-time basis. Many companies are redesigning customer service procedures by integrating various functional areas and departments [32]. This would not be possible without immediate access to product and customer information.

The framework in Figure 1 contends that an organization's OI exists for one or more of several purposes. Where there is a good fit of TI with OI, technology contributes to fulfilling the OI purposes. The framework also contends that the existence of OI permits one or more of several types of activities to take place. Where there is a good fit of TI with OI, technology facilitates at least some of these activities with respect to at least some OI purposes.

## 4. Purposes and activities

## 4.1. Purposes of OI

Organizations employ various resources in the pursuit of their goals [11]. An organization's effectiveness in meeting these depends on its OI and supportive TI. The notion of effectiveness is not monolithic. It can be viewed in various ways, each of which could be regarded as being a purpose. For instance, Campbell [7] has compiled an extensive list of effectiveness measures. There is, however, considerable overlap among them.

Fig. 1. An organization-based framework for studying computer support possibilities.

From this list and a review of more recent organization science literature, we have identified five as important and distinct purposes for which OI exists. The five are: achievement of efficiency, quality, innovation, motivation, and satisfaction. OI is intended to foster organizational effectiveness by enabling fulfillment of one or more of these purposes. Computer support needs to be investigated in terms of these five purposes.

Efficiency. Modern organizational theories focus on various organizational components and relationships from a system perspective [37]. Although they usually do not proclaim efficiency (or anything else) as the system's purpose, it is always one of the criteria for measuring system performance. Improving or maintaining the amount of work completed per unit of time and/or cost is thus a well recognized purpose of OI.

Quality. In general, quality refers to goodness of work results, irrespective of how efficiently the work is completed. Promoting quality of work is another widely recognized aim of OI [29]. OC research has attempted to assess the quality of results achieved by technological support of OI. For instance, the quality of decisions made by groups using group decision support systems has been studied [9].

Innovation. Innovation is the act of generating and adopting new ideas, products, and processes that are beneficial for meeting organizational objectives [41]. Its importance for the survival of organizations in a competitive environment cannot be overemphasized. OI influences both individual and collective factors that contribute to an organization's propensity to innovate. Discretion, feedback, and recognition are considered as the most important individual factors [40]. At the collective level, an organization's structure and its ability to identify and apply new knowledge are key factors in innovation [22]. Effects of various technologies on the ability of specific OIs to promote innovation have received little attention among researchers.

Motivation. Motivating participants requires appropriate organizational structure and reward mechanisms [25]. A purpose of OI is therefore to regulate the identification and selection of well-motivated individuals to fill appropriate roles. This involves institutionalizing incentive mechanisms, recognizing participants' individual motives, and providing positions that match individual motives. The extent to which technologies can help motivate persons has not received much attention from OC researchers.

Satisfaction. The literature on job satisfaction, quality of work life, and creation of high-involvement organizations indicates that satisfying the needs of participants is critical for an organization's success. Satisfaction influences an individual's performance, and thus the performance of the organization [34]. Organizations also have a social responsibility to be concerned about the well-being of participants [24]. A particular OI can be instrumental in achieving a modicum of satisfaction, whereas another could contribute to dissatisfaction. Another purpose of OI is therefore, to ensure that organizational roles are designed to comply with social obligations and government regulations.

## 4.2. Organizational activities facilitated by infrastructure

Not only does OI have purposes, it also facilitates organizational activities. As opposed to individual activities, organizational activities involve multiple roles in the process of performing related tasks. Organizational activities can be characterized in many ways. McGrath [26] developed a taxonomy of activities, having four basic quadrants: generating ideas or plans, choosing a correct answer or a preferred answer, resolving conflicting views or conflicting interests, and executing competitive tasks or performance tasks. These activities are shown in Figure 2 giving rise to eight types of tasks.

McGrath's characterization of organizational activities is based on the substance of what is done. Here, we introduce a complementary characterization based on structural rather than substance attributes. Progressing from an elemental structure toward greater complexity, these activity types are message passing, resource sharing, conducting meetings, managing projects, and governing infrastructure.

![](/api/attachments/KMR43SCF/fulltext/images/bc87f194d9079efe91eaa814cc8de06912ae1d38a5142f6bfb8c982839b0b298.jpg)  
Fig. 2. A taxonomy of organisational activities (adopted from Mc Grath,1984).

Each is enabled by and depends on OI and is susceptible to TI support.

As Figure 3 suggests, one activity can encompass other, more elemental activities. Sharing resources involves passing messages, etc. The activity of a governing infrastructure can involve all the activities. In this progression, each type of activity tends to take more time and involve more roles.

Interestingly, this progression and McGrath's taxonomy can be combined to describe organizational activities in terms of both content and structural attributes. For example, a meeting can be used to generate ideas, solve problems, make decisions, etc. Conversely, idea generation may involve message passing, resource sharing, meetings, etc.

Passing Messages. Message passing entails the exchange of audio, textual, graphic, and other visual messages. Structurally, this is the most basic type of activity permitted by OI and its supporting TI. Useful message passing activities are influenced and regulated by OI. For a given task, it determines the senders and receivers of messages as well as the broad contents of messages. Thus, roles and relationships define the topology of an organization's message-passing possibilities. Timing, sequencing, and formats of message passing are governed by infrastructure regulations.

Sharing Resources. Resource sharing involves the allocation of material, financial, human, and knowledge resources among roles. Achieving objectives is largely determined by available resource allocation. To begin with, OI specifies the tasks to be carried out, with types and amounts of resources needed varying across roles and tasks. Relationships provide a basic structure for sharing common resources. In cases of conflict, OI regulations often provide resolution mechanisms.

![](/api/attachments/KMR43SCF/fulltext/images/a3caad604e6bb5729d9eb5e0ccb4c92b8ace83be4bce71c30c3dfcbe56070928.jpg)  
Fig. 3. A progression of organisational activities.

Conducting Meetings. Conducting a meeting implies that the participants convene to work on a common task. An OI and its supporting TI facilitate meeting activities by giving role occupants access to each other, defining structures and dynamics (e.g., procedures and processes) for meetings, and establishing their objectives.

Managing Projects. Managing projects involves planning and control of project activities. Planning divides an overall project into interdependent subtasks and each subtask is then assigned to roles. Control ensures that subtasks are completed. OI enables both project planning and control; TI can facilitate these efforts.

Governing Infrastructure. A governing OI involves ongoing design, evaluation, and maintenance of the infrastructure that allows the other activities to occur. Design involves the creation and re-creation of roles, relationships, and regulations. Evaluation may call for a redesign or modification of existing OI. Maintaining OI entails monitoring organizational operations and adjusting the infrastructure in the context of a changing environment. The activity of a governing infrastructure itself depends on the existence of OI, and may benefit from supportive TI.

## 5. A taxonomy for evaluating computer support possibilities

Purpose and activity constructs can be regarded as dimensions in a taxonomy for evaluating computer support. Because they present opportunities for computerized support of organizations, we can categorize existing computer technologies and study their contributions. Table 1 provides the taxonomy. With five purposes and five activities there are 25 categories that can be applied for a given technology to assess that technology's fits with the OI and to identify computer support possibilities; e.g., to what extent does support of message passing have an impact on the efficiency of a particular OI?

As an example, consider the impact of E-mail. This technology has been studied primarily in terms of only four categories – with efficiency and quality impacted through E-mail support of message passing and resource sharing. Studies suggest that E-mail systems contribute to the improvement of organizational efficiency [8]; E-mail systems are able to reduce the time message senders otherwise waste in waiting and also help message receivers avoid unexpected interruptions. E-mail also allows knowledge to be shared at a faster pace, permitting participants to examine more information sources and have more time to make better use of available resources.

In contrast, the net effect of E-mail technology on the quality of message passing is unclear. On the positive side, replacing verbal communications with written messages can reduce possible misunderstandings and misinterpretations [4]. However, this depends on the clarity of a person's writing; many write ambiguous or unclear messages. On the negative side, E-mail may result in information overload or a flood of junk mail that interferes with critical messages.

When positioning an existing technology in the taxonomy, vacant cells imply that either the technology has not been used to support an activity or the effects of its support are not yet clear. Each empty cell in Table 1, for instance, suggests possible opportunities for further study, use, and enhancement of E-mail technology. E-mail technology has been little studied in terms of its impacts, if any, on aiding in the fulfillment of OI purposes of innovation, motivation, and satisfaction.

As another example of how a technology can be examined in terms of the framework, Table 2 indicates the primary impact of group decision support system (GDSS) technology. Although GDSSs give some support for message passing and resource sharing, they do so only in the context of supporting meetings. Considerable empirical research has examined the impacts of GDSSs on efficiency, quality, and satisfaction for meeting activities. In spite of mixed results, a recent review suggests that GDSS use can have positive impacts on each of these purposes [9]. As for innovation, one study maintains that GDSS promotion of anonymity and equal participation can foster creativity especially with respect to idea generation [30]. Effects of GDSS technology on motivation have yet to be studied.

Other technologies are oriented toward other rows in the taxonomy. For example, Rein, Holsapple, and Whinston [33] describe a prototype environment for organizational design and learning. Technologies employed in this environment serve as tools for generating and modifying descriptions of organizational work, structure, and processes.

Another way to use the taxonomy is to focus on a specific cell and explore computer support possibilities for it. What are the needs for achieving the purpose relative to that activity and how can computer technology support these needs? Some cells have been studied in detail, while others have not.

For instance, in conducting meetings, how can OI foster innovations and how can computer technologies support OI in doing so? Methods and theories concerning innovation can provide a theoretical foundation for studying these questions. As noted earlier, a GDSS is one type of technology that might be employed to promote innovation; teleconferencing may also have the potential to be adapted to support it. If both technologies can be used to foster innovation in meetings, then the question becomes: which technology is more appropriate, and under what circumstances?

## 6. Research models for studying organizational computing

Several research models can be derived from the OI framework. Each provides an approach to the design of empirical OC studies. In view of the framework, impacts of computer support should be evaluated in terms of how OI purposes are affected by the use of computer technologies. Thus, OI purposes are dependent variables. The TI, OI, and organizational activities serve as independent or moderator variables. The research models involving these variables are valuable for structuring the study of causal relationships and generating hypotheses for empirical testing. Here, we discuss three models and use GDSS studies as examples to illustrate the applicability of each.

The first model (illustrated in Figure 4) treats technology characteristics as independent variables whereas OI and organizational activities serve as moderator variables. This model is used to investigate how technological alternatives affect various OI purposes, provided that OI and organizational activity variables are held constant. Independent variables for these technological alternatives include (1) use of technology, (2) system functionality, and (3) user interface. Each is now illustrated for GDSS technology.

Many early laboratory experiments in GDSS research compared GDSS groups with non-GDSS groups involved in group decision-making activities. The effects of GDSS support were measured in terms of decision quality, efficiency, and satisfaction [6, 13, 15, 38]. Few, if any, of the studies measured the effect of GDSSs on innovation and member motivation. Although the results of these were not conclusive, they provided a foundation for subsequent in-depth studies.

System functionality denotes another class of technology variables, a class that has yet to be studied extensively. The same OI and organizational activities can be supported by computer systems with different features and even by different kinds of systems. For instance, Group Systems and SAMM are two systems that have been used in GDSS research. Because of their functional differences, it is possible that empirical results are dependent on which system is used. Thus, the independent variable can be the use of alternative systems.

A typical GDSS development system consists of a set of tools that can be used to perform various functions. What tools should be employed and in what sequence? Furthermore, GDSSs are not the only kind of system being used to support face-to-face meetings. Electronic boardrooms, equipped with computerized audiovisual technologies, can support face-to-face meetings [23]. Thus, the independent variable could involve alternative technology characteristics.

A second research model following from the framework is depicted in Figure 5. It treats OI characteristics as independent variables while TI and organizational activity serve as moderator variables. The independent variables can be organized according to three classes: those concerned with (1) roles, (2) relationships, and (3) regulations. Thus, based on this model, we can examine how variations in OI elements affect the achievement of OI purposes, in the context of using a particular technology and engaging in a certain organizational activity.

For a given technology, the composition of organizational roles can be adjusted to study impacts on the dependent variables. For example, a facilitator role can be manipulated in the context of using a GDSS to support a meeting. Broadly speaking, the task of this facilitator role is to plan and coordinate the use of a GDSS in a face-to-face meeting [5]. Exactly, what are the responsibilities of the facilitator? Should the facilitator be a pure technician in charge of system operations or be involved in planning and directing the meeting and its activities? What kinds of skills are required for this role? What facilitation techniques are most effective under what circumstances? Such studies are not suggested by the first research model.

The structure and nature of relationships among roles may significantly change the extent to which OI purposes are achieved. For instance, a number of studies have shown that anonymity can have an impact on the quality of meetings as well as the perceptions of participants. In comparing anonymous and nonanonymous groups performing an idea generation task, Jessup et al. [19] found that anonymous groups provided more comments and were more critical of the comments made by other participants. In another study, Jessup and Tansik [20] reported that anonymous group members perceived GDSS-supported meetings as most effective. It is not yet, however, clear what the effect of anonymous relationships would be for other types of tasks, i.e., activities other than meetings, or for all the dependent variables.

Regulations governing roles and relationships can also be a subject of empirical studies. Take the example of E-mail. Should there be any regulations to restrict the kind of messages being passed? If so, what should they be? An organization may limit the sending or receiving of messages via E-mail. This could ease problems of information overload and unnecessary messages, but restrict opportunities to experiment with new patterns of communication. Figure 6 presents a third research model which has been little used in OC research. For a given instance of OI and technology, organization activities are varied to examine their effects on OI purposes. For instance, is consensus building more productive than negotiation in resolving conflicts? Under what moderating circumstances (e.g., alternative technologies) is consensus building more productive?

Although the three research models examine independent variables from the view point of only one framework component at a time, it is conceivable that variables for multiple components can be administered simultaneously as independent variables. One may be interested in comparing GDSSs supporting groups of different sizes and evaluating their effects on group productivity. In this case, alternative GDSSs are values of a TI independent variable and group size is an OI independent variable. Such studies would require more resources and be more difficult to conduct than those adhering to one of the three research models.

Generic research models have two purposes. One is to guide generation of OC propositions for hypothesis testing. By identifying variables that may affect OI purposes, each model can lead to numerous propositions. For example, a GDSS issue that has not been studied is the effect of group composition and interaction patterns. Groups often consist of members having different ability levels with respect to a problem being solved jointly. One pattern consists of forming a subgroup of high-ability members and another of low-ability members. In addressing a problem, the high-ability subgroup works on devising a solution and their results are given to the low-ability subgroup for improvement. Another pattern reverses the process, with the low-ability subgroup taking the lead and the high-ability subgroup improving the result. These two patterns could be compared with a conventional configuration involving no subgroup partitions.

While it is essential for researchers to base their studies on well-grounded theories and prior empirical results, it is also important to put their work in a perspective of the whole field. The three models suggest a way to establish such a perspective. For example, we can identify the independent and dependent variables used for reported empirical studies of GDSSs. Each such study can then be classified in terms of which research model was used. This would indicate how the preponderance of GDSS research has been conducted, the most frequently studied independent variables, and what kinds of OI purposes have been most commonly measured. Thus, the models allow us to integrate existing OC findings, identify areas that have received little OC research attention, and provide a broad view of the OC field.

## 7. Conclusion

In the OC field, there is a need for systematic research on computer support possibilities and the impacts of computer support. Existing research frameworks related to OC focus on technologies and their application categories. This paper has introduced an organization-based research framework to guide investigations in the OC field. It centers on OI and develops the two dimensions of OI purposes and activities enabled by OI. It leads to a taxonomy for examining computer support possibilities, suggesting areas for technological advance and further empirical study. The framework also leads to OC research models that have not heretofore been explicitly stated in the field's literature.

To summarize we expect that the organization-based framework will be used to integrate existing and ongoing OC research findings, identify further research opportunities, and generate testable hypotheses for empirical studies. While the framework is based on organization science literature, there remains a need to investigate its soundness empirically.
