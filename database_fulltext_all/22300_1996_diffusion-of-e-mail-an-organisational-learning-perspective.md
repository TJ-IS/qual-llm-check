# Research

# Diffusion of E-mail: an organisational learning perspective

Celia T. Romm $^{a,*}$ , Nava Pliskin $^{b}$ and William D. Rifkin $^{a}$

$^{a}$ Department of Management, University of Wollongong, Wollongong, NSW, 2500, Australia $^{b}$ Department of Industrial Engineering and Management, Ben-Gurion University of the Negev, Beer-Sheva, Israel

## Abstract

Current research has gone beyond technical aspects to consider the social effects of E-mail on organisations. This paper follows the trend by considering organisational learning aspects. In particular, the paper explores the way in which organisation members learn to exploit E-mail for political manipulation. The paper is based on a case study which describes the introduction of E-mail to a university community. The case study demonstrates that despite heavy investment in the project, initial enthusiasm, and top management support, some employees chose to use E-mail to stage a rebellion against top management. This was eventually followed by management curtailing users' access to E-mail. The discussion focuses on how organisational learning concepts can explain the case events, concluding with theoretical and practical implications.

Keywords: Communities; Diffusion; E-mail; Implementation; Organisational learning ; Politics; Power

## 1. Literature review

In the years since its inception, the diffusion of E-mail has grown steadily. The number of E-mail users is estimated at fifty million, with additional one million added each month. While some of the new users are freelancers, many are members of existing organisations. Their use of the new medium has attracted a growing interest by policy makers and researchers.

The major theme that has characterised early research on E-mail is the question of diffusion. What factors facilitate or impede the successful diffusion of E-mail within and between organisations? The variables that were initially listed as undermining implementation were technological. Thus, early literature focused on ways of improving the new technology's "friendliness", making it more accessible, reliable, and flexible [6, 10, 11]. As data accumulated on impediments to proliferation of E-mail [29, 30], some authors suggested that innovative measures, such as rewarding users for using E-mail, must be applied by management to convince users about adopting the new technology [31].

Recently, a new trend has emerged, researchers have turned to non-technological variables [24, 32, 38]. Markus [25] was among the first authors to categorise non-technical explanations for resistance to a new technology as being either of the individual-level or the collective-level type. Issues that have been listed as individual-level explanations for resistance include: users' conservatism, fear of change, lack of involvement or motivation, and incompatible cognitive style. Issues listed as examples of collective-level considerations include: fear of undesirable effects on the organisational structure, power distribution, or the possibility of cultural conflict between the system and the organisation in which it is to be implemented.

An individual-level theory that has received considerable attention and criticism is the richness theory $[7, 8]$ . According to this theory, the choice between communication media depends on the individual's perception of how “rich” or “lean” in information carrying capacity each alternative medium is for the communication task at hand. The medium is considered rich if feedback is immediate, cues are multiple, and the language is natural and tailored to circumstances.

Daft and Lengel [7] were among the first to rate communication alternatives on a richness scale. According to their scale, face-to-face interaction is considered the richest alternative because it provides varied opportunities for immediate feedback and multiple cues in a natural language tailored to circumstances. Telephone communication was rated lower than face-to-face encounters as it contains fewer cues. E-mail was rated roughly in between the telephone and written alternatives. The argument was that E-mail is a written medium, without immediate feedback. A similar mid-range rating was attributed to Fax because of its delivery speed but delayed feedback relative to the telephone. Written messages were rated as leaner than both face-to-face and telephone communication.

Some critics have argued that the definition of richness must be expanded to account for the value-added features of emerging technologies, including E-mail. Sproull [43], listed three value-adding features of E-mail: multiple addressing, external recording, and computer processing. He suggested that multiple addressing adds value to richness through concurrent addressing from one to many users. External recording and computer processing add value because of the ability to document and modify communication flow. In fact, external recording and computer processing combine to produce “message-base manageability” which is analogous to “data-base manageability”. Other researchers [1, 47] tested these assertions experimentally, demonstrating that under certain conditions E-mail was indeed much “richer” than previously assumed.

Critiques of the richness theory have offered, over the years, a series of revisions that expanded the theory. Whereas initially the theory addressed mainly issues of uncertainty and equivocality, later expansions started to consider situational constraints, symbolic considerations, and social contexts [12, 39, 46]. Such expansions seem to cross the line between individual-level and collective-level theories.

Collective-level theories have been classified according to their relationship to (1) structure, (2) environment, (3) culture, or (4) politics [24, 32, 38].

1. Structure-oriented theories concern the match between the structural characteristics of an organisation and different system design attributes [24]. Thus, systems might be resisted when their implementation is seen as threatening the current structural characteristics of the organisation.

2. Environment-oriented theories concern the fit between system design characteristics and the environment of the organisation in which it is used [24]. The deployment of information systems as strategic weapons would fall into this category.

3. Culture oriented theories concern the fit between the organisational culture presumed in the design of the system and the actual organisational culture in the implementing organisation [20, 32]. A match between the actual and presumed cultures is likely to have a positive effect on the prospects for implementation success, while a cultural clash is likely to have a negative effect on the implementation prospects.

4. Politics-oriented theories suggest that resistance to implementation of an IS results from a redistribution of power that is unacceptable to the losers [24, 45]. These theories assume that individuals are likely to consider systems more or less appropriate if they feel that they might gain or lose power due to the implementation of the system.

In much of the early literature, resistance to E-mail was generally considered a dependent variable that is affected by other variables. It is only in recent years that a different view has been emerging: namely, that E-mail is an independent variable that affects its environment.

Laboratory based experimental work has uncovered a series of effects that E-mail can have on the performance of groups in organisations. A large body of research $[2, 13, 28]$ investigated how E-mail can facilitate group decision making. Other investigations $[3, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37]$ have also been important in demonstrating the effect of networks on group behavior in the workplace, with particular emphasis on how membership in networks affects members' attitudes about the new technology and promotes group cooperation and innovation.

Studies of computer mediated groups have also revealed a number of social effects. Kling [18] singles out several studies as the major contributions to the research on the social effects of E-mail. The first among these studies is the book Connections, by Sproull and Kiesler [44], in which the authors set the stage for examining the social effects of E-mail in organisations. These authors were among the first to argue that E-mail has the potential to democratise organisations. Other authors, however, uncovered a series of dysfunctional attributes of E-mail. These include flaming effects [23, 41], as well as dis-inhibition and de-individuation effects [16, 21].

Little research has explicitly linked organisational learning with E-mail diffusion and implementation. We intend to demonstrate that a successful diffusion of E-mail can be a product of a process of organisational learning. However, in order for the process to be successful, all potentially political aspects of this technology have to be understood by management and employees. If this is not the case, success of the early stages in the learning process can culminate in devastating political side-effects at the later stages of the implementation.

A review of the literature on organisational learning reveals that it can be categorised in terms of three leading theories. First is the management education theory $[4, 5, 14, 27, 40]$ . This theory sees organisational learning as an assumption sharing process. The starting point is that organisational learning is a necessary competence for organisations that allows them to detect and correct errors and increase their innovative capacity $[5]$ . And yet “learning to learn” is not an easy skill to acquire as it often involves “double loop thinking”, i.e. an ability to question the organisation’s basic norms, objectives, and policies. The conflict that is embedded in such an organisational learning process can be resolved only by setting new organisational priorities that enable, encourage, and reward individuals who attempt to change their organisation's assumptions. This can be done by: (1) deliberately changing individuals' cognitive maps [4], encouraging individuals to invite others to confront their views [5], and by teaching individuals to resolve conflicts that result from such confrontations in a way that will eventually lead to assumption sharing with other members of their organisation.

Second is the organisational science theory [9, 15, 17, 42]. It sees organisational learning as data processing that results in the development of an organisational knowledge base. The underlying assumption is that there is a two-way relationship between individual and organisational learning. Individuals are strongly affected by the social processes that surround them [9], but, at the same time, they affect the way that their organisations operate [15]. This two way learning process is achieved through four sub-processes [17]: (1) information acquisition; (2) information distribution; (3) information interpretation; and (3) organisational memory. Thus, the organisational science approach takes a descriptive rather than a prescriptive approach to organisational learning. Its major thrust is on describing how learning occurs rather than on changing or modifying the organisational learning process.

The third theory is the managerialist one [22, 26]. This theory considers organisational learning as an institutionalised innovation process and as a means for achieving organisational flexibility in complex and turbulent market environments. It does not attempt to describe the cognitive structures that are involved in the organisational learning process. Rather, it focuses on identifying impediments to innovation and describing structures and practices that can enhance organisational learning.

Kromer [19] draws attention to the fact that all three perspectives put little emphasis on the role of power and politics in organisational learning. When power and politics are mentioned, particularly by management educators and managerialists, they are seen as a detrimental factor that impedes organisational learning and should, therefore, be totally eliminated. Here we take a different approach. Rather than considering politics as an impediment to learning, we look at it as an important and potentially beneficial factor in successful organisational learning. In fact, as our case indicates, understanding the political implications of a new technology may be one of the most important lessons an organisation learns during the process of diffusion.

## 2. Data collection

Data for this study were collected by the authors at a medium-sized university employing about 500 faculty members and serving about 7000 students. Three major research techniques were utilised as sources of data for the case: interviews, textual analysis, and questionnaires. To maximise reliability, two of the authors were involved in all data analysis activities. They were able to reach agreement whenever findings or their interpretation of the findings were in dispute.

## 2.1. Interviews

In-depth, open ended interviews with twenty-two members of the organisation were the major source of data for this study. Interviewees were selected from three groups of employees:

1. The Academic Staff – There were ten interviewees from the academic staff: three of senior rank, two of intermediate rank, two entry-level lectures, two research assistants, and one teaching assistant. The academic sample was drawn from six different departments from all major divisions of the university. While selecting interviewees for the academic sample, an attempt was made to include all individuals who were directly or indirectly involved in the political events of the case. These included all three members of the “rebel” group (see details in the case).

2. The Information Technology Division – There were three interviewees from the Information Technology Division (ITD): one, the head of user services, was in charge of the E-mail implementation, and two were the past and present deputies of the head of ITD. The interviews with the members of this unit were particularly useful as sources of information about the technical aspects of the diffusion and implementation processes. They also highlighted many of the policy issues that underlay the decision to introduce E-mail to the University and eventually to curtail access to it by organisation members.

3. Administrative Staff – There were nine interviewees from the administrative staff. Two interviewees were from top administration: the Provost and the personal assistant to the President. Seven of the interviewees were secretaries. Each of the secretaries was from a different department of all major divisions in the university. The interviews with the administrative staff were important for eliciting information about policy issues that led to the implementation, the political events that surrounded it, and the consequences of the project as seen from top management perspective.

Interviewees were recruited over a period of six months as the interviewing process progressed. The interviews lasted for about an hour and a half to two hours, in some cases requiring more than one meeting. All interviews were taped and transcribed by the authors.

In addition to gathering personal details (such as background, careers, and future plans), interviewees were asked to describe the quality of work life while the implementation took place, staffing, relationships, and areas of responsibility. Interviewees were also asked to comment on the political events that accompanied the implementation, with particular emphasis on the changes that had occurred in the relationship between the “rebels” and management. Although the questions varied from one interview to another, the same topics were covered in all.

## 2.2. Textual analysis

A variety of documents were collected on the various stages of the implementation. These included: E-mail promotion materials, training kits, minutes of relevant meetings, and progress reports. Other texts analyzed were the organisational chart, and newspaper clippings. A major source of textual material for the case was E-mail correspondence. Over a hundred key e-mail messages relevant to the case were made available (in either hard or soft copy) to the researchers by the interviewees. These and the other texts supplemented data from the interviews by providing insights into the technical, organisational, and political aspects of the case.

## 2.3. Questionnaires

During the E-mail implementation, a survey assessing employee satisfaction was conducted by the “rebels”. Even though the survey was not constructed, validated or administered by the researchers, its results, which were highly relevant to the political events of the case, were made available. These results provided valuable information that complemented the qualitative data derived from the interviews and textual analysis.

## 3. Case history

CR, the University, is located at the center of a highly industrialised region. (The names of organisations and people have been withheld to protect anonymity). Within a few years since its inception, it has grown from a very small to a medium-sized institution of higher learning. CR's President has developed a reputation for being one of the most innovative academic leaders in the country, transforming the culture of CR from a traditional to a profit-oriented, corporate one. The President's interest in transforming the culture at CR was reflected in a series of changes in human resources policies, including the introduction of a personnel evaluation system that allowed differential pay according to demand/supply considerations and a shift from elected to appointed Department Heads and Deans.

The initiative for an E-mail project at CR was taken by the President in 1988. The espoused rationale for the decision was “to empower the individual user within both administration and academia by improving communication both within and beyond CR”. It was not a secret, however, that by improving internal communication, the system was expected to consolidate the organisational changes initiated by the President prior to the implementation. In early 1988, the President contacted the head of the

Information Technology Division (ITD) and asked him to investigate the possibility of implementing e-mail at CR. Shortly afterwards, the President appointed a steering committee, which he chaired, to lead the project.

In the middle of 1990, in order to help with a planned comprehensive launch of E-mail, a new project manager was hired. The new project manager concentrated her efforts on one major promotional event to which she invited about 100 secretaries. Contrary to her expectations, more than 150 individuals, many of whom were academics, attended the promotion presentation, in September 1990. During the month of October 1990, she contacted departments that could already access E-mail, offering to conduct training workshops. By mid 1991, there were 800 active users of both the MAC and PC platforms, double the expectation of 400. Their use of e-mail was mainly intraorganisational, for internal administrative purposes.

Shortly after a critical mass of 800 people (among 1200 potential users) were using E-mail regularly, a series of political events began to unfold. First, there was an announcement by the Provost that CR's long term objective was to gradually abolish internal research grants. Following this, a senior member of the Faculty of Science E-mailed a memorandum that criticised the financial conduct of the university. The message was sent to all active E-mail users on the academic staff, encouraging them to forward hard copies to non-users. In response, the President invited the academics to join a strategic planning forum, enclosing a document that outlined the agenda. At this point, another senior member of the Science Faculty, who accidentally came across the planning document, responded with a series of provocative questions, with copies to a campus-wide electronic distribution list. A third faculty member, a senior member of the Social Science Faculty, responded via E-mail to the two messages, again sending copies to a university-wide mailing list. He agreed with the previous two that university resources were mis-distributed and also criticised the planning document.

In response to the above exchange, a top CR administrator sent a letter to the local daily press accusing the three professors (“the rebel group”) with disloyalty and unprofessional conduct. The third professor reacted by writing a protesting response to the local newspaper, with E-mail copies to the university community. Over the next six months, the press became highly involved in the internal politics, providing extensive coverage of events.

In early 1992, the President convened the promised strategic forum. Since there was a rumour that one of the rebels was going to use this forum to request the resignation of the President, the meeting was attended by most of the CR community, as well as representatives from all major TV stations and the local press. However, despite the expectations for an outburst, the agenda was followed to the letter, the debates were civilised, and the meeting dispersed without a vote of no-confidence against the President.

Toward the end of 1992, the rebel group resorted to E-mail again. This time, a “satisfaction questionnaire” was distributed to the academic staff, eliciting views about administrative procedures at the university. Most of the academics responded by E-mail, and when the results were analyzed, it became apparent that dissatisfaction was overwhelming. The results were distributed by E-mail, to the dismay of top administration. During 1993, E-mail traffic among the members of the rebel group gradually decreased. It finally ended, mainly because some of it leaked to the press, which led the rebels to suspect that their E-mail correspondence was monitored. By the end of 1993, most other (non E-mail) communication within the group came to a halt as well.

Even though the rebel group disintegrated, on-campus political activity continued to rely on E-mail. Other members of the community, including students, begun to use E-mail to air concerns about a series of controversial issues, including gender relations, racism, and students' rights. Top administration carefully monitored the e-mail traffic. One particular message that a student distributed throughout campus late in 1993 led top administration to change e-mail policies and procedures: only students serving as research or teaching assistants for individual faculty members were granted E-mail accounts; universal access to the campus-wide mailing list was drastically reduced, and an E-mail code of practice was compiled, with the help of legal consultants, specifying penalties for "misuse" of E-mail.

## 4. Discussion

Before we consider the degree to which organisational learning theories can explain the diffusion process at CR, a few essential question must be addressed: was the diffusion of E-mail at CR a learning process? Was learning involved? Did it involve the whole organisation?

1. The implementation of E-mail at CR has definitely improved the communication capabilities of administrative staff. As reported by the secretaries, the interaction between them became more succinct, with less time spent on face-to-face or telephone exchanges. Secretaries also reported that they experienced improved access to both administrative superiors and academic staff. This access helped the secretaries organise their priorities better, and, consequently, to accomplish more administrative tasks during their working day.

2. Academics have experienced improved communication. Whereas prior to implementation many academics claimed that they felt lost and alienated within the growing university community, after implementation many felt much more in touch and had more confidence in their ability to communicate with others.

3. The implementation has also improved interorganisational communication. The new technology enabled individuals within the university community to establish exchange links all over the world. Individuals were also able to tap into global information services. This led to greater involvement with professional associations and international research activities.

4. Members of the ITD unit at CR have enhanced their information acquisition, distribution, and interpretation skills. Members of the steering committee have acquired knowledge about different E-mail products. The individuals directly involved in the project have become proficient at teaching users about E-mail. Finally, ITD management has become skilled in communicating with top management on the needs of users. In fact, thanks to the E-mail project, ITD managers have gained access that they never had before to top management, including the President. This allowed them to initiate implementation of a series of other networking applications.

It appears that in terms of all three learning organisation theories, the project was a success. Management educators would see it as an example of successful sharing of assumptions about the importance and usefulness of the new technology, using the fact that 1200 users were communicating with each other on e-mail within a period of three years as evidence. The organisation science scholars would consider this project a success, as a body of knowledge relating to e-mail use has been accumulated. Finally, the managerialists would hail the project as a great organisational learning success as it significantly outperformed, in terms of employee productivity, the initial expectations of its initiators and implementors.

However, the technical success was accompanied by several adverse political effects. Among these we can list:

1. In the case study, E-mail supported the rebel group in organising and attempting to change the power distribution. It helped the rebels to form a coalition and advance their interests.

2. When the rebellion eventually subsided, it proved to have detrimental effects on relationships among members of the university community. The rebellion resulted in personal loss, particularly to the rebels.

3. The events of the case led to the introduction of the “code of practice” that curtailed the free use of E-mail. The code significantly restricted the use of E-mail by all members of the community. Its introduction represented a form of censorship that did not previously exist and that CR members said detrimentally affected the culture.

4. Most importantly, from the point of view of top management, the E-mail rebellion adversely affected its authority and power. The wide coverage of the rebellion in the media raised questions about management practices, contributing to the early retirement of the president only a year after the case events took place.

How can organisational learning theories explain both the technical success of the E-mail implementation and its devastating political side effects? When we study the events of the CR case, it appears that two bodies of knowledge were involved in the CR “learning experience”. One relates to technical skills and the other to political skills. These two bodies of knowledge were eventually grasped by both management and employees, but not at the same time, not in the same way, and not for the same reasons.

The initiation of E-mail by the president was motivated by a belief that the new technology would serve as a vehicle for transforming CR's culture into one that supported the President's corporate, profit-driven vision. The problem was that the cultural transformation sought by the President had a price: mounting frustrations on the part of a growing number of academics who believed that they were being victimised by the President's initiatives.

From an organisational learning perspective, it can be argued that the negative feelings that were experienced by many members at CR were a two-edge sword. On one hand they were fertile ground for the technical success of the implementation because they motivated many organisation members, who were in opposition to the president, to master the new technology. However, once the diffusion process had reached critical mass, the same feelings of frustration and resentment triggered the rebellion, demonstrating that CR's employees had learnt to use the new technology for political purposes.

Management has also gone through a technical and political learning process. From a technical point of view, the implementation has resulted in an accumulation of marketing, strategic, and tactical knowledge about E-mail that management was able to put to use when other communication technologies were implemented. From a political perspective, the E-mail implementation has resulted in several important lessons. At the start, the President saw e-mail as a means for consolidating earlier changes to CR's culture, in particular, he expected e-mail to strengthen the corporate culture by centralising power at the top of the organisation. Later, the President realised that his initial expectations went against the essence of the new technology and, particularly, the multiple addressability feature, which makes e-mail a decentralising rather than a centralising technology. This led the president to regulate the use of E-mail, culminating with the introduction of the E-mail code of practice.

## 5. Conclusions

The first and most important implication of our research is that organisational learning concepts can explain complex phenomena that are involved in E-mail implementation and that may be difficult to explain by other factors. Comparing the technical success of the implementation at CR to other, less successful E-mail implementation projects that have been reported in the literature, it becomes apparent that E-mail implementation involves much more than mere installation. It is a complex learning process in which individuals learn new technical and political skills.

The second implication from our research has to do with the link between organisational learning and politics. Our study indicates that organisational learning and politics cannot be separated. Politics is involved in the decision to undertake new projects that may eventually lead to organisational learning. Politics is also involved in the negotiation process between management and employees that makes organisational learning possible. Most importantly, methods of political mobilisation and debate are an essential part of the body of knowledge that organisations acquire during an organisational learning process.

The third implication from our research relates to the political culture in which E-mail is to be implemented. Apparently, policy makers and IS professionals need to analyze the organisation well in advance of E-mail implementation. Potential social effects of the implementation, including its effect on the power distribution and politics in the organisation, must be considered.

The final implication from our research relates to the implementation of E-mail. Once managers have decided to implement E-mail, they need to consider which specific features should be enabled or disabled. Policy makers should be aware of the decentralisation effect of multiple addressability; it reduces the power distance between employees and top management. Because E-mail is socially potent, its implementation requires careful evaluation even when the project seems to be successful. Initial technical success does not guarantee long term usefulness or political harmony.

In sum, there are complex relationships between E-mail and social processes within organisations, particularly those involved in organisational learning. Awareness of social processes that surround the implementation may not prevent problems, but it can lead to more informed decision making by managers and IS professionals. If a pre-implementation analysis results in a decision to proceed with implementation (i.e., the implementation does not seem to be socially prohibitive), findings from the pre-implementation evaluation should still be considered when planning the logistics of the implementation. When all precautions have been taken, continuous evaluation for technology misuse is needed. Implementors need to make sure that aspects of organisational learning that can make the implementation a success do not go against organisational goals.
