# Electronic publishing: A product architecture perspective $^{1}$

Michael H. Zack

Northeastern University, College of Business Administration, 214 Hayden Hall, Boston, MA 02115, USA

## Abstract

Information products, like physical ones, require well-designed platforms from which multiple versions of a product can be easily and efficiently derived. This study investigated the design implications of converting a large subscription-based industry-research firm to an electronic publishing and distribution process. The analysis applied concepts used in designing architectures for physical goods. Electronic publishing technology, in addition to providing a new automated production platform, enabled the firm to move from a traditional document-based publishing paradigm to one more akin to an information refinery based on the storage and integration of modular information-units. That move had a significant impact on how documents were written, edited, and marketed. It was found that digitizing documents alone is not sufficient to create a flexible enough electronic publishing platform from which multiple versions of a document-based information product may be derived. Rather, the entire structure of the document must be reconceived in terms of more primitive information-units that can be digitized, indexed, and linked for retrieval in a variety of ways.

Keywords: Electronic publishing; Digital documents; Information products

## 1. Introduction

Product development is essential for the growth and survival of business firms. This is true for both physical and information goods. Efficient and effective development is achieved by designing architectures that enable many different product versions to be spun off quickly and cheaply for different markets and price-points. Additionally, successful firms continually renew their basic architectures and platforms to take advantage of new technologies $[8, 12]$ .

Product architecture has been addressed extensively with regard to assembled physical goods (e.g., [1, 5,

7]), and process architecture with regard to non-assembled physical goods (e.g., chemicals) [12]. Less attention has been paid to the architecture for the delivery of services [2, 4, 6], and almost no study has been made of the design, development, and delivery of information products and services.

To begin exploring the issues regarding electronic publishing, this paper presents a case study of the industry-research division of a large (over \$1 billion) information products firm which has begun converting its semi-automated, non-integrated document delivery process to a fully-digital, integrated process platform based on Lotus Notes. The conversion effort is analyzed according to an information product architecture framework developed by Meyer and Zack [10]. Using this framework, the results illustrate implications for standardizing document structure, for implementing composite design approaches, for achieving economic leverage from existing product families, and for achieving a strategic advantage in the marketplace.

## 2. Background

Publishing has traditionally centered around the document (e.g., book, article, report, etc.) as the basic unit of production. A document is an information product which, like a physical one, has a design architecture, either explicit or implied. In the physical arena, architecture has been shown to have a key influence on the efficiency and effectiveness by which products can be designed, developed, and delivered to meet market needs $[9]$ . Electronic publishing is based on documents that have been stored in a digital format which can be processed using computer-based information technologies. Representing a document digitally enables information technology (IT) to be integrated into the design architecture. Therefore, understanding the implications of digitizing documents for electronic publishing requires understanding the overall design architecture of the document-based product. Further, the effective application of IT to the creation and delivery of digital documents requires examining IT as a “manufacturing” technology functioning within the context of an information product and process architecture.

The literature on physical product design and architecture [e.g., [11]] offers guidance in understanding how to efficiently and effectively design products that address market needs, capture value-added elements, deliver them to markets, and integrate the entire process. The core concept is that of leverage created by building common product (for assembled goods) and process (for non-assembled goods) “platforms” from which derivatives (styles, models, etc.) can be created quickly and efficiently.

An assembled-goods product platform is a basic version that can be easily and efficiently transformed to a specific model (called a platform derivative), for example, by attaching a particular set of components selected from a wide range of potential add-ons. The key in this case is to create a robust platform, modular components, and standard interfaces among those components and between them and the platform. Automobile manufacturers have used some version of this approach for years (e.g., Chrysler's K-car platform).

For non-assembled goods such as petroleum, chemicals, or wire, the complexity of the process technology typically outweighs the complexity of the product itself. In fact, its “design” is primarily a function of the process for producing it. Again, the key is to create a basic process platform from which derivative processes (and thus products) can be efficiently created.

Information products comprise characteristics of both assembled and nonassembled goods, having both a product platform and a process platform (Figure 1). The physical analog suggested by this framework is that of an information “refinery” [3] or “pipeline” [14]. The pipeline model developed in [14] presents a means for describing the process platform used to create and distribute the information. The midpoint of this process model is represented by an information storehouse or repository from which actual information products can be derived. The information repository thus represents the product platform. It can be characterized by its content (e.g., text, numerical data, graphics, video, etc.) and the way that content is structured and arranged for retrieval, analysis, integration, association, and synthesis.

The repository is created from “raw” data or information that is acquired and refined or enhanced in some manner before being entered in the repository. Refinement can be physical (e.g., moving data from paper to an electronic medium) or logical (e.g., standardizing the format, removing errors, indexing the data, integrating data to common indices, adding some interpretation, etc.). Once created, the repository serves as the platform from which many different products can be derived, based on the format, distribution medium, content, and extent of value addition. If structured effectively, one platform should allow for the rapid and efficient spinning-off of many different derivatives for different markets at low incremental costs.

Underlying the process and product platforms is an IT infrastructure that should ideally support the seamless movement of data, information, or knowledge through the pipeline, from source to use. An organizational infrastructure can be mapped to these platforms as well, and should also support the seamless and effective movement of information through the pipeline as well as effective management and control of the process.

Finally, designs exist within the context of other firms in the industry representing suppliers, partners/collaborators and customers who form part of the overall value-added chain. Efficient and effective integration across firms (and in some cases across divisions of the same firm) requires that the firm's process and product platforms (pipeline and repository) mesh with those of suppliers, collaborating firms in the value-added process, and downstream customers. This is especially important where a product represents an integrated composite of information “subassemblies” provided by several different firms. In the physical arena, this approach is referred to as composite design.

From a strategic perspective, the relative value addition, cost addition, and barriers (both entry and exit) established for each component of the information product platform will have a significant impact on the firm's positioning in its industry [13]. One approach may be to classify firms by the level of front-end (acquisition and refining) vs. back-end (distribution and presentation) costs or entry barriers. The strategic value of a firm's information products may derive from exclusive access to information sources or from a unique expertise in some aspect of refining (front-end barriers) or from exclusive access to electronic distribution channels or modes of presentation (back-end barriers). This value must be compared to the cost for performing those process activities to determine the strategic impact and defensibility of the firm's position. These barriers and advantages can be significantly affected by the extent of digitization as well as the specific applications of IT to the process or product platforms.

## 3. Case study

## 3.1. Data collection methods

This section presents the findings of an in-depth study of an industry-research firm converting its traditional document publishing process to an integrated electronic publishing platform based on Lotus Notes. Data were collected by interviewing the Senior Vice President responsible for information and consulting services, the Director of Information Systems Strategy responsible for the electronic publishing project, the lead architect of the application, and a senior analyst/consultant to the project. Each person except the Director of IS Strategy was interviewed once for, approximately, one to two hours. The Director was interviewed three times for a total of about 5 hours. Additionally, the Director reviewed the collected data for accuracy.

Archival documentation including design documents, a discussion database used to support the project team, and related electronic mail messages were also reviewed.

## 3.2. The company

Technology Research Inc. (TRI – a pseudonym) is a leading international provider of market information and industry analysis to providers and users of IT. TRI employ more than 300 analysts and publishes annually more than 15,000 research reports addressing over 50 distinct subject areas (called “research programs”). Its hundreds of clients include corporate and government agencies. TRI’s strategic advantages have been its breadth and dept of subject coverage, its international scope of data collection, and its ability to deliver reliable, accurate and timely analysis.

TRI research programs are organized in major subject-area/technology divisions: Systems and Storage, Software, Personal Systems, Workgroup Applications, Communications, Distribution Channels, and International. Each of the more than fifty programs is generally managed as a stand-alone business (or profit and loss center – P and L), reflecting the entrepreneurial spirit of the company. Each research program has a separate P and L and is managed by a program director. Formerly, TRI had one consolidated research group. As the scope grew, TRI established discrete subject areas reflecting market segments.

New products are developed by identifying or creating a market for some type of research, establishing the data source contacts, creating the research report and selling a sufficient quantity of subscriptions. A limited amount of additional sales growth comes from cross-selling, that is, identifying a program not being purchased by a client yet similar to one currently being sold, and perhaps bundling the two under a special pricing plan. Additional opportunity exists to combine separate products into a composite one, although to date this had been limited by organizational, technical, and architectural barriers.

## 3.3. The product family

A set of related products derived from the same platform, or information repository in the case of information products, is called a product family. It can differ on several dimensions: the specific content extracted from the repository, its presentation format, the medium through which it is presented and distributed, and the extent of interactivity built into the product interface.

TRI offered several products and variations based on its research data repositories. Within each program, TRI's basic offering is a subscription to printed reports running typically from 5 to 30 pages. Reports may contain market data, case studies, technology and market trend and event analysis, and profiles of companies, markets, or products. Additionally, each program provides an annual Market Review and Forecast for its given niche. Each report includes text, tables, figures, and graphs, and is accompanied by an executive summary. Analysts provide an interpretation of their data and analysis. While specific formats may vary across programs, all reports are typically segmented into chapters and subsections. The primary medium is printed paper sent via the mails. The basic report is also available on CD ROM or computer disk, although the basic functionality of these products is similar to the printed version. TRI also packages this content in smaller, more frequently issued vehicles: newsletters, bulletins, and fax-flashes. TRI further exploits its content by providing clients a contracted amount of time per year of direct access to a team of analysts who use TRI's repository of research to answer specific ad hoc queries, typically via telephone.

TRI is in the process of testing the use of Lotus Notes as a distribution platform for its quarterly reports, with the other products to follow. The use of Notes for distribution, however, has not only affected the process platform, but has had a significant impact on the structure of the repository (product platform) and, therefore, the nature of the product family itself. Customers will be able to receive not only the document via an electronic connection to TRI, but may view the content of the report in more ways and at a finer grain than the traditional versions. TRI plans to track queries in the database to create a repository of customer query and answer history. This repository may provide a platform for additional products and services.

## 3.4. The process and product platforms

TRI's process platform (Figure 2) consists of two-stages: analysis and publishing, similar to the reporting and layout processes of a newspaper operation. Analysis involves collecting, interpreting and analyzing market data, and reporting the results. The output of this process is the input for the publishing process, where the report is formatted to common standards, published in a variety of media, and distributed. The link between the stages is represented by an editing loop between the author/analyst and a copy editor in the publishing department.

## 3.4.1. Stage 1: Analysis

Acquisition: Data is obtained via vendor and end-user interviews and surveys. The top few vendors in a market representing most of the volume are interviewed via the telephone. The remaining vendors and end-user community are surveyed via the mail. Data collection is based on ongoing relationships between the data collectors (analysts) and the companies. Each analyst “owns” the data and is accountable for its accuracy. Analysts have ongoing feedback and discussion/debriefing with key vendors to ensure accuracy and reasonableness of forecasts.

Refining: Incoming data are first captured and converted to a digital electronic form, typically in the form of data tables (spreadsheet or database) and textual notes. Data are made consistent in format to previous analysis and reporting for the same program. The research analyst interprets the accuracy, meaningfulness and credibility of the data. The process is similar to investigative reporting, in that analysts challenge the numbers and try to get “the story behind the numbers.” The goal is to build a picture of the market that all key players (vendors, analysts, and users of a particular technology) find reasonable. Data integrity is a function of the analyst’s capabilities and credibility. If a market is new, the analyst must create a reasonable story that defines and supports the assumptions on which the forecast is based. The analyst ultimately creates a text-based description and interpretation of the data accompanied by tables, figures, and graphs.

Fig. 2. Architecture of TRI's information products.

Storage Repository: Currently there is no central or standard repository for data and analysis. Each analyst stores his or her data and information on a personal computer in a form that is most convenient for him or her. Most data is stored in the form of word processing text files, electronic spreadsheets, databases, and graphics files. There is, generally, no standard format or software platform. Further, there tend to be different approaches to analyzing data and reporting results. Currently, data are reconciled across programs and countries only at an aggregate level to ensure that all related forecasts are consistent. For example, a forecast of PC sales and a forecast of PC printers should reflect the same assumptions regarding the forecast of PC units. TRI has consciously maintained an entrepreneurial, decentralized culture, and has enjoyed success with this model. However, several prototype projects are in progress to standardize subsets of the data repositories (e.g., survey data) across programs within a particular product cluster.

Distribution: Analysts distribute their reports in two ways. They send their work in various software formats (hard copy plus disk) to the production department for conversion to the standard publication style. They also may distribute a preliminary version of their report directly to the customer, reflecting the early data obtained via telephone surveys. These early reports are distributed via fax, electronic mail, or bulletin board service. One large client even uploads a report directly from one analyst's PC. TRI is also using a World Wide Web site for distribution.

Fax flashes are distributed in both ways: the analyst faxes the information directly, but also submits the fax to the production department where it is formally produced for printing and distribution through regular channels.

Presentation: In all cases, the reports sent directly from the analyst are in a format and style unique to that analyst and research program.

## 3.4.2. Stage 2: Production

Acquisition: Analysts submit a disk plus hard copy of their research report with a list of keywords to an editor.

Refining: All digital text, table, and spreadsheet files received from analysts are converted to MS Word for Macintosh format and assigned a unique document number as part of their file name. The files proceed through a series of folders beginning with “untouched”, then to “preliminary” for fact checking and technical data verification and, finally, to their own dedicated folder where the document awaits changes by the copy editor. Editors work with the analyst to create the final version. After editing the document components, text, figures and graphics files are imported to the Quark Express desktop publishing environment running on Apple Macintosh computers. It is here that documents are marked up to give a common look-and-feel regarding format, style, titles, tables of contents, etc. The structuring and formatting are primarily stylistic, in that the basic structure of the document content remains essentially the same as that created by the analyst. Thus, while the report is digitized, its basic structure reflects that of the printed document.

The refining process for Notes distribution is significantly different to that for the traditional products (viz., printed reports, CD ROM, and reports on disk). Documents to be published via Lotus Notes proceed through an additional conversion process. The primary structural difference is the explicit decomposition of each document into a set of information units, providing a much lower level of granularity than the entire document, enabling a more value-added approach to presenting and accessing the information contained in a report. TRI has defined one information unit to be a subtitle plus text, or a graphic element (figure, table, text box, etc.). Information units are also assigned to document summaries, table of contents, table of tables, table of figures, and document abstracts. TRI chose to map one information unit to one Notes display screen so that the segmentation of a document roughly equates one information unit with about one screen-full of text. An information unit could be of any length, but TRI did not want to have the user scroll through multiple screens. As a side benefit, this greater level of granularity provides more flexibility for accessing and displaying the content.

The primary content difference between the Notes version and the other versions is the assigning of technology and vendor keywords to each information unit, enabling a more efficient search than merely by scanning an entire document.

A hard copy version printed from the Quark system is sent to a Notes editor where it is marked up to indicate information units and their hierarchical level (up to three levels below the chapter are allowed). Additional keywords are added as needed, and hypertext links are indicated to link text, tables, and figures. The Word for Macintosh version is imported into Notes where the individual information units are cut and pasted online into information-unit entry forms and index values are entered. After this, the document is moved to the “ready-for-proofing” view. After proofing, the document is moved to the “ready-for-distribution” view. Using these views, editors can easily display and manage their work-in-process. Documents with ready-for-distribution status are periodically replicated to a distribution server.

Storage Repository: All documents are stored in the publication department workstation as digital files using a standard naming convention. Files (documents and their components) are not integrated or stored at a grain below the document. Managing the repository is a matter of managing files on a disk.

Documents converted to Notes are also stored in a database (production version) with each information unit indexed by program, document reference number, chapter, and level. This creates an integrated repository in which all document elements are stored and indexed in a similar manner. This enables production to view all elements of a document or a particular type of element across all documents. Production department personnel can view a listing of the Notes repository contents by program, document reference number, company, technology, chapter and level, and workflow status. A limited workflow management capability is available, as documents are also flagged indicating whether or not they are ready for proofreading and for distribution (i.e., replication to the distribution server).

Distribution: In addition to hard copy reports, digital versions of the printed document are placed on a disk or CD ROM. Printed and digitized versions are sent to the customer via the mail. Distribution of Notes based documents is via the Notes replication facility. Customers dial into the TRI external distribution server where they automatically receive the latest changes to the document database stored within their local Notes server.

Presentation and use: Hard copy reports are placed in binders by the customer, presumably stored by time period and program, and accessed via a traditional index and table of contents. Disk and CD ROM versions are used in essentially the same manner, but can be filed and displayed more efficiently using a computer, although cut and paste is not supported.

Notes-based documents utilize the views and text-search capabilities of Notes to allow searching and presenting the material much more flexibly than a traditional document. Users may employ full-text search, or may display a table of contents of all information units by company, technology, analyst (author), month of publication, or by program. The information units of each report are presented as a three-level (chapter and two sub-levels) hierarchical list. Thus, a user could directly locate and display a particular text, graphic or table, rather than have to scroll through an entire report. Information units are linked in a hypertext fashion so that a user may link from the text to a graphic or table directly and then return to the text. TRI currently is employing these links in a limited fashion, essentially to replicate the process of reading a hard copy report. However, this will make it possible to create a richer set of links as the needs of the customer are better defined.

## 3.5. Technical infrastructure

All documents, including the subset to be distributed via Notes, move through the same front-end technology infrastructure (Figure 3). At the acquisition stage, analysts using stand-alone PCs (and occasionally paper and pencil) store text, data, graphics, and spreadsheets in many different formats, software applications, and hardware platforms. These electronic files are ultimately converted to MS Word for printing an editing copy and then to Quark Express for a markup. Most software applications of the analysts can be converted easily without dropping or transforming characters. The result is a set of files with a standard electronic storage format, but not necessarily a standard content format.

For documents to be distributed via Lotus Notes, a hard copy is printed from Quark to be used for assigning information units and hyperlinks and to proof and edit the style elements of the Notes version to resemble the printed version. The MS Word version is imported to Notes, as Notes easily imports MS Word files with only limited dropping or transforming of characters.

The Notes publishing application is split into three components, each a separate database: publication, distribution-internal, and distribution-external; these databases are replicas of each other and are located on separate servers: the publication, hub, and external servers, respectively. The designs of the publication and distribution databases are different. The publication database has views designed for data entry and editing, while the distribution database provides views designed for presentation of data to the user. There is a design “firewall” which keeps design changes from replicating between them, but allows data to flow. Once documents in the publication database are flagged as being “ready-for-distribution”, they are copied to the distribution database. As an additional precaution, customers do not have direct access to the distribution database on the hub server. The hub server places an identical replica of the distribution database on three external servers. They can be remotely accessed by customers through dial-up telephone lines.

## 4. Discussion and implications

TRI's entire organization, which today still works within the traditional publishing paradigm, is being subjected by internal (technology-driven) and external (market-driven) pressures to change to one supporting an integrated and finer-grained architecture. It could be argued that TRI actually has two separate architectures that are only loosely integrated. TRI now uses considerable resources in maintaining a similar look-and-feel between its traditional products and its Notes-based products. This requires a large amount of proofing, editing, and manipulating of electronic versions of the documents involving both editors and analysts.

TRI's traditional products (viz., documents distributed on paper, CD ROM and disk) have included little to exploit the digital format of the information repository to enhance their variety or features. Although TRI has employed IT extensively to support individual analysts as they manage data acquisition, analysis, interpretation, and reporting, it has not yet employed the technology to enhance the productivity of the process as a whole: individual analysts continue to work with their privately maintained data repositories and authoring templates. Further, although TRI has also employed IT to increase the productivity of editing and mark-up process, the lack of a common authoring/editing technology environment has added a significant overhead to the process of converting acquired data to published information. Thus, the flexibility and efficiency of the process is constrained.

Employing the document as the primary repository unit for traditional products has constrained TRI from realizing a leverage and exploiting the commonalities among various forms of its products in each product family. The sooner units of information common to multiple products can be created and identified, the greater the potential leverage. However, each version of a product currently requires the analyst to recreate, to some degree, a repository starting at the basic level of raw data. The major source of process leverage has been at the final, and lowest-value stage of the process platform, i.e., distribution of the final report from the same source files across different media. Were more granular information units to be created by analysts at the outset of the process, mixing and matching these units in various product versions would be much easier and more efficient.

TRI's implementation of Notes has directly confronted the traditional paradigm. However, to limit the complexity of the project and avoid the desktop publishing limitations of Notes, the implementation has been directed at the downstream stages rather than encompassing the entire flow. Although analysts are still writing at the grain of an entire document rather than working with a set of information-unit building blocks to prepare their reports, the publishing unit has taken on the task of converting the document to an indexed and linked set of information units, thus building a flexible repository from which a much greater variety of products and services could be derived. It is important to note that the implementation of information units and the implementation of Notes are separate issues.

Despite analysts continuing to take a traditional document-centered approach to report-writing, the downstream segmentation of documents into information units is having an upstream influence on how a document should be written. Traditionally, when producing a document using a desktop publishing package and document-based medium, editors could structure the document in any way that was meaningful to the analyst, editor and, presumably, the reader. Analysts and editors could employ titles, graphic elements, subtitles, and lower-level titles as needed without regard to a standard form. For example, a document might have a subtitle without any text associated with it. The installation of Notes has changed this to some degree. Subtitles with no text, for example, result in a “null” information unit under one definition of an information unit (subtitle plus text), and in fact would appear in Notes as an empty screen. A document might have a subtitled graphic which would normally be read as one chunk of information in a printed report but which would represent two separate information units (one null) under the new information-unit scheme. The Editors (and indirectly, analysts) are now having to structure a document in more standard chunks using more formal rules. Essentially, this is forcing a change from a word processing/desktop publishing paradigm to a database management paradigm. Information units are more structured than documents and require a standard and replicatable definition of the information being stored, similar to that in a database. Documents are now changing over from a semi-structured format to a standardized and structured storage and retrieval format. In fact, the Notes repository of information units is best thought of as an information-base.

The use of linked information units is also changing the way users read a document and, therefore, how a document should be written. For example, because a text and its supporting elements are now separable, the reader may not call up the text before viewing the supporting element whereas, in print mode, both would appear together. The writer must now determine where redundant text and graphs are appropriate and whether each speaks for itself adequately.

In addition to influencing the traditional report writing process, new functions and tasks have emerged as a result of creating information units and storing them in an electronically searchable environment. Editors now have to assign keywords to information units where this was not required for traditional document versions. They are now having to function as an abstracting service. Because searchable keywords have never been needed, some confusion has arisen regarding keyword standards and approaches. For example, no standard spelling for company names or technological terms has ever been used. In fact, the editors even had to create a standard naming convention for the TRI programs as this did not previously exist, but is now a key index.

Managing the integrity of the keyword process is essential for maintaining the quality and value of a product. For example, the backlog of documents to be converted is resulting in editors assigning low-value but quickly assigned keywords, rather than giving each document a careful read and creating high-value topic-based keywords that add something beyond the text already in the document. Low-value key words (e.g., words taken from the text) essentially add little value beyond the user's carrying out a full-text search based on a user-defined keyword or phrase. Keywording procedures are also being defined as needed; e.g., a company memo describing how to determine the company and technology keywords that applied to a particular information unit is:

“Rather than including every company name that occurs in a unit, try to determine if a substantial portion of the unit is about that subject. We want to distinguish between where a company or technology is simply mentioned from those where it is the subject of the discussion.”

Keywording is a new “technology” for TRI to manage, learn, and standardize and editors will have to be trained in this new endeavor.

Standardization and training will also need to be extended to creating hyperlinks. Although the benefits of decomposing documents into finer units may be significant, the finer the grain, the more “overhead” associated with integrating such elements to exploit the granularity and to provide more value. TRI originally did not plan to embed links in the documents, but learned quickly that customers required this functionality just to replicate the reading of the document as though it were on hard copy. These demands will be expected to grow in time as customers discover new ways to search and integrate information units.

Information units are aggregated to form a table of contents serving as the primary means to access the document content. As customers become more familiar with the user interface, their needs will begin to expand. As users develop new demands and needs for information access, the information unit may be affected. Taking a process platform view suggests that different views on the data may require modifying the product platform itself in terms of new standards, definitions or content.

The issue of standards has become one of the most significant for TRI, as few exist. They are an issue that is in many ways counter to the culture of the organization. At the fundamental level, TRI has even been struggling to create a standard and consistent definition and implementation of an information unit, as the following excerpt from an editor's memo shows:

"In looking at how the report in DCI came out I have questions about how it was marked up. It seems to me that we really have three "Chapters", "Primary Market Analysis", "Secondary Market Analysis", and Appendix. rather than the ten chapters we show. Perhaps I'm missing something. Can we go over this together?"

The main objective for architecting a flexible yet standardized product and process platform is to be able to derive new products rapidly and efficiently from the existing product platform and, given the appropriate standardization of platform design and interfaces, build composite products that integrate multiple platforms. For TRI, this would translate as being able to “slice and dice” each program’s repository to squeeze more products out of each program, as well as exploiting opportunities for combining information units across programs to create integrated composite research reports for new markets.

The trend in market demand is toward interdisciplinary topics: e.g., mobile computing, or client/server. This is, in large part, a reflection of the dynamic changes in IT. Traditional clusters of knowledge about such technologies have similarly changed significantly over the years. These new emerging knowledge clusters cut across TRI's internal organizational boundaries and programs and represent a key product renewal challenge. Building a totally flexible and integrable set of cross-disciplinary repositories and information units can directly help meet this challenge.

TRI currently attempts to repackage the same data in different ways to exploit the repository. However this has been limited by the difficulty in linking the repositories of each program and in having to work at the grain of the entire document. Different versions may often be only a matter of changing the introduction of a report to address a different audience. However, given the profit and loss responsibility for each program, program directors need to be sure they have a relatively large minimum market before introducing new products. By moving toward a more modular and reusable platform, TRI would be able to remarket its repositories more easily, while, at the same time reducing the incremental cost for bringing out new products to where they could support small niches while remaining profitable on a program-by-program basis. Pushing this to the full extent to where TRI is creating composite products by linking across programs would not only require a more standardized and integrated set of repositories, but may also require a new TRI organizational architecture that would make cross-selling and integrating programs much easier. For example, a formal mechanism for establishing cross-product teams, performance tracking, and reward mechanisms may be required to build incentives for taking a risk in developing a new composite product rather than trying to squeeze incremental profit out of an existing program repository. An integrated marketing and sales approach would also be required, for example, providing hyperlinks to catalogs, abstracts, or marketing materials for programs, products and services currently not being purchased. The MIS manager at

TRI suggested that the electronic catalog serve as a central link to all published material, and derive its content from that body of material. TRI has begun experimenting with “virtual” programs by creating liaison roles across programs, thus maintaining the existing product line repositories and organization structure. Each cross-program product, however, requires creating a program repository from scratch.

The current TRI model is designed to sell an entire cluster of outputs and services. However, in an electronic context, each document could be sold separately. TRI has the opportunity to create new products and services and will need to understand how to market and price them. For example, TRI is experimenting with selling executive summaries, traditionally considered part of a main report, as separate products. The TRI model has also been to sell ongoing subscriptions. With electronic access, more people at a client site will, potentially, be customers for the reports. However, customer departments other than the traditional marketing research departments may have more limited needs. Many potential customers may only need limited access to a report perhaps for just a specific month or time period, or perhaps a specific graph only. The incremental technical effort would be minimal.

## 5. Conclusion

This paper has illustrated an example of how a product architecture framework can be used to describe the production of digital documents viewed as information products. Its main conclusion is that digitizing a document is not, by itself, adequate to exploit the electronic publishing opportunities for flexibility and innovation in the design and delivery of new information products. Such digitized documents must be structured within a framework of building a modular and flexible platform from which multiple variations can be rapidly and efficiently created as new market niches are identified. Information units are the critical structural element for creating modular digital documents.

Additionally, a robust, seamless and scalable technology infrastructure is a key to ensuring the flexibility required for a process platform. It must provide an unlimited set of “views” of rich, multimedia documents in an interactive mode, embed hyperlinks, and provide an efficient distribution channel to make it useful for building the foundation of a digital document process platform. Further, embedding discussion databases in the process could make the system more efficient in coordinating among analysts and editors, and provide an effective means to create a dialog with the customer regarding TRI's products and services. TRI is currently planning to experiment with offering its query service using this approach. The question and answer database that may result will provide an additional repository from which new products may be derived.

Finally, implementing this new architecture must be seen as an organizational and social, as well as, a technical intervention. Roles and responsibilities of TRI's analysts, editors, and IT professionals are changing. The move toward process and content standards has reduced the analysts' level of autonomy and discretion regarding writing format and style, while placing many of those decisions in the hands of editors and production clerks. Marketing integrated programs requires closer cooperation among decentralized and autonomous business units, contrary to TRI's prevailing culture. Ultimately, success in the new approach to publishing will be based as much on effectively managing organizational change as in implementing a sound product architecture and electronic publishing technology.
