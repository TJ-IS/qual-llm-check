# Identifying the value and importance of an information system application

Arik Ragowsky $^{a,*}$ , Niv Ahituv $^{b}$ , Seev Neumann $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Accounting, School of Business Administration, 132 Rands House, Wayne State University,, Detroit, MI 48202, USA $^{b}$ Computers and Information Systems Program, Faculty of Management, Tel-Aviv University, Tel-Aviv 69978, Israel

## Abstract

Information systems are vital to the operation and management of every organization. Managers who invest large amounts of money and other resources in information systems often do not know which information systems applications will benefit the organization. This article proposes and tests an approach for evaluating the benefit to the organization gained by the use of an individual information systems application. The test group was comprised of a random sample of 310 medium and large sized manufacturing organizations. We found that different organizations gain different benefits by using the same information system applications, and that the benefit an organization gains from using a computerized application increases as a function of the increase of two types of independent variables representing the level of complexity and uncertainty regarding a specific activity (customers, suppliers, etc.), and the impact of the decision supported by the added information on the organization's objectives.

Keywords: Benefit of information system application; Levels of complexity and uncertainty; Impact of information; Organizational objective; Organizational characteristics

## 1. Introduction

Manufacturing organizations can benefit from the use of computerized information systems (IS) in several ways. They can, for example, save 25–30% of inventory holding costs by reducing the amount of inventory, decrease purchasing and raw material costs by about 15% by enabling the buyer to obtain the best possible conditions, reduce working hours, and lower their response time to market demands by using computerized IS applications [13, 21, 24, 33].

However, it is difficult to estimate the benefits of IS applications, for several reasons. Some of the reasons are [1, 2, 3, 4]:

1. The real benefit can only be estimated long after the system has been installed.

2. To estimate the real benefit, the performance variables would have to be measured before installing the system, and then compared with performance data obtained after installing the system in order to evaluate benefits. Unfortunately, before-the-fact data are usually unavailable.

3. External factors, such as government policy, economic conditions, and technological innovation, affect the variables being measured. It is rarely impossible to isolate the effect of external factors and identify the direct contribution of the IS application.

4. Unlike cost reduction or sales increase, there are many less tangible benefits of IS applications. For example, decision making processes are improved and daily management is made more efficient. These benefits cannot be precisely measured.

Previous studies have examined the relationship between investment in information technology (IT) and the performance level of the organization [5, 7, 9, 15, 17, 23, 25, 32]. Many of the studies did not find a positive relationship between the two variables. However, none of these studies looked for differences between firms within the group under study. All the studies treated all firms in the sample as equal with respect to the benefit IS applications would yield. This study argues that differences in organizations produce differences in the benefits gained from the use of similar IS applications. In addition, all of these studies treated the organization's information systems as one cohesive entity. None tried to find the relationship between particular IS applications and their benefits to the organization as a function of the organizational characteristics. In these studies, organizational characteristics were alluded to only when task characteristics were said to be an important determinant of end user behavior [11]. Yet when task characteristics were considered, they were usually confined to descriptive measures such as organizational job level [e.g., [14, 22]]. The primary objective of this study is to construct a model and an approach to cope with these difficulties in assessing the investment in individual IS applications as a function of their benefits to the organization.

## 2. A model for justifying the use of IS applications

The main problem as mentioned above is the lack of linkage between the investment in IS and the performance of the organization, as Land, et al. [19] conclude: “Despite the continuous growth in the application of Information Technology and the success the technology has had in helping whole industries to be transformed, it is accepted that too many applications still fail to meet the aspirations of their sponsors (Strassmann, 1984) or to earn an adequate return on the resources devoted to their design and implementation." These same authors [19] explain that they began "with the hypothesis that many of the implementation problems met in introducing new information systems, or in making use of new Information Technology (IT)-based tools, stem from social and organizational factors rather than from shortcomings in the technology itself." Another study by Land et al. [18] supports this hypothesis, which we think to be correct with respect to implementation of the IS. Nevertheless, for planning and justifying the development, purchasing and implementation of the IS, there is a need to identify the benefit an organization CAN gain from using IS based on the organizational characteristics and needs, regardless of any social elements.

A recent study [28], also conducted by Strassman, found that “information technology makes sense only when it solves a company’s specific problems such as overhead cost of control, production management, or support of customer services.” In order to solve such problems by using information systems, an organization, of course, has to use individual applications such as processing customer orders. Hence, it would be useful to be able to measure the benefit to the organization derived from each IS application.

Strassman found that the “amount of computing power” different organizations need “would vary a great deal per dollar of revenue”, [29] implying that different organizations may derive different benefits from using the same information technology.

In order to help managers to plan their investment in IS, and in order to find new channels for research in this topic, we try to expand the findings of the Strassman studies and to look for variables that impact the benefit an organization may gain from using IS.

We hypothesize that firms differ in the benefits they gain from similar IS applications (e.g., suppliers and purchase orders, customers and sales). Therefore, the main problem is how to relate the benefit a specific organization can derive from an individual IS application to the organization's characteristics.

IS applications enable the organization to gather more information and to use this information for decision making by using decision support systems [4]. To determine what should be the investment in an information system, it is important to estimate the amount of information the organization really needs for a specific activity (e.g., inventory management, customer orders), and what the value of added information is.

Clearly, the amount of information needed by the organization is difficult to estimate. One way to approach this question is by addressing the uncertainty level associated with a certain activity. The greater the level of uncertainty, the larger the amount of information needed by decision makers $[26]$ . A decision maker needs the most relevant information regarding each probable future event in order to make a better decision. Consequently, the complexity of decision making is higher. Appropriate information can reduce random errors on decision variance and, consequently reduce task complexity $[8]$ . When there are many possible events, the level of uncertainty/complexity is higher, and, therefore, more information is needed. Daft and Lengel $[10]$ argue that “one of the basic reasons organizations process information is to reduce uncertainty.” Ghani $[11]$ suggest that “the information processing view of organizations is that organizational effectiveness is a function of the fit between task uncertainty and the information processing capacity of the organization.” However, additional information does not necessarily increase the value of the information $[6]$ .

The value of the information is a function of the impact of a decision on reaching the organization's objectives and increasing the organization's profitability [16, 27]. It is obvious that the relationship between added information and reduction of uncertainty/complexity is positive [22]. However, it is often hard to measure the monetary or utility value of information in terms of meeting an organizational objective.

It is possible that an organizational activity (e.g., suppliers, customers) involves a high degree of uncertainty/complexity, but has a low impact on the organization's objectives. For example, consider an organization whose corporate strategy is cost reduction and that has to deal with a very large number of suppliers of raw materials, where many differences prevail among the suppliers in terms of prices, lead time, etc. Suppose that a priori, no particular supplier is preferred. The organization has to examine a large quantity of data on the suppliers in order to decide on the best purchase. Here, the level of uncertainty/ complexity is very high. Assuming that the organization can reduce raw materials cost by 15% using a new computerized purchasing system, it may then seem justified to implement a computerized purchasing application. However, if the raw material cost of the final product is small, relative to labor and other costs, then even the best decision concerning suppliers will have little impact on meeting the organization's goal to reduce cost. For instance, if the material cost is 5% of the total costs of the product, the saving will be C./5% (15% times 5%). In this case, the information about suppliers is not particularly valuable, even though the level of uncertainty concerning suppliers is high.

Hence, reducing the level of uncertainty/complexity should not be the only determining factor in considering a new computerized IS application. In each case the decision maker must weigh the relative importance to the organization in terms of satisfying organizational objectives.

We argue that the contribution of IS applications varies for each organization and depends on the level of uncertainty/complexity and the impact of the decision on the organization's objectives. A simplified illustration of this idea is depicted in Figure 1.

Figure 1 roughly delineates four schematic regions related to the two variables. In region A the level of uncertainty is high. Much information is missing, but the impact of the decisions on the organization's objectives is low (e.g., the relative share of raw materials in the final product is low). Hence, there is no point in investing more resources to acquire additional information.

## 3. The study

## 3.1. Research questions and propositions

The research questions discussed above entail the following propositions:

1. Manufacturing organizations gain different benefits from using similar IS applications depending on organizational characteristics.

2. The greater the level of uncertainty/complexity involved in a certain functional area, and the greater the impact of the decisions on the organization's objectives, the higher the value of the IS application to the organization.

## 3.2. Sample and research instrument

The research is based on a sample of 310 manufacturing organizations in Israel. Questionnaires were filled out in personal interviews conducted between May 1991 and March 1992. (The questionnaire is available from the authors upon request). This questionnaire was divided into two sections. The first section included questions about the organization's characteristics (e.g., number of suppliers, relative share of raw materials cost in the cost of the final product, number of customers, average lead time to customers, number of products, number of production lines, volume of sales, number of employees). In the second section the respondent was asked to assess the perceived benefit the organization derives from the use of IS applications for each of several functional areas on a scale of 1–7, where 1 indicates very low benefit, and 7 indicates very high benefit. Ranking the benefit on semantic scales (usually, but not necessarily, ranging from 1–7) is a well-tested method for investigating the perceived benefit [3].

The weaknesses of studying the perceived benefit are well known: it is subjective since it reflects the value the individual associates with the information system. Ideally the real benefit derived by using IS (e.g., the value of cost reduction, the volume of the increased sales) is a much better measure [3]. However, it is difficult to estimate the real benefit of IS applications, as mentioned earlier.

A pilot survey conducted in several organizations could not determine the real benefit the organization gains by using IS because the data on performance differences was unavailable. The study in $[3]$ notes that “as the focus of IS research and practice moves towards unstructured decisions and high level IS, it seems that the perceived value will capture more attention.” Since the respondents were asked about the use of IS for unstructured decisions as well (e.g., competition), we decided to employ the perceived benefit.

A structured interview was conducted with a senior manager in each organization. The person selected was a user of IS, well aware of the benefits gained by using IS for different functional areas in the organization, and authorized to make decisions regarding investment in IS. The assessments of these managers is a good measure for the perceived benefit. Based on ANOVA we found no significant differences in the benefits perceived based on the respondent's role within the organization.

As mentioned above, the questionnaires were filled out by interviews. The authors trained each of the ten interviewers personally. Every candidate interviewer had first to learn the theoretical background regarding the study's issue and the questionnaire. Then the candidate joined the authors to observe two or three interviews. After the candidates felt they were ready for interviewing, they had to interview two or three respondents while the author was observing them. This training was designed to avoid bias due to differences among interviewers.

## 3.3. The variables

We concentrate here on data collected for two typical manufacturing applications:

## 1. Suppliers and purchase order management

## 2. Customer orders

Although there are many more applications in manufacturing organizations, the purpose of this study is to present and examine an approach to benefit evaluation, rather than to detail the benefits for each application. Therefore we present the analysis of only two applications.

## 3.3.1. The dependent variable

The dependent variable for each application represents the perceived value to the organization from using the application. Respondents were asked to assess the benefit of each application on a scale of 1–7.

## 3.3.2. The independent variables

The independent variables for each application include one variable representing the level of uncertainty/complexity, and one representing the impact of the decision (supported by the information system) on the organization's objectives. The values of the independent variables were divided into categories. Each category represents a group of organizations determined by its organizational characteristics. For example: regarding the number of suppliers, the first group includes organizations with a small number of suppliers, the second group a medium number of suppliers, and the third group organizations with many suppliers. With this partitioning we could examine the first research proposition using analysis of variance and identify differences in the benefits gained from using similar applications among the various categories. Since this is a pioneering study, there was no established partitioning for these variables. The partitioning was based on the interviewees' assessment. A sensitivity analysis $^{1}$ was employed to ensure the consistency of the partitioning.

## 4. Analysis method

To examine the research propositions we used analysis of variance with trend analysis. We expected to find a monotonic increase in the benefit gained by using the application (the dependent variable) as a function of the increase of the two independent variables. The two independent variables were combined in a joint independent variable that incorporates the degree of uncertainty/complexity and the impact of the decision on the organization's objectives.

Our examination found no interaction between the independent variables (see Appendix B). We expected to find that the benefit to the organization from the use of a specific application increases as the level of the independent variables increases.

## 5. Findings

In this section we present the analysis of the two IS applications selected for this study: Suppliers and Purchase Order system, and Customer Orders system.

## 5.1. Suppliers and purchase order systems

The suppliers and purchase order application manages all the details regarding the organization's suppliers and regarding each raw material purchase order. The information provided by this application helps the organization in planning the purchase of raw materials according to the anticipated needs of the production plan. By using this information the organization obtains the best prices for raw materials and receives the supply as close as possible to the date it is needed.

The independent variable relating to the impact of the decision on the organization's objectives is the proportion of the cost of raw material in the cost of the final product. Based on the approach presented in subsection 3.3.2, we divided the variable values into three categories as follows:

1. The proportion of raw materials cost is equal to or less than 30%

2. The proportion of raw materials cost is 31–50%

3. The proportion of raw materials cost is greater than 50%

The independent variable relating to the degree of uncertainty for this application reflects the differences among the suppliers. Suppliers can differ in terms of three attributes: price, quality, and supply lead time (i.e., different lead times for the same raw material across the suppliers). The variable values were divided into three categories ordered in an apparently ascending level of uncertainty:

1. Cases in which there is only one supplier for an individual raw material item, or several suppliers without differences in prices, lead time, and quality of raw material.

2. Cases in which there are several suppliers for an individual raw material, and there are differences among the suppliers in only one of the above attributes.

3. Cases in which there are several suppliers for an individual raw material, and there are differences among the suppliers in two or three of the above attributes.

There might be many other organizational characteristics that may represent the level of uncertainty/complexity and the impact of the decision on the organization's objectives. Since the purpose of this study is to present and examine an approach, we show the analysis for only two organizational characteristics.

## 5.2. The customer orders system

The customer orders system is the application that processes and tracks each customer order and provides technical details, production process, supply time to the customer, etc.

The independent variable chosen to relate to the impact of the decision on the organization's objectives is the supply time for customers (the period between receiving an order and filling it). One way to increase the sales volume is to decrease the supply time for the customers. The shorter the supply time, the higher the sales volume a manufacturing company can generate with fixed manufacturing facilities [12]. Based on the approach presented in subsection 3.3.2 we divided the variable values into the two following categories:

1. Short supply time: up to 25 days.

## 2. Long supply time: more than 25 days.

The independent variable related to the degree of uncertainty/complexity for this application reflects the production method. There are three production methods:

1. Process manufacturing: Processes in which standard and simple products are produced over a period of time, usually a week or few weeks (e.g., food, chemicals). Customers' orders in this case are relatively simple and differ only as to quantities and supply dates. Hence, the level of complexity is low, and so is the level of uncertainty

2. Work station manufacturing: In this method there are various work stations on the plant floor. Each customer's order can be processed at different work stations, or in a different order at the same work stations. For these customers' orders more information is needed to manage the production at maximum efficiency, because the level of uncertainty/complexity is higher than in process manufacturing.

3. Custom manufacturing: In this method each customer's order is a separate project. Sometimes the production process for each order is based at individual work stations. Much more information is needed to manage each customer's order, and hence the level of uncertainty/complexity is higher than in the two previous production methods.

## 6. Discussion and conclusions

The value of information for any organization is of paramount importance when considering investment in new IS applications. Many studies which have attempted to find the relationship between investment in IS applications and the performance level of the organization have not found a causal relationship $[5, 9, 16, 20, 32]$ . All of these studies have been holistic, relating the total IS to the organization as a single entity. They did not attempt to find a relationship between a single application and its benefit to the organization, or a relationship between organizational characteristics and the benefit the organization gains from using a specific application. This study indicates what types of organizational characteristics impact the benefit an organization can gain by using an individual application.

The study focused on two main factors that were expected to affect the potential benefit an organization can gain by developing and using an IS application. One factor is the level of uncertainty/complexity, and the other is the impact of the application on the organization's objectives through the decisions affected by the information. A joint variable representing these two variables was constructed to examine the relationship between it as an independent variable, and the benefit as the dependent variable. Although this study did not actually find a direct relationship between investment in IS application and the performance of the organization, it did find an approach to relate the organization's characteristics to the benefit the organization may gain from using a certain application.

The analyses performed in this study found a positive relationship between the joint variable and the benefit. This was that the benefit to the organization from a specific application increases almost monotonically as the level of the joint variable increases.

Another important finding of this study is that organizations gain different benefits when using similar applications for an individual functional area as a function of the organizational characteristics.

The importance of this study for the academic community is that it presents a new approach for linking organization's characteristics and the benefit the organization can gain by using an IS application. Hence, future research should look for more direct linkage between these factors. The purpose of such future study should be to formulate the relationship more rigorously, and to link them to investment in IT.

The importance of this study for practitioners is that manufacturing organizations could hone the criteria by which they consider investments in IS applications. Using the method and findings presented in this study, management can identify applications that would lead to higher benefits for the organization. The method allows managers to assess the benefits of IS application before they are installed. The approach underlying this research provides a new tool for identifying the importance of a specific application to the organization.

While this study considered only manufacturing organizations, the approach could be applied to other kinds of organizations (banks, insurance companies, government, etc.) by identifying the applications, the variables that represent uncertainty, and the variables that represent the importance of the information.
