# An empirical investigation on factors affecting the acceptance of CASE by systems developers

Patrick Y.K. Chau $^{*}$

Department of Information and Systems Management, School of Business and Management, The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, Kowloon, Hong Kong

## Abstract

This paper presents the findings of an empirical study of factors affecting the acceptance of CASE by systems developers. Based on two research models (technology acceptance and personal computer utilization) recently published in IS literature, an integrated model which consists of six variables and incorporates key elements of both models was developed to examine determinants of CASE acceptance. The findings indicate that ease of use has the largest influence on CASE acceptance, followed by long-term consequences. Both transitional support and near-term usefulness do not have a significant direct effect on the acceptance but exhibit their indirect influence through ease of use and/or long-term consequences. The implementation gap was found to have a relatively small and negative effect on CASE acceptance through its influence on ease of use, near-term usefulness, and long-term consequences. Implications of the findings, from both a research and a managerial perspective are discussed in this paper.

Keywords: Acceptance; CASE; Systems developers

## 1. Introduction

Computer-aided software engineering (CASE) has been promoted as one of the solutions that will assist in the production of quality software by automating its development life-cycle. Although evidence for this claim is limited $[1]$ and many benefits of CASE reported in the literature are either anecdotal or without rigorous testing $[5]$ . A recent CASE market study on a sample of 2500 IT professionals has reported that 70% of the respondents showed interest in CASE tools and, on average, planned to spend 24% of their computer budget for CASE tools [22]. Another recent survey of over 400 American and Canadian chief information officers conducted by Deloitte and Touche, entitled Leading Trends on Information Services also reported that the use of CASE tools continued to show steady growth with 62% of the respondents using the technology in 1993. This contrasts sharply with Kemerer's article [20] reporting one study which was conducted one year after the introduction of CASE, 70% of CASE tools and techniques were never used. In addition to limited functionality and low performance [37], Huff [12] reported that unexpected high costs was another important reason why organizations have not been using CASE.

Previous studies [7, 24] have shown that organizational and behavioral aspects are critical for the successful adoption of CASE. Keen [19] suggested that since dramatically improving software productivity was one of the major responsibilities for information services units in the 1990s, CASE tools were seen as one of the key building blocks for new disciplines in systems development. However, he also emphasized that CASE “demands major shifts in IS professionals’ work, skills, attitudes, and effort; they often show as much resistance to change as their users did when IS brought them new systems. Ramiller [30] acknowledged that introducing CASE in an organization was not an easy task. It requires a revolutionary change in the way software development and maintenance are accomplished.

Owing to the nature of CASE, its adoption is different from that of other information technology (IT) adoptions in at least two ways. Firstly, in some IT, such as decision support systems (DSS) and expert systems (ES), although the intended user may not have any authority in the adoption, he or she may still be able to “informally” switch back to the original (possibly manual) system for work [26]. The issue thus becomes one of a different levels of use. As for CASE, once an organization has decided to adopt this technology, it will be integrated in most parts of their work and systems developers are usually required to use it. The flexibility to switch back to the old system is therefore much restricted. Management can encourage the use through certain incentives or rewards [25] but whether or not the user enjoys using it—i.e., his acceptance of the CASE tool—is critical to productivity.

Secondly, the users of CASE are IS professionals. Unlike end-users of DSS or ES who may not be competent in IT and computer, CASE users can be presumed, on average, to have relatively positive attitudes towards technology and computers $[16]$ . However, attitudes towards change, uncertainty, and risk may still be a major barrier. “CASE is a system for systems people. The unique aspect of CASE is that the change agents are themselves required to change” $[28]$ . Keen emphasized that “it is not the software but the human side of the implementation cycle that will block progress in seeing that the delivered systems are used effectively".

The growing importance/popularity of CASE, the growing expenditures on it, and the distinct characteristics of its adoption make it important for management to know how to ensure its successful implementation (after its organizational adoption). Previous research on implementation of new IT has taken two approaches: factors affecting the implementation, and the processes that comprise it [15]. This study takes the former approach. In particular, this research emphasizes management-oriented factors: assuming that management can, at least theoretically, have some degree of control over those factors, Cheney et al. [6], in a study of end-user computing, suggested that factors could be classified as controllable, partially controllable, and uncontrollable. Using their terminology, this study focuses on the first two groups.

The focus of the research is also on the individual, not organizational, acceptance level. Leonard-Barton and Deschamps noted that research on innovations within organizations had been mainly at the organizational level, with relatively little attention to individual variables during implementation. They suggested that successful implementation of the new technology depended upon acceptance by organizational members targeted as its end-users. After the organizational adoption decision, successful implementation still depends on numerous individual secondary adoption decision by target users even after successive layers of management have passed along “the authority decision”. Although CASE tools are not often considered as a technology for individuals but for teams, this study argues that acceptance of the technology by individuals is still a necessity, though probably not an adequate, condition for successful adoption. White and Nelson [43] also asserted that it was the individual (user) who must adopt the technology for successful implementation.

Systems developers are generally the users of CASE. This research therefore examines factors that affect acceptance by systems developers, under the assumption that user acceptance is a necessary condition for the successful adoption of CASE. Gattiker [10] described acceptance as a person's psychological state; this reflects the user's career paths, etc. Therefore, an understanding of acceptance could give management insights into developing successful strategies for implementing CASE. From a broader perspective, the results should provide insights into the nature of the process by which an organization adopts new IT.

## 2. Conceptual model and research hypotheses

The theoretical grounding for this research comes from earlier studies conducted on IS/IT acceptance/adoption. The model proposed here is constructed by integrating two empirically tested and proven research models that have been reported in the IS literature. These models are the technology acceptance model introduced by Davis [8] and the personal computer utilization model suggested by Thompson et al. [38].

## 2.1. Technology acceptance model

Adapted from the theory of reasoned action, the technology acceptance model is tailored for modeling user-acceptance of IS. The goal of the model is to provide an explanation of the determinants of computer acceptance that is general, capable of explaining user behavior across a broad range of end-user computing technologies and user populations [9]. Figure 1 depicts a simplified version of the model. It posits that two particular beliefs perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are of primary relevance for computer acceptance behaviors. Perceived ease of use has a direct, positive effect on perceived usefulness, as efforts saved due to improved ease of use may be redeployed, thus enabling a person to accomplish more work for the same effort. Both constructs also have direct effects on acceptance of the technology. They can be affected by various external variables such as system characteristics, training, documentation, and the provision of user support. The model was empirically tested and shown to be valid.

## 2.2. Personal computer utilization model

The Thompson, et al. model of personal computer (PC) utilization comes from the work of Triandis. According to Triandis [40], behavior is determined by what people would like to do, what they think they should do, what they have usually done, and what they expect to experience as a result. Based on the model proposed by Triandis [41], Thompson et al. proposed seven factors influencing the utilization of PCs: long-term consequences of PC use, near-term job fit with PC use, complexity of PC use, affect toward PC use, social factors influencing PC use, and facilitating conditions for PC use (see Figure 2). The model was tested and four factors (complexity, near-term job fit, long-term consequences, and social factors) were found to have strong influences on utilization.

## 2.3. Integrated research model

These two research models show the effects of several independent variables on technology acceptance-related dependent variables. They share certain similarities. The factor “complexity of use” in the PC utilization model is defined as the degree to which the PC is perceived as relatively difficult to understand and use; thus, it can be viewed as an inversion of the factor ease of use in the technology acceptance model. Also, in the PC utilization model, Thompson et al. “expanded” “the perceived usefulness” construct in the technology acceptance model and distinguished the near-term usefulness of the technology from the long-term consequences of using the technology.

Based on the above concepts, a conceptual research model for CASE acceptance was proposed (Figure 3). The model consists of five factors that are hypothesized to have direct or indirect effects on CASE acceptance. The first two factors (implementation gap and transitional support) were selected as external variables in the technology acceptance model. The next three factors (ease of use-term usefulness and long-term consequences) were adapted from the technology acceptance/utilization models.

The study focuses on those organizational/ behavioral factors that affect the acceptance of CASE tools by systems developers. Therefore, although it is recognized that technical factors (such as the types of the CASE tools (upper, lower, or integrated), the quality of the tools, and the tasks that the tools support) influence acceptance, they are not specifically studied here. Instead, this study tests the direct/indirect effects of organizational/behavioral factors on acceptance of CASE tools by systems developers.

## 2.3.1. Implementation gap.

Using CASE requires new software development and maintenance procedures, new assignments of job responsibilities $[23]$ , and, for individual end-users, new skills and new knowledge $[21, 35]$ . The wider the gap between old and new, the longer will be the time likely to be needed for individuals to learn the new skills and acquire knowledge and thus to adapt to the new work procedures. As Kermerer noted, premature abandonment of CASE is a chronic problem: when the first project using CASE fails to deliver improved results, organizations tend to abandon the technology, when perseverance might yield a net benefit. Iivari also noted that in addition to learning, CASE adoption might require unlearning old practices. This would not be trivial if the underlying or supporting methodology of the tools was very different from the one currently being used [34]. Thus, the implementation gap is hypothesized to be an external variable (as in the technology acceptance model), that negatively affects the usefulness and ease of use of CASE tools as perceived by systems developers.

## 2.3.2. Transitional support

Another external factor is the transitional support provided by management during the transition period. Similar to other systems implementation, adoption of CASE involves a process of change. Empirical studies, however, have shown that the learning rate varies from two months to two years, depending on many factors, including the nature of the tool, management support, and the training provided. Corbitt and Norman stated that management support and timely training were key factors for successful implementation. Thompson et al. suggested “facilitating conditions” as a factor influencing the utilization of personal computers. “By training users and assisting them when they encounter difficulties, some of the potential barriers to use are reduced or eliminated”. Ramiller used the term transitional support to describe this type of assistance given by the management. Therefore, the level of transitional support given is hypothesized to have a positive effect on degree of ease of use and usefulness of the tools.

## 2.3.3. Ease of use

Previous research suggests “ease of use” as one of the key determinants for successful adoption of CASE tools. Davis asserted that a technology which was observed to be easier to use than another was more likely to be accepted. Research on adoption of innovations also suggests an important role of ease of use. In their meta-analysis of the relationship, Tornatzky and Klein [39] found that complexity of the innovation – the degree to which an innovation was perceived as relatively difficult to understand and use [33] – always has a significant effect on its adoption.Mosely [27], in her five-step method designed for the Westinghouse Electronic Systems Group in selecting CASE tools, included ease of use as one of the six evaluation-criteria categories. These findings lead to the hypothesis that there is a positive relationship between ease of use and acceptance of CASE

## 2.3.4. Near term usefulness

User's perceptions of a system's usefulness has been considered an important factor in system utilization. According to the expectancy theory of motivation [29, 42], individuals evaluate the consequences of their behavior in terms of potential rewards and base their choice of behavior on their desirability. Robey [32] stated that a system which did not help people perform their jobs was not likely to be received favorably in spite of careful implementation efforts. Davis et al. developed the construct “perceived usefulness” (defined as the User’s subjective probability that using a specific application system will increase his or her job performance) and found that it was strongly correlated with utilization. Thus near-term usefulness is hypothesized as having a positive effects on acceptance of CASE.

## 2.3.5. Long-term consequences

User's perceptions of a system's usefulness may extended beyond job performance and include long-term consequences, such as increasing the ability to change jobs or increasing the opportunities for more meaningful work, as well as intangible benefits, such as enhancing the user's prestige among his or her colleagues and professional peers. Beatty and Gordon [3] studied the implementation issue of CAD/CAM systems and found that there was a strong positive relationship between perceived long-term consequences of use and actual use of CAD/CAM systems. Interviewees of Thompson et al. also emphasized the importance of long-term consequences in adopting new technology, even though they were not convinced that it would assist them greatly in their current jobs. Therefore, the related hypothesis is:

Hypothesis 1: There is a positive relationship between the long-term consequences and acceptance of CASE.

## 2.3.6. User acceptance

User acceptance has been incorporated as a dependent variable in majority of the IT implementation research $[36]$ . Ives and Olson $[17]$ , and Baronas and Louis $[2]$ used “user satisfaction” and/or system use as surrogate measures of user acceptance while Davis et al. suggested that user acceptance was a prerequisite of system use. Regardless of the view adopted, the basic premise is that user acceptance is critical to successful implementation. In this study, user acceptance is also considered as a dependent variable. The objective is to understand the key factors affecting the acceptance of CASE by its users: i.e., systems developers.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. Operationalization of constructs

Instruments used to operationalize the constructs in this study were largely adapted from Ramiller, Thompson et al. and Davis. Of a total of 33 items, 24 were adapted from these studies while nine were developed specifically for this study. A seven-point Likert type was used, with anchors ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). Table 1 lists all of the measurement scale items that were ultimately selected.

A preliminary questionnaire was developed and pilot-tested using 10 IS professionals to assess logical consistencies, ease of understanding, sequences of questions, and task relevance. This resulted in decisions to make some modifications in the original questionnaire to clarify the meaning of some question. None of the response in the pilot test were used in the final analysis.

## 3.2. Subjects

The study was conducted in a large organization which has 1000 IT staff. The population of interest was the 200 systems developers, who used a CASE tool in their jobs. 97 questionnaires were completed and returned. Descriptive statistics relating to the respondents' experiences are shown in Table 2. Two different CASE tools were used by these 97 systems developers. APS, which runs on an IBM mainframe platform, was used by 31 respondents. The other 66 respondents used accelerator, which runs on a PC platform. Statistical tests were conducted on the results and no statistically significant difference could be observed between the CASE experiences and other variables for the two groups of respondents. Responses were therefore combined for subsequent analysis.

## 3.4. Data analysis

The data analysis was performed using LISREL Version 8.03 [18] to test the overall fit of the research model with the data collected, using sample covariance matrices and the relative strengths of individual paths. The estimation method used was maximum likelihood [4].

The five measures used were Chi-square/degrees of freedom, Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Bentler and Bonnett's Non-normed Fit Index (NNFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMSR). ## 5. Discussion

Implementation gap was found to have a significantly negative influence on acceptance of CASE tools in a relatively complex way. It had a direct negative relationship with both ease of use and near-term usefulness. Through these factors, it had a negative influence on the acceptance of CASE. These findings are consistent with prior studies. Prior research showed that computer experience and user training are negatively associated with computer anxiety and positively associated with perceived usefulness [14]. The implementation gap here may be due to a lack of CASE knowledge or lack of experience in using CASE tools; these may then lead to anxiety about accepting the technology.

An earlier research also showed that computer anxiety had a much stronger impact on perceived usefulness than organization support $[13]$ . Results here concur with this finding. The implementation gap was negatively related to near-term usefulness of CASE, whilst transitional support did not have any significant relationship with either near-term usefulness or long-term consequences. However, transitional support was found to have a strong relationship with ease of use which in turn, had a strong relationship with both factors. Computing the total effects of an implementation gap and transitional support on both types of usefulness, respectively, the (negative) impact of the implementation gap was still bigger than the (positive) influence of transitional support (implementation gap: -0.56 on near-term usefulness and -0.32 on long-term consequences;

transitional support 0.31 on near-term usefulness and 0.17 on long-term consequences).

In both the technology acceptance model and the personal computer utilization model, a stronger relationship has been found between perceived usefulness and acceptance/utilization of the technology than between ease of use and acceptance. Our results are not consistent with these findings. Ease of use had a stronger impact on CASE acceptance than perceived usefulness (near-term usefulness and long-term consequences combined in this study). There are at least two plausible explanations. Firstly, CASE tools are relatively new in the business computing environment and most CASE users have limited experience in using this technology. The pressure to complete projects on time may lead systems developers to put more weight on the ease of use to the tools, reducing the time required to learn how to use them. Future research is needed to investigate this. Secondly, if only the direct effect of ease of use was considered, the link between perceived usefulness and CASE acceptance became stronger than the link between ease of use and CASE acceptance. Therefore, it was the strong links between ease of use and near-term usefulness and between near-term usefulness and long-term consequences that made ease of use the most importance factor affecting the degree of CASE acceptance.

The results of this study indicate that long-term consequences, but not near-term usefulness, is a significant factor influencing CASE acceptance. This thus supports the separation of the “usefulness” concept into two distinct factors. Perceived usefulness influences user acceptance of systems due to the reinforcement value of outcomes. The findings showed that systems developers put more weight on the long-term value of CASE than on the short-term benefits gained in day-to-day work. The near-term usefulness may be driven more by the features provided in the CASE tool itself. The long-term consequences may be based more on a fundamental belief in the usefulness of CASE tools to the user’s career.

## 6. Limitations

This study was an initial test of integrating two technology acceptance/utilization models in a single model for the investigation of factors influencing CASE acceptance. Several limitations of the work are apparent and warrant mention. Firstly, this research employed a structural equation modeling technique. Since the data are cross-sectional, the causal links between the variables should be considered with caution and be subjected to additional future examination [31]. Secondly, the respondents were from one organization. Even though it could minimize the effects confounded by different CASE tools and different organizational environments or clusters, the generalizability of the results to other organizations remains to be determined. Thirdly, as the variables included in the research model were based on two prior models, other factors (such as top management support and CASE experience of the users) that might have significant effects on the dependent variable were not studied.

## 7. Conclusions

This study has demonstrated the usefulness of the two technology acceptance/utilization models applied to the acceptance of CASE. The findings suggest that perceived usefulness can be and is deemed appropriate to split into two constructs: near-term usefulness and long-term consequences. From a user's point of view, using a new technology can affect not only daily productivity but also career prospects. This study provides evidence of the need to split.

The research model developed here included only five variables, largely based on the concepts in the technology acceptance model and the personal computer utilization model. Although the model was found to be valid, it would be useful to test whether the observed relationships hold good in different organizational environments. Moreover, since the respondents of this study used only two different CASE tools, future research should examine whether and how the tool itself moderates the affective reactions of individuals to CASE acceptance.

Prior experience has been suggested and found to be an important factor on user acceptance of an information technology. Other studies have included prior experience as a variable that affects the six independent variable in the original model. The study here, however, did not include prior experience with CASE in the research model for two reasons. Firstly, most of the respondents had limited CASE experience of less than two years and used only three projects using CASE. The range was judged to be too samll to obtain any meaningful or significant findings. Secondly, we had no historical data, with only cross-sectional data.

From an organizational and managerial perspective, the findings suggest the need for selecting a CASE tool that is very easy to use and for cultivating an environment that foster this. The results indicated that implementation gap had significant negative impacts on both the perceptions of ease of use and near-term usefulness of CASE.

In addition to adopting a structured methodology, providing technical support during the transitional period can help reduce the negative impact of the implementation gap. This support can be provided in several ways. The most obvious is to provide formal training on CASE methodology and the CASE tool itself.

In addition, setting up a formal “help center” can assist new users to adapt to the changes required for the transition to CASE and to know where to turn for help. The support can be in the form of an informal network of support so that the users can have easy access to people with the necessary expertise, whenever needed. Managements should put more effort into providing sufficient support to their IT staff during the initial stage of CASE adoption.

The study also indicated that the perception of near-term usefulness of CASE was a strong factor in determining the perception of long-term consequences of using CASE, and this also had a significant effect on CASE acceptance. This suggests that, whenever possible, management should ensure their systems developers have a strong belief in the usefulness of CASE in their daily work.

In conclusion, this study makes a contribution to the growing IS literature on technology acceptance/ adoption by integrating two research models developed by prior research in a new research framework. The work should be useful for understanding and improving the chances of successful implementation of CASE tools.
