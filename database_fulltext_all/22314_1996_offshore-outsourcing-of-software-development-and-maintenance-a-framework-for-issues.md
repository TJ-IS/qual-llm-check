# Offshore outsourcing of software development and maintenance: A framework for issues

Michael Alan Smith $^{*}$ , Sabyasachi Mitra, Sridhar Narasimhan

School of Management, Georgia Institute of Technology, Atlanta GA 30332-0520, USA

## Abstract

Frameworks intended for identifying and organizing issues associated with offshore outsourcing of software development have focused on project or site selection. Existing frameworks are not comprehensive because they do not fully address one or more of the following issues: (1) the relationship between project characteristics and site suitability, (2) financial and intellectual property issues in the context of software development, and (3) the role of agents outside the outsourcing and vendor firms in the offshore outsourcing process. To overcome these limitations, we propose a more comprehensive framework that links projects (which require resource) and sites (which possess resources). It also includes environmental agents whose actions affect resource availability at the sites. The framework serves as an useful tool to systematically address the issues that arise in the context of offshore outsourcing of software development. We use the framework to review and classify existing literature.

Keywords: Offshore outsourcing; International outsourcing; Foreign outsourcing; Outsourcing software development; Information systems outsourcing

## 1. Introduction

The software development industry employs millions of workers throughout the world. Until recently, almost all of them lived in the industrialized West, especially in the United States, which has dominated software development since the creation of the industry. Beginning in the late 1980s, several articles have appeared in trade journals warning of the loss of jobs in software development to “offshore outsourcing” [1, 3, 27, 35]. Offshore refers to countries other than those that have traditionally dominated the software development industry. Falling hardware prices worldwide, the availability of software to facilitate systems development, greater ease of telecommunications, and the existence of many well-trained, low-paid analysts and programmers, now enable the developing world to compete for software development work [9, 29, 28]. Given the importance of the industry and its continuing globalization, both producers and consumers of software will benefit from an understanding of the factors that determine the best site for the production and maintenance of software, whether that be in-house, domestic, or offshore. From the perspective of an IS manager, the primary questions are:

\- What characteristics determine the suitability of a project for offshore outsourcing?

\- What factors affect the suitability of an offshore site for software development?

\- What roles do the government, trade and other organizations play in the process?

Several frameworks can be found in the literature that organize some of the knowledge and no comprehensive framework has been published. Previous attempts fall short, because they do not fully address one or more of the following issues: (1) the relationship between project characteristics and site suitability, (2) financial and intellectual property issues in the context of software development, and (3) the role of agents outside the outsourcing and vendor firms in the offshore outsourcing process.

In this paper, we propose a more comprehensive framework. It links projects (that require resources), sites (that possess resources) and environmental agents (whose actions affect resource availability at sites). We believe that such a framework will lead to a better understanding of the issues involved and act as a prompt for systematically addressing such issues. It will also aid in classifying existing literature and identifying issues yet to be addressed.

Although the term offshore refers to non-traditional sites for software development, especially those in the developing world, many of the issues addressed in the framework apply to any outsourcing arrangement that crosses national boundaries. We consider the case of non-traditional sites because of the wider range of issues it generates.

## 2. Relevant frameworks and models

Offshore outsourcing of software development brings together issues associated with software development, outsourcing information systems (IS), and the international use of information technology (IT). Relevant frameworks and models from existing work include those that model (1) IT governance and the decision to outsourcing information systems in general, (2) the use of IT across national boundaries, (3) the globalization of the software development industry, and (4) site and project selection for offshore development projects.

Loh [21] proposes an economic model for IT governance, including the decision to outsource. The model includes various cost factors of a firm, such as those associated with management, decision making, production, procurement, transaction processing and bargaining. The model suggests one possible approach for examining the way a firm manages IT, but ignores the impact of many other issues such as organizational culture, government regulations, and the strategic role of IT in the firm. It does not explicitly address the role of project characteristics in the outsourcing decision.

Considering four detailed case studies, Willcocks and Fitzgerald [33] presents a framework for explaining IS outsourcing decisions. It identifies several factors that affect the decision to outsource, such as the level of in-house IT expertise, the degree of business uncertainty reflected through changing requirements, the degree of systems interconnectedness and complexity, the role of IT as a business differentiator, and the relationship between IT and corporate strategy. The framework focuses on the intra-firm factors that affect the decision to outsource, and largely ignores the effect to external agencies on the environment, and consequently on the decision. The framework does not explicitly consider the offshore option.

Based on issues identified in a survey [6] of top IS executives in US-based multinationals, Deans and Ricks [5] proposes a framework of IT issues confronted by multinational corporations. This framework incorporates environmental elements in both domestic and foreign countries and recognize a variety of influence source including social/cultural, economic, managerial/strategic, political/legal, technological, and application of technology. The usefulness of this work is limited in the current context, because it is mainly concerned with the use of IT within one organization and does not address the outsourcing or offshore outsourcing options.

Kim, Westin and Dholakia [16] proposes a framework for organizing existing knowledge about the globalization of the software industry. The framework adopts a resource oriented perspective. It classifies resources as technological, human, individual cultural traits, and corporate culture. It argues that different areas of the world possess these resources in varying degrees, and that projects require such resources based on the phase of development. The framework enhances our understanding of the link between resource availability at sites and a project's requirements for these resources. Its primary limitations are that it ignores the impact of financial and intellectual property resources on the decision, it does not explain the impact of other project characteristics (not just the phase of development) on the decision to outsourcing or the choice of sites, and it ignores the role played by external agencies in affecting resource availability at sites.

Ravichandran and Ahmed [30] proposes a framework to aid in the selection of projects and sites suitable for offshore software development. It recognizes that the usefulness of the offshore option may vary with the phase of development, and that this usefulness depends on the characteristics of necessary interaction between developers and users. It also recognizes that several characteristics of a site, not just cost, must be considered during site selection. The framework is useful because it summarizes a list of issues that must be considered in selecting sites. However, it does not address the role of external agencies, the impact of other project characteristics (not just the phase of development) on the decision to outsource and site selection, or the importance of intellectual property and financial resources in the process.

Several articles, especially in trade publications, are editorials that describe and draw attention to offshore outsourcing ([4, 8, 17]). A few articles describe the state of the industry in various parts of the globe; e.g. India [25], Taiwan [15], Sub-Saharan Africa [26], Cuba [24], Russia [12], the Middle-East ([2, 11]), and less-developed countries in general [10].

## 3. Theory and proposed framework

Each framework described suggests ways to approach some of the issues associated with offshore outsourcing of software development. But none addresses all the issues involved. The framework we propose here considers the phenomenon from three perspectives. The resource perspective describes the money, people and systems required for software development and maintenance. The environmental perspective includes agents that affect the availability of resources needed for a software development project in an international environment. The project management perspective includes characteristics of an international software project that affect the resource requirement of the project. Figure 1 illustrates the relationships between these three perspectives.

## 3.1. The resource perspective

To classify resources, we begin by dividing personnel resources from the non-human, because the physical and legal factors affecting deployment of resources differ markedly between these resources. Non-human resources can be divided into physical and non-physical, i.e. hardware and software. This division also reflects differences in what is required to deploy the resources. For example, transportation of software can be made at very low cost, through telecommunications channels, whereas hardware must be shipped and assembled. Underlying the availability and application of personnel, hardware, and software are intellectual property (IP) and financial resources. Intellectual property is important because projects require that information, perhaps trade secrets, be shared between the client and the vendor. Financing of software can be a problem because it often has little value outside the firm; in international situations, the problem is made worse by changes in exchange rates. ## 3.2. The environmental perspective

The availability of resources at a site affects the projects which are undertaken. The environment of a project consists of the agents that affect the availability of resources required to carry out the project. We identified agents that affect the environment of international software outsourcing projects through a review of the literature on IS outsourcing ([13, 19, 20, 31]), software production in developing countries ([18, 22]), and on the international use of IT [32]. We considered only those agents whom we judged to be the most likely to have a direct impact on the issues considered. These agents were grouped into “contexts” depending on the scope of their activities. The list is presented in Table 2. Other agents could be included as required in a particular application of the framework.

## 3.3. The project management perspective

The project management perspective is based on characteristics of projects that affect resource requirements; these include the project's scale, scope, duration, difficulty, and strategic importance.

Scale refers to the amount of each resource required. Large scale projects may be unsuitable for in-house development, because inadequate resources are available within the firm. Alternatively, some small scale projects may high overhead and learning costs and may be more efficiently developed by a vendor with specialized knowledge.

Scope refers to the number of different systems that affect and are affected by the system being developed. These systems may be computer-based or manual. Higher levels of business uncertainty and changing requirements are generally associated with a project of greater scope, because more people and systems are affected by it.

Duration refers to the calendar life of the product, most of which falls in the maintenance phase. Projects with greater duration are likely to encounter a higher degree of business uncertainty through environmental change and the resulting changes in requirements.

Difficulty refers to the intellectual effort required to define requirements, to convert requirements into code, and to verify the correctness of the code. Factors that contribute to increased difficulty include low level of familiarity with the application, the use of new technology, new programming paradigms, and stringent response time and reliability requirements. Difficulty is related to other characteristics, in that, projects of greater scale, scope, or duration are usually more difficult. Difficulty is also closely related to the personnel resources of firm.

Strategic importance is derived from the role that the project plays in differentiating the firm from its competitors. This role is related to the project's intellectual property content. Obviously, there is a difference between a system that is “merely” essential and a system that is of strategic importance; e.g., an accounting system, though essential, has low intellectual property content and its strategic importance is limited, whereas, a software project to provide better links with customers may increase market share through greater customer satisfaction and may be strategically important to the firm.

The effect of the project characteristics on resource requirements may vary with the phase(s) of software development. For example, during the analysis phase of a strategically important project, it may be necessary to share trade sectors with the developer although they will not be apparent later in the programming specifications.

The project management perspective of the new framework is related to the user, application, and specification factors influencing adoption of offshore development put forth in earlier frameworks (see section 2). However, our treatment of these factors is more basic; e.g., we would regard user factors as an outgrowth of scope, application factors as an outgrowth of scale or difficulty, and specification factors as an outgrowth of duration and/or difficulty.

## 4. Combining the perspectives

Each of the perspectives provides a useful framework for considering some of the issues associated with offshore outsourcing of software development. A pairwise combination of the perspectives reveals a richer set of issues that must also be addressed.

At the firm level, both the client and vendor firm influence needs and availability through their existing systems and practices, and through their plans. At offshore sites, for example, firms trying to sell to U.S. markets make themselves more attractive for offshore work by employing English-speaking personnel. Vendor firms may also increase their ability to compete for offshore projects by installing compatible hardware and software. The financial characteristics of firms, expressed in such ratios as P/E and leverage, also affect their options for financing software development projects. Proper IP protection measures may also increase a firm's ability to outsource software development without risking loss of trade secrets through the vendor.

The multifaceted role in government in the development of the software industry, both in developed and developing regions, has often been described. Governments affect resource availability by restricting or facilitating the import and export of equipment, software, or data, restricting the use of equipment, and restricting emigration and immigration. Governments are also the main enforcers of intellectual property laws. Often, the government is the largest single consumer of hardware and software in a country. As such, government use encourages the development of standards and the education of people to fill roles as programmers and analysts. Governments may also take specific measures to encourage the development of the software industry, such as providing financial incentives and lifting foreign exchange restrictions.

Infrastructure providers include technology providers such as hardware, software, and telecommunication companies, as well as educational institutions such as universities, trade schools and certification/accreditation boards. A technological infrastructure, including telecommunications capability and the availability of service personnel for equipment, is essential. Infrastructure providers may also affect telecommunication standards. The provision of secure communication links supports the development of any industry. Educational infrastructure providers create programs to train needed personnel.

The business environment affects resources by encouraging the development of suppliers and setting price through competition. Agents include financial institutions, competitors, customers of both the client and vendor firms, and consumers of similar IT products and services. These firms may adopt standards that affect the availability of hardware, software and personnel. For example, the adoption of Unix as an industry standard in India in the early eighties has resulted in the availability of Unix hardware, software, and Unix-trained personnel in that country, allowing it to successfully compete in the market for Unix based products. Financial issues include the development of suitable schemes by financial institutions for software development, in general, and offshore software development, in particular.

International organizations include technical groups (such as the CCITT, ISO and the IEEE), trade organizations (such as the WTO, OAS, ASEAN, and the EU), and financial organizations (such as the World Bank, the IMF, Asian Bank, and EBRD). Technical organizations play a role in the standardization process, such as the development of the ISO seven-layered architecture for data communication. Trade organizations can affect the enforcement of software contracts and enact intellectual property protection agreements. The EU sponsors precompetitive research and software development and the creation of networks of contacts throughout Europe. International financial organizations (such as the World Bank and IMF) may also affect the software development industry by providing industry by providing suitable aid to develop the technological infrastructure and manpower in a country. International organizations as environmental agents present a special challenge because the standards and rules they promote are mediated by the actions of individual nation-states.

## 4.2. The resources and project management perspectives

Combination of the project management and resource perspectives reveals issues related to the resource needs of projects. We generate issues by asking questions in the form “How does $\langle characteristic\rangle$ affect the need for $\langle resource\rangle$ ”? Examples of such issues are presented in Table 4.

Suitability tests for offshore outsourcing may be based on project characteristics and resource requirements. For example, very difficult projects may require specialized and trained personnel not available within the outsourcing firm and may thus be a candidate for outsourcing. Projects of great scope may not be appropriate for international outsourcing because they need to interface with many other systems within an organization and so may require compatible hardware and software at the vendor sites and frequent interaction with users.

Investigating the level and type of hardware, software, and personnel resources required for projects with varying characteristics is a pertinent issue. For example, projects of larger scope may require greater interaction with users and compatible hardware and software at the vendor site. Projects with high complexity may require highly trained specialized personnel not available within the outsourcing firm. Funding requirements may also differ across projects depending on their characteristics. For example, projects of long duration may require a greater and longer, hence riskier, financial commitment and financial benefits may be obtained over a longer period of time.

Appropriate contract provisions based on project characteristics should also be investigated. For example, projects of greater scope may require the vendor to be responsible for proper interfacing with existing systems. For projects with high levels of difficulty and/or strategic importance, it may be advantageous for the client firm to specify training requirements so that it can retain in-house expertise and not be completely dependent on the vendor firm.

Another important issue is the use of different financial arrangements depending on the project characteristics. Should there be an uniform payment structure for all projects, or should the payment structure be tailored to the situation? For example, a phase-by-phase payment structure (rather than upfront payment) may be more suitable for projects of long duration. For projects with high levels of difficulty, the client firm may obtain a commitment of lower prices on subsequent projects if less learning is required.

Most articles listed in the tables are exploratory and span several classifications. Often, this is the result of a firm-oriented viewpoint that spans the resource perspective. We expect to find more focused studies in future articles. Many issues are not addressed in existing literature. In general, its exploratory nature, the lack of studies with theoretical and/or empirical bases, and the many research questions still unanswered, make this a very promising and productive area. For example, the design of the outsourcing contract is an important issue that needs further analysis. How should the contract be designed based on the project characteristics and the international legal environment? How should the deliverables be defined to minimize the likelihood of conflicts? What should be the optimal payment structure to minimize the client and vendor firms' risks? Empirical analysis of successful contracts is one possible approach.

The role of the government and infrastructure providers is another important issue. What steps can the governments of developing nations take to make their countries attractive sites for software development? Given the continuing globalization of the software industry, what type of projects should the governments of the developed nations strive to retain and what support must be given to the domestic software industry and educational institutions to offset the price advantage of developing nations?

The risks associated with offshore outsourcing should also be investigated. What are the financial risks involved? what risks are associated with losing in-house expertise? What risks are associated with the maintenance of a product by an offshore vendor? Are the price advantages worth the risks involved? Effective methods of cost/benefit analysis may also be investigated in this context.

The role of international organizations and treaties in formulating policies, enforcing software contracts and intellectual property protection is also important. What should be the role of these agencies in a world where primary decision making entities are still independent nation states?

## 6. Conclusions

We have proposed a framework for organizing the issues related to offshore outsourcing of software development. The framework is based on three perspectives (resource, environmental and project management) and the pairwise combination of these perspectives. We have used the proposed framework to classify existing literature and point out issues for future work.

The framework can be extended in several ways. The details of each perspective can be further refined. This may lead to the identification of other environmental agents, a different way of looking at the resources involved, or the identification of additional project characteristics that influence the offshore outsourcing decision. Different kinds of software projects have different resource requirements. Each of the countries of the world provides a unique environment for software development. The environmental and project characteristics perspectives can be refined or even redefined to better suit the characteristics of a given project type or development site. Since the actions of governments differ in different countries and sometimes depend on bilateral arrangements, the environmental perspective could take on unique forms depending on the pairs of countries involved in a project.
