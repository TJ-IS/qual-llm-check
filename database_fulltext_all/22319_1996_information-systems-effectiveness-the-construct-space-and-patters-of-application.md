# Information systems effectiveness: The construct space and patterns of application

Varun Grover $^{a,*}$ , Seung Ryul Jeong $^{a}$ , Albert H. Segars $^{b}$

$^{a}$ College of Business Administration, Department of Management Science, University of South Carolina, Columbia SC 29208, USA $^{b}$ Department of Management, College of Business and Public Affairs, Clemson University, Clemson SC 29364, USA

## Abstract

Measuring the specific contribution(s) of Information Systems (IS) to organizational effectiveness remains a critical concern of both academic and practitioner communities. Reflecting the importance of this issue, over the past decade, a large number of performance criteria have been developed and empirically tested. This growing array of measurement criteria provides a foundation for defining the construct space of IS effectiveness and prescribing criteria for specific managerial needs. Although important research has been started in this area, additional work is needed to define the domain of IS effectiveness further and provide insight into the context within which various classes of performance criteria are used. Through meta-analysis of leading IS journals, this study identifies six classes of effectiveness measures that are then mapped onto various evaluative contexts. The construct space and evaluative context developed in this study provide a new way of conceptualizing and applying the many evaluative criteria associated with IS.

Keywords: IS effectiveness; Effectiveness measurement; Meta-analysis; Organizational effectiveness © 1996 Elsevier Science B.V.

## 1. Introduction

The measurement of Information Systems (IS) effectiveness continues to be a central concern of both academics and practitioners. Capturing the contribution of IS-based assets to organizational performance provides a benchmark from which the many processes of the IS Function (i.e. design, delivery, maintenance) can be evaluated and refined. Without the benefit of these measures, IS assets may be undervalued by users and/or top executives resulting in curtailed budget allocations and lower managerial profiles for top IS executives. In other instances, the absence of reliable performance metrics may cause users and/or top managers to overvalue IS assets. Users and strategic planners may therefore be unaware of innovations adopted by competing organizations that are enhancing and/or changing their patterns of work and competition. The lack of validated and complete performance criteria in either of the two instances can result in misguided decisions regarding the acquisition, design, and delivery of IS.

Many researchers have sought to identify some criteria for IS effectiveness. Some of the more prominent of these measures include IS usage [26, 86,

102] user information satisfaction (UIS) [5, 48] quality of decision making [15, 59], productivity from cost/benefit analysis [18, 77, 80], and system quality [31]. Due to the difficulty in quantifying and linking costs and benefits to particular IS innovations, measures based on system usage and user perceptions have become particularly prominent within the IS literature [33]. Some researchers have suggested that UIS may be preferable to usage as a surrogate of IS effectiveness as (1) the relationship between usage and the quality of decision making tends to be weak [34], (2) usage can be a surrogate only if it is voluntary, and (3) UIS has potential impact in the user's quality of work life. Largely due to these lines of reasoning and the traceable link between system and user, a significant amount of research work has been devoted toward developing and refining measures of UIS [1, 5, 33, 48, 50].

While the results of UIS research have provided a solid foundation for evaluating individual response to system characteristics, the lack of a theoretical framework for placing the measure within the greater context of overall 'IS effectiveness' has caused some observers to question the scope of performance measurement inherent within UIS. In other words, does the total meaning of IS effectiveness comprise more than the UIS criterion? Melone [69] notes that there seems to be no clear explanation or theoretical model for using UIS as a surrogate indicator of IS effectiveness. Weill and Olson [113] argue that IS effectiveness is a multidimensional concept. They note that an arbitrarily chosen measure 'limits the amount of variance explained and contributes to low construct validity of the studies.' Delone and McLean [23] also suggest that IS effectiveness is likely to be more than any other single measure. Their model of IS success, based on Shannon and Weaver's [99] work in communications, consists of six interdependent constructs (System Quality, Information Quality, Use, User Satisfaction, Individual Impact, and Organizational Impact) and implies that measurement of overall success based on items arbitrarily selected from any one construct is likely to be inaccurate. Instead, the measure of overall success should combine individual measures from these constructs to create a comprehensive scheme for performance measurement. These authors also note that further research work is needed in defining the construct space for effectiveness criteria and for identifying the technological context within which effectiveness criteria are applied.

In general, it seems that research on IS effectiveness (with the exception of the development of UIS measures) has been non-cumulative and fragmented, lacking an overarching framework and information regarding the context within which effectiveness criteria are applied. While Delone and McLean's IS Success Model has done much to structure the concept of IS effectiveness, further study is needed to validate this initial conceptualization of IS effectiveness and to identify various contexts of application for the generated criteria. Hence, the primary purpose of this study is to build a theoretically-based construct space for IS effectiveness which complements and extends the IS Success Model. Through use of an alternative theoretical perspective (the theory of organizational effectiveness), the construct space defined within this study provides a unique means of cross-validating the IS Success Model. Based on this construct space, various measures of IS effectiveness are classified and then examined for patterns in application.

## 2. Review of literature on IS effectiveness

A careful examination of literature devoted to the measurement of IS effectiveness seems to suggest that there are four distinct research streams:

1. Criteria demonstration research – the theoretical development and justification of effectiveness criteria

2. Measurement research - the statistical development and evaluation of effectiveness measures

3. Criteria relationship research – the theoretical and statistical linking of effectiveness criteria and

4. Antecedents of IS effectiveness research – the theoretical and statistical determination of antecedents to IS effectiveness.

## 2.1. Criteria demonstration research

Several studies attempt to identify or justify the use of criteria based on theory drawn from other academic disciplines; they can be either economic or personal [33]. Economic criteria include cost/benefit analysis and other relative indicators of return realized from IS investment. Based on accounting theory, cost/benefit approaches have often been employed to justify investments in IS. Matlin [66] proposes new systems of accounting in order to measure more accurately costs and benefits associated with IS. Money et al. [77] present a complete methodology for quantifying the estimated value of systems. Similarly, Ein-Dor, et al. [27] attempt to measure the actual contribution of IS to organizational profit. Other groups of researchers have utilized measures drawn from industrial economics, such as return on assets, average growth, and so on, to measure the effects of IS on firm performance [17, 19, 29, 54]. While these measures are useful in justifying broad organizational impacts of IS, measurement issues, and particularly the difficulty in establishing that a causal relationship with IS inhibits the interpretation of results associated with research designs involving these measures.

Personal indicators of IS effectiveness are less directly linked to changes in profits. The two most popular are UIS and system usage. Other less-explored criteria include quality of decision making, information quality, number of alternatives, and decision confidence. Criteria of this type are very popular because they tend to be easier to measure and can be more readily linked to characteristics or the existence of IS.

## 2.2. Measurement research

A large body of research has emerged for developing robust (reliable and valid) instruments that can be used to measure complex patterns (constructs) of beliefs, attitudes, and perceptions associated with IS characteristics $[4, 20, 33, 84, 90, 106]$ . These studies have taken the form of exploratory research in which a complex construct is theoretically defined through extensive literature review and expert opinion $[104]$ . This definitional space is then operationalized through item indicators and associated rating scales. Together, the indicators and their associated ratings form a metric that reflects a respondent's position with respect to the construct of interest (i.e. how satisfied a user is with the information received from an IS). The constructs that have emerged have focused on output characteristics of IS (UIS, group cohesiveness, decision making velocity, decision quality, availability of CSFs), attitudes about IS (perceived confidence, equity, perceived usefulness, ease of use, satisfaction with IS, user expectations) and utilization characteristics of IS (usage, utilization, user performance, user productivity).

Increasingly, this stream of research is being supplemented with confirmatory measurement assessment $[43, 97]$ . In general, such studies apply more exacting statistical techniques, such as maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), to assess the stability of previously-defined measurement models. This allows for clearer estimation of causal relationships within a network of complex variables.

## 2.3. Criteria relationship research

Many researchers have investigated the relationships between various criteria of IS effectiveness. These associations include usage and UIS [4, 27], attitude and usage [90, 93], usage and quality of decision [27], utilization and firm performance [19], attitude and UIS [108], and user acceptance and usage [22]. Among these, the relationship between usage and UIS has received the most attention. Findings seem to suggest that UIS leads to increased levels of usage which in turn leads to higher levels of performance. This link between satisfaction and usage parallels Fishbein and Azjen's [28] theory of reasoned action. In addition to these findings there is considerable empirical support for a causal relationship between system usage and decision performance. Although some results are contradictory, this research provides important insight into the network of relationships inherent in the effectiveness construct.

## 2.4. Antecedents to IS effectiveness research

The final body of effectiveness research focuses mainly on determinants of IS effectiveness (i.e. the independent variables that might aid in providing effectiveness) rather than the criteria themselves. These antecedents can be broadly classified as organizational or individual in nature. Organizational variables capture aspects of business structure or cultural context that may influence the perceived effectiveness of IS. Examples include centralization of decision making, formalization of business rules and policies, and integration of work among various functional areas [78, 85, 86, 92]. In general, the results of these studies have been mixed due to the many factors that may cloud the relationship between organizational characteristics and IS effectiveness. Other studies have focused on antecedents based on individual characteristics, such as user involvement [4, 31, 47, 108], resources devoted to IS [49], and top management support [87, 92]. Unlike their organizational-based counterparts, these studies have reported relatively consistent findings. Higher levels of IS effectiveness have been associated with higher levels of user involvement, higher levels of IS resources, and higher levels of top management support.

## 3. IS effectiveness: Domain of the construct

The four research streams are diverse in what is being measured, who is measuring, and when it is measured. This initially seems surprising, given that all the studies are focused on measuring essentially the same entity – IS effectiveness. While it is tempting to debate the virtues of each criterion and research approach, it seems more plausible to consider these research streams and their associated measures to be unified. Each criterion and associated approach measures only a portion of the total construct space of IS effectiveness. Such a view is consistent with that of Delone and McLean and with well established studies on organizational effectiveness found in industrial economics literature [12]. Utilizing the theoretical perspective of organizational effectiveness, we now attempt to synthesize this seemingly disparate array of effectiveness measures and research approaches through definitional dimensions of (1) evaluative referent, (2) unit of analysis, and (3) evaluation type.

## 3.1. Evaluative referent

Evaluative referent describes the relative standard that is used as a basis for assessing performance. Within IS literature, several perspectives of evaluative referent have been developed. For instance, Hamilton and Chervany [38] present two definitions based on the goal-centered approach versus a system-resource approach. The goal-centered approach views IS effectiveness as the degree to which the objectives of systems, or of the organizational units utilizing the systems, are achieved. In the system-resource approach, IS effectiveness is determined by the attainment of a normative state rather than the achievement of specific task objectives. Ives et al. imply that IS effectiveness is the extent to which IS results in changes in organizational effectiveness. Combining these perspectives with the others found in studies on organizational effectiveness, three potential evaluative judgments emerge: (1) comparative, (2) normative, and (3) improvement. The evaluative perspective of comparative judgment attempts to compare the effectiveness of a particular system with other ‘similar systems’ (typically those set up in comparable organizations). A typical question in this mode is: How does our system’s performance compare with similar systems in comparable organizations? While this perspective is intuitive, it may be difficult to actually implement. Gathering accurate and timely information regarding comparable systems is difficult, if not impossible. Also, the basis of comparison might be invalid if those comparable systems are achieving less-than-desirable results due to factors unique to the their context.

Within the perspective of normative judgment, a relevant assessment question is: How does our system's performance compare against that of a theoretically ideal system? In essence the system is compared to 'standards of best practice' rather than those of the organization. Such an approach is amenable to research contexts provided that literature and/or expert opinion readily identifies these 'standards.'

Within the final perspective of improvement judgment, a typical question is: How much have the capabilities of the system improved over time? Thus, the focus is on assessing how IS has evolved or improved (over time) in supporting organizational needs. This approach is particularly useful in cases where the system is in its initial stages and has yet to reach steady state. However, within any context, the evaluation of past performance relative to current performance is an important indicator of system effectiveness. When examined over time, such metrics can give users and managers useful insight in determining how IS has improved in terms of resource use and organizational contribution.

## 3.2. Unit of analysis

In order to build a complete picture of IS effectiveness, evaluation must be conducted from both a macro and micro view. In other words, IS effectiveness should be considered at the organizational level as well as at the individual level. Such evaluation is necessary because IS supports individual decision making $[21]$ and can also provide competitive advantage in organizations $[14, 25, 46]$ .

From the macro perspective, the well-being of an organization is indicated by its overall competitiveness in a changing environment. Thus IS effectiveness is related to how much the IS helps organizations in gaining competitiveness. From a micro perspective the organization may be viewed as a socio-technical system [83] whose members require appropriate information. Hence, IS effectiveness is related to the extent to which IS satisfies the requirements of the organization's members. Thus, the relevant question is: What unit of analysis is being used? – i.e. evaluation of IS effectiveness must be appropriately framed within either a micro or macro evaluation perspective.

## 3.3. Evaluation type

Various types of evaluation can be considered when assessing effectiveness. In the organizational effectiveness literature, three types of evaluation have been proposed [8]: (1) process, (2) response, and (3) impact. Process evaluation assumes that when resources are limited, organizational members work to ensure their efficient use. Such an evaluation also encompasses an assessment of the degree of institutionalization. As Trice and Treacy [110] suggest, this assessment is based upon user dependence on the IS, user perceptions of system ownership, and the extent to which IS permeates organizational administrative and operating procedure. Thus, the effective utilization of IS resources, which may be evaluated by the degree of use and the influence of IS on organizational control may be important evaluative considerations in this approach.

The second is response which assesses the reaction of the individual or the organization (or its external entities) to the IS service or product. For instance, in the case of a power conflict caused by IS, potential sources of system inhibition (e.g. user resistance) must be identified and measured in order to implement the innovation effectively. Similarly, complex variables such as user's beliefs or attitudes toward IS are important response-based criteria in assessing the various post-implementation effects of IS. On a larger scale, the response of suppliers, customers, and competitors to IS introduction constitute an important class of response-based evaluative criteria when assessing the competitive aspects of IS-based strategy. In some instances, the introduction of innovative IS may place the initiating firm at greater risk due to improved system design and higher technological thresholds imposed by competitors with greater resource bases [111].

The final evaluative type is impact, which is the most comprehensive and the most difficult to assess. It is associated with the direct effects of IS implementation on individual or organizational performance. A relevant question in this domain is: What are the outcomes and consequences of IS on those aspects of organizational members' performance that are critical to organizational effectiveness? For example, success is significantly influenced by the quality of the decision making. Therefore, the extent to which IS enhances decision performance may be an important consideration in impact evaluation. At a higher level of analysis, a relevant question is: What are the financial benefits associated with investment in IS-based assets? Although many factors cloud the relation between IS investments and returns, impact evaluation remains a top priority of practitioners and academics seeking to justify increasing financial and competitive reliance on emerging IS [54].

## 3.4. Six classes of effectiveness measures

Integrating the three definitional perspectives of evaluative referent, unit of analysis, and evaluation type, produces six classes of IS effectiveness measures that define the overall construct space of IS effectiveness. This is depicted in the form of a decision tree in Figure 1. As shown, evaluation of IS is initiated through choice of evaluative referent and each of the three referents may be applicable to all classes of effectiveness measures. The first three classes of effectiveness measures are associated with macro-level evaluation of IS. Class I variables, infusion measures, capture the extent to which IS permeates the organization in terms of completeness, efficiency, and accuracy in analysis and distribution of information. This may also identify the extent to which IS influences management control in the organization. Class II, market measures, capture the reactions of internal and external customer markets to the introduction of IS. In essence, these measures reflect the dynamics of market mechanisms as a consequence of system introduction. Class III, economic measures, capture the effects of IS introduction and use through economics-based financial or productivity criteria. In general, this reflects quantitative changes in financial or competitive positioning resulting from investment in IS.

The other three classes are for IS evaluation at the micro level. Class IV, usage measures, capture the extent to which users utilize IS, the ease of IS access and motivational aspects associated with IS use: if, when, how much, and why users depend on IS for accomplishing their business activities. Class V, perceptual measures, capture user attitudes, beliefs, and perceptions toward IS. These are surrogates for complex mental models (schemes) that users develop as a result of IS introduction. Class VI, productivity measures, reflect how IS influences or enhances the performance of organizational members. These outcome-based measures reflect the success of system implementation in terms of managerial performance and productivity.

Similar to the IS Success Model of Delone and McLean, this model delineates individual and organizational measures of IS effectiveness. However, the construct space developed in this study does seem to further structure, and in some respects expand, the specific nature of measures associated with macro and micro levels of analysis. For example, in the macro level branch of Figure 1 organizational measures of effectiveness are structured across three possible levels of analysis. Micro level measures parallel the structure of macro level measures reflecting the assessment of effectiveness captured at a different level of organization impact. This parallelism is not reflected in the IS Success Model reflecting perhaps the linear nature of communications theory (i.e. sender, channel, receiver) in contrast to more symmetric theory of organizational effectiveness (i.e. process, response, and impact, measured at macro and micro levels). In general, it seems that both theoretical depictions strongly imply that IS effectiveness is multidimensional in terms of types of measures and level of analysis.

## 4. IS effectiveness: Context of application

While definition of construct space provides a useful lens of analysis for determining the specific nature of complex constructs, the development of a context of application is needed to show how measures of effectiveness are applied in practice and research. As noted by Delone and McLean, the selection of effectiveness measures should consider contingency variables such as technological context (i.e. characteristics of the system being evaluated) and evaluative perspective (i.e. the constituents who actually evaluate the system). While other characteristics may also be associated with the choice of evaluative metrics, these broad dimensions capture the essence of evaluative context and provide a prescriptive framework for applying the classes of effectiveness measures implied by the developed construct space.

## 4.1. Evaluation perspective

From whose perspective is effectiveness being evaluated? Effectiveness must be assessed from an explicit viewpoint. Cameron and Whetten [13] suggest that one reason no 'best' criterion exists is because there is no 'best' constituency. Though an IS may be viewed as an effective system from one standpoint, it may be interpreted as ineffective from another. Hamilton and Chervany [39] compare four different viewpoints on IS effectiveness: users, IS personnel, management, and internal audit. In the organizational effectiveness literature, various constituencies – such as top management [94], external resource providers [70], and a dominant coalition [11] can offer their own, parochial viewpoints [103]. Similarly, in evaluating IS effectiveness, the specific view of four different constituencies users, top management, IS personnel, and external entities (suppliers, customers, etc.) – should be considered.

All perspectives can employ both an individual and an organizational unit of analysis depending upon such factors as the evaluation purpose and the domain being focused on. For instance, top management are concerned with organizational success, so they may tend to focus on IS productivity and various business opportunities that IS can provide. They might also focus on individual level concerns because members' attitudes and abilities are critical to organizational success.

## 4.2. Domain of study

On what domain(s) is the evaluation focused? They may arise from the primary tasks of the organization. A variety of domains can be identified by various types of IS. McLeod [68] classifies these as: data processing systems (DPS), management information systems (MIS), decision support systems (DSS), office automation systems (OA), expert systems (ES), and telecommunication systems (TCS). Evaluators should specify clearly the target system(s) being assessed. Specifying the domain affects the selection of relevant criteria for evaluation and limits contradictory results and inaccurate assessment. In evaluating many advanced IS, impact or response assessment tends to be emphasized. Huber [44] discusses a theory of the effects of IS-based communication and decision-aiding information technologies (IT) on organizational design, intelligence, and decision making. He suggests that these IT (e.g. e-mail, computer conferencing, ES, DSS) influence organizational processes, structures, and performance. However, traditional IS, such as DPS and MIS, tend to be evaluated by process or response assessment.

## 5. Review of IS effectiveness studies

As a means of validating the developed construct space of IS effectiveness and further exploring the context of application for the various measurement classes, a review of relevant articles published over the fourteen-year time period of 1980 through 1994 was conducted. Eight publications were selected: Communications of the ACM, Decision Sciences, Information and Management, Information Systems Research, ICIS Proceedings, Journal of MIS, MIS Quarterly, and Management Science. These publications are typically selected for similar meta-analytic studies and are generally considered important outlets for IS research [40, 41]. Each study was examined to determine the type of criteria used to measure IS effectiveness, its level of analysis (organizational vs. individual), and its evaluative type (process, response, impact). In addition, the context of application (domain of analysis and evaluation perspective) was identified. Importantly, each study was independently classified by each author with disagreements being resolved through group consensus. In general, the identified criteria were mapped onto the construct space consistently (inter-rater reliability = 90%). The results of this classification are depicted in Table 1.

Since many researchers noted the multifaceted nature of IS effectiveness $[23, 26, 113]$ it is unlikely that any single overarching indicator of IS effectiveness will emerge. Many studies measure IS effectiveness in terms of a single universal criterion. Usually, UIS or usage was chosen. While this is still popular it is difficult to justify as a comprehensive or adequate measure of IS effectiveness. Some other studies utilize multiple criteria for IS evaluation. Such integrative studies are generally more comprehensive and may alleviate the problem of limiting the amount of variance explained. They demonstrate (or at least hypothesize) how the variables are related to one another. Therefore, these attempts to use multiple criteria are promising. However, it should be noted that the use of multiple criteria could be a major limitation in cases where such criteria are in conflict.

## 5.2. Evaluation perspective

Individual level of analysis has been the dominant evaluation perspective. This is consistent with the popularity of perceived criteria and usage measures. Also, it is not surprising to see that few studies deal with the perspective of external entities. One possible reason is that researchers have examined effectiveness mainly within an organizational context. This is relevant when IS research focuses on data, information, or a decision, but it is not appropriate when the focus is on the strategic impact of IS. Another reason may be the practical difficulty in identifying and operationalizing external reference points.

In some studies combined viewpoints are utilized. Hamilton and Chervany [39] suggest incorporating multiple viewpoints into the assessment of system effectiveness. Combined viewpoints help to increase awareness of the value of the IS and to understand the multidimensionality of IS effectiveness.

## 5.3. Domain and frame of references

A number of studies attempt to evaluate MIS/EDP. Thus process or response evaluation tends to be employed. Studies of DSS or ES have identified criteria based on the nature or definition of those systems. Therefore, these criteria are very specific and most studies utilize impact evaluation. Several studies do not specify systems assessed implying that their criteria may be used in various domains. These researchers are far less specific as to how their criteria are equally applicable to different systems.

Most studies evaluate IS in terms of normative or comparative judgement. A few utilize improvement judgement. Trait judgements are rarely used, possibly because evaluators are not generally concerned with technical characteristics of the system.

## 5.4. Implications

This study defined the construct space for effectiveness criteria based on a $2 \times 3$ typology. This is useful in delineating the boundary of the construct and for generation of more context-specific criteria. Also, this is a valuable framework across which disparate studies on IS effectiveness can be compared. By integrating and contrasting different studies, it attempts to synthesize fragmented research efforts. The dimensions used emphasize the need to focus more at the organizational level: i.e. this study advocates expanding relevant criteria of IS effectiveness to include more than the relatively narrow and largely micro view criteria used so prevalently in prior work. This is increasingly important as we shift our emphasis in IS research toward organizational effectiveness. Fourth, this study confirms the unbalanced research effort in evaluating IS, as noted by several researchers (e.g. [95]).

Many researchers have attempted to identify the impact of IS in various areas such as organizational form and function $[30]$ managerial work $[73]$ , and gaining competitive advantage through inter-organizational systems $[51]$ . It is time to pay attention to developing criteria and measurements that assess those effects. Also, with the increased role of IS in organizational effectiveness, the viewpoint of external entities should be employed more often, whether alone or with other complementary perspectives.

This study also has implications for practitioners. The framework provides insight for approaching the problem of deciding on an indicator of performance. It suggests that a clear understanding of target system(s), frame of reference, evaluation perspectives, and even purpose of the evaluation is a prerequisite to determining the criteria.

## 6. Conclusion

Improving IS effectiveness is generally the goal of IS research. Application of the results should lead to IS effectiveness or success. Further, recent trends in the application of a myriad of information technologies in business process change and electronic commerce makes evaluation of investments even more important and complex.

The framework illustrates the complexity of the IS effectiveness construct. As such it provides a useful tool for IS effectiveness research because it attempts to delineate the boundaries of the construct as well as providing a common set of dimensions, albeit broad, for comparing and contrasting studies. In fact, the substantive classification of studies provide validity to the typology and its use as a taxonomy for classifying IS effectiveness constructs. The dimensions defining this construct space must be recognized and articulated in future studies. Doing so will enable acceptance of a common paradigm, a basis for establishing validity and ultimately psychometrically sound measures that can be applied in a variety of contexts. The value in building a cumulative tradition for this research cannot be overemphasized. The evaluation of IS practices, deployment, design and procedures is contingent upon understanding and measuring the dependent variable.

The study also shows the importance of more focus on impact evaluation and organizational level of analysis due to the changing orientation of the field. This does not imply reduced importance for process or response evaluation or for the individual level of analysis. Rather, it calls for more attention to matching the appropriate type of evaluation with the unit of analysis, evaluation perspective, domain of study, frame of reference, and purpose of evaluation.
