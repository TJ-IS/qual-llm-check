# Techniques

# An empirical investigation of the contribution of IS to manufacturing productivity

Uma G. Gupta $^{*}$ , Margaret Capen

Department of Decision Sciences, East Carolina University, Greenville NC 27858-4353, USA

## Abstract

Next to the financial sector, the manufacturing industry is the second largest investor in information systems (IS) and technologies (IT). However, in recent years, many CEOs have expressed skepticism and disappointment on the returns from investments in IS. There is reluctance on the part of these CEOs to continue to invest in IS because of a nagging feeling that existing systems are often under-utilized. The dissatisfaction of CEOs with returns on them combined with the suspicion that existing IS are under-utilized clearly has serious implications for the IS community. Using empirical survey data of small and large manufacturing firms in the electronic industry, this paper investigates whether, in fact, IS are under-utilized and, if so, explores the underlying reasons for this situation. In particular, we explore the differences, if any, between small and large manufacturing firms in their ability to deploy and utilize IS to achieve organizational excellence.

Keywords: Productivity; Manufacturing; Small manufacturing firms © 1996 Published by Elsevier Science B.V.

## 1. Introduction

During the 1970s and 1980s, many manufacturing companies invested millions of dollars in IS as quick-fix solutions to complex and nagging problems on the shop floor. However, these problem-solving approaches were often piece-meal and ad hoc, resulting in what the popular literature termed as automated mess: decreasing organizational productivity and increasing redundancies caused specifically due to failed attempts at automation [2, 6, 11]. Ironically, companies had to make substantial investments simply to get out of the mess that automation created! [15]. Today, there is a more cautious and somewhat enlightened approach toward IS investments [13, 14]. In a recent survey of American manufacturers, it was found that 52 percent of the respondents spent anywhere between 1 percent and 5 percent of their total operating budgets on IS, while another 26 percent spent more than 5 percent on IS. Further, almost 87 percent of American manufacturers are actively engaged in computer integrated manufacturing [4, 18].

Although investments in IS continue, CEOs in many industries are somewhat skeptical about the role and contribution of IS to organizational productivity $[16]$ . Managers of mid-sized companies, in particular, believe strongly that the returns are often disproportional to the investments $[3]$ . They argue that, while IS personnel continue to demand newer and more advanced technologies as being essential for the smooth operations of the business, the returns from existing systems are frequently undocumented and questionable [7, 9]. Some managers are even convinced that real returns can be gained more from total quality management, continuous improvement, business process re-engineering, cycle time reduction, and improved customer service, than from IS [1, 10].

Several surveys and opinion polls indicate that CEOs are concerned about the returns and underutilization of existing IS, including specific manufacturing technologies, such as CAD/CAM, MRP I, and MRP II [4, 12, 22]. However, few studies have been made on the causes of this phenomenon. While the popular literature periodically addresses the above issue, academic journals, in general, do not discuss why CEOs may be discontented with IS. Given the growing evidence that manufacturing companies are shifting from a labor-intensive to a knowledge-intensive work force where information and knowledge form the crux of good decision making [5], this research assumes new importance.

We were also interested in exploring whether plant managers at small manufacturing firms perceived the contribution of IS differently from those at large firms $[17]$ . It is commonly believed that large firms are more intense and successful in deploying technology than small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). Large firms are often regarded as being more adept at using computer-based technologies to capture markets and better serve their customers. Although SMEs constitute the majority of suppliers and subcontractors to multinational manufacturing corporations, they are often laggards in embracing technology, failing to embrace technology with the same passion that their customers do $[19, 20, 21]$ . This is primarily because SMEs do not have the monetary resources nor the time required to adapt state-of-the-art technologies. Information-based technologies transform the nature of manufacturing products, processes, companies, and industries by way of lower entry and setup costs, easier customization of products, and product differentiation.

In a market economy, technology is at the heart of industrial structure. How labor, capital, and other resources can be combined to produce goods and services, viz., the technological possibilities, set the boundaries within which firms choose to operate [25].

Thus, the reluctance on the part of SMEs to embrace and keep up-to-date with computer technologies can have serious implications on the number and nature of jobs in the manufacturing sector. For example, between 1976 and 1984 alone, manufacturing employment in the USA increased from 21.4 million to 22.5 million and small businesses were responsible for all of these new jobs. Further, computers have far reaching implications, both for the firm and the environment in which they operate by serving as a window into the manufacturing process – holding the power to make or break a company [23]. The benefits of IT, especially CAD/CAM, include enhancing product quality, increasing organizational productivity and flexibility, and reducing cycle time. The importance of SMEs embracing computer technologies cannot be over-emphasized.

Against the above background, our secondary focus was to investigate if there was empirical evidence to support the hypothesis that small- and medium-sized manufacturing firms are less passionate than larger firms in embracing computer technologies. Do small firms perceive such technologies as playing only a limited role?

## 2. Research methodology

Surveys were used to collect data. They were first pre-tested on a group of APICS $^{1}$ members whose primary area of expertise was manufacturing and IS and their recommendations for change were incorporated into the survey. 220 questionnaires were mailed out; 117 questionnaires were returned (53 percent response rate); of these, 97 were usable. 41 (42 percent) were large firms, defined as companies whose annual gross sales were 250 million or higher, while the remaining 56 firms (58 percent) were classified as medium to small firms $^{2}$ .

H1: Company size is associated with the type of information systems and technologies used on the shop floor.

Intuitively, large firms appear more likely to invest in more sophisticated technologies than smaller firms (Harrison). The respondents in this survey indicated that they use a wide variety of computer-based manufacturing technologies, such as CAD, CAM, CIM, EDI, MRP, MRP II, and robots. The authors measured the variable USAGE as a dummy variable with yes representing use of the particular technology and no representing non-usage. This hypothesis investigates any possible association between company size and type of technology used. The chi-square test of independence was employed to examine this hypothesis since all variables in the analysis are measured categorically. Since a firm potentially could use more than one type of technology, eight separate chi-square tests were necessary.

H2: Company size is associated with the type of computer hardware used.

Due to considerations such as costs, large firms have a greater likelihood of investing in more sophisticated computer hardware than smaller firms. The hardware types examined include mainframes, minicomputers, microcomputers, workstations, local area networks, and wide area networks. The authors made no distinction between the extent of usage: limited or extensive usage. A chi-square analysis tested for the possible association between company size and each type of hardware employed.

H3: A difference exists between large and small firms in the contribution of IS toward achieving specific organizational goals.

Since more computer systems are deployed in larger firms than in small firms, we anticipate a significant difference in the contribution of computer systems toward achieving organizational goals. What is the extent to which companies use IS to achieve their goals? In order to address this issue, the survey asked respondents to rate, on a scale of 1 to 5 (1 being very effective and 5 representing no positive effect), the contribution of IS toward achieving the following four goals:

\- Provide strategic advantage

\- Improve market share

\- Meet changing market demands

\- Improve information flows

A multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) procedure was utilized to test simultaneously for possible differences in mean responses for all four goal areas of the firm on the basis of company size.

H4: Company size is associated with the perceptions of plant managers regarding the characteristics of MIS personnel.

The survey employed a 4-point scale to evaluate the contribution of MIS personnel (1 representing strongly agree and 4 indicating strongly disagree). The authors collapsed the scale into two categories: agree (items 1 and 2) and disagree (items 3 and 4) $^{3}$ [8, 24]. Eight different perceptions of plant managers were examined against company size, with the use of a chi-square test of independence.

H5: A difference exists between large and small firms in the extent of support that the MIS department provides to different functional areas in the firm.

This hypothesis tests the assumption that since large firms have access to more advanced technologies and technical personnel, their ability to use IS to achieve functional goals may be greater than for smaller firms.

The respondents rated the extent of support provided by the MIS department in eight functional areas. The 5-point scale ranged from 1 representing essential support, to 5 measuring insignificant support. The eight functional areas included:

\- strategic planning

\- shop floor automation

• control of manufacturing processes

\- process improvements

\- quality control

• enhance customer service

\- implement CIM

• concurrent engineering

We utilized MANOVA procedure to test simultaneously for possible differences in mean responses for all eight tasks for large and small companies.

H6: Company size is associated with perceived success in leveraging IS to achieve organizational goals.

Do larger firms, equipped with more sophisticated technologies, perceive themselves as being more successful in leveraging IS to achieve the overall goals of the organization? [26] To investigate this, participants used a 4-point scale (with 1 representing very successful and 4 representing not very successful) to rate the overall success of information systems in the following areas:

• productivity improvement

\- cost reduction

\- reduction in product life cycle

\- added value to the product

• enhanced competitive position

\- improved financial position

For the purpose of this hypothesis, IS was considered successful if the respondent answered 1 or 2, and not successful if they chose 3 or 4. A chi-square test of independence tested for possible association between company size and perceived success in these six areas.

## 4. Conclusions

Several interesting results emerged from this study, some of which have been reported elsewhere [Gupta, 1994]. Our investigation supports the intuitive theory that large firms are likely to invest more in advanced technologies than small firms. In particular, there is a significant difference in the usage of CAM, CIM, EDI, and robots between large and small firms. While almost three out of four large firms use CAM, approximately one in five small firms use CAM. Similarly, almost one in every two large firms use CIM compared with only 16 percent of small firms. In the case of EDI, the difference between large and small firms is not surprising, since, even large firms have been slow to embrace EDI and sometimes did so simply because of pressure from powerful elements along the supply chain.

Are large firms more successful than small firms in deploying IS to achieve organizational goals? We found no difference between large and small firms in their ability to successfully deploy IS to achieve organizational goals. In other words, although small businesses are slower to adopt sophisticated computer technologies, they appear to be equally adept at applying existing technologies to achieve organizational goals.

There is also no difference by firm size in the extent to which IS support various functional tasks, such as strategic planning, shop floor automation, control of manufacturing processes, process improvements, quality control and concurrent engineering. Our study shows that IS is equally successful for both large and small firms in cutting operational costs, reducing product life cycle, increasing value addedness, and enhancing the competitive and financial position of the company. However, the role of IS in enhancing organizational productivity was much higher in large firms. Our study shows that even though small firms may invest less in advanced technologies than large firms, they appear successful in employing those technologies to achieve the functional goals of the organization.

The study also found basic similarities between large and small organizations with respect to the characteristics of their MIS personnel. The significant major exception was with the statement “do not get along well with the ‘plant guys’.”
