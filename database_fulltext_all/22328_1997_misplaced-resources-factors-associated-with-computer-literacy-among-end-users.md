$^{a}$ Faculty of Business, P.O. Box 3015, MS 8264, University of Victoria, Victoria, BC, Canada V8W 3P1

## Abstract

Some organizations provide a support infrastructure (e.g. information centers) and training to assist end-users and boost the computer knowledge of their workforce. Here we explore the relationship between support infrastructure, training, various computer configurations, and the computer literacy of work groups. Data were collected in a multi-year study of 77 computer-using work groups; this included two interviews with managers and two questionnaires completed by workers. Analyses showed that none of the measures of training were associated with computer literacy and only one kind of infrastructure support was found to be related to computer literacy: obtaining information from a resident expert in the work group. In contrast, many aspects of the configuration of the computer systems were associated with computer literacy. Implications of these findings for the management of end-user computing are discussed. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: End-user computing; End-user support; Computer literacy; Computer knowledge; Support infrastructure; End-user training

## 1. Introduction

Workers gained unprecedented access to computer resources during the 1980s as terminals, personal computers, and software application packages proliferated throughout organizations. End-users are now a large group in many organizations, and they are assuming more and more of the responsibilities that were previously those of data processing experts.

Many potential benefits should accrue from end-user computing, including increased productivity [12]. However, actual productivity improvements among white collar workers have not been shown to follow the introduction of technology into the office [9]. This may be due, in part, to the lack of attention paid to the role of IS literacy in aiding the end-user to be efficient and effective. The relationship between an adequate understanding of IS and the realization of the full potential of end-user computing has been strongly supported [3, 32, 38].

Workers who have a good understanding of computers (i.e. are computer literate) should be able to use their computer systems in a more sophisticated or effective manner or use them for more tasks, and so they should be more productive. Conversely, when users do not understand their tools, expensive equipment may be severely underutilized and an enormous amount of staff time may be wasted in trying to make equipment produce the desired result. In addition, high levels of computer knowledge may well enhance an individual's or a work group's power in organizations that are dependent on computers [24].

Computer knowledge can be learned in school, but the majority of workers left formal educational settings before they were able to learn how to use the computers and programs that are required for performing their new jobs. So, the computer fluency of workers in an organization may depend on the extent to which the organization facilitates or encourages high levels of computer literacy among workers. The three types of organizationally provided facilitators discussed in the literature on desktop computing are: the infrastructure of computer support [11, 33], training in computer use [6], and the configuration of the computer system used by the workers. The question addressed here is: How do the organizationally provided facilitators available to work groups of end-users who are highly computer literate differ from those available to groups that are not?

## 2. Computer literacy

‘Computer literacy’ is a widely used term, but it is not precisely defined. “Computer literacy is being knowledgeable about the computer and how it works in our daily lives. It also means being able to operate and use a computer, at least to perform basic tasks [34].” Since the average end-user does not operate mainframes or minicomputers, functional computer literacy is mainly concerned with proficiency in operating microcomputer-based systems to complete a variety of standard, common, office tasks.

Functional computer literacy includes knowledge of concrete ‘facts’ as well as abstract concepts. To further explain this, we make an analogy and differentiate between general and functional literacy. Functional literacy is considered to be the ability to read and write well enough to function in society (e.g. read a newspaper or write a note to one’s supervisor). The broader, classic type of literacy – general literacy – implies a much higher level of ability (familiarity with the major works of literature, ability to write critical essays). Similarly, functional computer literacy or fluency includes the basic knowledge required to use computers in various white-collar work settings, including computer systems encountered in society. It implies a much higher level of skill, including the ability to write programs and design systems. Functional computer literacy also includes understanding the strengths and weaknesses of the computer, and how to use it to achieve organizational goals [40]. The greater one's level of fluency, the greater the variety of settings in which one could use computer systems to accomplish standard types of common work-related tasks.

Some might argue that, in today's environment of user-friendly interfaces, an end-user does not need to understand the inner workings of a computer or general computer terminology, but only how to make the correct keystrokes. We posit that a functionally computer literate person must have enough general knowledge about computer systems to figure out how to solve problems and to adapt quickly to new systems and packages with a minimum of retraining. Being able to do this comes from an understanding of the system well beyond just knowing the names of parts of the computer or memorizing a sequence of keystrokes. Research in systems has found that users form mental models (often analogies) of their understanding of how their systems work. These are seen as internal representations of the system's structure and functions that provide explanation and power to the user. Elaborate and correct mental models are consistently related to better task performance (e.g. greater accuracy, higher efficiency) [23, 26, 36, 37]. They can be developed by using the system, by drawing analogies or metaplots to similar well-known or familiar systems, and by assimilating a conceptual model communicated during training. Computer literacy is probably associated with a more accurate and complete mental model. Users become computer fluent through some combination of education, training, and experience.

In summary, functionally computer literate workers have both concrete and abstract knowledge of computers and are able to apply their knowledge to new systems and situations with a minimum of retraining.

## 2.1. Resources that may enhance computer literacy

Workers, managers, and work groups vary enormously in their understanding of IS and in the level of sophistication with which they can use their equipment. Many organizations have found that the average level of IS knowledge among their workers is inadequate [1]. There are two common methods to compensate for this lack of knowledge, providing an infrastructure of support and training.

## 2.1.1. Support infrastructure

A support infrastructure can include specialized assistance, such as: an on-call helpline, a formal information center, a staff of consultants to assist end-users, or a software library. These resources are provided to encourage the communication of technical information to end-users and they have been associated with enhanced end-user literacy [4, 18, 19, 25, 31, 35]. In addition information centers have been associated with improved company performance, possibly as a result of the support they provide the organization's end-users [17].

H1: Members of work groups with access to an information center, consultants, systems maintenance help, and programmers to write programs and help users will be more computer literate than groups without access to these forms of support.

An additional type of support is the availability of a co-worker or a friend with computer expertise who can answer questions on an ad hoc basis: the local resident expert. This person may or may not be somebody inside the work group and may be considered a part of the organization's formal support infrastructure [2].

H2: Members of work groups that can have their computer-related questions answered by a local resident expert within their group will be more computer literate.

## 2.1.2. Training

Organizations may also communicate computer-related information to end-users through training to enhance their computer fluency. Training has previously been related to computer literacy when measured as a self-reported rating of computer knowledge and to the development of more accurate and complete mental models, although the sophistication of the training can vary widely. Some employers provide computer equipment and software and assume that workers will be able to learn on their own. Research specifically focused on end-user computing has identified education and training as critical to its success, but there is still considerable debate in the literature regarding the best method to use [8].

General information system's knowledge, as well as technical and product-related skills, can be provided through classes developed by vendors or staff within the organization. Group workshops with hands-on training are the most widely used technique in industry. Classes can be geared to the specifics of particular application programs, or they can be general courses in computer literacy that focus on how computers work and what can be done with them. Focus on only the specific keystrokes required to perform a given task would be unlikely to lead to formation of a complete and accurate mental model of the computer system and so should not result in computer fluency: however, communication of general principles and analogies and use of a computer, during training, should.

H3: Members of work groups in organizations that provide training that uses computer terminals, covers general concepts, and includes group workshops will have higher levels of functional computer literacy.

## 2.1.3. Computer configuration

Rather than focusing on organizational mechanisms provided to communicate computer-related information to end-users, the nature of the available computer system may itself be related to computer knowledge. End-users working on mainframe and minicomputers and those running task-specific software may develop a less accurate mental model and have available a smaller repertoire of analogous systems from which to draw than users of microcomputers running multifunctional software that permit more customized use. Prior research has demonstrated a positive relationship between availability and the use of computers $[10, 27]$ . A similar relationship may exist between availability and computer knowledge (perhaps mediated by computer use).

H4: Members of work groups that use microcomputer-based systems that are user friendly, less specialized in function, and were adopted earlier, provide a larger number of general use applications, and have a low ratio of fulltime equivalent employees per terminal will be more computer fluent.

Partly, because little research has used adequate quantifiable measures of end-user computer literacy [28], we have relatively little information on the relationship between it and the support infrastructure, training, and system configurations available to workers. This paper attempts to illuminate the relationship between these three resources and computer literacy.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Participants

Data for our analyses came from a larger field study of IT and the structure of computer using white-collar work groups. Consistent with previous research [15], we defined a work group as four or more people engaged in some common information-related process or product, including at least one level of supervision.

Work groups were thus eligible under the following conditions if:

1. They consisted of at least four people;

2. Computers have been used for at least one year (this avoids confusion due to temporary organizational change or implementation issues);

3. Computers were necessary for the work of the group as a whole but not all in the group needed to use a computer.

Forty-nine different organizations, recruited through personal contact, agreed to participate and provided 89 work groups for the study. This constituted a convenience sample of computer-reliant white-collar work groups; it was not possible to draw a stratified random sample, since the characteristics of the parent population are unknown. A diverse set of work groups were studied, including the management consulting department for a defense contractor, the emergency vehicle dispatch group for a paramedic response team, the appointment scheduling group for a health maintenance organization, and a design and manufacturing group for another defense contractor. The average work group size was 10. Commonly, work groups represented either intact departments performing homogeneous work or a team of workers that performed one distinct portion of the work in a multi-functional department. Analyses were conducted primarily at the group level, because (a) the unit of analysis in the larger study was the work group, (b) computers were used for the work of the unit and systems were usually available to all workers in the group, (c) workers' limited technical knowledge of IS precluded collecting such information from them directly, and (d) about 95% of the workers in the groups used computers in their work. Groups that were sought were diverse in size, product, computer implementation, and use, as well as in computer equipment configuration.

## 3.2. Data collection

Data for this study were gathered at two points in time, about 18 months apart. Data from questionnaires were collected both times, and interview data were collected at the first point in time. Now, we describe our procedures for collecting this data.

Naturally, many end-users have limited technical knowledge about the computer systems they used. Consequently, a questionnaire concerning aspects of IS about which all end-users were knowledgeable (e.g. the nature of the work performed by the individual, the use of computers, and reactions to work) was handed out to all members of each work group during a site visit to the work location. At the same time, information of a more technical nature was sought from each group's supervisor (including questions about the nature of the work performed by the group, the computer system available, and the computing support infrastructure). The interview format allowed us to rephrase and clarify questions, and seek help from other members of the organization whenever supervisors were not sure of the correct answer, thus increasing the likelihood the information we gathered about the IS available to the group was accurate. If, at any time, managers were unable to answer any question, correct answers were solicited from more knowledgeable members of the organization. Interviewers also performed independent ratings of several aspects of the work groups, including their degree of IS sophistication and knowledge. Subsequent analyses of these data highlighted the importance of end-user computer knowledge as rated by the interviewers, and this led to the research questions described in this study. $^{2}$

A second questionnaire that evaluated levels of computer literacy among end-users was developed, and is described in the next section. The instrument was administered to workers in 77 of these same groups that agreed to participate in our follow-up study of computer literacy 18 months after the first study. This paper presents analyses based on the responses of 571 respondents in 77 work groups who answered the second questionnaire and the original interviews with their managers. The demographic characteristics of our sample are shown in Table 1.

We developed a reliable and valid 23-item measure of functional computer literacy that would be appropriate for computer-using workers with varying levels of sophistication. A detailed description of this instrument and evidence of its reliability and validity are provided elsewhere [16]; it is summarized in Table 2. In preparing questions to be included in our measure, we consulted microcomputer user manuals, items from measures of general computer literacy, items of computer aptitude or other related measures, computer experts and colleagues, and previous surveys and studies. Some of the items were adaptations of questions, items, or statements from manuals and studies and others were written ‘from scratch.’ A pilot questionnaire was developed, evaluated by independent experts in end-user computing, tested on two local work groups, and altered where required.

Questions cut across hardware and software applications, and tapped both abstract and concrete knowledge. Though not all items may be relevant to each individual's current job, the aggregate knowledge represents a set that indicates a worker's ability to perform a variety of tasks commonly included in white-collar jobs that involve using microcomputer-based systems. We included more than one response format in order to determine whether all four measured the underlying construct equally well. Based on the available literature and consultations with end-user computing experts, items were written or adapted to cover various aspects of computer knowledge. The instructions for each type of question and a sample question are given in Figure 1.

A principal components factor analysis scree test indicated that a one factor solution was most interpretable [13]; one factor was extracted that accounted for 38.5% of the variance present in the data; its eigen value was 9.23. Though the single factor only accounted for 38.5% of the variance, this indicates that many of the items tapped unique variance not shared by the other items. The remaining 61.5% of the variance was tapping a portion of the construct that was not covered by other items in the measure.

The 23-item scale showed good internal consistency as well as discriminant and convergent validity in two different samples. Consistent with previous qualitative research [21], managers and professionals scored higher on our measure than did clerical or secretarial workers ( $t_{(575)} = 11.86; p < 0.001$ ). For analyses at the group level, each item was standardized (to weight different response formats equally) and then the average of all members in a work group was calculated to yield an estimate of the average level of functional computer literacy of the work group. Validity was also assessed at the group level. The average computer fluency of a work group was related to interviewer's ratings of the group's knowledge of computer technology, conducted as part of site visits performed 18 months earlier ( $r = 0.70; p < 0.0001$ ). Though an individual worker's level of computer knowledge may change rapidly, the average level of an intact work group should change relatively slowly provided that the nature of the work performed and of the IS used does not change. Even when there is turnover, new employees are likely to have levels of computer fluency that are similar to those departing, since the requirements of the jobs filled seldom change solely because the incumbent has changed.

Organizational support: During the interview, supervisors were asked about formal organizational support for computer use and about the training provided to workers. Specifically, supervisors indicated whether or not the work group members had access to an information center, computer consultants, help with systems maintenance, or computer programmers.

Supervisors also indicated where workers found the experts who helped them with computing questions: within the work group, outside the work group but inside the company, or outside the company. Concurrently, workers were asked in the computer use questionnaire about the frequency with which they received answers to their questions from someone in their work group (a local resident expert) or from someone outside of their work group. Responses were coded 1=never; 2=occasionally; 3=usually;

4=always, and then they were aggregated to the group level. The means for receiving answers from within and outside the group were 2.79 (s.d.=0.44) and 3.11 (s.d.=0.48), respectively.

Organizational training: In the interview, supervisors also indicated whether or not new and continuing employees received computer training, whether training would be performed at a terminal, whether the training emphasized specific, concrete steps involved in performing the job, and whether it included broad generic principles of how computers work. Also supervisors indicated, in broad categories, that the proportion of people in the work group who joined the group already knowing how to use a computer and supervisors described the nature of the training available to workers (learn on their own, trained individually, trained in a group, etc.).

Computer configuration: Supervisors also described to the interviewers the computer configurations available to workers. The average group began using computers for their work late in 1980s (s.d.=5.28 years). Microcomputers were the most plentiful with 92% of groups reporting access to at least one. Most groups (56%) had one or more mainframe computers available and less than half (44%) had access to one or more minicomputers. An objective measure of computer intensity (the ratio of full-time equivalent employees to terminals of any kind) was included in the interview and varied from more than four terminals per person to an outlier of 12 people per terminal. When the data were Windsorized (i.e. the one outlier recoded to the next largest number – four people per terminal), the ratio averaged a little over one person per terminal (mean=1.35; median=1.08; s.d.=0.81).

An objective measure of IS specialization was also assessed during interviews. Each supervisor was presented a list of eleven computer applications (e.g. word processing, creating spreadsheets, communications, etc.), and asked to identify those which his or her system could perform. In addition, managers were asked the number of special applications (not mentioned in the list provided to them) available on the system. On average, systems could perform a little more than eight functions (mean=8.42; s.d.=3.50). A list of the functions and their frequency in work groups are shown in Table 3. A subjective rating of system specialization was also made by the interviewers by assessing the number of different tasks or functions for which the computer system was actually used by workers in the group (coded 1=only one; 2=several; 3=many; 4=an unlimited number). Over half of the groups' systems were used for several functions and about one third were used for many functions. Supervisors also rated their system's user friendliness (1=not at all or not very; 2=somewhat; 3=very friendly) with a mean of 1.94 and a s.d. of 0.75.

## 4. Results

We next explore some relationships among work group functional computer fluency, organizational support and training, and computer configuration. Since the outcome of interest (computer literacy) was continuous, analyses differ according to the nature of the predictors. Continuous predictors (i.e. full-time equivalent workers per terminal, year of computer adoption, number of functions) are correlated with the outcome measure. Analysis of variances (ANOVAs) or t-tests are performed for categorical predictors. A score above 0.002 means the group has more computer knowledge than average.

## 5. Discussion

Although support infrastructure and training may be important for an organization's implementation and use of computers, neither appears to be associated with the computer knowledge nor literacy of workers, with one exception: the presence of a local resident expert. In contrast, several aspects of computer configuration were related to the average level of computer literacy.

In general, the findings suggest that the factors within the work group were associated with higher computer literacy. Some configurations of hardware and software seemed to demand more knowledge from users than others and the association between computer literacy and the use of a local resident expert highlights the central role of communication networks in supporting end-user computing. Even with these findings, however, it is not clear why the presence of a resident expert and certain types of systems were associated with groups that have higher levels of computer fluency.

## 5.1. Limitations

It is worth noting that the dependent variable, computer literacy, was assessed with a questionnaire distributed to workers in the groups; most of the independent variables were assessed in interviews with managers of the groups. Thus, our findings were not inflated by common method variance, since two different methods of data collection were used.

A large number of tests of significance were performed increasing the overall likelihood of Type I error; however, this is a risk worth taking in exploratory descriptive research. The extent to which our results are generalizable to other samples is also unclear. It is also important to note that causality cannot be firmly inferred from our findings, even though most of the independent variables were measured earlier in time than was the dependent variable, computer literacy. For example, the type of training available in the summer of 1988 or the fall of 1989 was not necessarily the same as the training when the computer literacy of workers was measured in the winter of 1989–1990. Nevertheless, it is possible that a support infrastructure and training may boost the computer literacy of a work group if the groups having these available were especially low in computer literacy at the start.

## 5.2. Conclusion

Though training and support infrastructure may have improved literacy, it is clear that they have not made workers highly computer literate. Perhaps more advanced assistance would help. It is also possible that formal support may make it easier for end-users to perform their jobs with only cursory knowledge and does not promote (or actually inhibits) an in-depth understanding of computing [14].

Providing more advanced training, an on-line help system, or an information center may not be the most appropriate way to increase levels of computer literacy. A centralized approach, such as an on-line help service, an information center, or standard training courses, may be only somewhat effective in creating computer knowledgeable workers because the experts generally do not know each user. It may take a while to understand the user's level of knowledge so that the appropriate level of information can be conveyed. Instead, providing computer systems which require that users acquire a certain level of knowledge, coupled with a local resident expert to help them, may be more effective than providing advanced training or an information center. It is also possible that some users may not improve their level of computer literacy even if they receive advanced training, though they may be perfectly able to use their specific 'do it' knowledge. $^{3}$

Our findings suggest that distributing knowledge among users is associated with greater computer literacy, though it is not clear from our study why this should be so. It is possible that, in a decentralized mode, experts develop relationships with users and have a good understanding of what the user does and does not know. Thus they are able to provide information at the appropriate level quickly and effectively. Such experts should be better able to communicate computer concepts using terms that are familiar to work group members. He or she could also exert a strong influence on workers' attitudes regarding computer use and toward enhancing their own computer literacy. A decentralized approach of making microcomputer equipment and software packages available and infiltrating each work group with an enthusiastic computer expert may be the most effective way of increasing computer literacy in work groups. However, causality cannot be firmly inferred from our results and should be addressed in future research.
