# An examination of designer and user perceptions of JAD and the traditional IS design methodology

Russell Purvis $^{a,*}$ , V. Sambamurthy $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Management, College of Business Administration, University of Central Florida, Orlando, FL 32816-1400, USA $^{b}$ Information and Management Sciences, College of Business, Florida State University, Tallahassee, FL 32306-1042, USA

## Abstract

Software design methodologies abound, with each claiming to be the solution for enhancing user participation in the systems analysis and design process. Joint applications design (JAD) is an approach that draws upon the strengths of group dynamics to facilitate requirements determination. JAD has been advocated for enhancing user participation and tapping user creativity in the systems design process. However, these claims are based on case studies and anecdotal evidence with limited empirical justification. This research compares the effects of the JAD and the traditional IS design methodology relative to the interactions between users and designers, consensus management, and user acceptance of the design specifications. This effort draws upon data from 94 pairs of designers and users associated with 57 different projects that utilized either the traditional IS design or JAD methodology. Results suggest that designers perceived JAD as being superior to the traditional IS design method with respect to the quality of user-designer interactions, effectiveness of consensus management, and user acceptance of design specifications. Users were less enamored on finding that JAD only promoted superior user-designer interactions. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Information systems design; User-designer perceptions; Semantic gap; Joint application design; Systems development methodologies; Linear regression

## 1. Introduction

User involvement is a critical requirement of the process of design and implementation of information systems in organizations $[3, 4, 14, 30]$ . User participation is believed to increase user acceptance by enabling development of realistic expectations about the proposed system, providing an environment for constructive negotiation on design issues, creating a spirit of system ownership, decreasing user resistance to change, and building user commitment [16, 19]. User participation is also believed to increase the quality of the system being developed by improving the users' understanding of the system, providing more complete and accurate information requirements, avoiding development of an unacceptable system, and providing necessary expertise about the organization and work processes that will be supported by the system [18, 28].

Spurred by the search for prospects of enhancing user participation, researchers and practitioners have evolved and evaluated the merits of a variety of development methodologies. An IS methodology is a systematic approach for conducting analysis, design, testing, etc. of a software product. It usually consists of a set of guidelines, activities, techniques and tools, based on a particular philosophy of system development. The traditional IS design methodology is not a specific model as much as a composite of common factors found in the hundreds of published life cycle methodologies and thousands in use. It takes a top-down approach to evaluate the development process and the data flows that connect these processes. A crucial element of the traditional IS design methodology is that it limits user participation to a consultative role, where the bulk of the design decisions are made by the IS developer or analyst. Users provide their inputs primarily through one-on-one meetings with the designers [13, 25]. Critics have suggested that the traditional IS design methodology suffers from shortcomings in facilitating high quality interactions among users and IS designers. This is a particular problem when the information systems under development span multiple functional areas in organizations [5, 33].

Joint application design (JAD) is an alternative design methodology that has received prominent attention $[12, 21, 22]$ . In contrast with the traditional IS design methodology, JAD expands the scope of user participation to a representative role, where users collectively articulate, negotiate, and develop systems specifications. Typically, with JAD, user participation occurs through group meetings that employ a structured agenda, techniques for the effective management of group dynamics, and facilitators for managing creativity and conflict resolution among the participants. These meetings become the key event around which the rest of the systems design activities revolve.

Prior case studies, anecdotal evidence, and claims made by the advocates of JAD attribute a variety of advantages to JAD over the traditional IS design methodology, primarily because of differences in how these methodologies promote user participation during the design and development process (Table 1). However, there have been few attempts at empirically verifying the superiority of JAD. This paper presents a study that compared users and designers on the effects of JAD and the traditional IS design methodology. The data were gathered from pairs of 94 designers, 94 users and 57 project managers that worked on 57 systems development projects employing either JAD or traditional IS design methodology.

## 2. The research model

A systemic problem with prior comparisons of development methodologies is the difficulty of defining, much less measuring, the ‘success’ of a methodology [32]. Curtis et al. [7] suggest that a deeper understanding of how specific tools, methods, and practices actually affect the nature of a systems development process could be obtained by targeting attention at the behavioral factors characterizing the design process.

Communication behavior between users and designers is viewed as fundamental to the successful development of information systems (e.g. [6, 15]). The underlying assumption in communication is that different parties bring different work styles, routines, approaches, and information use behaviors associated with their experience and organizational areas. This 'semantic gap' results in different ideas about the scope, specifications, and nature of the proposed application system [10]. The greater the semantic gap, the greater the need for communication to achieve a convergence in understanding between users and designers on the specifications of the proposed system.

Differences in user perceptions and expectations often promote conflict and politics during the design process. IS and group literatures suggest that this conflict is a critical variable in influencing team effectiveness. Potential for conflict is high in information systems that cut across departmental boundaries, especially when there exist tensions across departments in the form of relatively incompatible goals and differing criteria for success. In addition, users and designers often work together on projects that are under resource pressures and time constraints and face conflicts over priorities about the systems specifications and deliverables. If these conflicts are ignored in the rush toward an immediately workable solution, the system may be dramatically less useful and lead to additional problems, including user rejection, or attempts at subversion [20]. When handled correctly, conflict within the design team is dialectic, or educational in nature. The experience is useful as team members learn from one another. Thus, the success of the systems design process is related to the ability to manage conflict and achieve consensus over a shared set of specifications. These two behaviors, communication and negotiation, are important social behaviors that strongly influence the systems design process. The impacts of a systems design methodology could be evaluated through an observation of their effectiveness in promoting communication and negotiation among stakeholders of the proposed information system and their influence on users' acceptance of the design specifications. As illustrated in Figure 1, our research utilizes three variables to evaluate the extent to which the traditional IS design methodology and JAD facilitate these behavioral objectives. The first two – quality of user-designer interactions and effectiveness of consensus management – are process-oriented measures of the behaviors, whereas the variable, user acceptance of design specifications, is an outcome-oriented measure of the behavioral impact of the systems design process.

The quality of user-designer interactions captures the extent to which conditions necessary for effective communication occur during the design process. These conditions are: active user/designer participation, open and frank communication, user influence in the design process, user commitment to sharing information and knowledge, and a feeling of partnership between users and designers during the design process. These conditions allow users and designers to exchange their knowledge and views about the proposed system and revise their understanding through an exposure to others' ideas and perceptions. This allows users and designers to expand the domain of their initial knowledge and understanding, revise some of their initial assumptions and expectations, and develop a more complete understanding about the system [1].

The effectiveness of consensus management refers to the extent to which the design process is characterized by counterproductive conflict and exercise of political manipulation by the involved actors. Mason and Mitroff [23] suggest that such a process also enables participants to negotiate their conflicts and differences more productively. To facilitate effective communication, the differences in users' expectations and the resultant conflict must be handled in a such way that diverse perspectives are not stifled, members' commitment is maintained, and group cohesiveness is built. The eruption of counterproductive conflict and political manipulation can hamper the ability of designers and users to engage in open and honest communication, learn from exposure to alternative perspectives, and negotiate differences toward a shared view of the design specifications [27].

Finally, user acceptance of the design specifications is likely to depend on the degree to which effective communication and negotiation occurs during the design process. When the design process is characterized by high levels of communication and negotiation, the design group should be successful in managing the process in a way that individual members' opinions are not stifled and group cohesiveness is built and maintained. As a result, users should have favorable perceptions about the design specifications, perceive their overall objectives as being met by the design, and be more satisfied with the design specifications.

## 2.1. Control variables

Our research model also includes factors other than the design methodology that could influence the designers' and users' perceptions about the process and outcomes of systems design. To permit a useful comparison of effects of the two methodologies, these factors were included as control variables. Four of these factors, project size in staff days, project size in development cost, departmental scope, and project complexity are characteristics of the IS projects. Literature on project management has identified these as being influential [11, 17, 26]. Projects characterized by large staffs, high development costs, and a larger number of departments are expected to create more pressures and difficulties in communication, negotiation and the resulting user acceptance of the requirements. Similarly, project complexity is expected to affect the degree to which project members experience difficulties in managing the behaviors required for project success.

The other two control factors are designers' confidence in applying the design methodology and designer's experience with and knowledge of the methodology. Indeed, while the traditional IS design methodology has been the paradigm for systems design for a long time, JAD is a relatively newer approach. Most IS designers should have a greater degree of knowledge, experience and confidence with the traditional IS design methodology. Further, the JAD approach is more complex, requiring the designers to blend a structured meeting agenda with visual aids, process facilitation, and group creativity and conflict resolution techniques for managing the session and evolving design specifications. The emerging literature suggests that IS designers' knowledge, experience, and confidence with the methodology are important factors associated with the beneficial use of the approach [2, 31].

## 3. Contrasting the features of traditional IS design and JAD methodologies

The two methodologies under comparison in this paper differ in how they permit the elicitation of user knowledge and support the behavioral processes of communication and negotiation. While the traditional

IS design method can use several methods of knowledge acquisition, the dominant approach is interviewing. During one-on-one meetings, the designer attempts to understand critical work flows, factors that influence operations of the system, and the key documents, procedures, and policies of the system. As the designer attempts to construct design specifications from the volume of information gathered, two additional problems can arise: the conflicts in requirements posed by different users and incomplete knowledge that individual users possess concerning the IS design. The process is also hampered by problems such as slow communication, long feedback time, and a we-versus-they gap between users and designers.

Instead of one-on-one interviews, JAD knowledge acquisition centers on structured meetings involving prominent users and designers of the proposed information system. The methodology attempts to utilize the power and creativity of group dynamics, whereby users and designers work as a team to analyze the requirements, generate innovative ideas, and make decisions that shape the system's design. Users and designers are collectively involved in the learning, communication, and negotiation processes, and they observe the evolution of the design specifications. The approach facilitates learning by exposing individual participants to the ideas and proposals posed by other participants. Such an exposure triggers synergy as participants build upon the ideas in an interactive manner and converge toward a shared set of expectations about the proposed information system. The structure of the JAD meetings is designed to facilitate effective communication among participants. Further, since some stakeholders are participants in the JAD session, the approach allows members to present differences in their expectations about the proposed design specifications and negotiate these differences constructively through the structure of the JAD meeting. Studies have indicated that groups are more effective at negotiating conflict through the use of structured group techniques, process facilitation, and an agenda.

We anticipate that the JAD method will be perceived as facilitating richer quality interactions among users and designers. JAD involves collective interaction among users and designers in a group meeting, whereas the traditional methodology involves one-on-one interactions between the designer and individual users. When users have different perspectives about specifications of the proposed system, a group meeting enables richer exchange of viewpoints and expectations and facilitates the users and designers in developing a better understanding of alternative viewpoints and the nature of the final specifications of the proposed system [8]. Second, the effectiveness of a group meeting depends upon the ability to extract, use, and synthesize the potential contributions of group members during the process of group interaction. The group interaction process, and thereby group performance, is often impeded in the meaningful exchange of opinions, and synthesis of different viewpoints, by the existence of a variety of inhibitory barriers [24]. Since JAD provides structures for managing the group meeting, it is expected to further enhance the richness of the user-designer interactions. Thus, we anticipate that:

H1: The JAD methodology will be perceived as being superior to the traditional IS design methodology in its ability to facilitate richer user-designer interactions.

Users and designers exhibit different perceptions about the nature and outcomes of the systems design and development process. Hence, our investigation focused attention upon both designers' and users' perceptions about the quality of user-designer interactions, effectiveness of consensus management, and user acceptance of the design specifications. Given this explicit focus, we offer two separate corollaries to this hypothesis:

H1a: Users will perceive the JAD methodology to be superior to the traditional design methodology in its ability to facilitate richer user-designer interactions.

H1b: Designers will perceive the JAD methodology to be superior to the traditional design methodology in its ability to facilitate richer user-designer interactions.

Generally, structure, as available within the JAD approach, improves the quality of consensus management activities in groups [29]. Process facilitation and an agenda are helpful in focusing members' attention on the productive resolution of conflict. In contrast, in the traditional IS approach, the design team is assigned a central role in reconciling the differences in expectations elicited from individual users in the one-on-one meetings. Since users are not collectively involved in a process of negotiation, effective consensus is not expected to result. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H2: The JAD method will be perceived as being superior to the traditional design methodology in its ability to facilitate consensus management.

Again we offer two corollaries:

H2a: Users will perceive the JAD methodology to be superior to the traditional design methodology in facilitating effective consensus management.

H2b: Designers will perceive the JAD methodology to be superior to the traditional design methodology in facilitating effective consensus management.

Finally, we anticipate that since the JAD methodology facilitates richer user-designer interactions, it will lead to higher levels of user acceptance of the design specifications. Further, effective consensus management processes have been found to lead toward greater satisfaction with the product and process of the group activities. As a result, we anticipate that:

H3: The JAD methodology will be perceived as being superior to the traditional design methodology in the level of user acceptance of design specifications.

Again, we separate the effects in terms of the user and designer perceptions:

H3a: Users will perceive the JAD methodology to be superior to the traditional design methodology in the higher level of user acceptance of design specifications.

H3b: Designers will perceive the JAD methodology to be superior to the traditional design methodology in the higher level of user acceptance of design specifications.

## 4. Research method

Data for the study were gathered through a questionnaire survey. The questionnaire was tested for face and content validity with a sample of 10 users and systems designers in a state government agency. This pilot test resulted in minor revisions to the item wording. The revised version of the questionnaire was used to gather the user and designer responses.

Since the focus of our research is restricted to a comparison of the effects of JAD and traditional IS design methodology, we deemed a deliberate sampling strategy to be valid for selecting the population of projects for this research; this allowed us to impose control over the primary independent variable, the systems design methodology. First, we identified firms and projects that would form an appropriate sample. While a multitude of organizations existed that used the traditional IS design methodology, greater effort was required in identifying organizations that used the JAD methodology. A variety of sources, including the trade press and publications were scanned to identify the firms that had adopted the JAD design methodology. In addition, members of a JAD users group were solicited to participate in the study. Overall, a sample of 100 firms, representing industries such as utilities, transportation, insurance, and state government agencies, was generated. IS executives at these firms were contacted and informed of the study's objectives. The executives were asked to identify development projects that had been completed within the last twelve months, regardless of whether they were successful or not. Contact information about IS project leaders for the specific projects was also obtained.

Affiliated project managers were contacted and requested to participate in the study. Detailed descriptions of JAD and the traditional IS design methodology were given to the project managers to ensure the integrity of the independent variable. The project managers were also asked to identify users and designers that had key roles in the systems design process. Detailed cues were given to the project managers so that they would identify appropriate team members to respond to the questionnaire. Next, we mailed questionnaires to the identified project team members. The project managers' questionnaire gathered data about the project characteristics while the user and designer questionnaires collected information on the perceptions of the key team members. Data from all three sources were required to form a complete case of observations for this research. In all, data were gathered from 57 projects, with most organiza-

Items for measurement of the perceived effects of design approaches tions providing responses about a single project. 221 pairs of user-designer questionares were mailed and 94 pairs of responses were returned for a response rate of 42%.

To strengthen the validity of the survey, the designers were asked to indicate the methodology used during the project by checking one of the two options, or by briefly describing their methodology under a third category, titled 'other.' Of the 57 projects, designers circled 'other' for three projects. In these cases, follow-up telephone calls with the designers and project managers revealed that the design methodology fit the definition of the traditional design methodology and these responses were retained for the analysis. Data on project size, project development cost, and departmental scope were obtained from the project managers. Data on project complexity were obtained from designers by asking them to indicate, on a 5-point Likert scale, the relative complexity of the specific project compared to other projects that they had worked on in the past. Data on the designers' confidence, experience, and working knowledge of the development methodology prior to the starting of the project, were gathered through single-item responses from the IS designers. For measuring user and designer perceptions about the quality of interactions, effectiveness of consensus management, and user acceptance of design specifications, 11 items were generated from a review of previous claims about JAD. Some of the items were framed as negative statements to minimize the respondent bias toward agreement with every statement. Respondents were presented with a 5-point Likert scale, with cues ranging from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree.’ Table 2 illustrates the specific items used for the study.

Table 6 displays the MANCOVA analysis results that pertain to effects of the design methodology on designer and user perceptions. For designer perceptions, significant differences were observed across all three perceptions (p<0.10). Designers indicated that the JAD methodology facilitated higher quality user-designer interactions, more effective consensus management, and a better user acceptance of the design specifications. Thus, the analysis based on designers' perceptions appears to the hypothesized superiority of JAD over the traditional design methodology (Hypotheses H1b, H2b, and H3b). With respect to the users' perceptions, however, no significant differences were detected in their belief in the effectiveness of consensus management and its result on acceptance of the design system. However, users did think that JAD produced higher quality user-designer interactions compared with the traditional IS methodology (significant differences at p<0.05). Thus, support was found for hypothesis H1a, but not for hypotheses, H2a and H3a.

## 6. Comparisons between user and designer responses

Since the data were gathered from pairs of users and designers on specific projects, a paired t-test analysis was conducted to explore differences between user and designer perceptions of projects employing the

JAD and traditional design methodologies. Table 7 provides the results of the analysis. For projects employing the traditional IS design methodology, significant differences do not appear to exist between the users' and designers' perceptions. In fact, on the quality of user-designer interactions and effectiveness of consensus management, significant positive correlations existed between the users' and designers' perceptions. These results indicate that both users and designers shared similar perceptions about effects of the traditional IS design methodology on the design process. For the JAD methodology, significant differences were observed between perceptions of users and designers across all three dependent variables. Overall, the designers had more favorable perceptions of JAD than did the users. Significant negative correlations found between user and designer perceptions imply the existence of differences in their perceptions of the effects of JAD and the traditional IS design methodology.

The two sets of analyses suggest that designers and users do not share similar levels of favorable perceptions about JAD. Our sample suggests that designers were less confident with the JAD methodology and possessed lower levels of experience and working knowledge than with the traditional IS design methodology (Table 4). Further, we can assume that designers' level of confidence with the design method was perceived by the users, and had a significant effect on users' perceptions of the beneficial value of the design methodology (Table 5). Taken together, these results suggest that lower levels of designer confidence with the JAD method may be one reason for differences in designer-user perceptions about the effects of the method.

Several reasons could be responsible for designers' lower levels of confidence. First, JAD is a complex methodology, requiring skills at planning, communicating, negotiating, and learning. While these skills are just as important in one-on-one interviews, designers are more likely to be able to cope with deficiencies in these skills in one-on-one interviews. Second, designers do not get to exercise and develop their skills at facilitation very often. JAD is usually employed on new development projects which are relatively rare and most often lengthy in project time. It can be years between projects when a designer can use JAD, let alone the span of time since formal training. Third, Davidson [9] found that designers often bend the advocated rules of JAD; for example, by shortening sessions to several hours, not involving actual system users in the JAD sessions, not training business area personnel on useful techniques, and continuing to use modeling and process techniques that are difficult for users to understand. Modifications to design norms and outcomes can be expected to be limited under these conditions.

## 7. Discussion and conclusion

User participation is one of the dominant concerns in improving the nature of systems development processes in organizations. In this context, joint application design has been touted as overcoming the limitations of the traditional IS design methodology. While a variety of claims has been made about the superiority of the JAD approach, limited attempts have been made to verify these claims. This research study sought to examine the effects of the traditional design and the JAD approaches on the quality of user-designer interactions, effectiveness of consensus management, and user acceptance of design specifications. To facilitate comparison of effects, the study included key project characteristics and designers' confidence, experience, and working knowledge with the methodology as control variables and tapped both the designers' and users' perceptions about the design process and outcomes.

Overall, the results provide only limited support for claims made about the perceived superiority of JAD over the traditional design methodology. Both users and designers agreed that the JAD methodology promoted greater and richer interactions (user participation and influence, partnerships, and good communication) among participants in the systems design process. In addition, designers perceived JAD to be superior in promoting effective consensus management and user acceptance. However, the users did not perceive any significant differences between the two methodologies on these two factors. Our analysis also revealed that designers were less confident with the JAD methodology and reported having less experience with and working knowledge of the JAD methodology, compared with the traditional IS design methodology. While designers perceived JAD to be beneficial despite their lower levels of confidence with the methodology, users appeared to be more skeptical of the methodology.

We speculate that different dynamics underlie users' and designers' perceptions about the effects of systems development methodologies. From the designer's point of view, JAD shortens the time required to gather information and enables them to transfer some of the responsibility for communication and negotiation to the users. Using one-on-one interviews necessitates designers to shoulder the responsibility of resolving conflicts in stakeholder design specifications and filling in any missing design specifications.

Users could be disenchanted with JAD for several reasons. First, it adds responsibility for communication and negotiation that occurs in JAD sessions which require users to confront differences in their expectations and collectively arrive at a shared view of the design specifications. Second, IS designers were not comfortable in facilitating JAD sessions which require the facilitator to be experienced in a variety of creativity and conflict management techniques. Third, previous research has shown that as JAD is implemented, it is usually adapted to the status quo, limiting the potential for substantial benefits. Many ISD organizations could be adopting JAD to enhance legitimacy more than to benefit from tangible improvements. Finally, while JAD can reduce the time span required to gather information, the sessions usually require larger time commitments from users. This added commitment could be resented and viewed as wasteful by users.

In conclusion, the eventual success of JAD will require gaining the same degree of enthusiasm and favorableness of perceptions from users as designers. This is particularly important since the success of the JAD approach depends upon active user participation and commitment.
