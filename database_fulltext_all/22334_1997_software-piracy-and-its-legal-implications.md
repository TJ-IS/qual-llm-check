# Applications

# Software piracy and its legal implications

Clifford M. Koen Jr $^{a}$ , Jin H. Im $^{b,*}$

$^{a}$ Department of Management, University of New Orleans, New Orleans, LA, USA $^{b}$ University of New Orleans, New Orleans, LA, USA

## Abstract

Software piracy is a pervasive phenomenon in the wake of the proliferation of microcomputers in the end user computing environment. Software vendors and the software vendors' trade association, the Software Publishers Association (SPA), have been very aggressive in the US in pursuing infringements of copyright, with strong legislative support from the U.S. Congress. Unwittingly, an organization may be exposed to the risk of being held liable for copyright violations made by its employees.

This paper discusses (1) types of software piracy, (2) organized anti-piracy campaigns, (3) forms of intellectual property protection for software, such as trade secrets, patents, licensing, copyrights, civil liability, and criminal liability, and (4) several cases of lawsuits and settlements. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Software piracy; Copyright act; End user computing; Microcomputers; Civil liability; Criminal liability

## 1. Introduction

U.S. software manufacturers have vigorously fought the problem of software piracy since the mid-1980s [1, 4, 12]. In 1994, piracy in the US is estimated to have cost the software industry an estimated \$1.05 billion in lost revenue. This is a decline of nearly 50 percent from the estimated \$2.08 billion lost in 1993 and the first decline since the industry began estimating average annual losses of more than \$2 billion in 1990 [13]. While industry efforts are paying off, the problem remains pervasive.

## 2. Types of software piracy

'Software piracy' has, to a great extent, become a generic term for the illegal duplication of copyrighted computer software. General use of the term 'piracy' encompasses what can be seen as three distinct categories of loss: (1) commercial piracy; (2) corporate piracy; and (3) softlifting. Commercial piracy refers to the illegal duplication of software for the purpose of distribution and sale. Corporate piracy rarely involves copying software for direct financial gain; typically, it takes the form of a piece of software being passed around the office and placed on multiple hard drives or copied onto a file server which is accessed by many people. Thus, a corporation will have purchased only one or a few copies of a program, yet dozens or hundreds of employees will be using copies of that program. This category of piracy encompasses the activities of educational institutions and government entities as well as corporations and businesses. The final category is softlifting, the software equivalent of shoplifting. When a person copies a friend's software or brings a copy home from work for personal use, softlifting occurs. This form of piracy is not intended for direct financial gain and is incorrectly believed by many to be legal.

## 3. Organized anti-piracy campaigns

Since 1989, the Software Publishers Association (SPA, home pages: http://www.spa.org and http://www.spa\_europe.org), the principal trade association of the personal computer software industry, has conducted a number of highly publicized raids and copyright enforcement actions against a variety of organizations. A toll-free hotline (800-388-7478) encourages company insiders and former employees to report corporate piracy. In 1994, SPA averaged nearly 30 phone calls per day. Based on leads received from their anti-piracy hotline, in 1994 the SPA took action against 447 organizations. The actions taken included 197 audits and lawsuits resulting in the payment of \$2.7 million in penalties, bringing the total received from the anti-piracy campaign, which began in 1989, to almost \$14 million. Ninety-five percent of these audits and lawsuits involved corporations; the remainder were bulletin board services and educational institutions. In addition, SPA sent out 250 cease and desist letters asking organizations to voluntarily destroy illegal software and purchase legal copies [3].

A vast majority of the SPA's investigations never reach the courts. Before pursuing costly litigation, the SPA attempts to perform voluntary company audits. If association representatives find pirated software, the offending organization must contribute to the SPA's anti-piracy fund in an amount equal to the value of the pirated software. Offenders are also required to destroy all unauthorized programs and repurchase a sufficient number of copies to ensure compliance with the law. According to Sandra A. Sellers, Director of Litigation for the SPA, “There are two primary reasons that companies want to cooperate: (1) they desire to avoid the bad publicity associated with being named as a defendant; and (2) voluntary audits and settlements are accomplished in a much more cooperative atmosphere and achieve the same end results as litigation.” $^{1}$

The SPA's success is in no small measure due to some unique legal weapons available to stop piracy. A copyright owner may file a petition in any district court, asking the court to seize, impound, and ultimately destroy any copy of a copyrighted work. If an adequate showing of copyright infringement is made out in the petition, the court may order a United States marshal to visit the accused infringer's place of business unannounced and seize the infringing copies and any means used to make the copies. A former SPA attorney has described one raid where SPA auditors and marshals were checking equipment on the first floor while company managers were hurriedly wiping out disks with illegal copies of software from equipment on the upper floors [9]. Such a defensive tactic is not effective, since the SPA can detect the software even if it has been deleted from the disk directory.

Overseas, anti-piracy activities are handled primarily by the Business Software Alliance (BSA, home page: http://www.bsa.org), an affiliate of the SPA. The BSA promotes the continued growth of the software industry through its international public policy, education, and enforcement programs in the US and more than 60 other countries. BSA represents major U.S. software companies including Lotus, Microsoft, and Novell. It operates a U.S. hotline (800-688-BSA1) and 34 others around the world for callers seeking information about piracy or reporting suspected incidents of software theft.

## 4. Forms of intellectual property protection for software

When a person or corporation purchases a copy of a software program, the purchaser has a personal property interest in the copy. That interest allows the purchaser to view, sell, or dispose of the copy at will. The personal property interest, however, does not give the purchaser the right to copy or make derivative works of the program. Those rights are retained by the person or corporation holding the intellectual property rights. Generally, developers of computer software seek legal protection for intellectual property by using traditional legal mechanisms found in copyright, trade secret, patent, and licensing. Of these forms of protection, the most easily attainable protection is through copyright law. Copyright law will be addressed following background information on each of the other areas.

## 4.1. Trade secret

State statutes and common law may be used to protect trade secrets embodied in computer software. Generally, a trade secret is any formula, pattern, device, or information used in the operation of a business to provide the business an advantage over competitors who do not know or use it [7]. The owner of a trade secret must take reasonable care to prevent it from becoming known to individuals other than those selected by the owner. To maintain this element of secrecy so that the software will be protected by trade secret law, software developers may use license agreements. The typical agreement will include a statement that the purchaser will not copy the program or disclose it to any third party without the developer's consent [10].

## 4.2. Patent

Patent law is designed to protect the idea behind an item, not merely the particular form in which the idea appears. A patent protects inventive advances in a technological process, a product, or a machine design. The holder of a patent has rights to exclusive use of the subject matter of the work for seventeen years. Patent protection affords more protection than copyright because it covers anyone selling, using, or making a claim of invention without the owner's permission. However, there are several factors that discourage developers from seeking patent protection. The patent application process is costly and time-consuming. The cost could easily reach several hundred thousand dollars, and it could take two to three years for issuance. Thus, patent protection is not appropriate for most software producing organizations – other than the largest ones [6].

## 4.3. Licensing

The scope of protection provided by a license agreement often varies with the manner in which the respective software is marketed. For example, in software licensing transactions involving custom software (direct sale software), a license agreement is usually negotiated and signed by both parties. These agreements are generally enforceable and typically include clauses restricting the purchaser/licensee in the use of the software and clauses designed to enhance trade secret protection. To protect mass-marketed software, where the sales transaction is relatively impersonal and the purchaser often has little or no contact with the software developer, shrink-wrap license agreements are used. This agreement, like any other license agreement, attempts to limit the buyer's use of the software and protects and enhances the developer's rights. The shrink-wrap license purports to bind the buyer of software when the package is opened. The license states that the buyer's opening of the software package constitutes acceptance of the terms stated in the agreement. Another form of license agreement has recently appeared where the terms of the agreement appear on the computer screen when the software is initially installed. The buyer is requested to verify that all of the terms in the agreement have been agreed to before the software is used. However, such 'agreements' are under attack in the US courts today. The shrink-wrap or screen-based license agreements may already be a dead issue unless action is taken by the Supreme Court or the US Congress.

License agreements emerged because of the perceived inadequacy of copyright and trade secret protection. Their purpose is to retain software title in the developers, thereby giving publishers the right to determine the uses to which their software may be put. By retaining title, developers avoid the provisions of the Copyright Act's Section 117 that grants certain rights to modify and make backup copies to owners of software. Typical terms of a license include clauses that prohibit copying, rental, reverse engineering, modification of the software, and limit use of the software to a single central processing unit. The purchaser/licensee is also assumed to disclaim all warranty protection and agrees to limits on legal remedies that might otherwise be available. This amalgamation of various clauses would appear to preserve some if not all of the interests important to the software developer. Federal patent and copyright laws may sufficiently protect the interests of the software developers, especially in light of recent changes in the Copyright Act of 1976. $^{2}$

## 4.4. Copyright

Simply stated, U.S. copyright law makes it illegal to make or distribute copies of copyrighted material in the US without authorization. The Copyright Act enumerates both the copyright owner's exclusive rights and the limitations on those rights. The author of copyrighted software has five exclusive and separate rights: the right to (1) reproduce the work; (2) adapt or make derivative works; (3) publicly distribute copies; (4) publicly perform the work; and (5) display the copyrighted work. $^{3}$ These rights are protected for the life of the author plus 50 years if the author is a person. If the author is a corporation, if the work is an anonymous or pseudonymous work, or if it is a ‘work made for hire,’ the rights last for 75 years from publication or 100 years from creation. $^{4}$

The rights of the copyright owner are limited in several important ways. The purchasers of a copy of a copyrighted computer program may make an archival copy (one copy to keep as a backup copy) of the program or adapt the program to their own specific needs, if they do so solely for their own use. $^{5}$ Also, ‘the fair use’ doctrine, most applicable to educational institutions, allows the program to be copied for purposes of teaching, research, or scholarship without constituting infringement. $^{6}$ The Copyright Act is capable of delivering a powerful one-two punch to those who violate its provisions. A civil lawsuit may be filed by the copyright owner. In addition to civil liability, the infringer may also face criminal prosecution.

## 4.5. Civil liability

If the copyright owner decides to sue in civil court, there are a number of pre-trial and post-trial remedies that are at the plaintiff's disposal. Sections 502 through 505 of the Copyright Act provide a variety of remedies to stop the infringement, compensate the plaintiff, and punish the defendant. The Act provides that a court in a copyright case may grant temporary or permanent injunctions to prevent or restrain infringement. In general, issuance of an injunction requires that the plaintiff be able to show the court some combination of four factors: a reasonable likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits of the case; the threat of irreparable harm to the plaintiff if the injunction is not granted (e.g. the creator's loss of control over the work or the possibility of future infringements); a balancing of the hardship to the plaintiff in the absence of an injunction against the hardship to the defendant if the injunction is granted; and the effect on the public interest by the grant or denial of the injunction [5]. Generally, these four factors are easily found in the piracy context.

The Act also gives the court power to issue impoundment orders at the pre-trial and post-trial stage. The court may also order the destruction or other reasonable disposition of all copies made in violation of the copyright. If read literally, the statute authorizes a court in a software copyright case to impound the defendant's entire computer system on the day the case is filed. Section 504 of the Act provides a copyright plaintiff a choice of either the traditional legal remedy of an award of actual damages or the special copyright remedy of statutory damages. The decision of which remedy to seek need not be made at the time of filing of the suit. If a plaintiff chooses to receive actual damages, the award will usually include any profits of the infringer that are attributable to its piracy. Actual damages are measured by lost sales due to illicit duplication of the copyright holder's computer programs.

If the choice of damages and profits proves to be insufficient, a copyright holder may choose at any time prior-to-final-judgment to collect statutory damages. The foundation of the statutory damages provision lies in the recognition that the nature of copyright is such that proof of actual damages is often difficult or impossible to provide. Statutory damages generally range from 500 to 20,000 for all infringements with respect to any one work. $^{7}$ Repeated infringement of the same copyright (i.e. multiple pirated copies of the same program) counts as a single instance of infringement. Thus, a plaintiff may collect statutory damages for each program that is pirated by an infringer, and not for each instance of piracy.

It is a judge's discretion to determine the amount of damages assessed for each infringement. To determine damages in any given instance, courts have relied upon such factors as: profits reaped by the infringer, expenses saved by the infringer, revenues lost by the copyright holder, value of the copyright, deterrent effect on future infringers, and the culpability of the infringer. If the infringement is shown to be ‘willful,’ the court may, in its discretion, increase the award of statutory damages to as much as \$100,000. $^{8}$ Thus, an infringer who willfully pirates ten different computer programs is liable for up to \$1 million in statutory damages – an amount sufficient to gain the respect of anyone contemplating piracy. The final remedy provided by copyright law is the award of costs and attorney’s fees to the prevailing party. $^{9}$ The potential award of attorney’s fees is a powerful incentive to plaintiffs to litigate all possible claims against the infringer.

What may be the most critical aspect of damages in software cases is contained in Section 412 of the Act. This section prohibits an award of statutory damages or attorney's fees where the infringement commences before the effective date of registration of the copyright. Thus, a delay in registering the work until after the infringement commences could result in a less than acceptable award, since the only remedy available to the copyright holder would be recovery of actual damages.

## 4.6. Criminal liability

In addition to civil liability, a copyright infringer may also face criminal prosecution under the provisions of the Copyright Act. Under Section 506(a) of the Act, any person who willfully infringes a copyright for purposes of commercial advantage or private financial gain is punishable by a maximum fine of \$25,000 and up to one year in prison. In addition to this general criminal provision, other recently enacted US legislation specifically addresses commercial software piracy.

In 1992, the Copyright Act was amended by the Copyright Felony Act which elevated software piracy to a felony. $^{10}$ Prior to this amendment, only unauthorized copying of sound recordings, motion pictures, or audiovisual works was a federal felony. Primarily because of the problem of large-scale computer software piracy, the scope of the Copyright Act was broadened to protect all copyrighted works, and the numerical thresholds for felony sanctions were lowered. The 1992 amendments to the statute establish more stringent penalties. First offenders convicted under the Copyright Act may be imprisoned for up to five years and/or fined up to \$250,000 for an individual and \$500,000 for an organization, or twice the gross gain from the offense. If the offender has been convicted under the statute previously, the maximum prison sentence increases to ten years. $^{11}$

A felony offense has occurred where an infringer has reproduced or distributed, within a 180-day period, at least ten unauthorized copies or phono records of one or more copyrighted works with a collective retail value of more than \$2500. For any copyright infringement failing to meet the numerical thresholds for a felony, the Copyright Act prescribes a misdemeanor sentence of up to one year. To obtain a conviction under the Copyright Felony Act, the government must first establish that an act of copyright infringement has occurred. A defendant must be proven guilty by proof beyond a reasonable doubt. In a criminal infringement proceeding, the elements to be proven are the same as those that must be proven by a preponderance of the evidence in a civil copyright infringement action. To establish a prima facie claim of either civil or criminal copyright infringement, two basic elements must be proven: (1) ownership of a valid copyright in each infringed work; and (2) 'copying' by defendants (or a violation of another of the exclusive rights provided to a copyright owner by the Copyright Act). Unlike civil copyright infringement which requires no intent, for a conviction on charges of criminal copyright infringement, the government must prove a specific criminal intent to infringe (i.e. the act must be done willfully). Thus, it is not possible to incur criminal liability innocently. Without the requisite intent, no criminal violation has occurred, even if the number of unauthorized copies reproduced or distributed is significant [11].

Although both the Copyright Act and the Copyright Felony Act use the term ‘willfully’ in describing criminal copyright infringement, the term remains undefined by statute. The definition of the term has been left for the courts to determine. In the context of criminal copyright infringement, the courts have interpreted the term ‘willfully’ in two ways. A majority of the courts interpret the Copyright Act as making criminal copyright infringement a ‘specific’ intent crime; that is, a prosecutor must prove that the alleged infringer specifically intended to violate the copyright law. A minority view, followed by the Second and Ninth Circuits, holds that in the context of copyright infringement, ‘willful’ means only intent to copy, not intent to infringe. This view places a substantially lower burden on prosecutors, and, as one might suspect, most criminal copyright infringement prosecutions occur in the circuits that have adopted this view.

## 5. Enforcement: Lawsuits and settlements

Enforcement efforts of the SPA, the BSA, and in some cases, independent actions by software manufacturers have achieved some significant results. Some of the most aggressive anti-piracy efforts have come from the world's largest software manufacturer, Seattle-based Microsoft Corporation. Microsoft attorneys and investigators have actively worked with federal prosecutors to generate several criminal indictments for software piracy. Defense attorneys have complained "that Microsoft is too cozy with the feds, encouraging criminal prosecutions even when the company has bled an adversary dry in civil litigation." One Microsoft investigator has summed it up with one statement: "This is a war and a war Microsoft intends to win." [8].

The SPA has been extremely successful with its enforcement efforts. Since 1989, of the more than 200 lawsuits filed by the SPA, all were settled out of court except for one. Companies are not interested in going to court. After the evidence of piracy has been discovered, they realize that litigation will serve no purpose. The only question remaining is how much money the company is willing to pay for its wrongdoing. In the only lawsuit filed by the SPA that ever proceeded to trial, a federal court ruled in favor of the

SPA members. The civil copyright infringement suit, decided on March 28, 1995, was against Global Software and Accessories, Inc. for unauthorized rental of computer software. $^{12}$

Recent settlements negotiated by the SPA illustrate the continued success of enforcement efforts. In 1991, the SPA received its largest public settlement to date in an action against Parametrix, Inc., an environmental engineering firm. The company paid \$350,000 plus attorneys' fees in settlement. The largest confidential settlement totaled nearly \$500,000. $^{13}$ That same year the first software copyright infringement suit was brought against a public institution of higher education. In a settlement with the University of Oregon, the University agreed to pay the SPA \$130,000 and agreed to organize and host a national conference on copyright law and software use. $^{14}$

A recent SPA-negotiated settlement typifies enforcement efforts to combat software piracy. SPA received a call on its anti-piracy hotline that Harper Hospital, based in Detroit, Michigan, was allegedly using unauthorized copies of software. After further investigation and in lieu of filing a lawsuit, SPA asked the hospital to conduct a full audit of its installed software, and Harper complied. According to a hospital official, “SPA offered us a chance to work cooperatively to investigate and resolve the situation. This collaborative approach was beneficial for both SPA and Harper Hospital. Before undertaking the audit, we believed that our existing policies and procedures were adequate to prevent the use of unauthorized software. Upon undertaking the audit, we discovered that we were not as diligent as we needed to be.... This certainly was a learning experience for us....” The audit revealed unauthorized copies of software programs published by 38 SPA member companies. The settlement was for an unannounced six-figure amount. $^{15}$

News releases from the Business Software Alliance tout similar victories against software piracy. For example, recent settlements include: October 1994 – \$260,000 paid by Utica Enterprises, Inc., a leading auto parts manufacturer in Detroit; and January 1995 – \$200,000 paid by Louis Berger International, Inc., a civil engineering firm in New Jersey. In both cases, an investigation began after a call was made to the BSA anti-piracy hotline. In February 1994, as a result of a surprise raid of the computer training center at Colorado Free University in which unlicensed software was found on classroom computers, the school agreed to pay \$60,000 to settle claims of copyright infringement. The school also agreed to purchase original software programs to replace the illegal copies. Bob Kruger, BSA’s Director of Enforcement, said that “... schools using software as a teaching tool need to be especially careful that they do not send the wrong message about software piracy to their instructors and students.” $^{16}$

In 1987, a federal court in California rendered a decision $^{17}$ that caused great concern among software publishers [2]. The plaintiff, a California software company, sued the University of California at Los Angeles for \$70,000 alleging infringement of seven copyrighted computer programs. In ruling for the university, the court held that the sovereign immunity clause of the Eleventh Amendment protects states and state agencies from being sued under copyright law. The court's decision was upheld by the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals. $^{18}$ The decision dealt a severe blow to the software industry. The ruling in effect allowed states to violate federal copyright laws with virtual impunity.

The U.S. Congress responded with bills designed specifically to overturn the case. With broad backing by the publishing industry, the Copyright Remedy Clarification Act $^{19}$ was enacted in 1990. ## 6. Conclusion

Though there is no way to avoid it entirely, a clear understanding of the nature of software piracy and its consequences is the first step in minimizing the risk involved.
