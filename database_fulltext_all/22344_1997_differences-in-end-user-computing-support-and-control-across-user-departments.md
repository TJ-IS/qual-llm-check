# Differences in end-user computing support and control across user departments

C. Speier $^{1,a}$ , C.V. Brown $^{b,*}$

$^{a}$ College of Business Administration, University of Oklahoma, 206A Adams Hall, Norman, OK 73019, USA $^{b}$ School of Business, Indiana University, 801 W. Michigan St., Indianapolis, IN 46202-5151, USA

## Abstract

Although the diversity of end-users is well recognized in the end-user computing (EUC) management literature, a potentially important context factor remains little addressed: user department variables. The objective of this exploratory study was to identify variables at the department level that may be important for explaining differences in usage of support sources and awareness of control policies at the individual level. Semi-structured interviews were held with user managers in three functional departments and information systems (IS) managers within the same organization. The interview data were then used to customize questionnaires for end-users on the usage of EUC support sources and awareness of EUC control policies in the same three departments. Significant differences were found across the departments. The interview data suggest that, while the business activities of the department may explain some of these differences, three other department-level variables appear to be influential: (i) the department manager's preferences for EUC support sources, (ii) the department manager's perceptions of EUC risks, and (iii) EUC policies at the department level. Our findings imply that department-level context variables need to be taken into account in future EUC research, and a 'one size fits all' approach to EUC support and control may not be the most effective IS management approach in practice. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: End-user computing; Information center; IS controls; Support services; User-developed applications; Workgroup computing

## 1. Introduction

The growth of end-user computing (EUC) over the past decade has resulted in a large body of literature assessing organization- and individual-level factors important for effective end-user computing. The wide diversity of end-users and end-user applications is well recognized in this literature. Little addressed, however, is an important context factor: the user department. Just as individual employees vary in their EUC tool and management support needs, not all departments in the same firm may use EUC tools for the same tasks and have the same EUC support and control needs.

Some differences in EUC support and control needs may be predicted by the role of the department in the organization; that is, the specific business tasks for which applications are developed by end-users within that department. For example, end-users in a finance department may make heavy use of spreadsheets to perform what-if scenarios on financial information, while end-users in a manufacturing department may make heavy use of database tools for monitoring inventory. However, other variables unrelated to the departmental role may also be influential. For example, facilitating conditions, such as management support, and social factors, such as the influence of managers on peers, have been found to affect end-user tool acceptance and utilization [38, 39]. Some recent researchers have called for support and control functions to be placed in the hands of departmental managers to facilitate more effective EUC management [25, 36], yet a 'one size fits all' EUC management approach reportedly characterizes most information centers [30]. As firms move toward client-server environments, the need for a more thorough understanding of influential department variables is expected to become critical.

This article reports on an exploratory study designed to investigate potential differences in EUC support and control across departments within the same firm. Although other EUC management research has examined differences across workgroups in multiple organizations, our inquiry focuses on EUC support and control differences at the department level within the same organization.

## 2. Prior relevant research

Based on a review of end-user computing management literature, Brancheau and Brown [6] proposed a research framework for EUC management that includes four components (Figure 1). Support services delivered by the information center (IC) and organization-level policies for PC purchases are management action variables at the organization level. The business task for which an end-user uses a tool to develop an application is an individual-level factor. The outcome factors are modeled at the application, individual end-user, workgroup, and organization levels, and the antecedent component includes both external and internal context factors. The user department is a context variable classified as part of a workgroup factor. These authors suggest that an end-user's workgroup is an important contextual factor that has received limited empirical examination in previous EUC research. They also specifically point to the lack of knowledge about EUC management actions initiated by user department managers.

No published studies that specifically address departmental differences within the same organization are known to exist, but a few individual-level studies have explicitly acknowledged user department differences. For example, Brancheau and Wetherbe [7] explicitly controlled for department by research design; their sample of end-users of spreadsheet software was only from finance and accounting departments. Goodhue and Thompson [19] tested for potentially important differences in support services at the department level by using a dummy variable, and found that an individual's department is a significant predictor of task/technology fit. Only one published study is known to have investigated application differences due to functional department, and it is a decade old: Lee [27] reported that PC-based spreadsheet applications were associated with users in finance departments, while mainframe programming applications were associated with technical departments.

However, a recent descriptive study $[29]$ which identified 27 different types of applications associated with EUC from single respondents in 74 organizations suggested that PC-based applications still vary widely across departments. For example, engineering departments are likely to make heavy use of CAD/CAM, manufacturing departments make heavy use of process control systems, and other departments would have little or no use for these application types. On the other hand, other researchers $[23]$ have suggested that end-users in different functional areas are developing applications that perform similar types of business tasks (e.g. planning, budgeting, forecasting, trend analysis).

Thus we found some empirical evidence to support the notion that user-developed applications can differ across departments in the same organization, and that some of these differences can be explained by the business activities of a given department. Of interest for this study is whether or not differences in business activities fully explain differences that might exist in EUC support and control across departments. Lee [27] suggested that an additional influential variable may be a Departmental Manager's perceptions about EUC support needs and EUC policies, which may differ from a corporate IS manager's views.

## 2.1. Prior research on EUC support

Brancheau and Brown defined EUC support as organization-level actions for ‘managing the development of software applications by end-users’ and identified 13 support services from prior literature. A few studies that investigate EUC support variables have compared end-user workgroups, typically defined as end-users under the same manager or supervisor. For example, Lepore et al. [28] investigated variables associated with the quality of worklife across 38 workgroups in 10 companies and found that quality of worklife is associated with the availability of adequate support. Winter et al. [40] investigated variables associated with computer literacy across 77 workgroups and found that the presence of a 'local expert' resulted in higher computer literacy, but the presence of organization-level support and training did not. EUC support from within an end-user department or another non-IS unit has also been identified as an important source of support by other researchers [5, 8, 16].

More common, however, is research that focuses on the facilitation of EUC from an overall organization perspective. For the most part, this research describes a 'one size fits all' approach to EUC support at the organization level with no workgroup context variables captured. Most studies examine EUC support provided by a centralized unit – usually, the IC – that typically provides a range of services, including basic PC software and hardware acquisition support, software training, some type of application consulting, etc. (e.g. [9, 11, 14, 22, 31]). Recent researchers have suggested that the IC approaches generally do not take into account differences among users when designing and providing support services [30]. The same criticism appears to hold true for differences among user departments.

In summary, prior research examining EUC support has emphasized centralized support services, typically delivered via a separate IC unit. However, this literature appears to present an incomplete picture of the sources of support available to an end-user. In particular, there is evidence to suggest that a department may sometimes encourage ‘local’ sources of EUC support. In some cases, a formal departmental support role may be implemented organization-wide, while in other cases it may be an informal, or more ad hoc support role that varies by department.

## 2.2. Prior research on EUC risks and control policies

Some of the earliest EUC management literature is concerned with the inherent risk associated with enduser application development and the need to develop EUC policies and procedures to minimize these risks. Models have been proposed to assess risks at the application level, based on the application development process [1], application usage [32], or both [18]. Schultheis and Sumner [35] found that larger applications tend to be riskier, and Sumner and Klepper [37] reported that the biggest risk associated with critical database applications was the loss of a user developer to another user department or organization.

Other researchers have documented the existence of organization-wide policies and the extent to which end-users were aware of them. Policies for hardware and software acquisition were among the earliest to be adopted [4] and continue to be the most widespread [29]. Other common policies include hard-disk backup procedures, security controls, and quality reviews for critical applications. Some researchers report that although organization-wide policies may be in place, few developers may be aware of them [20] or, if aware of them, may not follow them [12]. There is also evidence that peer influence is an important predictor of end-user attitudes about tools [15], and that informal group norms are good predictors for the adoption of ‘security-related behaviors,’ such as backup procedures and documentation development [13].

Very little research specifically addresses differences in EUC control across departments. Jenson [24] reported a linear relationship between the application's potential impact and the control environment – as espoused by Pyburn [32]. For example, an end-user developed application that assesses large capital investment options may carry higher risk than an internal sales department application that tracks current sales by customers for a single region. This suggests that the business activities of a department may predict differences in control policies. Again, Lee [27] suggests that Departmental Managers may perceive EUC risks differently, resulting in different policies across divisions.

In summary, organization-wide policies that are intended to influence individual end-users are common. However, there is some evidence that an individual end-user's awareness of an organization-wide policy cannot be assumed. Further, it is likely that a department may have its own EUC policies, based on the Departmental Manager's personal assessment of risks associated with EUC. There is also some evidence that workgroup norms and peer influence in general are good predictors of end-user attitudes and behaviors.

## 2.3. Research questions

The prior research suggests that differences in EUC support and control may exist across departments in the same organization, even in the presence of organization-wide policies and a centralized support unit, such as the IC. There is also some evidence that the business activities of a given department may explain some, but not all, of these differences. This suggests that context variables that capture EUC management variables at the department level may be important in explaining EUC differences across departments within the same organization. The research questions (RQ) to be addressed in this study are:

RQ1: How do EUC support sources and the usage of these sources differ across departments in the same organization?

1A: If there are significant cross-departmental differences, what context factors at the department level are potentially important predictors?

RQ2: How do awareness and management of EUC risks differ across departments in the same organization?

2A: If there are significant cross-departmental differences, what context factors at the department level are potentially important predictors?

## 3. Research method

Due to the lack of prior research that addresses departmental differences in EUC support and control, an exploratory field study was designed to collect data from different functional departments within a single organization using both interview and survey methods. On-site interviews were first conducted with the Director of IS and his IC Manager to understand the underlying context of EUC within the organization and to collect data on organization-level support services and policies. Then Departmental Managers were interviewed to better understand the functional department contexts. One of the Departmental Managers also provided access to the PC Coordinator in his department. A semi-structured interview format helped to make sure that influential department-level variables not in the research model could be elicited. Finally, end-users in the same departments as the sample of Departmental Managers responded to a customized questionnaire that measured application characteristics, their perceived benefits, usage of EUC support sources, and awareness of EUC policies. Two end-users were interviewed prior to distributing the questionnaire in order to ensure instrument validity with respect to appropriate terminology and completeness of alternative responses.

The Director of IS facilitated the researchers' access to the Departmental Managers. Three functional departments were selected. Two of the departments (Sales and Financial Operations) were those which the researchers preferred, based on prior work experience. The third department, Marketing, was selected because the Director of IS believed it to have a number of end-users with a great deal of application diversity. Although there is potential for selection bias on the part of the Director of IS, it would appear to be minimal. All of the Departmental Managers were interested in the research project and exhibited an interest in receiving our conclusions.

The Departmental Managers distributed the surveys to end-user subordinates in their department who met the definition of end-user, as discussed with them in the interview and described on the cover letter of the survey: developers of applications of specified types, using any combination of computer hardware and software, who were not employees of the MIS department. It is possible that not all departmental employees meeting this definition received a questionnaire due to Departmental Manager oversight or even selection bias. Although the questionnaire distribution was outside the control of the researchers, involvement by the Departmental Managers provided an ‘unofficial’ sanction for spending time to fill out the questionnaire, and was likely to increase the end-user response rate.

Semi-structured interview methods were used to gather information from the Director of IS, the IC Manager and Departmental Managers. The Director of IS described the overall vision and philosophy of IS support for EUC. The IC Manager was asked to provide an overview of the IS department and EUC within the organization, including a description of the user base, as well as data on the specific IC support services and control policies. Departmental Managers were asked to provide the number of end-users in their department as well as any EUC characteristics (e.g. tool requirements) that might be specific to their department. Departmental Managers also provided their perceptions of EUC risks within their departments, as well as data on any 'local' support roles and department-level EUC policies. In addition, follow-up phone-calls to the IC and Departmental Managers were placed after the end-user data had been collected; this helped us better to interpret the data.

A number of exhibits were used as part of the interview protocol to aid in the discussion of organization- and department-level variables, and to facilitate data comparisons across departments. Now we will describe the specific questions we asked, and the content of the exhibits. (Copies of the complete interview guide and exhibits are available from the first author.)

All managers and end-users were asked about the support services provided to end-users. Mirani and King [30] suggest that there is no commonly accepted measure of EUC support. The IC Managers and Departmental Managers were therefore shown a list of support activities that had been identified in previous research and were asked during their interview if a given activity was available at the IC or department level. These support activities include hardware and software selection/configuration, finding and accessing data, application development assistance, application maintenance, data backup, measuring cost/benefits, as well as availability of a helpdesk, software library, and newsletter (e.g. [10, 21, 34]). End-users were given the same list on the survey form, but were asked to indicate if they used these support activities and from whom they obtained the support. Response options for sources of support were: the IC, a departmental IS specialist, a peer inside the department, a peer outside the department, a vendor, or N/A (not applicable).

IC Managers and Departmental Managers were also asked to articulate any formal or informal guidelines that had been established for EUC. In addition, these managers were asked to address the perceived EUC risks that a given policy might address. Lists of potential EUC policies and risks were created from prior literature to facilitate this discussion. Policies identified in prior literature include: hardware and software selection/configuration, chargeback system for services, documentation standards, obtaining vendor support, data backup procedures, audit trails, application quality reviews, designated service level, and an evaluation procedure for services (e.g. [17, 33]). Potential risks identified in previous literature include: an inability to specify the system, users develop requirements without formal techniques, lack of end-user quality assurance, security of data, lack of documentation, lack of continuity when end-users leave, unauthorized use of equipment for personal reasons, and pilferage of hardware/software (e.g. [13, 26]). End-users examined the same list of EUC policies via the survey form and were asked to indicate if they were aware of the existence of a given policy, as well as how beneficial a given policy was with respect to developing applications.

To establish differences in EUC across the three departments, characteristics of the user-developed applications were also collected from end-users via the survey form. The application characteristics included hardware and software tools and the types of applications developed. When instances of software application packages were documented in an initial interview, the specific package (e.g. Lotus 1-2-3 and Excel) was given as an example in the survey. Finally, the IC and Departmental Managers were also asked to articulate the benefits of EUC at the individual, departmental, and organizational levels, and each end-user was asked to select the benefits obtained from developing applications at these same levels by responding to a list of specific benefits based on prior research [2, 3].

## 3.1. Sample description and analysis of end-user data

Three functional departments located at corporate headquarters participated in this research project. The primary role of the Sales Department was to devise and support the domestic and international salesforce. Types of end-user activities included sales representative compensation programs and the introduction of laptop computers into the salesforce. The Marketing Department had primary responsibility for merchandising of the company's products, product development, and developing programs that provide close contact with the firm's customers. The Financial Operations Department housed the controllers and was responsible for the financial operations of the organization. User-developed applications included those that monitor expenditures and budgeting applications. A total of 22 end-users participated: six from Sales, seven from Marketing, and nine from Financial Operations.

The end-user responses for each department were aggregated and differences between departments regarding policies to minimize risk were examined using chi-square and Kruskall–Wallis statistical techniques. The chi-square technique was used to test for differences across departments when the data were collected using pre-defined categories. The Kruskall–Wallis non-parametric technique was used to test for differences across departments when the data were collected using interval/continuous data. Given the exploratory nature of this study, a significance level of 0.10 was used in the tests for statistical significance.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Organizational context and end-user base

The organization in which this field study was conducted was a large manufacturing company which was termed as CBB. As a leading manufacturer of products in a well-established industry, CBB set itself apart from its world-wide competition by the innovative use of sales support and customer service arrangements. Its primary manufacturing operations and corporate headquarters were located in a midwestern community in the US (housed in different facilities). In addition, there were a number of distribution centers located across the country as well as regional and divisional sales offices. The local manufacturing and corporate base employed approximately 3500 individuals. However, CBB corporate headquarters supported the IS and end-user computing needs for all the satellite operations.

All IS personnel were centralized in a corporate MIS department. The Director of IS reported to the Chief Financial Officer of CBB. The IC was one of five units of the MIS department; the IC Manager reported directly to the IS Director.

EUC had been active at CBB since 1982. The IC Manager indicated that benefits such as better decisions, quicker response time, and more control over information had been mentioned by users. However, EUC benefits were not formally assessed by the IC; no formalized cost/benefit analysis was performed at the company level to assess the impact of user-developed applications on the organization.

The IC support staff peaked at four individuals in the late 1980s. At the time of data collection, the IC consisted of the IC manager and one consultant, supporting a base of approximately 500 users. Some of the EUC management support functions had been supplanted by outsourcing to another organization or to other company (non-IS) functions. According to the IC Manager, the staff was as ‘streamlined as it can be.’

The support services provided by the IC to end-users are listed in Table 1. The five hardware and software support services were viewed as primary IC support activities. Even though two of these services were outsourced (hardware setup/configuration and software installation), the IC managed them; formal requests for these services were initiated by the end-user to the IC. The IC also provided formal training programs; users enrolled in courses on specific operating systems, application software and communications. The remaining application support and other support activities were viewed as secondary services provided by the IC. These activities were provided on an ad hoc basis when requested by a user and ample resources were available. For two of these activities (i.e. accessing corporate data, assistance with finding data) the IC played a facilitation role and channeled the user to the most appropriate source of support. For example, the IC personnel authorized user access to the client-server network, but a different IS unit worked with individual users to provide access to specific data.

A number of related support services was also provided outside the IC. For example, CBB's strategic vendors offered technical fairs to increase user knowledge of hardware and software. Although the IC previously published a newsletter, information regarding EUC was being published in a company-sponsored newsletter.

EUC activity at CBB had reportedly been bolstered by the role of local experts. The IC Manager cultivated relationships with capable users in each department when they contacted the IC for support. Over time, these users became 'PC Coordinators': designated users who provided local support to end-users as requested and were a communication liaison from the IC to end-users and vice versa. The PC Coordinator provided insight into potential problems and additional assistance that a user might require, or identified the need for new or different tools. This role was recognized by both the IC and Departmental Managers. However, it was not a formal part of the user's job description.

CBB had very few company-wide EUC policies and those that existed were unwritten. First, the IC Manager had established a list of approved vendors. The current CBB practice was for PC hardware and software purchases to be approved by the IC Manager (the actual expense is charged to the budget of the functional area that requested the purchase). Second, it was policy that all ‘mission critical’ data were to be backed-up and necessary procedures for accomplishing this process were taught. The IC Manager admitted, however, that this policy was ‘rarely done’ and not enforced. According to the IC Manager, recommended software back-up procedures were generally ignored, until important data were lost.

2A: Differences in task type

According to the Director of IS, CBB's philosophy regarding risks and associated policies was: “..we don't necessarily know or are concerned about stand-alone EUC applications...our concern is in the interface from one process to another...especially at the exiting point of the company.” Although applications that interface or produce information exiting the company were viewed as high risk, there was no formal policy requiring application development approval or a UDA quality review. Users, however, were ‘strongly discouraged to conduct this activity independent of MIS.’

CBB seemed to have a ‘laid back’ attitude with regard to EUC risks. The EUC risks ranged from end-users developing applications without knowing the proper tools and techniques, to the suggestion that the organization may suffer from a lack of continuity when an application developer leaves. The majority of potential risks that were discussed in the interview were acknowledged with ‘it happens, but not often’ or ‘it’s not a big deal.’

All three Departmental Managers had different visions about the centralized IC roles. The Manager of the Sales Department indicated that the individual who served in the PC Coordinator role in sales provided a great deal of support to his end-users. The Operations Manager was an active end-user developing his own applications and encouraged application development within his department. Users often approached the Operations Manager with needs (e.g. requests for access to specific company data) and the manager helped by obtaining the requirements. The Marketing Manager appeared to have the least day-to-day involvement regarding EUC activities in his department. This manager indicated that the IC provided solid support to the end-users, although he suggested that 'a lot more could be done with supporting users if the IC group had more time.'

## 4.2. Application tasks and benefits by department

Based on data collected from end-users, several differences in application characteristics existed across the functional groups. For example, two of the departments (Marketing and Operations) were significantly more likely to develop forecasting, budgeting, problem finding, and trend analysis applications (Table 2). Similarly, these two departments were significantly more likely to use spreadsheet tools than the third (Sales) department (p = 0.05). In addition, the Operations Department made heavy use of existing applications, while the Sales Department was more likely to use an application only once $p = 0.028$ . The Sales Department also often relied on an internal team (as opposed to an individual) to develop applications; a typical application would involve a decision support system directed at applying what-if scenarios to evaluate sales force compensation programs.

A follow-up interview with the Financial Operations Manager revealed that this outside source was a former member of the Operations Department who continued to provide informal application support after a promotion to a different department. These different patterns of support reported by end-users in the three departments appear to match the preferences for sources of support captured in the interviews with the Departmental Managers. The Sales Manager formalized the role of the PC Coordinator by openly encouraging users in his department to make use of the PC Coordinator's computing skills. The users in this department relied more heavily on departmental peer support than the other two departments. The Marketing Manager was a strong proponent of the usage of the IC, and users in this department had the greatest percentage of the IC support overall. Finally, users in the Operations Department obtained much of their support from a former member of their department. The Operations Manager encouraged this arrangement, thereby creating an informal norm for the department.

## 4.4. RQ2: Differences in awareness and management of EUC policies across departments

As with EUC support, the Departmental Managers also had different perceptions regarding EUC risks. The greatest concern of the Sales Manager was the entry of 'faulty data.' He was much less concerned with faulty programs or application/data backup. The Sales Manager also believed that a policy was not an effective means to deal with such risks; he was unaware of any formal IC policies, and believed that no additional policies designed to control end-user application development were needed at CBB. (Even the PC Coordinator in the Sales Department suggested that he was unaware of any policies developed by the IC to support EUC).

The Operations Manager also articulated a number of risks, but was primarily concerned with the impact of incorrect formulas entered into spreadsheets. He believed that this was the greatest risk to the department because of the difficulty associated with detecting and controlling this type of risk. The Operations Manager required that users in his department seek IS Department help for any application that interfaced with existing database files. In addition, users were restricted from performing direct updates to data residing on the mainframe. End-users were given 'as recent as available' data to manipulate within their developed applications.

The Marketing Manager believed that there was little or no risk involved with EUC. When provided with the list of risks previously identified in existing EUC literature, the Marketing Manager indicated that “those aren’t really risks....that is just not achieving 100% efficiency.” He further suggested that if a specific EUC application gets the job done, then there is no downside to EUC.

Table 4 presents the results from end-user responses on awareness of organization-level policies. The IC had developed formal hardware and software specifications and all purchases required the IC approval; in addition, CBB had a formal policy addressing equipment compatibility and an informal policy directed at backup procedures. The data suggest that end-users across all departments were generally aware of the IC policy regarding software purchases. However, there are significant differences across departments regarding awareness of the other three IC policies: end-users in the Operations Department exhibited high awareness of these policies, while users in the Sales Department were, generally, much less aware of their existence. These differences at the individual end-user level reflect the Departmental Managers' perceptions about the need for organization-level policies: the Operations Manager expressed concerns about policies and procedures that impact data integrity, while the Sales Manager discounted the role of organization-level policies.

## 5. Conclusions

This exploratory study set out to examine differences across functional departments in EUC support and the perception and management of EUC risks (i.e. EUC control). We assumed that functional departments can differ in user-developed application tasks and tool usage, and this assumption proved to be true at CBB. We also found significantly different usage of EUC support sources and EUC policy awareness across departments at CBB. Further, these differences appear to be best explained by department-level variables other than business activities.

As in the prior EUC literature, the IS management at CBB perceived the management of end-user computing to be an IC function. The IC provided a large number of activities to support user application development on both a formal and ad hoc basis. The IC at CBB had also developed a few policies directed at controlling EUC activity, and these policies ensured hardware and software compatibility within the organization. The policies were unwritten, but were perceived as formal policy by the IC management.

However, departmental norms that impacted the support and control of user application development had evolved within each department. The importance that the Sales Manager placed on the departmental support role of the PC Coordinator appears to have influenced the end-users' support sources in this department: end-users in the Sales Department relied more heavily on a peer within the department than end-users in either of the other departments. Similarly, the Marketing Manager was a strong advocate of the IC, and end-users in the Marketing Department made the greatest overall use of the IC. Finally, users in the Operations Department sought much of their EUC support from a peer outside the department. This support preference is related to the types of applications used in Operations, the relocation of a user-developer to another department within the organization, and the Departmental Manager's perception that this former employee was a source of effective support.

Differences in end-user awareness of EUC policies also reflected departmental norms. Users in the department where the manager believed formal policies were an ineffective means to deal with EUC risks were least aware of the IC policies. Users in the department whose manager perceived the greatest EUC risks were significantly more aware of policies. In addition, these users were aware of informal policies within the department that had been implemented by the Department Manager to better control and manage EUC. Although some prior literature suggests that end-users do not find policies beneficial, these policies were generally perceived to be beneficial to the end-users in this department. In fact, it would appear that policies that were designed to protect individuals and workgroups from computing risks may have contributed to the perception of greater EUC benefits by the end-users in this department.

As with any study, the contributions from this research must be evaluated with a recognition of the study's limitations. A small number of end-users provided data for this research project. In addition, only three functional departments in a single organization were sampled; this provides limited generalizability.

Nevertheless, our findings suggest that four department-level variables are potentially important predictors of differences in EUC support and control across departments: business activities, departmental policies, departmental manager preference for EUC support sources, and departmental manager perception of EUC risk. In Figure 1 presents the EUC management framework and Figure 2 builds upon and incorporates these variables into a research model. Our conclusion is that research on EUC support and control at the individual level needs to take into account both organization-level management actions as well as work-group context variables at the department level.

Our findings also have implications for EUC research and practice in general. For example, differences in perceived risk at the department level suggest that policies should be tailored for departments. Different support mechanisms and perhaps different chargeback mechanisms for centralized support services may also be appropriate. Although additional research examining departmental differences within the same organization is needed, our results suggest that as firms move toward client–server environments, customized solutions for each department may be the most effective approach to EUC management.
