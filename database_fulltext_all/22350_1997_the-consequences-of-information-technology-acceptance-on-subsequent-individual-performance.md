# The consequences of information technology acceptance on subsequent individual performance

M. Igbaria $^{a,b,*}$ , M. Tan $^{1,c}$

$^{a}$ Program in Information Science, The Claremont Graduate School, 130 East Ninth Street, Claremont, CA 91711, USA $^{b}$ Faculty of Management, Tel Aviv University, Ramat Aviv, Tel Aviv 69978, Israel

$^{c}$ Department of Decision Sciences, The National University of Singapore, 10 Kent Ridge Crescent, Singapore 119260, Singapore

## Abstract

As more information technology (IT) is deployed in organizations, it is important to understand its impact on individual performance and organizational productivity. Most past research has concentrated on identifying determinants of computer acceptance. This may be inadequate in determining the value and return on investment due to IT. Organizations are able to deploy IT more effectively if the consequences of its acceptance are obviously valuable. This study seeks to investigate the implications and consequences of IT acceptance by examining the relationships between IT acceptance and its impact on the individual user. The research model involves three components: user satisfaction, system usage, and individual impact. It is hypothesized that user satisfaction and system usage affect individual impact and that usage partially mediates the effect of satisfaction on individual impact.

A comprehensive questionnaire on computer acceptance was used to collect data from 625 employees of a large organization in Singapore. The results suggest that user satisfaction is an important factor affecting system usage and that user satisfaction has the strongest direct effect on individual impact. The results also demonstrate the importance of system usage in mediating the relationship of user satisfaction on individual impact. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Acceptance; Success; Satisfaction; Usage; Impact; Singapore

## 1. Introduction.

Due to the rapid growth of user computing in organizations, practitioners are compelled to investigate the impact of information technology (IT) acceptance on individuals' performance and productivity. Although many authors have helped to identify key causes of IT acceptance [15, 27], the literature lacks an examination of the consequences. The studies so far have restricted their focus to the determinants of IT acceptance.

There are apparently several indicators of IT acceptance. The most generally accepted measures are user satisfaction $[9, 10, 16]$ and system usage $[7, 17]$ . These measures have been selected as the appropriate indicators of IT acceptance in this study.

It is important to understand the implications and consequences of IT to the extent that the changes to an individual or to the social system can occur as a result of its acceptance or rejection. Rogers [26] suggests that instead of asking “What factors affect information technology acceptance?” as previous research has done, future investigation ought to ask “What are the impacts of accepting or rejecting information technologies?” IT acceptance, the main dependent variable in past work, should become a predictor of a dependent variable, the impact of IT acceptance. This study focuses specifically on individual impact, which refers to the influence of IT on the perceived performance of the individual on the quality of his or her decision making.

In spite of the importance of understanding IT impacts, they have received little attention. Some recent studies in the area (e.g. [19, 22]) have examined the impact of IT on the user's job satisfaction and quality of working life. Although such studies have been useful in identifying the broad nature and domains of the impact of new IT on individuals, they contain many contradictory and inconclusive reports about the consequences of its acceptance. Further, most of these studies use the 'soft' approach in their investigations, as they are based mainly on case studies. In this respect, additional work based on 'hard' or quantitative methods is needed to examine the consequences of IT acceptance. Therefore, this study seeks to examine the relationships between information technology acceptance and its impact on the individual users.

Although speculations about the impact of computers on the individual's social and work experience date back nearly two decades [21], a systematic body of literature about it has started to accumulate only recently. Many different empirical studies seem to suggest clearly that computer technology may have effects on the nature of officework, job satisfaction, and the quality of social and work life of the office worker. Turner [28] has reported that office worker's interactions with clients, perceived task environment, and well being were all seen to be influenced by the type of system interface used. Coates [6] and Kaye and Sutton [18] found that computerization had affected officework productivity, as well as the quality of work life of those involved in officework. Millman and Hartwick [22] found that middle managers believe that office automation has given them increased autonomy, more freedom to do their work, and have greater responsibility for the results of their work. Similarly, Bikson et al. [5] reported that the majority of users felt that their work had been enriched. Contradictory findings regarding the impact of the new technology on individuals have also been emphasized by Attewell and Rule [3].

The concern about these studies is that most seem to have been conducted primarily in the context of traditional mainframe environments. Further, most of the attention seems to have been focused on end-user satisfaction as the sole indicator of the system's success. However, in recent research, system usage is also noted as an indicator of computer acceptance. It is the primary variable through which IT may affect individuals' performance. Therefore, research is needed to examine the impact of both indicators of IT acceptance, as measured by system usage and user satisfaction. The study addresses the effect on an individual's productivity and performance at work.

## 2. The proposed model and hypotheses

Figure 1 presents the structural model of the relationships between IT acceptance and individual impact. The model has three variables: (1) user satisfaction (2) system usage and (3) individual impact. It was based on the theoretical perspectives provided by DeLone and McLean [8] and the conceptualization of consequences of innovations by Rogers. It is hypothesized that the degree of user satisfaction positively affects the amount of use. Additionally, that user satisfaction and system usage singularly and jointly affect individual impact. This suggests that user satisfaction and system usage are the determinants of individual impact and that system usage also partially mediates the effect of satisfaction on individual impact.

User satisfaction and system usage reflect the interaction of IT with the users. Both are frequently used as surrogates for evaluating IT success and both have occupied a central role in IT implementation research. The significance of attitudes in relation to organizational IS derives from the proposition of attitude theorists [11] who state that individual attitudes toward an object play an important role in influencing subsequent behavior toward it. They showed that people's actions and behaviors are related to their attitudes. Applying this notion to the investigation of computer technology acceptance, a number of researchers have found that user attitudes toward computerized IS are related strongly to their acceptance. The relationship between user satisfaction and system usage has been emphasized with respect to the theory of reasoned action, the computer technology acceptance model by Davis et al., and an empirical study [4]. Based on these, others have reported that behavior (i.e. usage) is affected by attitudes (i.e. user satisfaction). Since user satisfaction refers to one's attitudes toward a variety of factors that are related to IS services and products, it is proposed that the degree of user satisfaction affects the amount of use. Furthermore, DeLone and McLean propose that user satisfaction affects individual impact. It is proposed that users who are more satisfied with the system will report a greater level of individual impact on their job performance. Therefore, we state:

Hypothesis 1: User satisfaction affects system usage and individual impact.

System usage is a key variable in most of the theoretical frameworks in IT research literature focusing on the adoption of computer technologies by individual users. It is an important variable in IT acceptance as it appears to be a good surrogate to gauge the effective deployment of IS resources in the organization. Davis et al. and Thompson, et al. argue that usage is a necessary condition for ensuring productivity payoffs from IS investments. However, user satisfaction has direct and indirect effects on individual impact, partially through system usage.

The concerns over possible negative effects of computers at the macrosocial and organizational levels [30], as well as at the individual level, are not at all new in the IS literature. However, little consensus has emerged over the years regarding such effects, possibly due to the use of a wide variety of mutually incompatible research perspectives [25]. More recent social and IS research studies also seem to suggest that while IT may increase productivity, it can degrade the work and the social life of those who use it [24]. However, contradictory findings have been reported about the nature and direction of these effects of information technology on workers and their jobs. Zuboff [31] has argued that computerization has added intellectual content to work, whereas Kraut et al. have found that for individuals who use the system, the quality of work life became poorer and had less variety. On the other hand, Bikson et al. and Mittman and Hartwick reported that with the presence of office automation, middle managers found their job more enriching, and the quality of work life was enhanced. Kaye and Sutton claim that “the overall impact seems to have been positive for the majority of participants,” even as they acknowledge the possibility of both positive and negative impacts, while Rogers states that there are advantages and disadvantages of innovation, and researchers should examine the consequences of innovation.

The confusion created by such contradictory findings has been well articulated by Attewell and Rule. They argue that research evidence is ‘fragmentary and very mixed’ and that none of the studies mounted so far have been capable of yielding a persuasive and comprehensive view of the consequences of computer adoption. DeLone and McLean propose that system usage and user satisfaction are direct antecedents of individual impact. Therefore, we state:

Hypothesis 2: System usage has a direct effect on individual impact.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Sample and procedure

The data for this study were gathered by means of a survey questionnaire administered to a large government research and development organization in Singapore. The company agreed to participate in a comprehensive study of the factors affecting computer acceptance and its consequences. This company is 25 years old and has approximately 650 employees. It has a wide-area network connecting several sites. Every employee in the organization has a personal computer (PC). All PCs, UNIX workstations, and minicomputers are connected to the corporate network. All inhouse developed software is designed to run solely on Microsoft Windows, which is the standard user interface. Besides PCs, the staff also have UNIX workstations. Employees used some of the computerized applications that run on PCs, such as Electronic Telephone Directory, Leave Application Systems, Electronic Scheduling, and Electronic Mail.

A pilot survey was conducted to test the comprehensives, average time taken to complete the questionnaire and the ease of filling it. Ten participants from the organization were invited to take part of the pilot survey. They were comfortable with the time required to complete the survey and its content. The instruments used here were well established and have been validated and retested.

A survey was distributed through the organization's internal mail system and was addressed individually to each of the 625 employees. It was accompanied by a cover letter stating the nature and purpose of the study. Participation was voluntary, and participants were assured confidentiality. Top management lent its support and strongly encouraged participation in the study offering to publicize the results of the findings in the company's newsletter. The employees were requested to respond within two weeks. Approximately 3–4 weeks after the distribution of the survey, all employees were reminded to complete the survey. Completed surveys were received from 371 of the 650 employees (57.1% response rate).

In order to assess the possibility of non-response bias, a comparison of responses was conducted between early and late returns [2]: a multivariate analysis of variance was undertaken to determine whether differences in response time (early versus late) were associated with different responses. The test indicated no significant differences in any of the variables of interest (Wilk's $\Lambda = 0.71$ , $p = 0.69$ ). Thus non-response bias was not considered to be a problem.

Two hundred sixty-nine of the participants (73.5%) were men. Age ranged from 19–47; the mean age was 28.5 years (S.D. = 4.77). The majority of the participants held professional and non-supervisory responsibility (83%). Approximately two percent of the participants had polytechnic education and 64 percent were college graduates. The average length of service in the current organization was 3.9 years (with a S.D. of 3.6), and the tenure in their current job averaged 3.25 years (with a S.D. of 3.2).

The success of the sample is reflected in the non-significant differences between users in their functional areas, gender, and organizational and job tenure. Our analyses indicated that there are no significant differences between females and males and among users employed in different functional areas in terms of their user satisfaction level, system usage, and individual impact.

The scale is a measure of overall user satisfaction that includes a measure of the satisfaction of the extent to which computer applications meet the end-user's needs with regards to information content, accuracy, timeliness, format and ease of use. Each item was measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from (1) 'almost never' to (5) 'almost always.'

System usage. Based on several studies, two indicators of system usage were included in this study: (1) number of computerized applications used by employees; and (2) number of business tasks for which the system is used. Since our respondents used the system very frequently (approximately 96% at least once a day and 86.3% several times a day) and spent at least two hours on the system, we did not include frequency of use and time of use in our analyses.

The number of tasks involving the use of the computer was used to measure the extent to which respondents use a computer in their work. Eight tasks (e.g. planning, budgeting, writing reports, scheduling meetings, communication with others, making decisions, etc.) were included in the survey list and a 'yes/no' response was requested. The total was used as the indicator.

In terms of the number of applications used, the questionnaire listed sixteen different types of software applications, such as word processing, spreadsheets, database management, electronic mail, graphics, project management. The integration between microcomputers and host computers in the user computing environment brings more variety and diversity to the software applications available. In this company, users have a wide choice of software packages. Observation of the diversity of such end-user applications provides a good indication of overall system usage.

The participants were asked to indicate whether they personally used these applications. The number of applications (ranging from 0 to 16) was the overall measure. These are typical of the kinds of self-reported measures, particularly in cases where objective use and acceptance metrics are not available. Use logs were not available in the study since participants used different microcomputers and different applications for different tasks. Self-reported usage should not be regarded as a precise measure, although previous research suggests it is appropriate as a relative measure.

Individual impact. This was measured by perceived performance impacts, since objective measures of performance were unavailable in this field context. The four questions involved the perceived impact of computer systems on decision making quality, performance, productivity, and effectiveness of the job. Each was measured on a 5-point likert scale ranging from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree.

## 3.3. Data analyses

Partial Least Squares (PLS) testing was used. It is a second-generation multivariate technique that facilitates testing of the psychometric properties of the scales used to measure a variable, as well as estimation of the parameters of a structural model (i.e. the strength and direction of the relationships among the model variables) [20].

The test includes estimation of the reliability coefficients (composite reliability) of the measures, as well as an examination of the convergent and discriminant validity of the research instruments. In determining the appropriate minimum loading required for the inclusion of an item within a scale, we used Fornell's [12] recommendation to retain items that loaded highly (0.70 is considered to be high since the items explain almost 50 percent of the variance in a particular construct) on their respective constructs. Fornell and Larcker's [13] criterion that an average extracted variance should be 0.50 or more was used to assess the average variance extracted for all constructs. We also used the guidelines recommended by Hair et al. [14] in determining the relative importance and significance of the factor loading of each item (i.e. loadings greater than 0.30 are considered significant; greater than 0.40 are considered important; and 0.50 or greater are considered very significant). Finally, the criteria suggested by Nunnally [23] were applied to determine the adequacy of the reliability coefficients for each measure.

To assess discriminant validity of the measures (i.e. the degree to which items differentiate among constructs or measure different concepts), we examined the correlations between the measures of potentially overlapping constructs. If the items comprising an instrument that measures a construct correlate more highly with each other than with items measuring other constructs in the model, the measure is determined to have adequate discriminant validity.

The evaluation of the structural model was conducted with the total sample. The computer program used for this analysis was LVPLS 1.6 (Latent Variables Path Analysis using Partial Least Squares), developed by Lohmoller. To test the estimated path coefficients, t-statistics were calculated using a nonparametric test of significance known as jackknifing [29].

The path coefficient represents the direct effect of that variable on the endogenous variable. An indirect effect represents the effect of a particular variable on the second variable through its effects on a third mediating variable. It is the product of the path coefficients along an indirect route from cause to effect by tracing arrows in the headed direction only. When more than one indirect path exists, the total indirect effect is their sum. The sum of the direct and indirect effect reflects the total effect of the variable on the endogenous variable [1].

## 5. Discussion

The study examines the consequences of IT acceptance. Results indicate that computer acceptance has a significant effect on an individual's performance. The analysis provides strong support for the model. In particular, the results demonstrate the importance of examining user satisfaction in explaining individual impact. They also show that individual impacts are a function of both system usage and user satisfaction, which in turn show how IT adds value to individual performance. It should be noted that user satisfaction, an intrinsic variable, has a stronger effect on individual impact than system usage, an extrinsic variable. The effect of user satisfaction on individual impacts is partially mediated by system usage.

The results may suggest that system usage and user satisfaction are indicators of a performance impact. These results are consistent with prior research, which suggest that computer system acceptance (i.e. satisfaction and usage) may produce performance value and operational effectiveness. Our results show that IT acceptance helped individuals to accomplish their tasks more effectively and increased their productivity. The significant link between acceptance and impact has some implications for practitioners. The financial importance is obvious – when making investment decisions prior to purchasing IT, management should consider the impact of the proposed system on individual productivity and, eventually, on organizational profitability.

In addition, the results show that the individual impact is also affected by satisfaction. Thus, users who are satisfied with the system were more likely to report a greater level of use and, eventually, a greater level of individual impacts. In practice, organizations can indeed add value from the adoption of IT. However, in its adoption, the results seem to demonstrate the need for careful planning in creating and establishing a higher level of user satisfaction.

She is also the Chair of the Asian Assembly for the Decision Analysis Society of INFORMS. Recently she had been appointed an associate member of ORBICOM, Network of UNESCO Chairs in Communications. Her research interests include IT productivity, software quality, virtual workplace, internet diffusion and adoption, and the national information infrastructure in the Asia Pacific. She has published articles on communication behaviors of systems analysts, knowledge acquisition for expert systems, MIS personnel, and IS quality management in various journals. She is also on the editorial boards of various journals.
