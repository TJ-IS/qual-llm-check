# Applications

# Managing EDI for corporate advantage: A longitudinal study

F. Bergeron $^{a,*}$ , L. Raymond $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Département Systèmes d'information organisationnels, Faculté des sciences de l'administration, Université Laval, Québec G1K 7P4 P.Q., Canada

$^{b}$ Département des sciences de la gestion, Université du Québec à Trois Rivières, C.P. 500,, Trois-Rivières G9A 5H7 P.Q., Canada

## Abstract

This research attempts to identify the benefits that organizations gain by using EDI, the conditions under which these advantages can be attained, and their variation with time. Using a longitudinal design, 65 Canadian organizations were surveyed twice over a three-year period. In phase 1, factors such as the organizational support, the implementation process, the control procedures, and the integration of EDI were found to be positively related to the attainment of EDI advantages whereas, the imposition of EDI by business partners had a negative effect. In phase 2, the implementation process and the imposition of EDI lost their importance, imposition having only a positive effect on the organizational context. The reasons for these changes are discussed. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Electronic data interchange; Inter-organizational systems; Longitudinal study; IS management

## 1. Introduction

Electronic data interchange (EDI) is an application of information technology that allows business partners to send, receive, and process commercial documents (e.g. invoices, orders) electronically from computer to computer instead of by writing and mailing them. The number of organizations using EDI has grown rapidly in the recent years. Whole industries are now being integrated through this technology: witness for example, the Australian automotive industry [38]. Countries such as Singapore now provide EDI infrastructures to enhance their commercial effectiveness. Also, trading blocks such as the European community are orienting their policies to facilitate trade through EDI [35], including the use of

EDIFACT (Electronic Data Interchange for Administration, Commerce and Transport) as the international standard [20].

In order to secure the diffusion and effective use of EDI by a larger number of organizations, it is extremely important to understand various aspects of this technology. The advantages obtained from EDI and the conditions under which these advantages can be obtained are the most critical of these aspects $[17]$ . Our research identified the benefits that organizations attain by using EDI, the determinants of these benefits, and their variation over time. Indeed, unlike most research on EDI, this study elicits conclusions based on observations over a three-year period.

## 2. The advantages of electronic data interchange

EDI is considered to be a key technology for inter-organizational systems in the 1990s and the 21st century. Among its advantages are improvements in administrative costs and transaction costs, information quality, operations management, and some strategic advantage $[2, 6, 8, 42]$ . However, some researchers and consultants have questioned whether business and other enterprises have realized any significant cost savings or other durable benefits from implementing EDI $[23, 59]$ . Others have questioned the possibility of gaining any advantage from using this technology, particularly when the use of EDI is imposed by a business partner $[9, 16, 21, 36]$ .

The literature is also replete with descriptions of the various conditions that should be met in order to realize these benefits. Among the most frequently mentioned are the need for an adequate level of organizational support, the rigor of the implementation process, the presence of control procedures, and the integration of EDI with the firm's intra- and inter-organizational processes [10, 26, 30, 40, 58].

Questions remain, however, about the validity of these research results in the longer term. Recently, several empirical studies dealing with EDI have been published. Among these, very few, if any, have employed a longitudinal research design. Hence, there is insufficient knowledge about the evolution of EDI advantages over time. Many authors deplore the lack of longitudinal designs in organizational research and in IS research in particular [45]. As noted by Vitalari [60], IS researchers are confronted by certain difficulties, one of which is the inability to make statements about the nature and characteristics of longer-term effects of IS on a variety of dependent measures. In this sense, a longitudinal study of EDI is a significant contribution. Thus, the purpose of this study is to answer the two following questions:

1. What are the relationships between EDI success factors and the actual attainment of corporate advantages from this technology?

2. How do these relationships vary over time?

## 3. Research model

There are many ways to look at the conditions under which investments in IT (such as EDI) can impact organizational performance or the bottom line. Most researchers use ‘variance’ models, in which it is assumed that changes and change effects can be observed simultaneously, applying cross-sectional analysis. For example, Lucas [37] hypothesized that firm performance depends upon the design of IT, the technology itself, and its appropriate use. Business performance or value can be also viewed as the result of an appropriate combination of business strategy, IT characteristics, IT management, and IT impacts in the form of transformed business processes, improved products and services, or better information [52].

Others value ‘process’ theories instead of the more prevalent ‘variance’ theories [41]. Process models assume that change happens over time and does not influence or impact all aspects of an organization simultaneously. This approach is used by Beath et al. [5] to explain changes in business value due to IT. They argue that human relationships and technology assets leverage organizational processes (development cycle time, strategic alignment, and productivity of operations) which in turn improve human relationships and technology assets as a feedback loop. The final result is an increase in the use of the product or service by the client, and therefore the business value. The process approach is also advocated by Soh and Markus [56] who explain that organizational performance results from three consecutive and overlapping processes: the conversion process (of IT expenditures into IT assets), the use process (of IT assets into IT impacts) and the competitive process (of IT impacts into organizational performance).

Another way of explaining IT impacts is through the ‘alignment’ perspective. This approach is used by Bergeron and Raymond [11] and Chan and Huff [19] to explain why organizations are more effective when there is a fit between organizational strategy and IT strategy. A similar approach is used by Raymond et al.[46] to show performance implications from the match between information technology and structure.

Diffusion of innovation theory can also be used to explain IT impacts [50]. When applied to EDI in small firms [31], this theory argues that EDI impacts depend upon perceived benefits, promotional efforts, organizational readiness (including financial, technological and managerial assistance), external pressure (including coercive influences), and the EDI adoption and integration process.

The model adopted for this study is inferred from the literature and previous conceptual models. This approach was judged appropriate in theory construction (as opposed to theory testing), following Blalock's [12] argument. Indeed, given the relative newness of the EDI field and the research aims, it was difficult to rely on a reference discipline or to find an adequate existing theoretical framework that would allow the validation of the reported critical conditions of EDI success and their interrelationships.

The research model includes three independent variables: the imposition of EDI by business partners, the organizational context (comprised of organizational support, implementation process and control procedures) and the level of integration. There is only one dependent variable: the advantages of EDI (Figure 1).

## 3.1. Organizational context

There are three major elements of the organizational context of EDI. The first is the level of organizational support: the various elements put in place and activities conducted by an organization to assure the effective introduction of EDI, including personnel training, top-management support, and the presence of an organizational structure specific to EDI $[3, 15, 27]$ .

The second factor is the rigor of the implementation process: this includes aspects such as the planning approach to EDI adoption (e.g. prototyping or pilot project), testing and evaluation of initial transactions (e.g. in-house tests, error rate, transaction costs) [18, 54]. The last contextual factor is the presence of control procedures which are mainly data integrity, data security, and standards [1, 13, 28].

## 3.2. Integration

The second factor is the level of integration of EDI, both internally and externally. Internal integration reflects the variety of the value chain functions (orders, shipments, inventory, etc.) interconnected through EDI within the organization $[7, 53]$ . External integration refers to the variety of trading partners (clients, suppliers, distributors, etc.) with which the organization interacts through EDI $[58, 62]$ . Integration thus evolves from an initial state when EDI is first adopted, through later stages as the organization adds functions and partners.

## 3.3. Imposition by partners

The third factor refers to a firm's obligation to implement EDI to satisfy a major client or supplier. Firms may balk at implementing EDI under pressure, because of the difficulty of controlling critical aspects of the project, such as the timing of the implementation, the cost of EDI function, and the complexity of the technology involved. Organizations of all sizes may have to adopt EDI only because they have been obligated to do so by a business partner. Expected benefits may vary, depending upon the firm's bargaining power and level of cooperation with partners [47, 48, 61].

## 3.4. EDI advantages

The advantages of EDI, the dependent variable, include various types of operational, managerial, and strategic benefits that can be derived from EDI use. These advantages relate to improvements in information quality, transaction speed, administrative costs, competitive advantage, and operations management [4, 14, 44]. Bergeron and Raymond [10] confirmed empirically the content of this construct and the hierarchy of advantages accrued from EDI.

## 4. Hypotheses

Six variables (organizational support, implementation process, control procedures, internal and external integration, and imposition) are expected to explain the dependent variable. Additionally, imposition is seen as affecting the organizational context and integration. The research hypotheses are:

Hypothesis 1: There is a positive relationship between the level of the organizational support and the advantages obtained from EDI.

A favorable organizational environment is a necessary condition to obtain advantages from EDI. The support of top-management, the training of personnel, and the presence of a dedicated organizational structure have been reported as important dimensions supporting the introduction of new approaches or technologies such as EDI [34].

Hypothesis 2: There is a positive relationship between the rigor of the implementation process and the advantages obtained from EDI.

As with other types of technology, implementing EDI requires a series of activities, including analysis, testing and implementation, and approaches such as prototyping. These activities are considered helpful when introducing new systems or developing new applications $[39, 55]$ . It is expected that a similar relationship will be observed in the case of EDI.

Hypothesis 3: There is a positive relationship between the presence of the control procedures and the advantages obtained from EDI.

Control procedures are meant to assure data security and integrity. Making sure that data are valid and meet acceptable standards of quality will prevent costly errors, allow quick corrections when needed, and contribute to a stable organizational climate as well as positive relationships with business partners.

Adequate control procedures for EDI are therefore needed and they should help to produce the intended advantages [57].

Hypothesis 4: There is a positive relationship between the level of integration and the advantages obtained from EDI.

The level of integration of EDI can be either or both internal and external. In the case of internal integration, economies of scale, improved production cycle, decreased transaction time, quality improvements and inventory reduction are among the possible advantages of integrating a larger number of activities within the firm's value chain [32]. In the case of external EDI, increased external links allow the firm to capitalize on the investment made in EDI. Its integration with all types of upstream and downstream partners is considered important and even higher rewards can be obtained by firms implementing strategic applications with an outward rather than an inward orientation.

Hypothesis 5: There is a negative relationship between the level of imposition by business partners and the advantages obtained from EDI.

When an organization is forced to implement EDI, crucial aspects of this implementation, such as the targeted business function, the timing, and the choice of the value added network are not under the firm's control. Benefits can thus be more slanted toward the firm that initiates EDI or imposes EDI on others. For instance, the implementation of EDI by a large manufacturer can significantly affect the bargaining power of distributors and thus lessen their advantages [22].

Hypothesis 6: There is a negative relationship between the level of imposition by business partners and (a) the level of the organizational support, (b) the rigor of the implementation process, (c) the presence of the control procedures, and (d) the level of integration.

Earlier research has suggested that there is a negative link between the level of imposition and the organizational context of EDI. Indeed, the organizations upon whom EDI is imposed usually have less time to prepare for implementing this technology. A lack of a comprehensive organizational support, a sketched implementation process, and neglected control procedures are among the expected consequences. Level of imposition is also expected to show a negative relationship with the level of integration in the short run as the firm would tend to respond to the dominant partner by implementing only the required functions (say, orders, invoices).

Each scale was anchored on three points (no: 1, more or less: 2, yes: 3) and a total score was obtained by averaging on all scales. The level of internal integration was determined by counting the different types of EDI applications internal to the organization, whereas the level of external integration was a count of the different types of trading partners. For that purpose, the respondents were asked to indicate the exact types of EDI applications presently implemented (e.g. purchasing and billing) as well as the types of business partners presently communicating with the organization through EDI (e.g. retailers, suppliers, and clients).

The imposition scale was a single item that asked the respondents to evaluate the statement: 'The initial decision to implement EDI was imposed by your trading partner(s)' (no: 1, more or less: 2, yes: 3). This question was asked only in phase 1, since the first contact of the organizations with EDI could not have changed over time.

The dependent variable included benefits thought to be provided by EDI, in terms of administrative costs, transaction speed, information quality, operations management and strategic advantage (Table 4). The individual responsible for EDI in each enterprise was asked to rate the scales by the following statement: "Please evaluate the impact of the EDI implementation relative to the functions presently supported in your organization," by indicating on a 5-point Likert scale to what extent each of these advantages had been obtained by the organization (strong decrease: 1, slight decrease: 2, no change: 3, slight increase: 4, strong increase: 5).

## 5. Methodology

A longitudinal design was used to investigate the research questions and to test the hypotheses. This design consisted of two surveys made over a three-year period. For the first survey (at time $t_{1}$ ), a questionnaire was sent to the individual responsible for EDI in 560 Canadian enterprises. This instrument had been formerly pre-tested through direct interviews with EDI managers in 10 enterprises. One week after the first mailing, a recall letter was sent to maximize the response rate. A total of 140 usable questionnaires were obtained for a response rate of 25%. Three years later, (at time $t_{2}$ ) 119 of the 140 individuals who participated were contacted again to participate in phase 2; 21 questionnaires were re-directed (at time $t_{1}$ ) to respondents who were not clearly identified in terms of name or mailing address, thus it was impossible to reach them for the second phase of this study.

## 6. Results and discussion

The participating organizations operate in various sectors as follows: 43.7% manufacturing, 15.6% distribution, 7.8% transportation, 6.3% wholesale, 4.7% financial institutions and 21.9% others. The respondents hold various management positions: 3.1% are CEO, 26.5% are IS managers, 20.3% are managers,

14.1% are EDI managers, 9.4% are computer managers and 26.6% others. The organizations had median annual sales of \$200,000,000, a median number of employees of 700 and a median annual EDI budget of \$17,500. Their EDI relationships were as follows: 76.2% with clients, 42.9% with suppliers, 23.8% with financial institutions, 22.2% with distributors, 18.8% with government agencies, 14.3% with shippers and 9.5% with others. On average, they had been using EDI for 6.5 years (whereas it was 3.5 years in phase 1).

## 6.1. Measurement properties of the research variables

A confirmatory (as opposed to exploratory) factor analysis approach was used to assess the measures (variables) underlying the theoretical constructs found in the research model at both points in time, with the exception of imposition, internal and external integration which are each measured by a single-scale variable $^{1}$ . This approach uses structural equation modeling as opposed to traditional principal components factor analysis. Four sets of (manifest) variables were used as observable indicators that reflect the meaning of four unobservable constructs (latent variables), namely, organizational support (Table 1), implementation process (Table 2), control procedures (Table 3) and EDI advantages (Table 4). Structural equation modeling was used to validate the measurement of each variable and its corresponding items, using the partial least squares (PLS) method. PLS is very robust, allowing for non-multivariate normal and non-intervally scaled data, and for small sample sizes, when compared to an alternative method, namely, LISREL [24]. Matched sets of data were obtained by keeping only those 65 firms (out of the initial 140) which had answered the survey at both points in time.

The first measurement properties to be assessed are the unidimensionality and discriminant validity of the variables (the extent to which the variables are unique from each other). This is done by examining the loading pattern matrices of the measurement items on their corresponding variable, for each of the four constructs. Using 0.5 as an acceptable level [49], one can see in Tables 1,2,3 and 4 that most items loaded adequately on their corresponding variable, while not loading more highly on any other. This led to the removal of only three items, deemed to be unreliable, from the original 58. Importantly, the loading patterns were the same at both points in time, confirming the stability of each construct's factor structure and thus allowing for valid comparisons. Internal consistency, that is, minimizing the occurrence of random error, is another measurement property that is assessed in PLS by computing Fornell and Larcker's [25] rho coefficient, that is, the sum of the loadings all squared divided by the sum of the loadings all squared plus the error terms $^{2}$ . Looking at the bottom of Tables 1–4, the rho values for all thirteen variables indicated an adequate level of consistency in both phases of the study. Note that the Cronbach's alpha values are also provided for comparison purposes.

## 6.2. Stability of the research variables

The descriptive statistics of the independent variables are presented in Table 5. Paired T-tests were used to test for the ‘stability’ of the EDI success factors, that is, to determine if their mean level had changed over time, following Kenny’s [33] approach to correlation and causality in longitudinal research.

The results first indicate that on average, the level of organizational support is perceived somewhat less favorably than it was initially regarding the structure of the EDI function. This could be attributed to the fact that, after three years, the organization is more familiar with EDI and its main structuring activities, realized during the initial implementation, are now more taken for granted. Similar reasons can be invoked to explain the decreased perception in the quality of the testing process in the implementation of EDI. The increased knowledge gained after three years would lead the organization to require fewer tests prior to implementing new EDI functions. EDI is also presently more integrated than it was previously, especially from an internal perspective as functions other than ordering and billing were added gradually to increase the processing capabilities of the EDI system. Note that the level of imposition was not measured at the second point in time as this variable refers conceptually to the initial introduction of EDI in the firm, and not to the functions that were added on later.

Table 6 presents the descriptive statistics and T-test results showing the stability of the advantages obtained from EDI (dependent variables) over time. The one advantage to have shown a significant increase is the quality of information (in terms of accessibility, accuracy and service to clients). As firms become more experienced and integrate more internal functions through EDI, it is plausible that they find new ways and become more adept at extracting valuable information from the transaction data provided by this technology.

## 6.4. Test of the research model and hypotheses

As a comprehensive and powerful validation method involving the multivariate analysis of the structural equations, PLS was also chosen for its emphasis on theory development [29]. The research model and hypotheses can be assessed by comparing the two sets of path coefficients obtained from the two analyses of the 65 firms (Table 7 and Figure 2). This being a longitudinal study, one is interested in ascertaining if the causal structure of the hypothesized relationships changes over time. Here, the key assumption is that of 'stationarity': that is, that the direction and strength of each relationship remain the same at both points in time [33]. Note that an analysis of the cross-lagged relationship between the independent and dependent variables found only one significant correlation (internal integration at $t_2$ with administrative costs at $t_1$ ). This overall absence of cross-influences would support the stationarity assumption [51].

## 6.4.1. Hypothesis 1

The first research hypothesis stated the existence of a causal link between a firm's level of organizational support for EDI and its attainment of advantages from this technology. This was strongly confirmed by the equally significant path coefficients obtained at both points in time. This means that more benefits will accrue to organizations when top management demonstrates its full support for EDI, and insures that the EDI function is structured appropriately and the required training is provided to all involved.

## 6.4.2. Hypothesis 2

This assumes that success is predicated upon the rigor of the EDI implementation process. It was confirmed initially by a significant path coefficient at $t_{1}$ . However, this influence totally disappeared later, as evidenced by a null path coefficient at $t_{2}$ . Given that global information needs were initially analyzed, organizational learning must have occurred and operations managers have integrated EDI within their decision making, the final implementations would be less important in this regard. In other words, the rigor of the implementation process would affect the initial applications, but not the later ones, because of the initial experience gained by the firm.

## 6.4.3. Hypothesis 3

The presence of adequate control procedures was also found to be essential for the effective use of EDI. This hypothesis is confirmed at $t_{1}$ , and again three years later, with a second path coefficient that is as significant as the first. This causal evidence thus reinforces the notion that the integrity and security of transaction data must be a constant managerial preoccupation in any EDI network or system, if it is to realize its true potential in the organization without too much risk.

## 6.4.4. Hypothesis 4

The research model posits that the more EDI is integrated, the more benefits can be reaped from its use. Equally significant path coefficients show this to be confirmed at both points in time. Also, this relationship remained stationary even though, as noted previously, the level of EDI integration in the sampled firms has significantly increased during the period. From an internal perspective of integration, as more functions, such as procurement, inventory, shipping, billing and payments, are joined through EDI, more data are rendered available; these data are more integrated and can therefore be easily transformed into more comprehensive, timely, and relevant information for decision making, thus providing managerial advantages. From an external perspective, as EDI links the firm's value chain to the value chains of both its upstream and downstream partners, 'just-in-time' (in manufacturing) or 'quick-response' (in retailing) approaches can be implemented to reduce cycle times and improve service, thus providing operational and strategic advantages. Note however, that in the United States, strikes and floods in the Mississippi proved that just-in-time can be disastrous if they are impediments: backup solutions are thus needed.

## 6.4.5. Hypothesis 5

The fifth hypothesis was that a firm would obtain less advantages from EDI when its initial implementation had been imposed upon it by one or more business partners. This proved to be true initially, as imposition was the most determinant factor of all: that is, the one with the strongest path coefficient. However, the weakness of the second path coefficient means that imposition lost all of its direct influence three years later. In other words, the firms which had initially implemented EDI involuntarily were able to overcome this initial handicap. Given that it is the smaller firms that are the most imposed upon here, this last result could be tentatively interpreted as the compensating effect of the increased assistance provided by large pivot firms in EDI networks (either proprietary or third party) to their smaller, less IT-sophisticated partners, who could thus learn how to obtain both operational and managerial advantages from EDI. With such assistance, firms that see EDI only as an added cost to satisfy important clients or suppliers eventually learn to better manage this technology and thus seize the strategic opportunities it can provide.

## 6.4.6. Hypothesis 6

The last hypothesis, that imposition would have a negative influence on a firm's organizational context and level of integration of EDI was initially proven to be the case, with all four path coefficients being negative and significant. Three years later, this remained true in the case of integration with an equally significant and negative path coefficient. Firms which implemented EDI voluntarily showed a more proactive attitude and a more integrated approach, looking at more diverse functional applications of this technology (e.g. from a business process reengineering or total quality management perspective). The others simply react to outside pressures by implementing only those EDI functions that are obligatory.

The situation has changed completely for the organizational context at $t_{2}$ , the level of organizational support and the presence of control procedures being now positively rather than negatively affected by imposition. From a network perspective, these advantages can be unequal but also interdependent among business partners of unequal size and power. This change in the effect of imposition could possibly reflect the realization by larger firms which initiate EDI systems that they must eventually provide increased assistance, such as training and technical support to their smaller partners, which would, in turn, result in a more favorable organizational context for EDI.

## 7. Conclusion

Longitudinal results of EDI implementation were obtained by twice surveying 65 organizations over a three-year period. The organizational context (organizational support, implementation process, control procedures) as well as the levels of EDI integration and imposition in the firm were all initially determinant in attaining operational and managerial advantages from this technology. It was also confirmed that imposition negatively affects the other determinants. Three years later, while the organizational support and control procedures maintain their importance, the implementation process and imposition lose theirs. Also, the imposition of EDI that negatively affected all other factors is now positively related to the organizational context, although maintaining its negative effect on the integration level.

Many organizations and EDI associations have worried about the negative impact of EDI imposition on business partners. This longitudinal study has shown that while imposing EDI is associated with lower EDI advantages in the short run, it does not have such an effect in the long run. Indeed, to compensate for the limited advantages obtained initially from EDI, imposed upon firms tend to increase their level of organizational support and the quality of control procedures to the point where they eventually attain a wide range of EDI advantages. Overall, the level of EDI advantages increases with time, especially information quality. EDI will thus be a lasting technology bringing immediate as well as longer term improvements to the firms that adopt it.

From a practical point of view, there are still too few organizations that have truly profited from EDI. The adoption rate of this technology has not been as high as predicted a few years ago. Too many firms, especially smaller ones, still consider EDI simply as an added cost of doing business. Future extensions of EDI networks initialized by large organizations and normalization efforts (e.g. the ISO with the EDIFACT standard) should include EDI planning, implementation and training frameworks. These frameworks would guide firms on how to provide a favorable context for EDI, and how to better integrate their intra- and inter-organizational systems to fully benefit from this technology.
