## Abstract

Previous literature in the executive information systems (EIS) area has proposed the existence of four functional types of EIS. Starting with these, the purpose of this study was to modify or develop an EIS user taxonomy by querying 98 of the Corporate 1000 CEOs on the ways in which they used their EIS. These were then grouped and compared with the proposed EIS functions. The findings indicated three EIS user types whom we have termed: (1) 'converts,' (2) 'pacesetters,' and (3) 'analyzers.' Further investigation revealed three dimensions underlying these types: (1) organizational monitoring; (2) information access; and (3) organizational understanding. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Executive Information Systems; EIS; End-user; User types; Field study

## 1. Introduction

Executive Information Systems (EIS) are challenging to develop $[11, 14, 17, 27]$ as evidenced by their large number of past failures. Recent studies have shown that defining systems objectives is one of the most common problems $[22, 29]$ . While considerable work has been done on identifying the steps to be taken in developing an EIS $[28]$ , little work has been performed to provide a framework describing how the product serves the end-user. Development of conceptual models for EIS has not taken place as rapidly as the evolution for other Management Support Systems. Recently, there has been an increased interest in the study of EIS $[24]$ , indicating a critical need for conceptual models of EIS and their empirical validation.

Previous literature has posited the existence of four functional EIS types. However, despite the conceptual appeal of this taxonomy and the possible benefit to be gained through its study, there appears to be no empirical study conducted to validate this or any other model of EIS use. Therefore, this study attempts to fill a gap by advancing the study of EIS user types from ‘grand theory’ (generated from logical assumptions and speculations about what ‘ought to be’) to a ‘grounded theory’ (derived from data) [5].

Determining the types of EIS users has several benefits. First, taxonomies provide descriptions which are rich and informative for discussion and communication in a field [10]. Second, identifying common types of EIS users can provide insight into the information requirements determination (IRD) methods that best capture the necessary executive information needs for a specific type of user [30]. Ultimately, the goal is to allow the user to specify the desired outputs of the system, then the appropriate IRD methods would be applied to enhance the likelihood of successful implementation. Additionally, IS researchers could investigate the IRD methods used in EIS implementation and determine emerging patterns and relationships between success, method, and usage. Third, a taxonomy for EIS users would enhance studies by reducing a large number of potential research variables to a manageable (yet powerful) few [8]. Fourth, a taxonomy would provide an excellent starting point to aid executives in determining and communicating their true goals. It is well known that executives find it difficult to identify the information they need [1]. A set of EIS users profiles should lessen overload resulting from irrelevant, useless information. Fifth, systems development practitioners need the descriptive relevance that a taxonomy can provide [23] in order to provide guidance in assisting successful system implementation. Finally, any advance in the field of EIS can advance the understanding of all MSS [2]. Thus, empirically testing the applicability of a proposed taxonomy should add value to EIS theory.

## 2. EIS user types

Volonino and Watson [26] referred to the four functional types of EIS described by Gulden and Ewers [6] as systems to: (1) to improve information access; (2) improve communication; (3) solve problems; and (4) monitor performance.

Systems to improve information access provide data focused on indicators or dimensions of interest to the executive. Much of this is traditionally available from a transaction processing system (TPS) and typical management information systems (MIS), but not in the meaningful, focused, and customized fashion desired by an executive [12].

Systems to improve communication provide terminal-based access to electronic mail and computer conferencing. The executive's terminal is usually networked to others in the company. Access to sources outside the organization (e.g. the Internet, etc.) may also be available.

Classical DSS capabilities allowing the executive to perform random and unstructured analysis of data, or modeling is provided to help solve problems. The computer may be used to perform ad hoc querying of corporate databases to help in sophisticated mathematical modeling. The type of analysis differs from manager to manager. Some users merely compute new ratios or extrapolate current trends, while at the other extreme, some users may learn to do actual programming [18].

Systems to monitor performance, or control systems, provide managers with access to a predetermined and preformatted set of electronic reports. The data used to generate these may be updated hourly, weekly or sometimes less often depending on the nature of the data. Executives also need 'read only' access to the latest data or reports on key organizational variables. Early EIS efforts were directed toward developing systems to improve information access. More recently, EIS types with advanced functionality have become increasingly available [15].

While these above descriptions relate to types of EIS rather than types of users, it is reasonable to believe that these system types were proposed to EIS users. Thus, these system types provide an acceptable starting point. After decomposing the four types into their functional components, EIS users can be asked about their use of the components. Usage patterns can then be analyzed to determine if the four types exist, if the model needs to be modified, or if a new model must be developed.

Thus, the hypothesis driving this study is:

HYP 1: Four significantly different functional types of EIS users will be identified, based upon the clustering of purposes for which executives use their information systems.

Additionally, Gulden and Ewers [6] suggested that the functional types differed along two underlying dimensions: (1) change and (2) support.

## 3.1. Sample

The population used in this study was the 1000 Corporate Elite CEOs identified by Business Week. This population was chosen as it is believed to possess an above-average percentage of EIS users.

## 3.2. Instrument

Questionnaires were sent to the CEO of the organization. The questionnaire stated – “An executive information system can be defined as a computerized system that provides executives with information that is relevant to their work.” The first question asked “Using the above definition, does your organization now have an executive information system?” CEOs answering ‘yes’ to the above question were provided with a list of 17 purposes for which they might use an EIS.

This list was a combination of capabilities extracted from the definitions of EIS functional types discussed in the literature and a list of items previously identified as EIS characteristics.

EIS capabilities to improve information access are assumed to include: (1) providing information which was formerly provided in a written report; and (2) 'drill down' capabilities. Similarly, EIS capabilities to improve communication should include access to: (1) outside news services; (2) network television; (3) electronic mail; (4) computer conferencing; and (5) company news. Likewise, EIS capabilities to solve problems should include: (1)ad hoc querying of organizational data bases; and (2) analysis of organizational data. Finally, EIS capabilities to monitor performance should include providing: (1) information from predetermined and preformatted reports, and (2) information on key organizational variables. Six additional capabilities have been identified by Watson, et al. that do not necessarily fit into any of these categories; they include: (1) word processing; (2) automated filing; (3) calendar scheduling; (4) automated rolodex; (5) tickler files; (6) external databases.

Respondents were asked the relative frequency with which they used the EIS for any of the 17 purposes or to identify additional purposes. This self-typing approach is consistent with methods used to empirically test typologies in other disciplines, such as organizational strategy type [20] and decision making type [25].

Watson, et al. [29] received 60 responses, 43 of whom had an EIS. Bergeron, et al. [3] collected data from 38 EIS users. Rainer and Watson [16] interviewed 48 EIS developers. Nord and Nord [13] received 152 responses, 47 of whom had an EIS. Watson, et al. [28] received 112 responses, 50 of whom had an EIS. Therefore, our returns are consistent with similar studies.

The usable respondents were compared with the general population of the Corporate 1000 organizations (the sample) to determine how well they represent the overall population. Comparisons were made along four relevant dimensions: (1) geographic location; (2) number of employees; (3) annual sales in billions of dollars; and (4) industry.

## 4.2. EIS types

Cluster analysis was performed to identify existing EIS user types from the respondent EIS usage profiles. The 17 purposes were clustered together to determine the types of EIS users. The SAS AVERAGE linkage method (originated by Sokal and Michener [21]) of the CLUSTER procedure was used to obtain the group average via the unweighted pair-group method using arithmetic averages [19]. During the partitioning stage, observations were clustered together in a step-wise manner based on the average linkage. The analysis began with an individual cluster for each respondent using an EIS, and terminated when all the observations had been collected.

A major issue with all clustering techniques is the manner in which a meaningful number of clusters is selected [7]. Unfortunately, no standard, objective selection procedure exists. For this study, a heuristic measure was used to determine a meaningful number. Clusters with average intercluster distance greater than one were treated as meaningful, and this heuristic was used to terminate the clustering.

Using this analysis, three distinct clusters of EIS users emerged. As part of the interpretation, the dimensionality of the problem was reduced to include only those purposes of the original 17 that significantly differentiated the clusters. This reduction was performed via t-tests between the mean scores of each cluster. The SAS TTEST with the COCHRAN option [4] was performed by clusters on the mean answers of each of the 17 purposes. Ten purposes were found to have significantly different means between at least two clusters at the 0.05 level. The other seven were removed from further analysis.

## 5. Discussion

The three EIS user types found in this study can be mapped to the four proposed EIS functional types suggested in the literature: Cluster 1 – ‘converts’-closely corresponds to ‘using systems to improve information access’; Cluster 2, – ‘pacesetters,’ – most closely corresponds to ‘using systems to improve communication and systems to monitor performance’; and Cluster 3, – ‘analyzers’ – most closely corresponds to ‘systems to solve problems.’ Therefore, there does appear to be limited support for the hypothesis, although only three distinct groups were identified with improved communication and performance monitoring grouped.

From the factor analysis, one can examine the apparent underlying dimensions in greater detail. Factor 1 can be interpreted as the ‘organizational monitoring.’ This dimension reflects the use of the EIS to monitor both qualitative variables (via e-mail and company news) and quantitative variables (via drill down capabilities and the latest data on key organizational variables).

The second factor can be identified as an ‘information access’ dimension. This dimension reflects the use of the EIS to access information both inside the organization (via predetermined and preformatted reports and information formerly provided in a written report) and outside the organization. The EIS is used to access information that was earlier accessed in some other way.

Finally, factor 3 can be interpreted as an ‘organizational understanding’ dimension. This reflects the use of the EIS to expand the users’ understanding via unstructured access and analysis of organizational data. Computer conferencing allows unstructured access and analysis of organizational members.

## 6. Summary and conclusions

This study has several limitations. First, the CEOs examined represent a sample from U.S. industry. Second, the small sample size, while consistent with previous research, limits the generalizability of the findings. Third, if a significant number of CEOs passed the questionnaire on to their subordinates, then the results of the study may be skewed.

Past theoretical supposition has proposed a taxonomy of four types of EIS for executive use. Our study empirically investigated this, and found evidence suggesting three basic ‘types’ of EIS users: referred to as converts, pacesetters and analyzers. Further analysis of the findings suggests three underlying dimensions related to these EIS users types. These dimensions were referred to as ‘organizational understanding,’ ‘information access,’ and ‘organizational monitoring.’

This study, therefore, has proposed a revised taxonomy of EIS user types along with corresponding dimensionality to further explicate the taxonomy.
