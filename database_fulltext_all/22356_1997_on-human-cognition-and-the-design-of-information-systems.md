# On human cognition and the design of information systems

J. Wayne Spence $^{a,*}$ , Ray J. Tsai $^{b}$

$^{a}$ BCIS Department, University of North Texas, Denton, TX 76203, USA $^{b}$ BCIS Department, St. Cloud State University, St. Cloud, MN 56301, USA

## Abstract

For almost two decades, cognitive style has been employed as a factor in the explanation of human interaction with computer systems. However, previous findings based on its use have proven inconclusive. The experiment discussed here utilized 101 subjects who were presented with four tasks. Cognitive style was assessed for each subject, as were the cognitive processes utilized in each task solution. The results demonstrate the fallacy of using cognitive style to explain task-based performance and offer evidence suggesting that cognitive process may better explain differences in human performance across multiple tasks. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Cognitive style; Cognitive process; Task type; Task complexity; Decision making

## 1. Introduction

A long-held tenet of information system (IS) design is that a system has a better chance to succeed if it satisfies its users' needs. It has been further assumed that in meeting those needs, the system should support the users' information processing style. Since Mason and Mitroff [23] presented their views on the importance of psychological type to IS design, cognitive style has been employed as the mechanism of choice in explaining human interaction with computer systems. However, previous research findings on the feasibility of using cognitive style as a guideline for IS research and design are confusing and inconsistent (e.g. [3, 4, 18]). Problems, such as the preference for or the effectiveness of information presentation formats, need to be re-examined, and the argument over the applicability of cognitive style remain unresolved [25, 32].

Cognitive style has been defined as “the behavior that individuals exhibit in the formulation or acquisition, analysis, and interpretation of information or data of presumed value for decision making” [34]. Although, Carl Jung [17] and many other researchers have defined the characteristics of individuals who possess a certain cognitive style in gathering and evaluating information, no research evidence has been provided to support the consistency of cognitive style over a variety of tasks; that is, previous research has either concentrated on a single task or the research was not task-based. It is possible that the ways in which decision makers sense and perceive information might differ under different environments (e.g. different tasks). IS research seems to have ignored this possibility. Existing approaches appear to measure what is convenient in lieu of measures that have a behavioral meaning.

In order to make research findings more meaningful and to provide a means to generalize the results to new environments, this study investigates the possible change of a person's decision-making processes when presented with different tasks. The results of this study help explain Huber's [14] criticism of cognitive style research and provide insights into the possible use of cognitive processes in IS design. Thus, the purpose of our research is to investigate the relationship between cognitive style and cognitive process under multiple task environments.

## 1.1. Research questions

This study explores two questions:

1. Do decision makers with different cognitive styles perform tasks differently?

2. Is the decision-making process consistent under different task environments; that is, do they elicit different task behaviors?

Our study investigates the importance of behavioral variations that result from task environments. Thus, the answers provided by this study could suggest whether cognitive style is a reliable indicator for predicting human information processing.

## 1.2. Prior research

In the past decades, studies have been conducted concerning the effect of cognitive style on decision-making behavior and performance (e.g. [20, 36, 33]). These were motivated by the belief that the user's cognitive style should be considered in the design and implementation of IS. Churchman and Schanblatt [7] and Huysmans [15] pointed out that individuals may ignore information presented in a format incompatible with their cognitive style. Thus, it has been posited that individuals tend to develop a preference for their use of information.

Many cognitive style models have been developed by previous researchers (e.g. [2, 9]). Jung indicated that human behavior is orderly and consistent and that an individual has his or her own pattern in perceiving and judging information. Due to differences in cognitive style, information to one person might not be information to another. Therefore, theoretically, cognitive style might have a substantial impact on the use or design of an IS.

Despite the perceived importance of cognitive style, the results of prior research are inconclusive and discouraging. Huber observed:

The currently available literature on cognitive styles is an unsatisfactory basis for deriving operational guidelines for MIS and DSS designs.

and

Further cognitive style research is unlikely to lead to operational guidelines for MIS and DSS designs.

Thus, knowledge of a user's cognitive style is unlikely to yield consistent operational guidelines for IS design. This has been attributed to: (1) the lack of a developed theory of cognitive style, (2) unreliable and invalid measuring instruments, and (3) inadequate research designs [16]. In addition to these three problems, the differences in the experimental tasks could also have a substantial impact [8].

The stability of decision-making processes under different task environments has not been established. If cognitive style is responsive to different types of tasks, a dynamic model based on cognitive process should provide a more comprehensive understanding of the human interaction with IS [31]. However, if there is no reliable relationship between cognitive style and task performance, then more research efforts need to be expended toward other, more promising constructs.

Cognitive process may be viewed as dynamic or the reflection of a state – the approaches used in sensing, concept formation, decision making, and problem solving. Although there is no universally accepted definition of cognitive process, Marzano et al. [22] classify its constituency into concept formation, principle formation, comprehension, problem solving, decision making, research, composition, and oral discourse. These are not distinct from one another since they overlap and may occur in varying sequences. Furthermore, each individual may be faced with limitations in terms of his or her cognitive capacity [21].

Research into the cognitive processes underlying judgment and choice has shown “the sensitivity of judgement and choice to seemingly minor changes in tasks” [10]. This implies that cognitive processes in decision making are not consistent and might be contingent on the demands of the task. Studies from the American Institute for Research [11, 26, 37] also indicate that exhibited behavior differs from task to task; that is, individuals appear to adapt to their environment by changing attitudes and behaviors. Thus, a process view of contingent decision behavior should provide a better understanding of the activities associated with decision making than heretofore provided through cognitive style [29].

Cognitive process research focuses on the specific influence on an individual's thinking processes. The influence of strategies and structures on a decision maker's information processing may provide more implications to system design and implementation than simply looking at the individual traits (cognitive style) of a decision maker. The confusion of cognitive style research in the IS area, indeed, reflects the inherent problem of disregarding the behavioral changes in dynamic environments.

## 2. Variables and hypotheses

In our study, the independent variable is cognitive style. For the purpose of this research, it is split into analytic and non-analytic. The analytic group contains subjects with a thinking type of cognitive style, whereas the non-analytic group is represented by the feeling type. The Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) is used to differentiate subjects based on Jungian typology. The MBTI is a self-reported measure and is made up of four bipolar sub-scales: thinking–feeling (TF), sensing–intuition (SN), judging–perceiving (JP) and introvert–extrovert (IE)[27]. Previous research has suggested that the MBTI is a valid measure that can discriminate behavior relevant to IS design and use [5, 18]. It should be noted that the MBTI was employed in this experiment because of its significant utilization in IS research, that is, it appears more frequently in the literature than other instruments, such as the Wilkin's Group Embedded Figures Test (GEFT), Kirton's Adaptor Innovator scale (KAI), Kolb's Learning Style Inventory (KLSI), and so forth. However, many researchers have registered a number of issues concerning the operational properties of MBTI and whether it measures cognitive style. In spite of these significant concerns, MBTI continues to be utilized in IS research.

Cognitive process is the dependent variable for this study and it has been used to investigate whether individuals with different cognitive styles will use different decision-making processes. A cognitive process involves using a sequence of cognitive skills to process information. Therefore, the pattern of skills characterizes the cognitive processes. Cognitive skills are grouped into 21 categories (see Table 1).

To confirm the relationship between the skills and tasks performed, a multi-phase pilot study was conducted. In phase I of the pilot study, a concurrent verbal protocol analysis was conducted as each of seven subjects performed the experimental tasks. The responses provided in phase I were then matched with the cognitive skills and a self-reporting, pencil-and-paper instrument was developed to elicit identical responses from the respondents in phase II. This instrument consisted of 30 items, each bearing a yes/no response to indicate the presence of each skill followed by a 9-item importance scale ranging from 'not important' (scale value of 1) to 'extremely important' (scale value of 9).

In phase II, six subjects again performed the tasks. In this iteration, data were collected both by concurrent protocol and via the (retrospective) pencil-and-paper instrument. These results were then compared using matched-pair analysis, which confirmed that the responses were indeed statistically identical. Furthermore, exit interviews with phase II subjects indicated that they generally had greater confidence in the accuracy of the pencil-and-paper instrument. Thus, the pencil-and-paper instrument was adopted for use in the subsequent execution of the experiment. Further details of this process are presented in Appendix A.

Since the process of decision making might differ across tasks, 'task type' and 'task complexity' were used as independent variables to test for possible variations in cognitive process. The treatments employed were represented by four independent tasks representing two task types (quantitative and qualitative), a factor suggested by Agarwal et al. [1] to be relevant to problem solving, and two task complexities (lower and higher). The quantitative tasks were extracted from the 1976 CMA exam, which involve the mathematical manipulation of numerical data to reach a decision on a production problem. The qualitative tasks, compiled from Payne [28], were based on the problem of selecting an apartment from among a set of alternatives. This problem statement was presented in a textual format, required no mathematical manipulation, and the decision outcome was a function of personal preference.

Task complexity refers to the number of inputs, input variation, number of sub-tasks, and number of operations or procedures involved in the completion of a task. For information processing or decision making, a task that utilizes fewer information cues is considered as having lower task complexity than one with more cues. The higher the complexity, the more sub-tasks the decision maker must complete [38]. Complexity does not have an absolute value, thus it can only be measured in a relative sense. For purposes of this study, complexity was classified as either 'lower' (fewer cues) or 'higher' (more cues). The sample of the tasks used in this study are presented in Appendix B.

The overall hypothesis for this study is that an individual's cognitive style has no relationship to exhibited decision-making behavior. The hypotheses to be tested are:

H1: Cognitive style has no impact on the cognitive process used during a decision-making task.

H1a: There is no significant difference in cognitive process between analytic and non-analytic types of decision makers when processing a lower complexity/quantitative task.

H1b: There is no significant difference in cognitive process between analytic and non-analytic types of decision makers when processing a lower complexity/qualitative task.

H1c: There is no significant difference in cognitive process between analytic and non-analytic types of decision makers when processing a higher complexity/quantitative task.

Hld: There is no significant difference in cognitive process between analytic and non-analytic types of decision makers when processing a higher complexity/qualitative task.

H2: There is no significant difference in cognitive process within the analytic (or non-analytic) group of decision makers when solving different tasks.

## 3. Experimental procedure

This study used a volunteer (convenience) sample of 101 senior-level students attending two geographically separated universities. Subjects ranged in age between 20 and 39 years, with a gender distribution of 52 males and 59 females. At the outset of the experiment, subjects completed demographic items and the MBTI. On the basis of MBTI responses, they were ultimately coded as either ‘analytic’ (thinking) or ‘non-analytic’ (feeling) based on the scoring procedure set forth in the MBTI manual.

Four experimental tasks were given during two separate sessions, one week apart. During each session, subjects completed one quantitative (production) problem and one qualitative (apartment selection) problem. To avoid the consequences of learning effect and other biases, the order of presentation of the tasks was randomized for each participant by task type and task complexity (e.g. a subject could have received a sequence of low-complexity production problem, high-complexity apartment selection problem, low-complexity apartment selection problem, and high-complexity production problem, or permutations thereof, during the two sessions). No time restriction was imposed for the completion of the tasks in either session.

## 4. Results and analysis

To provide a more complete understanding of the results, an analysis of subject demographics was conducted. A chi-square test of independence was used to evaluate the distribution of gender and major with cognitive style. This analysis shows an apparent relationship between gender and cognitive style and between major and cognitive style at a significance level of 5%. Males are found to be more analytic than females. This result is consistent with previous findings [24]. Although gender was found to have a relationship with cognitive style, it was found to have no significant relationship with cognitive process.

Cognitive styles were also analyzed relative to the students' major. CIS students appear to be more analytic than non-CIS students. Although the difference exists, this one-shot test does not explain whether students are influenced by their cognitive style in the selection of their major, if the difference results from training received in their major, or the results are unique to the sample used in this study.

## 4.1. Hypotheses testing

Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was used to evaluate the data related to each hypothesis. Analysis of the data indicates that cognitive style has no significant relationship to an individual's decision-making processes (see Table 2).

## H1: Effect of cognitive style

This hypothesis explores whether individuals with different cognitive styles would use different processes to solve problems in different task environments. The analysis of data failed to reject all four subhypotheses at the 0.05 level of significance. The difference in cognitive processes between analytic and non-analytic decision makers was not statistically significant, but cognitive style could influence qualitative tasks (H1b and H1d) somewhat more than quantitative tasks (H1a and H1c), based on the p-values of these results.

The results imply that the two categories of decision makers did not perform differently under each task environment, although they have different cognitive styles. Thus, the impact of cognitive style on an individual decision-making process is not supported. Cognitive style research only provides information on an individual's general information processing traits without consideration of task demands. Based on our analysis, including users' cognitive styles (analytic and non-analytic) regardless of task influences in a system design is of doubtful value. The broad impact of cognitive style on cognitive process and task performance is relatively meaningless.

H2: The stability of cognitive process across tasks

Do subjects with a particular cognitive style have the same cognitive process regardless of the change in the task environment? The data indicated that neither an analytic nor a non-analytic individual had a consistent cognitive process throughout the four tasks. In both analytic $p=0.001$ and non-analytic $p=0.008$ groups, decision makers significantly changed their cognitive processes when the task changed. This suggested that subjects with a particular cognitive style do not always use the same approach to solve problems. A task will elicit certain behaviors from a decision maker based on the characteristics of that task. This finding is consistent with a number of studies [13, 19, 30, 35] that indicate that different individuals formulate the same problem differently – they employ different strategies to perform the same task.

Based on the results, it is confirmed that subjects with different cognitive styles do not appear to perform differently under these four task environments. On the other hand, subjects changed their decision-making processes across the four experimental tasks. It appears that cognitive style is not an appropriate determinant for predicting an individual's behavior in a decision-making process. This result further confirms that the task, rather than cognitive style, will activate, organize, and direct human response [6, 12]. Since cognitive style only indicates general traits of information-processing behavior, cognitive style is neither appropriate nor useful in providing operational guidelines for IS design characteristics which are task based.

## 5. Conclusion

This study presents evidence to clarify one of the most confusing topics in IS research – the applicability of using cognitive style as a guideline for systems design. The analysis of data indicates that cognitive style (analytic and non-analytic) does not affect a user's action in a specific task environment. Subjects with different cognitive styles did not perform differently. Furthermore, within a cognitive style category, members of the same group exhibited significant differences in cognitive processes across four experimental tasks. These findings suggest that the relationship of cognitive style and decision-making performance from previous research may be produced because of the different contingent factors involved in the experimental tasks.

Research on cognitive style in the IS field has focused on the general influence of an individual's information processing and ignored the potential impact of environmental factors, such as task characteristics. Consequently, further research aimed at differentiating decision behavior with respect to task performance that is based on a cognitive style assessment would be fruitless.

The thrust of IS design is to augment the strategies and structures of decision makers. As a result of our study, cognitive process has been demonstrated to be responsive to changes in task environments. To improve decision-making performance or to design IS to support decision making, cognitive process should be considered in future research. Investigation of the decision makers' approaches used in information filtering, processing, organizing, controlling, and adaptive processes should provide useful guidelines for building better systems.

## Appendix A

## Pilot tests

The experiment was preceded by two pilot tests. The first pilot test evaluated the experimental tasks and served as the foundation on which questionnaires were developed. The second pilot test compared the results from concurrent protocol analysis and the questionnaires to confirm the adequacy of the questionnaire design.

Seven subjects participated in the first pilot test. Each subject experienced two short protocol exercises before working on the assigned tasks. A post-test debriefing demonstrated that each task presented the desired distinguishable differences and that the instructions for completing each task were adequate. While solving each task, subjects' concurrent verbal protocols were recorded. These protocols were evaluated and served as the basis for questionnaire design.

Six subjects participated in the second pilot study. The purpose of this evaluation was to compare the results from the concurrent protocol and self-reported questionnaires. The results from a Mann–Whitney U-test demonstrated that insignificant differences existed between the results of verbal protocol and the questionnaires. To confirm these results, a pair-wise evaluation of all subject responses was performed. The purpose of this evaluation was to determine whether the individual identified as using a particular cognitive skill from protocol analysis was the same person identified from the questionnaires. Out of 84 pairs (four tasks and 21 skills), only four unmatched pairs (5%) were found. Thus, the questionnaires were judged to produce results consistent with that of verbal protocol.
