# Lessons from notebook/mobile computing

Rob R. Weitz $^{*}$

Department of Computing and Decision Sciences, Stillman School of Business, Seton Hall University, South Orange, NJ 07079, USA

## Abstract

Information technology in general, and the Internet in particular, present both opportunities and challenges to business schools. While a number of institutions stand out as having creatively integrated technology into their curricula, current data indicate that the majority of higher educational institutions in North America are still searching for direction. This article describes a real-world case study of a pilot program for introducing and leveraging instructional technology via notebook/mobile computing. A primary objective of this paper is to provide stimulus for thinking about how business schools can implement information technology to improve pedagogy and strengthen their position in the increasingly competitive world of business education. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Mobile computing; Instructional technology; Case study

## 1. Introduction

... the pace of innovation in higher education can be measured by the 40 years it took to get the overhead projector out of the bowling alley and into the classroom [4].

Information technology in general, and the Internet in particular, present both opportunities and challenges to business schools. Many researchers have described the opportunities instructional technology provides for pedagogy $[1, 3, 7, 8]$ . A different perspective is presented by Ives and Jarvenpaa $[6]$ who delineate the threats resulting from advances in information technology to the hegemony of the university in its role as the primary creator and disseminator of business knowledge. While a number of colleges and universities stand out as pro-actively responding to these challenges by creatively integrating technology into their programs [9, 10], current data indicate that the majority of institutions have only gingerly embraced instructional technologies, and apparently are still searching for direction [5].

This article describes a case study of a pilot program for introducing and leveraging instructional technology via notebook/mobile computing; a primary objective of this paper is to provide stimulus for thinking about how business schools can implement information technology (IT) to improve pedagogy and strengthen their position in the increasingly competitive world of business education.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 outlines current strategic threats to university-based management education. A snapshot of the current status of the use of instructional technologies in North American colleges and universities is presented in Section 3. Section 4 describes in detail the mobile computing project including the rationale for the project, planning procedures, faculty coordination, support requirements, problems encountered and lessons learned. Conclusions and recommendations are presented in Section 5.

## 2. The challenge to business education

Ives and Jarvenpaa [6] describe a number of private sector competitive threats to business schools. These include

\- Companies relying more and more on their own in-house programs; increasingly these programs focus on ‘just-in-time training’ using groupware such as Lotus Notes, or virtual reality technologies.

\- The creation of virtual classrooms by cable operators and telecommunications companies.

\- Innovative multimedia products, developed by publishers and software houses, aimed at substituting, rather than augmenting, traditional courses.

\- Audio, video and distance courses produced by business school faculty working for themselves or as consultants for private organizations.

\- The use of telecommunications technologies by top-tier institutions, allowing them to compete on a local level with local institutions.

In addition to these competitive pressures, Ives and Jarvenpaa suggest that institutions face a technology-enhanced educational environment which will include virtual learning communities, just-in-time rather than just-in-case education, pull rather than push (i.e. more personalized learning, where greater control is exerted by the student), more of an emphasis on visual rather than textual learning, and an increased focus on doing (i.e. students using simulation software) rather than watching.

Responding to these challenges will require radical changes in current business school pedagogical procedures, infrastructure, integration of IT, training of faculty, reward structures, and technical support standards.

Notwithstanding a few well-known initiatives by some institutions of higher education, a snapshot of the state of instructional technology on campus does not support optimism that universities in general are successfully addressing these challenges, or indeed have a coherent plan to do so. The next section documents this position.

## 3. The status of instructional technologies on campus

A good overview of instructional technology is provided by Albright and Graf [1] in their introductory article to a collection on the subject. Comprehensive on-line bibliographies, ranging from ‘Assessments of Multimedia Technology in Education’ to ‘Using Computers to Teach Basic Skills,’ are furnished by Kotlas [4]. Leidner and Jarvenpaa [5] provide a critique on the use of instructional technologies in management education.

The National Survey of Desktop Computing and Information Technology in American Higher Education is the largest continuing survey of campus planning, policies and procedures related to the use of computers in American higher education. The 1996 survey [5] indicates that:

... less than half (43.4%) of American colleges and universities have a strategic plan identifying institutional goals, objectives, or implementation priorities for the role of IT.

... just over one-fourth (28.1%) of the nation's colleges and universities report a budget model for amortizing and routinely replacing computers and software...

... less than a fourth (22.1%)... report that user support, another key element of technology infrastructure, is ‘very good’ or ‘excellent.’

In terms of explicit applications of instructional technologies, the numbers are bleak: just some 25% of college classes use e-mail, the use of presentation handouts stands at 28.4%, only 9.2% of college courses use materials from the World Wide Web, 18.5% of college courses use commercial courseware, and 14.4% of all classes make use of computer-based simulation. (And growth from 1995 for most of these categories was modest to nonexistent.)

When asked to provide ‘the single most important’ information technology issue at their institution in the coming years, the top four responses were: assisting faculty integrate technology into instruction (27.3%), providing adequate user support (24.1%), expanding/enhancing the computer network (17.6%), financing the replacement of aging hardware and software (17.4%).

In particular, it appears that problems range from the conceptually simple though financially challenging (How do we deal with upgrading technology on a regular basis?) to the more complex (How can technology be productively integrated into instruction?) This current snapshot of the state of practice seems to indicate that those institutions with well-publicized, broad-based instructional technology programs are distinctly in the minority – that is, the opportunities afforded by IT are not being capitalized on, and the challenges to traditional educational institutions are not being met. Again, it is hoped that this case study will provide some guidance.

## 4. The mobile computing project

## 4.1. Overview

During the academic year 1995–1996 Seton Hall University embarked on the pilot implementation of a notebook/mobile computing environment. The objective of the mobile computing pilot project (MCPP) was to explore the advantages, disadvantages and requirements of this architecture, with the expectation of expanding the project to the entire university. The project was part of a broader initiative focused on using information technology for improving the teaching and learning environment university-wide.

Approximately 20 first-year students and their instructors were provided with notebook computers equipped with a suite of software applications, and several network and Internet connectivity options. One novel aspect of the notebooks is that they were equipped with infrared PCMCIA cards, enabling wireless connection to the campus network (via infrared connectors installed in the ceilings of several classrooms and other sites on campus). The participating students, part of an honors program, traveled together from class to class during their first year at the university.

In brief, the overarching goal of the project was to test a pedagogical environment where instructional technology is available to professors and students anytime and anywhere.

## 4.2. Background and motivation

Seton Hall University is located some 50 miles west of New York City, in suburban South Orange, New Jersey. The university comprises the Colleges of Arts and Sciences, Education and Human Services, and Nursing, and the Schools of Business, Theology, Graduate Medical Education and Law.

The MCPP grew, at least partly, out of a broader vision outlined in the university's Information Technology Strategic Plan, of a transition to a network-centered, student-oriented, mobile educational environment. Concurrent with the MCPP, the Plan established the Center for Academic Technology, and a number of other organizational and funding mechanisms for supporting faculty learning about, and implementing, technology-enabled curriculum improvements. See Appendix A for more details.

Another principal motivating force was an initiative coming from the Dean of the Business School, in collaboration with IBM. IBM has a general interest in exploring applications of information technology in academic settings; here, in particular, their focus was on field testing their infrared (IR) technology in a university classroom environment. Joint project sponsorship can provide synergistic benefits to both participating institutions in instructional technology initiatives $[11]$ . In particular, the MCPP was seen as a starting point toward exploring possible future collaboration between IBM and Seton Hall.

The business school, and the university in general, were attempting to address the following questions. How can we:

\- continuously provide up-to-date technology to students and professors when new generations of computing cycle through approximately every two years?

\- take full advantage of new telecommunications technologies (i.e. the Internet) in teaching?

\- improve the learning experience as perceived by both students and faculty?

\- provide a structure for exploring new ways of using technology in and outside the classroom?

\- keep technology costs in control?

\- facilitate technical proficiency for students and faculty?

The Leadership Program, an entity distinct from mobile computing, was in its formative stages in the spring of 1995. Its goal is to provide a particularly challenging academic experience for the ‘top 20’ business majors in the entering freshman class. As the name suggests, the focus of the program is to develop and enhance the leadership competencies of outstanding undergraduate business students. The students are selected based on both academic achievement and extracurricular accomplishments. The students in the Leadership Program take all their classes together during their first year of university study. As the mobile computing concept began to take shape, it seemed a natural synergy to tie the two programs together – the mobile computing pilot would be undertaken in conjunction with the Leadership Program.

The window of opportunity presented by IBM's interest in infrared technology, and the start of the leadership program meant an extremely brief lead-time. The project began in earnest during the summer of 1995, with the implementation for the fall (of 1995).

## 4.3. Hardware

For the pilot, the notebooks were loaned to the students. (The students were expected to insure the notebooks against theft, loss or damage, but aside from this there were no costs to them.) The configuration was as follows:

IBM ThinkPad 360, 8MB RAM, 340MB HD, VGA passive matrix color display, 2 PCMCIA slots used for a 14.4 kps modem, and the infrared card. An ethernet card was also provided as backup.

The classroom required projection equipment; the decision was made to purchase a portable setup, comprising an LCD panel and a high-intensity overhead projector.

Several locations had IR receivers installed in the ceiling; the principal classroom for the Leadership Group was equipped with IR receivers, power and ethernet outlets. During the year, an area in the business school student lab was set up to accommodate notebook ethernet connections.

## 4.4. Software

Each notebook was equipped with the following: (1) Windows 3.1, (2) PerfectOffice Suite, including WordPerfect, Quattro Pro, Paradox, Presentations, GroupWise (e-mail, scheduling) and GroupWise Remote (e-mail from off-site via modem), (3) software for 'standard' connection to the university network via the IR cards or via ethernet card; also for dial-in access to the World Wide Web (WWW) via Netscape, (4) an anti-virus utility and (6) a hypertext text editor.

## 4.5. Faculty and courses

The first year curriculum follows. Nine courses were included in the Leadership Program: the students took one elective during the fall semester.

A total of nine faculty taught in the pilot during the first year. (The Quantitative Methods course was team taught by two professors – one from the Business School and one from the Mathematics department in the School of Arts and Sciences.) The Deans of the School of Business and the School of Arts and Sciences invited faculty to participate based upon their teaching proficiency and enthusiasm (both for teaching and for the project). Several of the faculty selected had very little technical background.

## 4.6. Support personnel

Two technical support personnel were assigned to the project: the Business School LAN manager, and director of the university's Center for Academic Technologies (CAT). For both of these individuals supporting the MCPP was added to their then current responsibilities. In preparation for the start of the pilot the LAN manager (1) acted as a liaison with IBM, (2) coordinated installation procedures (i.e. wiring, installation of IR connectors in the classrooms), with university physical plant personnel, (3) configured and loaded the software (with a consultant), and (4) coordinated delivery of the notebooks to faculty and students. During the course of the year she was responsible for continued communication with IBM, for managing the repair/replacement process for hardware problems, and for handling software upgrades and replacement. The director of the CAT was less formally linked to the MCPP; she was available to answer faculty questions concerning hardware and software, and served as a link to other university programs. Lastly, a librarian participated in the planning and training sessions, and acted as a liaison between the project and university library services.

## 4.7. Pedagogical preparation: Institute for Academic Technology

In late August, the participating faculty, support personnel, Director of Career Services, Dean of the Business School, and Faculty Fellow for Computing and Information Technology (in effect the acting CIO for the university) spent two days at a custom-designed workshop at the Institute for Academic Technology (IAT) in Durham, North Carolina. (More information on the IAT may be obtained via their web site, at http://www.iat.unc.edu/.) The purpose of the visit was (1) to gain experience with the various application technologies associated with the project, (2) to step back from the applications and to more generally examine critical project issues, and (3) to facilitate the process of the members of the MCPP coming together as a group.

Hands-on workshops included fundamental ‘care and feeding’ of the notebooks, and tutorials on the use of a WWW browser and a hypertext markup language (HTML) editor. One workshop focused on using Internet resources for higher education; others included demonstrations of multi-media systems for instruction, and an overview of the tools used to build them.

At a higher level, organizational, political, and pedagogical concerns were addressed. These ranged from general discussions on how to integrate technology into the classroom, to policy issues regarding hardware and software standards, to legal matters regarding the notebooks, to career concerns of the participating faculty. (These issues and others will be discussed in more detail below.) Finally, the visit included a session from a representative from Wake Forest University outlining their experiences requiring entering students to equip themselves with computers (most of them notebooks)[10].

Overall, the two days at IAT served as a springboard into the year. We gained some hands-on experience, were exposed to (and had some time to think about) the myriad issues relating to mobile computing, and the visit served as a opportunity for us to come together as a group.

## 4.8. Show time

As might be expected, there were a number of minor glitches at startup. For example, the document prescribing the student's responsibilities vis-à-vis the notebooks had not cleared the university's legal department as of the first day of the semester. As the students were required to sign this form prior to receiving their notebooks, there was a short delay in distributing them. Overall however, the implementation went fairly smoothly.

## 4.9. Project results

There were a number of positive outcomes to the MCPP.

(1) Student enthusiasm remained very high over the academic year. They appreciated the ability of 'on-demand computing,' whether in the classroom, in the dorm, or at home. Their interest in the basics of computers seemed to be enhanced through ownership.

(2) From the instructor's perspective (particularly for an IS instructor), not having to reserve the computing lab, or make special arrangements to organize demonstrations were significant benefits.

(3) Electronic mail facilitated students posing questions outside of class; it also meant that announcements could be made (e.g. changes in schedule or assignments) at a moment's notice. All the homework assignments in the IS class were 'handed in' via email. The use of an integrated package, in this case PerfectOffice, meant that all applications sent over email retained their formatting. For example, spreadsheet files were simply sent as attachments; they could be viewed directly, or saved to disk and opened at another time. Corrected assignments and/or comments were easily returned to the students electronically.

Aside from providing the students with ‘e-mail experience,’ this approach meant that no assignments could get lost, and an automatic record was kept of who handed in what, and when. Feedback to the students was quicker, as I (the IS instructor) typically responded before the next class period. There were, however, two organizational implications. First, I felt an obligation to respond more quickly, and needed to be well organized in order to do so. Second, managing the process became more onerous when multiple assignments were handed in simultaneously. (And I suspect the problem grows exponentially with class size.) I found that requiring standard subject lines and file names for each assignment minimized organizational problems.

(4) A number of the courses used newsgroups to manage discussions outside of class. (These newsgroups were accessible via Netscape, and like e-mail could be accessed on campus or remotely via phone line and modem.) In the Political Science course, students engaged in lively debates on topical subjects posted by the professor. (She commented that at least one student who never participated in class made positive contributions to the newsgroups.) In general, results seem to indicate that when newsgroups were set up for a clearly defined goal, for example, exploring a topic the students had an interest in, they remained active. In cases where newsgroups were simply created and available to students, involvement tended to taper off.

(5) The notebooks facilitated in-class individual and group exercises. Examples include data analysis problems in the Political Science class, group writing exercises in the Communication class, and working on math problems in the Business Math class.

(6) The project served as a rapid initiation to instructional technology for the faculty involved, most of whom had limited experience in this area. (Several in fact had only slight computer experience – I conducted a ‘fundamentals’ workshop for these professors prior to the start of the semester.) On a very basic level, the participating faculty learned about the mechanics, advantages and disadvantages of computer screen – LCD projection; they discovered the limitations of teaching via ‘keystroke’ classes – that is, leading the students through exercises where all the students move together, keystroke by keystroke. Having the computers at every class encouraged the faculty to gain experience with the 'master guider' as opposed to 'sage on stage' approaches to teaching; (corresponding with the objectivist vs. constructivist approaches outlined by Leidner and Jarvenpaa[8]). In general, the project encouraged participating faculty to rethink their course design.

As a case in point, in the calculus portion of the Business Math I course, much less time was devoted to hand-working differentiation and integration problems; the instructor focused instead on the concepts. Students solved some examples by hand to get the idea, and from then on used a software package on the network for these calculations.

(7) Internet access was easily available, facilitating writing and research projects in the English and Political Science classes. Students used their notebooks and presentation software for presentation of assignments in the Oral Communication class.

(8) The MCPP positively affected the overall teaching environment. It:

\- provided a focus for members of the faculty from disparate areas to get to know each other and to work together on improving teaching;

\- provided a supportive initiation for members of the faculty without sophisticated technological skills to learn about and implement technology in the classroom;

\- produced a core of instructors who could share their knowledge and expertise with others; and

\- generally pushed the university up the learning curve with respect to the productive use of technology in education.

(9) Finally, the project initiated an ongoing, mutually beneficial relationship between the university and IBM.

As with any pilot, there were some important lessons and challenges.

First, we found the IR LAN to have some limitations; principally its limited bandwidth resulted in sluggish response times when the class of students was engaged in network Windows applications. (We found that the one DOS application tested, used in the math class, operated more satisfactorily, as did Windows applications when fewer students were on simultaneously.) For this reason, we switched to the backup ethernet cards midway through the year. Again, IBM's interest in this project was in testing their IR technology in a classroom setting; our experience suggests that at present this technology is better suited for lower bandwidth applications, such as transferring files between computers, or printing.

Second, the project indicates that significant support services are required for this type of commitment toward instructional technology. (Ours were stretched to the limit.) Support here takes many forms:

\- training for faculty in the use of the technology per se, as well as in innovative instructional techniques;

\- technical support to maintain the notebook computers, to coordinate under-warranty repair work, and to provide replacement machines;

\- software specialists to load standard packages, install new software (or provide mechanisms for students to do so themselves), and to reconstruct hard disks;

\- media services support for faculty using overheads, LCD panels, etc. Portable equipment must be delivered to the classroom on time and in working order. For portable or fixed equipment, frequent testing is required to make sure that all systems are in working order. Ideally, a technician should be on call during class hours in case of problems.

\- ideally, a coordinator of instructional technologies. Tasks for this individual include identifying instructional technologies (hardware, software and procedures) and assisting faculty in evaluating them, acting as a liaison with related groups on and off campus, and scheduling and coordinating seminars, workshops and meetings. (A coordinator for the MCPP was appointed midway through the year.)

Third, significant organizational issues need to be addressed. This type of project requires a sizeable contribution of faculty time and energy – resources that may otherwise be focused on research output or on other classes. This is likely to be a particular concern for nontenured faculty members. Therefore a suitable reward structure should be instituted for participating faculty. At Seton Hall, it was decided that a cash stipend equivalent to teaching an overload course would be distributed to each of the participating faculty members. In general, support by deans, senior faculty and high-level administrators certainly contribute to a positive view of participation in this type of project. (It remains to be seen how taking part in the MCPP will affect tenure decisions.)

Fourth, the required ceiling IR connectors dictated that the principal MCPP classroom, which happened to be equipped with desks and chairs in rows facing the front, be used for all courses. We quickly determined that this setup was less than ideal for group discussions involving the entire class, and any exercise requiring 'break-out' session. This design supports the lecture format, but is disruptive to other approaches. Further, lighting options were very limited – essentially either fully on or fully off. Reducing the lighting to enhance the projection of the professor's computer screen meant that the students sat in the dark – a situation hardly conducive to student–professor or student–student interaction. Most of the faculty commented that seating and lighting flexibility would be a major improvement.

## 5. Conclusions

The competitive challenges to conventional educational institutions and processes are clear. Notwithstanding some well-publicized cases of leading edge applications of technology, apparently the vast majority of higher educational institutions are still wrestling with the ways and means of utilizing IT for improving pedagogy, and in turn their competitive position. This article presented a case study of a pilot program for mobile computing – one approach for integrating IT in higher education. According to The Chronicle of Higher Education, some 20 institutions of higher education require their students to have access (in some form) to computers [2]. It is hoped that our experience will provide useful guidelines to all those interested in improving higher education via notebook computing in particular, and the use of instructional technologies in general.

Conclusions drawn from the project indicate that the notebook/mobile computing model:

\- provides a mechanism whereby current, required technology is available to the students and faculty;

\- extends the learning environment outside the classroom setting;

\- excites the students in a way which encourages them to learn;

\- motivates the faculty to rethink and redesign the teaching/learning experience;

\- is a mechanism for providing competitive advantage to the institution.

It requires:

\- significant service levels including: (a) media services assistance in the classroom, (b) computer support for hardware problems, software loads/updates for faculty and students and (c) technical support for help in identifying and evaluating instructional software;

\- ongoing training for faculty on how to incorporate technology into the learning experience;

\- a suitable reward structure for participating faculty; and

\- flexible, well-designed classroom space.

We believe that the notebook/mobile model provides significant benefits to students, faculty, and the institution in general. The pilot (using ethernet connectivity) is currently continuing during the 1996–1997 academic year. Currently, planning is underway to expand the program in the fall of 1997 to include the entire entering freshman business school class, the Arts and Sciences honors program, and select other areas (for a total of a third of the entering class). Under this plan, students will lease standard hardware/software configurations through the university. The program will be rolled out to the entire entering class in the fall of 1998.

## Appendix A

In addition to the Mobile Computing Pilot Project, the Information Technology Strategic Plan provided for:

\- the Curricular Development Institute (CDI) – a faculty funding mechanism for the support of ten projects per year, focusing on the technology-enabled redesign of course curricula.

\- the Peer Consulting Network – financial and organizational support for faculty willing and able to consult with their colleagues on teaching issues, including the use of technology.

\- the University Teaching Fellows Program – year-long support for eight junior faculty. During the first semester, Fellows participate in a bi-weekly seminar focusing on pedagogy; the focus of the second semester is on incorporating technology in service to pedagogy.

\- significant infrastructure upgrades, including wiring of the residence halls.

\- stepped-up workshops in computer applications for faculty members.

\- implementation of a professional quality help desk available to the entire university community.

These projects, and the CAT, receive organizational support from the New Jersey Institute for Collegiate Teaching and Learning (NJICTL). NJICTL was founded in 1989 by the New Jersey Board of Higher Education to improve the quality of teaching and learning on the State's 56 campuses, both public and independent. As one of two such state-wide programs in the nation, NJICTL has become a nationally recognized pioneer in multi-institutional approaches to faculty and instructional development. It is funded by the State of New Jersey, Seton Hall University, and by national grants and funding agencies. It is located on the campus of Seton Hall University.
