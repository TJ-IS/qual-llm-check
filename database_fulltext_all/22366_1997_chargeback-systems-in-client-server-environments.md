# Research

# Chargeback systems in client/server environments

D.H. Drury $^{*}$

Faculty of Management, McGill University, 1001 Sherbrooke St. West, Montreal, PQ, Canada

## Abstract

Effectively using information processing resources has been difficult ever since their initial implementations in organizations. The importance of effective cost control has increased with the pace of technological change, increasing investments, and a growing diversity of users and usages. A widely advocated method of bringing order to this state of affairs is the use of a chargeback system whereby users are made responsible for the information costs that they incur.

Some organizations have adopted chargeback systems in the earliest applications. This paper traces the progression of chargeback systems and extends this development to client/server environments. The empirical results indicate that many firms have not applied their existing chargeback systems to client/server technology. Organizations are only partially satisfied with their chargeback systems. The reasons stem from the technological environment and the culture of their use. The principal difficulties are in the approach and metrics. Organizations are adopting other management procedures in order to overcome these difficulties. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Chargeback systems; Client/server; MIS cost control; IT management; IT cost management; Information management

## 1. Introduction

The technological advances that have made client/server environments a major development in the 1990s have brought with them some advantages in being able to customize user requirements and add applications incrementally [32]. According to a study by Carey [17], client/server computing was rated by managers to be the number one new technology, and at the same time, the most frequently mentioned concern of management. Managing this changing IS culture is one of the top three management issues according to a recent study by the Society for Information Management [67].

IT managers who have moved to client/server environments have found no easy technological fix and are encountering new problems to effectively use them. True user self-sufficiency has proved elusive to attain [39]. Established information centers are experiencing a high demand for their services as a result of advancing technology and rising user expectations. Furthermore, many information centers have been required to offer a great deal of support and so have become demand-driven and burdened with responsibilities. There are increased pressures resulting from dwindling budgets, more demanding senior management and a greater variety of clients, external competitors, and alternatives for parts of operations, including outsourcing [54].

Companies are spending a lot of money on expensive technology and, increasingly, IT departments are finding that they have to demonstrate their worth, resulting in an increasing emphasis on metrics in order to support IT as a cost- or revenue-producing center $[19, 34]$ . The costs of new technology are also higher. While the hardware costs tend to be less than mainframe hardware and software costs, ongoing costs of operations tend to be much higher $[18]$ . Further, these costs are expected to increase in the future. At the same time, perceptions of price/performance are slipping according to a study by the Sentry Market Group $[62]$ .

Many organizations have adopted control systems which measure usage, and users are charged for IT services by means of a chargeback system, also called a cost recovery or billing system, to resolve these difficulties. A chargeback system accounts for who uses a company's computer resources and allocates the costs. Chargeback encompasses management and control of an organization's computer related assets. Writers in the field have not only encouraged the use of chargeback systems, but have also predicted increased usage with changes in technology [59, 61].

Chargeback systems have been used since the earliest applications of information technology. While many organizations find chargeback to be a valuable financial and business management tool, others disagree with chargeback's aspect of control and limited scope [14]. They argue that chargeback systems are not ready for the new environment, and that managers are not ready to use chargeback systems with client/server technology [15, 40]. These divergent views question the applicability and usefulness of chargeback systems in the current business context. Whether chargeback systems (or users) have yet to evolve to the sophistication required, or whether they are inappropriate in the environment of client/server technology, is an important difference in the future direction in managing IT resources.

## 2. Chargeback systems

The problem of allocating the cost of computer resources to users was important in the late 1960s.

Computing had been allowed to expand to meet demand and computer costs were rarely allocated to users. In addition, the operating systems of that era did not permit the easy capture of the statistics necessary for accurate job costing. Many organizations turned to centrally controlling IT costs consistent with their treatment of other administration costs. But the mild recession of the late 1960s indirectly promoted an interest in allocating costs and pricing of computer services to users as a means of controlling costs [26]. Enke [29], Smidt [64], and Greenberger [33] discussed chargeback systems in government and universities. By 1970, Nielsen indicates that many organizations adopted chargeback systems [53].

Discussion was primarily prescriptive, taking a strong advocacy approach to the use of chargeback systems $[10]$ . There were many theoretical papers but very few related to actual issues $[41]$ . Chargeback systems were clearly in vogue and received a great deal of attention in the technical literature of the period. Dearden and Nolan $[24]$ discussed the advantages and disadvantages of chargeback systems. Wiorkowski $[70]$ presented the various approaches to developing chargeback algorithms and Cushing $[23]$ , the accounting issues involved. By the late 1970s, surveys revealed that more than half of the organizations had already established chargeback systems.

While many organizations adopted chargeback systems, others that could have used this approach, chose other methods of controlling IT resources. The basis of inquiry changed to trying to understand why, and under what conditions, organizations would adopt or not adopt chargeback systems. Investigations of the 1980s focused more on organizational properties and context rather than on the methods themselves [8, 47, 55]. These empirical studies postulated and tested conditions affecting their usage or non-usage. For example, maturity of use [43, 69] and decentralization of decision making [36] have been found to affect the usage of chargeback systems.

In the late 1980s, the adoption of chargeback systems seemed to have reached maturity and saturation [13, 16]. Both the technical and the management literature reveal that interest in chargeback systems waned. But with the development of client/server systems, proliferation of applications and users, and a shift to more control by users, chargeback systems have found a new resurgence of interest [1]. Management has sought to control the mushrooming expenditure and change in IT. Many writers, particularly writing to IT managers, now advocate the use of chargeback systems, for example, Ref. [49, 65].

Important benefits have been associated with using chargeback systems since their inception. Cost control for managing the rapidly escalating investment in IT, and user involvement to effectively capture the gains in computing capacity and applications were critical management benefits $[25, 35, 45, 46, 68]$ . While these principal benefits are still being expounded $[28]$ , many additional reasons for using, and improving, chargeback systems have been proposed as summarized in Table 1.

The number of proposed benefits have expanded greatly and become more specific. Many new incentives for using chargeback systems are offered, including assisting in IT investment decisions and aligning IT objectives with those of the organization. The emphasis in the earlier part of the list was on users, their effective use and involvement in IT. However, more recently, the emphasis has shifted to the IT resources themselves. Also, the first part of the list focuses on cost recovery, whereas those benefits that are more recent, include controlling IT costs, assisting downsizing, and saving money.

## 3. Sample characteristics

Involvement in IT chargeback systems was initially gauged through directing a short-form questionnaire to 239 organizations. The purpose was to identify and determine characteristics of organizations which were using any form of IT chargeback system. The response rate was 76%. In our initial survey, we asked organizations whether they used any form of chargeback system and 51.9% replied affirmatively; 48.1% that they did not. These general statistics do not however, reveal the entire story since recently, 15.9% of the organizations dropped and another 10.9% have adopted chargeback systems reflecting the changes which are occurring. Respondents were asked to indicate reasons for their choices.

From initial responses, the sub-set of 64 organizations that are currently using client/server chargeback systems were sent another, more extensive questionnaire; 42 replied to it (65%). In addition, six questionnaires were received with incomplete data; these were eliminated. The non-responders were sent questionnaires on three separate occasions; these finally resulted in the 42 complete and usable responses. Of the remainder, our telephone inquiries indicated that the primary reasons for non-response were (a) companies were in the process of major systems revisions and (b) business time pressures. Chi-square tests comparing the samples revealed no significant differences in the industry and other general variables that would lead to a non-response bias.

Specific questions were asked about client/server chargeback practices, and particularly, current factors affecting their effective usage. Respondents were also asked to assess the success of their chargeback systems using scaled questions. The write-in portions of the questionnaire allowed us to address the changes that are occurring.

The demographic data assist in putting the results into perspective. In our initial mailing, 52% were in manufacturing, 9% financial, and 39% service industries. The responses were 53% manufacturing, 2% financial, and 45% services. Seventy-three percent have more than 1000 employees. In addition, 33% of the organizations have more than 100 people in the IT function. The results should therefore, primarily be interpreted in the medium-to-large size category. Organizations which use client/server tend to be larger than the general population of organizations. All have used computers in business for more than 20 years. IBM accounts for 51% of the sample. By comparison, IBM held 53% of the market for large-scale systems and 43% for mid-range systems in 1995 [51]. These comparative demographics assist in putting the study results into context.

The primary classification of chargeback system types into (a) operations costs, and (b) systems and programming costs, has been used for a long time $[27, 57]$ . Karon $[38]$ argues that organizations which have a culture of using chargeback systems (i.e. operations and systems) are likely to adopt client/server chargeback systems. From our initial survey, we compared the usage of the types of chargeback systems for the set of organizations that use chargeback systems. The structure of the results is contained in Fig. 1.

Operations chargeback systems are used most frequently, followed by systems and programming. Second, the overlap between these systems is examined in the bottom half of Fig. 1. While these organizations have a culture of using chargeback systems, they have not automatically extended it to client/server costs. In order to further examine this issue, it is necessary to examine the data from the extensive questionnaire that was received from the set of organizations using client/server chargeback systems.

A major difference between traditional and client/server computing is that the cost structure is different. Capital outlays for client/server hardware systems, software, and contract maintenance can be one-third to one-fifth mainframe costs for comparable performance. Technical support, end-user operations and administration can end up accounting for 85% of the cost of client/server systems. Technical support is where client/server costs can be substantial, and it is this cost category which is most often underestimated by IT managers [50]. Other studies estimate that the cost of labor in support, maintenance, etc. comprises about 70% of the total cost of client/server environments [2]. Further, there are many hidden, unexpected costs that can make them more expensive to buy, build, implement, and operate than centralized, host-based systems.

The client/server costs that are charged back are presented in Fig. 2. Applications costs were charged back by 43.8% of the sample followed by hardware, 38.7%, and support 34.6%. The least charged back were maintenance 28%, administration 25.2% and server access 17%. However, none of the costs are consistently charged back across the sample. There is a wide diversity of charging algorithms for client/server chargeback systems.

Diversity may signal flexibility and customization of approaches, in which case, organizations would regard their chargeback system to be successful. We asked respondents to indicate whether their client/server chargeback system is successful in achieving its objectives. From the results shown in Table 4, the majority found them only partially successful.

Gartner group has identified five principal types: (1) distributed access, (2) remote data access (3) distributed function (4) remote presentation, and (5) distributed presentation [6]. Unlike mainframe environments, for which the basic physical structure was defined, the options in client/server required defining which alternative platform structure is used and then allocating functions to the platform environment. According to a recent study by Seung-Hee Ko, the majority of IT personnel say that their managers are first and foremost concerned with predictable results through a structured approach to management that includes chargeback systems [5]. The rate of change and diversity of structures do not easily lend themselves to stability and predictability over an extended time frame.

We expected that the number of different applications would have an impact on the chargeback systems, since volume and diversity increases. However, the variety of applications apparently has no effect on the objectives.

Two other variables have very little impact on client/server chargeback systems: the central control of IT costs, and the effect on investment decisions in IT. Chargeback systems distribute costs and the total is not affected by the centralization of control. The literature suggests that chargeback should be helpful in identifying investment opportunities but the empirical results indicate that client/server chargeback systems have little impact on this.

A fundamental characteristic of client/server environments is that they provide more options for the users in meeting their processing needs, not the least of which is to outsource completely some of their requirements. A chargeback system provides the basis for a ready comparison of internal options and thus, the variety of users' alternatives affects the chargeback system. Unlike traditional chargeback systems, which are directed toward efficiency, client/server systems should enhance users' awareness of costs, which in turn affects the way in which the information from the chargeback system is used in making IT computing choices.

The objectives of a client/server chargeback system are also influenced significantly by managerial emphasis. The fact that senior management was not committed to client/server technology, which ultimately affects the chargeback system, was a principal difficulty cited by controllers. Garrity [31] found that top management support was an important determinant of IT success. Barki and Hartwick [7] similarly found that management involvement is important. Boynton [12] found that managerial IT knowledge is a dominant factor in explaining high levels of IT use and that both managerial IT knowledge and IT management process effectiveness are influenced by the IT management climate. Chargeback systems are also an information system and they cannot be successful unless supported and used by management.

The final variable is the use of alternative cost controls. The organizations examined in this study do not look at chargeback as a panacea but increasingly it will be supplemented and supported by other techniques.

Organizations were also asked about the difficulties that they are experiencing with client/server charge-back systems. The results are contained in Table 6.

The principal difficulty is technical in that organizations do not find them easy to implement; that is, there are difficulties in allocating fixed costs. Chargeback packages target particular proprietary environments. Further, the few choices in chargeback products for distributed platforms compound the difficulties for users $[52]$ . The fixed costs are infrastructure costs that differ between environments and are not easily handled as direct cost incurred by users. Many users have their own budgets for IT and feel that infrastructure costs should be borne by the organization and not incrementally added to user charges that are not directly controlled by them.

In a client/server environment most users are able to finance projects. But their willingness to do so (20%), and consequently the effort and costs of chargeback is a difficulty. Resistance is often caused, not by the system itself, but the way in which it is used. If the chargeback system is viewed as a negative constraint, it is not likely to be effective. On the other hand, if it provides information helpful to users in managing their IT usage and resources, it can be a positive force.

Since many of our respondents indicated other difficulties, this added further insights. Respondents stated that they found that chargeback systems are not beneficial to the decision making process, that users introduce questionable technologies to the network, and that difficulties typically occur with parts of the system. The most frequently mentioned problem was the difficulty in educating on the effective use of chargeback systems. Many users have not received more than a basic level of training. Balancing the costing system environment with user requirements for simple and understandable cost reports was often cited as an important problem.

## 5. Conclusions

From the earliest computer applications, organizations have sought to understand and control the costs of information technology. Chargeback systems grew in popularity as the size of the investment increased and it became more important to use information systems more effectively. Changes in technology, user empowerment, and increased pressures on cost justification have triggered a renewed interest in chargeback systems in client/server environments.

This empirical study has followed the same framework in extending chargeback issues. From our initial questionnaire, the general use of chargeback systems has remained stable. Most organizations that have a culture of using chargeback systems continue to use them. However, they have not been readily extended to client/server systems. From our second questionnaire, we find that those organizations which have extended chargeback to client/server systems are not entirely satisfied. The issues are the rapid change of technology, the client/server environment itself, and management emphasis and support. There is a great deal of variety in the chargeback of costs. IT has become part of the infrastructure encompassing diverse users whose focus is productively utilizing the technology and users believe that chargeback systems should support that view.

Few organizations understand the true costs and benefits of information technology. Much more of the total cost of IT is outside of the IT department, a substantial part is of labor costs, and there are many hidden costs. What is emerging is that a prerequisite to charging users in an equitable and informative manner is understanding the cost structure first. In order to be effective, chargeback systems must be part of the broader control structure supported by other techniques such as:

(a) Using a Business Satisfaction Survey – this is a yearly in-depth survey of employee satisfaction with the IT services and products.

(b) System availability reports, gathered through hardware failure reports – they specify the number of troubled systems disrupting user productivity each month.

(c) Production program failure reports – showing software failures or questions lodged with the help desk. If the problem is with the software, the IT department solves it; if it is due to lack of training, better training is provided.

(d) Object creation and reuse reports – these are tracked monthly by object-oriented developers.

(e) Benchmarking of IT cost and performance against current and desired performance.

These techniques add information that goes beyond the narrow focus on costs. The management of chargeback systems needs to evolve along with IT changes. Chargeback systems must be as fluid as the technology which they measure. The stability and predictability of the environment and its effects on chargeback practice are challenged in client/server environments. Thus, new challenges have emerged in making them effective.
