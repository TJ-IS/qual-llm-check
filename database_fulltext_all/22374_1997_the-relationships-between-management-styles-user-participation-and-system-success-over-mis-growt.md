# The relationships between management styles, user participation, and system success over MIS growth stages

Hsi-Peng Lu $^{1,*}$ , Jyun-Yu Wang

Department of Information Management, National Taiwan University of Science and Technology, Taipei, Taiwan, Republic of China

## Abstract

MIS growth can be viewed as an on-going process of organizational change. An MIS manager has to deal with people and lead them in different ways. Situations change as the MIS growth stages alter and thus each stage may require different management styles and participation–success relationships. This paper investigates the relationships between management styles, user participation, and system success over MIS growth stages. Surveying 172 information managers in different organizations, we found that the relationships between management styles, user participation, and system success are different over different MIS growth stages. Information managers are therefore assumed to be adaptable according to the requirements of MIS growth stages. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Management styles; User participation; MIS growth stage; Nolan's stage model; MIS success

## 1. Introduction

The relationships between user participation, management styles and system success are not straightforward, but complex and multi-faceted. To capture the complexity and transform it into usable knowledge for IS managers, a great deal of systematic research is required. This includes empirical studies investigating fundamental mechanisms driving user behavior which could increase our understanding of these relationships. The work presented in this paper is the result of one such study.

User participation is one frequently cited factor to overcome MIS development failure, because it may gain user commitment, avoid resistance and ensure that user requirements are met. The importance of user participation in MIS success has been studied extensively (e.g. [7, 11]). It seems to be causally related with MIS success. However, mixed results have been reported. For instance, Olson and Ives [55] reviewed 22 empirical studies published from 1959 to 1981. Only 8 (36%) of them showed a positive relationship between user participation and MIS success. After that, Cavaye's review [11] also indicated that only 7 (37%) of 19 empirical studies carried out between 1982 and 1992 show a positive participation–success link. One effort to explain the mixed results is to adopt a contingency approach. Several factors such as the organization, the environment, the task, personal and interpersonal characteristics, as well as MIS staff characteristics and policies have been identified as influencing user participation [1, 3, 4, 6, 13, 17, 20, 22, 23, 25, 26, 29, 34, 36, 37, 42, 44, 47, 48, 49, 50, 52, 61, 65, 68, 69]. One factor, MIS growth stages, is believed to be an important contingent determinant of participation outcome. Situations change over the MIS growth stages because of goal displacement, organizational learning, task demands variety, personal needs, and environmental forces. The first research question this paper tries to answer is – whether the effect of user participation on system success changes over MIS growth stages?

Management style is believed to be another determinant of system success and user participation. It is defined as management's way to influence, coordinate, and direct people's activities towards group objectives [2, 57]. The literature indicates that each phase of organization growth life style requires different managerial considerations [59]. Management style of effectiveness is also influenced by situations [19, 28, 66, 67, 24, 43]. For this reason, the management styles MIS managers are believed to be adaptable to promote user participation and system success according to the requirements of each MIS growth stage. The other research question of this paper is – what are the appropriate management styles to promote user participation and system success over the MIS growth stages?

## 2. Conceptual model and research hypotheses

In developing our model, we built on the theoretical frameworks used or suggested in previous research. Participative decision making provides the rationale for the hypothesized relationship between user participation and system success. Behavioral theory supports the hypothesized styles—participation and styles—success relationships. Organizational change theory and the contingency theory of leadership provide the theoretical basis for the hypothesized relationships between management styles, participation, and MIS success over MIS growth stages.

Fig. 1 presents the model examined here. It represents an integration of the theoretical perspectives and posits that MIS success is a function of user participation and management styles, and the degree of user participation is related to management styles.

## 2.1. Participative decision making

User participation in MIS development had been viewed as a special case of participative decision making (PDM) involving users and MIS managers as the participants rather than employees and superiors [15, 29, 51, 60, 65]. Positive relationships between user's degree of participation in decision making and decision satisfaction have also been empirically found by previous PDM research [27, 39, 62]. This would suggest that there is a positive relationship between user participation and MIS success. Accordingly, it is hypothesized that

H1: the degree of user participation will be positively related to system success at each of the MIS growth stages.

## 2.2. Behavioral theory

Behavioral theorists believe that we can identify management styles that differentiate effective from ineffective managers [46, 64]. It is possible to train people to be effective leaders. Many studies [10, 32, 35] have categorized management styles into people-oriented and task-oriented styles following the Leader Behavior Description Questionnaire (LBDQ) developed by the Ohio State University. People-oriented style is defined as the extent to which managerial behavior results in mutual trust, friendship, respect, and warmth. People-oriented managers consider interpersonal relations and accept individual differences among members. Task-oriented style refers to the extent to which a manager is likely to define and organize the tasks, standards, and manager–member relationships for goal attainment. Task-oriented managers tend to emphasize the technical or task aspects of the jobs. Behavioral theorists believe that a high-high manager (i.e. high on both people and task styles) would achieve high employee performance and satisfaction more often. However, a high-high style did not always yield positive results [38, 54]. For example, high task concern may lead to lower level of satisfaction in performing routine tasks, because of greater rates of absenteeism and grievance. High people concern may be negatively related to performance ratings of the managers by their superiors. Although mixed results have been found in behavioral research, behavioral theory still provides the theoretical basis for the following hypotheses:

H2: The degree of management styles will be related to user participation at each of the MIS growth stages.

H3: The degree of management styles will be related to system success at each of the MIS growth stages.

## 2.3. Organizational change theory and the contingency theory of leadership

A model of organizational change is useful because it identifies the stages and the possible issues a manager will face. Among the models, Lewin's three-stage model [40] of planned change is one of the most popular ones for promoting organizational development. Three stages were suggested: (1) Unfreezing is the time when people perceive the need to initialize and accept change, (2) Change is the process of moving systems from an old state to a new state, and (3) Refreezing occurs to set the new systems in permanent change.

By identifying the MIS growth stages or situations, MIS managers can develop appropriate managerial behaviors for their management. A successful manager is often the one whose style matches the requirements of the situation. For instance, House's path-goal theory [28] explains how the styles are influenced by the environment demands and subordinate characteristics. Vroom et al. [66, 67] proposed a normative leadership model to clarify leadership styles into five categories, from completely autocratic to completely participative, depending on the situation. Each MIS growth stage implies a particular situation and thus requires different management styles and degree of participation [43]. Managers good at the initial stage may not be as good at the control stage, where more structure is required. Thus, it is hypothesized that

H4: The management styles, user participation, and system success will be different over the MIS growth stages.

H5: The relationships between management styles, user participation, and system success will change over MIS growth stages.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Sample and procedure

The research sample was taken from members of the Association of Information Managers, who are working as IS managers in Taiwan. The questionnaire was sent to MIS managers of their companies, along with a cover letter assuring respondent confidentiality. Four hundred and ninety seven questionnaires were mailed. One hundred and seventy two questionnaires were returned. The response rate was therefore 34.6%. It includes 102 (59.3%) manufactures and 70 (40.7%) service businesses.

## 3.2. Measurement of variables

Management Style. Management styles were measured using the 16-item instrument developed by Sergiovanni et al. [63]. The instrument is based on the LBDQ which splits management styles into people and task-oriented. For each item, the scale ranged from(1) - never to (5) - always.

User Participation. User participation was measured with the instrument developed by Torkzadeh and Doll [16]. This 8-item instrument was found to have adequate reliability and criterion-related validity across a variety of applications. Since this research concerns user participation over MIS growth stages, it is appropriate to use an instrument that can be used for both end-user computing systems and traditional applications. This instrument meets our needs. For each item, the scale ranged from (1) - never to (5) - always.

System Success. There are many ways of viewing the system success. In this study, user satisfaction is the surrogate for system success and measured by an abridged version of Bailey and Pearson's questionnaire [5, 56] recommended by Ives et al. [30]. For each item, the scale ranged from (1) – disagree completely to (5) – agree completely.

MIS Growth Stages. This is defined as the overall status of an organization's MIS within its growth process. The level of MIS growth is often represented by Nolan's six benchmarks [53]: DP expenditure, technology, application portfolio, DP organization,

DP planning and control, and user awareness. The features of each stage are listed in Fig. 2.

This model describes MIS growth in stages:

1. At the initiation stage, the information systems meet basic organizational needs and computers are used by only a small number of users. System control and planning are lax.

2. At the contagion stage, computers or systems are adopted by many users. Application proliferation and expansion results in high cost, and the effect of lack of integration becomes visible.

3. The control stage has a high level of control. MIS development puts attention on system planning.

4. The integration stage puts more emphasis on integration of applications, and the use of the database.

5. The data stage focuses on data administration rather than applications. Some slack management is used to allow development of a system that contributes to the strategic advantage of the organization.

6. Finally, at the maturity stage the application portfolio is complete and matches the organizational objectives.

Many empirical studies have either tested or adopted the stage model as a surrogate measure of MIS growth [8, 9, 28, 42, 45]. In this study, the indicator of MIS growth level is based on the measure developed by Li and Rogers [41]. This instrument includes questions adapted from Nolan's second-level benchmarks [53], Gibson and Nolan [21], and Kanter [33].

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Scale reliability

The reliability of the responses to all instruments was assessed by means of the Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient [12]. The coefficient alphas for people-oriented management style, task-oriented management style, MIS growth, user participation, and MIS success are 0.85, 0.88, 0.70, 0.88, and 0.92 respectively.

## 4.2. MIS growth

On the basis of the composite benchmark, the average stage of this sample is 3.85 (standard deviation is 0.89). According to Nolan's growth stages, none of the firms is located at the first stage, and most of them are at the third stage and above. Fig. 3 shows the cumulative distribution. According to Fig. 3, there are two apparent transition points at 3.0 and 4.2 that separate MIS growth into Lewin's [40] three stages: (1) unfreezing or installation stage (score < 3.0), which includes Nolan's first two stages, (2) change or development stage (3.0 ≤ score ≤ 4.2), which roughly contains Nolan's control and integration stages, and (3) refreezing or maturity stage (score > 4.2), which contains the last two stages of Nolan's model. In this sample, 31 are at the initiation stage, 89 are at the development stage, and 52 are at the maturity stage.

## 5. Discussion

The findings of this study have several implications. The following sections discuss the implications and further research based on our findings.

## 5.1. Management styles and user participation over MIS growth stages

The results indicate that each MIS growth stage implies a particular situation and thus requires different management styles to promote user participation:

1. At the initiation stage, only people-oriented style is significantly positively relevant to user participation. The initiation stage often creates resistance. Managers need to make people perceive the need to initialize and accept change. At this stage, people-oriented managers can facilitate user participation because they care for user's feelings, are willing to compromise, accept loose control, accept suggestions, communicate easily, and show warmth to others.

2. At the development stage, not only people-oriented management style, but also task-oriented management style are significantly positively correlated with user participation. Existing applications need to be restructured and integrated from an organizational point of view. People-oriented managers promote trust and openness in relations and share goals with others. They avoid conflict and seek acceptance, but sometimes may be insecure. For this reason, procedures must be established and formalized. Task-oriented management style is also preferred.

3. At the maturity stage, both management styles no longer have a significant impact on user participation. Users are accountable for computer-resource use within the organization.

## 5.2. Management styles and system success over MIS growth stages

Management styles are related, not only to user participation, but also to system success over the MIS growth stages:

1. At the initiation stage, both management styles have nothing to do with system success. Here, the computer is introduced into the organization and users must learn the new technology. Due to lack of familiarity with the new technology, users usually learn the new technology by 'trial and error.' Although the people-oriented style is related to user participation, neither management styles affect system success and system satisfaction. At this time, information technology skills and training may be more important than management styles.

2. At the development stage, both people-oriented and task-oriented management styles have statistically positively significant relationships with system success. This stage emphasizes control and integration because of the cost crises resulting from application proliferation. Existing applications need to be restructured, documented, and integrated. Task-oriented management can facilitate document and development control. People-oriented management can facilitate an integrated view of system development. As a result, both management styles are positively and significantly correlated with system success (satisfaction).

3. At the maturity stage, both people-oriented and task-oriented management styles have statistically positively significant relationships with system success. This stage emphasizes data administration instead of application development. Data no longer belong to individuals but to the organization. Information becomes an organizational resource and it is used strategically and creatively. People-oriented managers tend to be creative, use data from multiple sources and consider many alternatives. Task-oriented managers set rules and procedures to administer data sharing within organizations. Both management styles are significantly positively related to system success.

## 5.3. User participation and system success over MIS growth stages

The relationships between user participation and success are not always statistically significant at the 0.01 level for all three stages. The lagged relationship may account in part for the mixed results of earlier studies of user participation on system success.

1. The initiation stage often creates resistance and is generally characterized by high risk, 'hands off' user attitudes, lax control and planning, application proliferation, and superficially enthusiastic user awareness. User participation can reduce resistance to change at the start-up phase and is positively and significantly related to system success at the 0.05 level of significance, but not as significant at the 0.01 level. Without appropriate technology skills and training, user participation itself is not strongly correlated with system success at the 0.01 level.

2. The development stage involves planning, analysis, coordination, and problem solving. User participation can increase mutual understanding, sense of control, and make the systems more successful.

3. At the maturity stage, information technology is used strategically. User participation can enhance innovation in strategic use of information technology, sense of system ownership, and then make the systems more successful.

## 6. Conclusion

This research attempted to answer two research questions: Do the effects of user participation on system success change over MIS growth stage? and What are the appropriate management styles to promote user participation and system success over the MIS growth stage?

The first question was tested by Hypotheses 1,4, and 5. The results of Hypotheses 1 reveal that user participation is not always significantly correlated with system success at the 0.01 level of significance, especially at the initiation stage that growth strategies and appropriate technologies may be as important as user participation. The belief that user participation will be strongly related with system success may not always be true. The major task at the initiation or unfreezing stage is to make different individuals perceive the need to initiate and accept change. Introducing change often creates anxiety and fear. Participative leadership can increase mutual understanding and feelings of system ownership, which may make the system more successful.

The second research question was tested by Hypotheses 2,3,4, and 5. The findings suggest that MIS managers have to be ‘adaptable’ over MIS growth stages. At the initiation stage, only people-oriented management style is suggested to promote user participation, and management styles have nothing to do with system success. At the development stage, both people-oriented and task-oriented management styles may be related to user participation and system success. At the maturity stage, management styles no longer have significant impact on user participation, but both styles are significantly correlated with system success because good social relationships and rules need to be maintained in the maturity stage. The results are consistent with the contingency theory of leadership. Are people adaptable? People usually behave on the basis of previous experiences. However, although individuals have different preferences in styles, they can be trained to be skilful if they acknowledge the contingency theories and are willing to change.

## 7. Limitations of this study

The results of this study should be interpreted and accepted with caution, since the survey data of this study have certain limitations. Statistical analysis only provides numerical relationships. Interpretation of those numbers is an author's subjective appraisal. The other limitation is generalizability. The subjects of this study are MIS managers in Taiwan. Cultural differences may exist between countries.

User participation and management styles to system success are complex and multifaceted. The results indicate that organizational factors, such as the relative growth stages of MIS may intervene and that such factors are worth pursuing in future studies. More systematical research investigating fundamental mechanisms driving user behavior can increase our understanding of those relationships.
