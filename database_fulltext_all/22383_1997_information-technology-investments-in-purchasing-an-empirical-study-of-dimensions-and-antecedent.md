# Information technology investments in purchasing: An empirical study of dimensions and antecedents

Ven Sriram $^{a,*}$ , Rodney L. Stump $^{a}$ , Snehamay Banerjee $^{1,b}$

$^{a}$ Earl G. Graves School of Business and Management, Morgan State University, Baltimore, MD 21251, USA $^{b}$ College of Business, Clark-Atlanta University, Atlanta, GA 30314, USA

Accepted 12 June 1997

## Abstract

Despite the fact that U.S. companies have made substantial investments in information technology (IT), there is a paucity of empirical research on the nature of IT investments and boundary conditions that determine the extent of these investments. This study reviews the extant IT literature, develops a series of propositions related to the multidimensional nature of IT investments and their relationship with several antecedent conditions, and presents the results of an empirical test of the propositions in the context of the purchasing function of U.S. firms. Our results support the premise that IT used in purchasing is not a homogeneous phenomenon, but can, instead, be represented by three dimensions: base computer systems and support, purchasing-specific applications, and vender communications. Our findings further indicate that IT dimensions respond in a varying fashion to different antecedent conditions. Support was also found for the building-block nature of IT investments. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Information technology; Purchasing; Heterogenous (multidimensional) phenomenon; Building block approach; Internal infrastructure

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, many companies have made and continue to make substantial investments in information technology (IT). U.S. government figures indicate that IT expenditures account for almost half of all durable equipment spending [37]. For U.S. businesses, investments in IT account for 2.5% of sales on average [35] and vary considerably by industry [52]. For example, Wett [57] reports that IT represented 10% of capital investments made by the chemical industry in 1970 and it is expected to reach over 30% in 1996. Similar evidence from Europe [5] and Canada [12] indicates that IT investments are perceived to be increasing in importance in other parts of the world.

Given the financial stakes involved, determining the impact of IT investments on performance and organizational processes has been and continues to be an important research concern for both academics and practitioners. In the last decade, research into IT issues has followed several different streams and evolved from anecdotal reports to more empirically-based studies. Along the way, concerns about research philosophies to be employed in investigating IT phenomena have been raised [44]. Early research chronicled the types of IT investments and the extent of their use (e.g. [56]), yet little attention has been paid to the antecedents of these investments. More recently, the focus has shifted to exploring IT's role as a source of firms' competitive advantages [34, 40] and the effect on financial performance [16, 42]. Farbey et al. [18] argue that there remains a need to investigate a more comprehensive array of the benefits of IT, such as the non-financial rewards (e.g. improved customer service, shorter lead times) that accrued to a small French company from its investments in electronic data interchange (EDI) and other information technologies [30]. Turning from the firm level, other researchers have explored the impact of IT use in functional areas such as sales [15], marketing [23, 49], and purchasing [53] to reduce costs, improve productivity, and improve exchange relationships.

While there is a growing body of IT research over the range of organizational functions, we concentrate our attention specifically on purchasing for this study. Purchasing is increasingly recognized by both academics and practitioners for the critical role it plays in organizational strategy and it is a functional area that receives significant management attention and resources $[36]$ . In the U.S. manufacturing sector, for instance, materials purchases comprise over 50% of each sales dollar on average $[54]$ . Reviewing the IT literature devoted to the purchasing and logistics area, we find that much of it has focused on investigating the impact of one aspect of IT: electronic data interchange (EDI) (e.g. $[4, 17, 29]$ ). Thus, it appears that further research into IT's, not merely EDI's, effect on purchasing is warranted.

In response to this gap, this paper takes a broader approach of examining IT investments, rather than solely those that are EDI-related, in an attempt to contribute to the increased understanding of IT investment behavior in the context of this important organizational function. Our basic premise is that, before we can fully determine the impact of IT on the purchasing function or more generally at the firm level, we first need to ascertain where these IT investments have been focused and also isolate the factors that have prompted the investments. Recognizing that previous studies have explored the multidimensionality of IT investments from the perspective of the firm, we extend such studies by positing that IT investment is also a multidimensional phenomenon when examined at the functional level and, more specifically, that this is an important trait of IT investments used in purchasing. Furthermore, we maintain that IT investment decisions are likely to be influenced by multiple motivational factors, as well as the characteristics of the investing firm and the function where the IT investments will be deployed.

## 2. Previous research

The IT literature in recent years has concentrated primarily on the consequences of IT investments, particularly with regard to value and success, performance, and intra- and inter-organizational impacts (e.g. [25, 50, 52]). While several past studies (e.g. [10, 32]) have recognized that a firm's general need for information resources is a function of its overall business strategy, they have not examined the specific factors that drive particular IT investment decisions.

While there are many inconsistent definitions of what constitutes IT, a growing consensus argues that IT should be defined broadly to encompass hardware, software, telecommunications (including voice, facsimile, and e-mail), as well as the personnel and resources dedicated to supporting IT $[51, 56]$ . However, anecdotal accounts from the trade literature and the limited number of empirical studies suggest that the actual types of IT investments made by organizations can vary considerably. The investments are often instituted on a project basis $[41]$ and may consist of different combinations of hardware, software, systems, and/or support and training $[28, 45]$ . Consequently, one important question that still needs to be addressed relative to the purchasing function is whether these components of IT are universally present, or instead, occur in distinct, though related, groupings; that is, is this a unidimensional or multidimensional phenomenon?

The extant literature suggests that IT investments may serve multiple purposes and that they may be implemented in a comprehensive, company-wide fashion, or more selectively to support one or more specific functions. Weill has argued that much of the IT investment research has erroneously made the assumption of IT homogeneity. He contends that IT can serve three distinct purposes: strategic (i.e. IT investments which aim to further a firm's competitive advantage and whose impact is reflected in market share growth, sales growth, etc.), transactional (i.e. IT investments which largely automate formerly labor-intensive procedures, such as payroll and order entry), and informational (i.e. IT investments which enhance the effective dissemination and use of data for decision making, such as decision support systems used in budgeting and planning). This informational role is often played when IT is employed to improve communication within organizations. For example, laptop computers allows British Airways' salespeople in the field to have access to the airline's computer system without having to go through desktop terminals [46]. Overall, this suggests that one or more purposes may be underlying the IT investment decisions of firms and these motivations should explicitly be included in models that examine the nature and consequences of IT investments.

Surprisingly, the results from empirical studies on the impact of IT investments on performance are quite equivocal. Kauffman and Weill's [31] review of IT performance studies has revealed that, in general, there is very little evidence of the value contributed by IT investments (e.g. [38]) at either the micro or macro level [3]. This contrasts with the results of individual studies that report a positive relationship between IT investments and performance (e.g. [26]). Taking the purpose of investing in IT into consideration, Weill has found that heavy use of transactional IT investments was significantly associated with financial performance, whereas heavy use of strategic IT was neutral in the long term and was associated with poor performance in the short term. Informational IT investments were not found to be significantly associated with financial performance. This suggests that IT investments that focus on improving the operational efficiency of a firm do positively impact the financial performance of firms, whereas the impact of other IT investments may not necessarily be reflected in financial performance outcomes.

This has lead Kauffman and Weill to conclude that studies of the value of IT expenditures have “failed to explain how and why IT creates leverage.” Such inconsistencies among the reported performance outcomes from IT investments are of great concern and need to be resolved. While the literature acknowledges the strategic necessity of IT and its value as a competitive weapon (e.g. [14, 19]), the specific influences behind an organization's decision to make such commitments have yet to be fully explored. We contend that IT investments arise from complex decision processes. Thus, recognizing the multiple motivations for IT investments and the nature of the IT investments actually made should help to resolve the inconsistencies in reported performance of IT investments.

## 3. Research propositions

Nature of IT Investments Used to Support Purchasing. While many researchers have focused on the consequences of making IT investments, the issue of just what types of investments are being made has not received sufficient empirical attention. Consistent with the broad definition of IT and Weill's taxonomy of general purposes, IT investments installed to support purchasing are not expected to be homogeneous, but instead, will be better represented operationally as a multidimensional phenomenon. IT investments related to purchasing can be undertaken, not only to enhance the performance of customary purchasing tasks, but also to facilitate intra- and inter-organizational interactions.

Purchasing plays a pivotal role in organizations, mediating the flow of critical production inputs into the firm. Increasingly, the purchasing function is viewed as an integral part of closely coordinated, cross-functional systems such as materials requirements planning (MRP) and just-in-time logistics (J-I-T), whose effectiveness can be enhanced by IT investments that serve to develop a shared internal information infrastructure. IT investments are also increasingly being used to augment and/or automate ordering processes and purchasing vendor evaluation and performance monitoring activities. Purchasing trade publications are replete with purchasing-specific applications, ranging from software programs to turn-key systems. A third arena where IT investments are being used to support the purchasing function is communications linkages with vendors, where traditional telephone messaging and transaction paper flows are being supplanted by on-line inquiries, e-mail, and EDI [11].

Therefore, our initial research proposition (P 1) reflects this potential for multiple foci:

## P1: Purchasing-related IT investment is not a unidimensional phenomenon.

Antecedents of IT Investments. One of the impacts of certain types of technology investments is that they change the nature of relationships. For instance, technology investments can facilitate the development of closer inter- and intra-firm relationships [53]. Increasingly, the adoption of a quality emphasis serves as the impetus for purchasing to develop closer relationships externally (e.g. with suppliers) and internally with other functional areas (e.g. production, logistics) and concurrently to invest in IT. Programs with a quality emphasis, such as Total Quality Management (TQM), Statistical Process Control (SPC), and J-I-T, all depend heavily on the dissemination of critical information in a timely fashion, often in real time. Consequently, our next research proposition (P 2) is:

P2: All forms of purchasing-related IT investments will be positively influenced by a firm's commitment to quality.

At the intra-firm level, IT investments are often made to standardize equipment and procedures. In many instances, the motivation is to increase the efficiency and control of internal processes. Typically, this increased efficiency will result in improved productivity and avoidance of costs for increased volume processing, with the indirect benefit of improving customer responsiveness. Such investments can also enhance internal communication efficiency and accuracy, which serve to facilitate cooperation between purchasing and other organizational functions. Thus, we propose:

P3: IT investments shared internally by purchasing and other functions and purchasing-specific applications will be positively influenced by the firm's desire to improve internal efficiency. This internal motivation should have no impact on the remaining form of purchasing-related IT investments.

Anecdotal evidence, as well as empirical findings, suggests that budgetary constraints are often important inhibitors of IT investments. Additionally, factors such as the availability of appropriately trained staff and adequate top management support also facilitate IT investments. Thus, the existence of adequate financial resources, staff and executive support, which are indicative of the organization's willingness and ability to engage in such commitments, would appear to be prerequisites to any IT investments [22, 39]. Hence, we posit:

P4: All forms of purchasing-related IT investments will be positively influenced by the extent of the firm's resources and commitment.

Bacon [1] has reported that 71% of the firms in his sample used legal/government requirements as a criterion in determining IT investments. Firms can be expected to respond to relevant laws and regulations at the federal, state, and local levels in order to achieve reporting compliance (e.g. sales and use taxes, hazardous materials, etc.) or to take advantage of tax incentives. Thus, we propose:

## P5: Governmental influence will positively impact all forms of purchasing-related IT investments.

Several authors have also argued that IT investments are often made because of competitive necessity and thus can serve as a means to gain a strategic advantage, or at a minimum to maintain competitive parity. In light of the recognition of purchasing's strategic importance, along with its high degree of operational integration with other functions, IT investments have the potential to enhance a firm's overall efficiency and effectiveness. Consequently, we propose:

P6: All forms of purchasing-related IT investments will be positively influenced by the firm's competitive climate.

Our final proposition deals with our supposition that purchasing-related IT investments are not approached in a piece-meal fashion, but instead tend to be undertaken in a distinct building-block pattern. Anecdotal reports gathered during field interviews suggest that installation of organization-wide IT, often networked PCs and general application software (e.g word processing, etc.) tends to precede IT investments in purchasing-specific applications. Additionally, a high degree of automation of internal purchasing activities appears to be a prerequisite for turning to EDI and related IT investments. We therefore propose:

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Measures and development procedures

After reviewing the extant academic and trade literatures related to IT use in purchasing contexts, as well as conducting a series of field interviews with purchasing professionals, measures were developed following the procedure recommended by Churchill [13]. Multi-item scales were developed for each of the variables included in our research questions. (The specific items are shown in Appendix A.) Single-item measures of relevant structural and contextual factors were also obtained for control purposes.

Dependent Variables. Using the broad definition of IT posed by Strassmann, Weill, and our knowledge of purchasing operations, we operationalized IT investments as a multidimensional phenomenon. Based on the IT and purchasing literatures and field interviews, we developed multi-item scales for three outcome variables: the shared internal infrastructure, or base computer systems (hardware and software) and support (BASE); purchasing-specific applications and support (PURCHASE); and vendor communications interface (VENDOR). Each of these represent particular dimensions of IT investments relevant to supporting different aspects of the purchasing function.

Independent Variables. Our five independent variables, quality assurance programs (QUALITY), departmental interactions (INTRAORG), organizational resources and commitment (COMMIT), governmental influence (GOVT), and competitive climate (COMPETE), represent the key contextual factors that are expected to influence a firm's decision to invest in purchasing-related IT.

Control Variables. Four additional single-item measures are included for control purposes: company size, as indicated by the number of employees (EMPLOYEE); annual purchasing volume (in millions of dollars) (PURCH\_ \$); number of active vendors being used (\#\_VEND); and dummy variables for industry group, based on the two-digit SIC classification, (SIC). $^{2}$

## 4.2. Context and data collection procedures

The empirical setting chosen for this research was U.S. firms representing multiple industry sectors. The sampling frame for this data collection consisted of the membership list of the National Association of Purchasing Management (NAPM). A sample of 2000 purchasing professionals was drawn from the NAPM list in proportion to the number of member firms representing each industry sector. Since the variables of interest in this study are social phenomena, those who subsequently participated did so in the role of key informants [47].

## 5. Validity assessments and tests of the research propositions

## 5.1. Construct validity

The validation procedures suggested by Churchill and Gerbing and Anderson [21] were followed. $^{3}$ We began by examining the item-to-total correlations and exploratory factor analysis loadings. Next, unidimensionality was assessed by including all of our multi-item scales in a single confirmatory factor model using EQS software [7] and the elliptical reweighted least squares (ERLS) estimation technique [48]. The factor loadings and fit statistics associated with this model are reported in Table 2. Overall, the fit indices show an adequate fit to the data [2, 8] ( $\chi^{2}=1056$ , df 444, p<0.01; Comparative Fit Index (CFI)=0.94), which allows us to conclude that each scale was measuring a single latent construct.

Having determined unidimensionality, the construct reliability of the multi-item scales was calculated. These estimates are all above the traditional benchmark for exploratory research [43].

In our original measurement model, each of the traits were allowed to correlate freely. To estimate discriminant validity, a series of additional models were estimated in which one intertrait correlation was constrained to unity in each model. The difference in chi square value between the original measurement model and each of the additional models was found to be significant: this provides evidence of discriminability.

## 5.2. Substantive tests

In addition to the alternate measurement models used to test discriminant validity, a final alternative measurement model was estimated; in this model the correlations among the three IT dimensions were all constrained to unity to assess whether our IT items were better represented as a single construct.

P1: The results of this alternate model showed a considerably worse fit to the data ( $\chi^{2}=1238$ , df 447, p<0.01; CFI=0.92). Furthermore, the difference in chi square value between the original measurement model and this unity model was found to be significant ( $\chi_{\Delta}^{2}=182$ , df $_{\Delta}$ 3, p<0.01); this provides additional evidence of the multidimensional nature of IT. Thus, our initial proposition is supported.

## 6. Discussion

Overall, our results are consistent with Weill, who has argued that IT investments are not a homogenous phenomenon. While we strengthen this argument, it must be recognized that we operationalized IT multidimensionality in the purchasing function differently from the categories defined by him. His IT dimensions are based on whether the investments served a strategic, transactional or informational purpose, whereas our dimensions reflected the scope of use: that is, to support inter-departmental interactions (BASE) by providing a standardized internal IT infrastructure, to foster intra-departmental operations (PURCHASE), and to support inter-firm communication and exchange (VENDOR). While the present study is largely exploratory, it provides empirical support for the premise that IT investments for supporting specific functions must be treated as a heterogeneous phenomenon.

The significant effect of quality assurance programs on purchasing-specific and vendor-related applications of IT is also consistent with the existing literature. Quality assurance programs typically involve high degrees of performance monitoring and more intense communications patterns inherent to the closer relationships being maintained with vendors: both of these can be facilitated by IT. Therefore, implementing programs like TQM will require IT investments for purchasing activities and for communication with vendors. However, quality programs also typically entail high levels of interdepartmental coordination, which makes the finding that quality programs had no effect on base systems somewhat surprising. One conjecture is that companies implementing such policies are forward-looking firms that may have already had substantial base systems in place.

The positive effect of inter-departmental interactions and organizational resources and commitment on base systems and purchasing-related IT is also consistent with the informational and transactional orientations suggested by Weill. For the former IT dimension, the installation of an electronic infrastructure to be shared by multiple functions is motivated by the desire to foster greater efficiency and control in routine inter-departmental interactions.

For the latter IT dimension, the information-intensive nature of inter-departmental operating and logistics systems, such as MRP and J-I-T, provides a logical rationale for the positive results we encountered. The effective implementation of such systems, in part, hinges on an organization's ability to closely scrutinize and disseminate performance data on many vendors; such data can be accessed through purchasing-specific application IT investments.

Interestingly, the expected positive impact of U.S. governmental influence was only found with IT investments dealing with vendor telecommunications and inter-firm networking, but not with the base systems or purchasing-specific applications. One possible explanation of government's minimal influence on base systems and purchasing-related IT investments is that recent changes in the tax code have drastically reduced the tax advantages associated with capital investments (in hardware and software), thus lessening this factor's motivational impact on such investments. However, in light of the significant changes in telecommunications technology (e.g. EDI, Internet, and the World Wide Web, etc.) and their increasing use in business and government, the role of government as a significant influence on vendor communications does not appear to be anomalous. While standards toward such technologies are still evolving and national policies to regulate these technologies are still being debated, many businesses may remain skeptical about investing heavily in this area of IT. However, the federal government has undertaken several initiatives in recent years to facilitate adoption of such technologies. For example, the Department of Defense is sponsoring the creation of electronic commerce research centers around the country, while the Small Business Administration is creating programs and support for small businesses adopting EDI. Moreover, many governmental units and agencies are moving toward requiring present and potential suppliers to have EDI capabilities as a condition for being certified as qualified vendors [24]. Similarly, the recent introduction of FACNET (Federal Acquisition Computer Network) and changes in laws like FASA (Federal Acquisition Streamlining Act) can also be expected to impact telecommunications-related investments. Thus, firms may be adopting EDI to ensure their status as qualified government vendors, hoping to later capitalize on potential synergies by having their purchasing function also forge these electronic linkages with their upstream suppliers. Therefore, it is quite possible to see a significant governmental influence solely on communications-related IT investments.

The apparent strategic necessity of IT investments is reflected by the strength of the competitive climate on two of the three dependent measures. Inherently, each of these IT dimensions can contribute to developing or enhancing the competitive positions of firms [6] by enabling greater responsiveness, cooperation, and control, as well as improving efficiency. However, while the existing literature has studied the subject from an organizational perspective, our study validates those conclusions for a specific function within organizations. Our findings also suggest that particular purchasing-related IT investments are sensitive to the firm's prior IT investment decisions. In particular, our results indicate that the extent to which an internal IT infrastructure already exists influences purchasing-specific application IT investments, which in turn impact the degree of vendor communications IT investments undertaken. Thus, it appears that investing in purchasing-related IT is not a discrete event; instead, it takes a building block approach that initially focused internally before venturing heavily into interfirm use.

With regard to our control variables, higher IT investments appear to be associated with larger firms; this finding is consistent with those of earlier studies and suggests that economies of scale and/or scope may be present.

Interestingly, neither overall purchasing volume nor the number of vendors had any significant effect on IT investment decisions. In light of recent trends toward buyers rationalizing their supply networks and concentrating their procurements with fewer vendors, these results may be explained. For firms developing strategic alliances with key suppliers, the impact on IT may be reflected in the intensity of specific relationships and concentration of purchases with critical vendors, not the overall procurement budget or the size of a firm's supply network.

Regarding the industry type control variable, we found a significant negative effect for DUMMY 1 and DUMMY 2 on vendor communications IT; this suggests that the Food and Beverages and Printing and Publishing sectors lag behind other industries in deploying IT links with vendors. This is somewhat surprising, given the highly automated production processes in both contexts.

The results of this study also have managerial relevance for both sellers and buyers of IT. For instance, there are important implications in developing and implementing marketing strategy. The antecedent variables can serve as additional bases to segment industrial markets for firms that market IT equipment, software, and systems. While the control variables included in this study encompass traditional, broad demographic variables, the substantive antecedents represent both operating variables and situational factors; these have been suggested by Bonoma and Shapiro [9] as criteria to use in developing more precise market segments. These variables could be used to determine which targets to select (e.g. firms that emphasize high quality); also, their influence on particular IT dimensions further suggests implications for positioning and designing the marketing mix. For example, to serve the needs of firms that are quality-oriented or require a high degree of internal coordination, an industrial marketer's product strategy could reflect an emphasis on bundled systems that allow more effective intra- and inter-firm interactions than stand-alone hardware or applications. However, as Webster [55] and others have noted, identifying where particular firms stand with regard to relevant operating variables or situational factors places a burden on the marketer to develop this information.

For buyers of IT, our results reflect the need to approach IT investment decisions for the purchasing function in a comprehensive fashion. Not only must IT investment decisions be based on the objectives, strategies, and tactics of the purchasing function, but they should also be sensitive to the firm's policies, strategies, and resources, its history of past IT investments, as well as the competitive and governmental sectors of the external environment.

## 7. Limitations and directions for future research

The results and implications drawn from this study are subject to the limitations of the research method. Our research considered only the purchasing function of U.S. firms. However, Fernie [20] reports that one of the reasons for the differences in the amount of stock held in the U.S. and U.K. grocery supply chain is the varying rates of adoption of information technology, such as EDI usage. Additionally, the influence of the government requirements as a factor in determining IT investments is likely to vary from country to country. Consequently, our ability to generalize the results of this study of IT investments behavior to other functions or other countries is limited.

While the tests of our model yielded results that were generally consistent with our research propositions, we also have not examined whether interrelationships exist between the antecedents.

To summarize, this study has contributed to our knowledge of information technology use by examining both the types of IT investments made to support the purchasing function and the antecedent factors that have determined them.
