# Testing the technology acceptance model across cultures: A three country study

Detmar Straub $^{a,*}$ , Mark Keil $^{a}$ , Walter Brenner $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Computer Information Systems Dept., College of Business Administration, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30302-4015, USA $^{b}$ Information Management Dept., Technical University of Freiberg, Gustav-Zeuner-Str. 10, Freiberg/Sachsen, 09596, Germany

Accepted 13 June 1997

## Abstract

In recent years, the technology acceptance model (TAM) has been widely used by IS researchers in order to gain a better understanding of the adoption and use of information systems. While TAM has been widely applied and tested in North America, there have been no attempts to extend this work to other regions of the world. Given the globalization of business and systems, there is a pressing need to understand whether TAM applies in other cultures. This study compares the TAM model across three different countries: Japan; Switzerland; and the United States. The study was conducted by administering the same instrument to employees of three different airlines, all of whom had access to the same information technology innovation, in this case, E-mail. The results indicate that TAM holds for both the U.S. and Switzerland, but not for Japan, suggesting that the model may not predict technology use across all cultures. The implications of these findings are discussed. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Technology acceptance model; Cross-cultural studies; E-mail; Information technology (IT) acceptance; IT diffusion; IT adoption; IT use

## 1. Introduction

Recently, there has been a highly prolific stream of theoretical research on the acceptance and use of information technology (IT). In this work, it has been argued that users develop perceptions about the usefulness and ease-of-use of various technologies and that these, in turn, influence actual system use. This model, known as the technology acceptance model (TAM), is widely regarded as a relatively robust theoretical model for explaining IT use. From a practitioner perspective, TAM is useful for predicting whether users will adopt new information technologies.

Replication of the original work by Davis [10, 11, 12] suggests that TAM may hold across technologies as well as across persons, settings, and times, the latter being a requirement for robust theories [4]. Reinforcing this robustness, TAM has also been shown to demonstrate good predictive validity [38].

In spite of the recent activity in understanding and applying TAM, this line of research has not yet been extended beyond the boundaries of North America. Given the ongoing rapid globalization of business and systems, there is a pressing need to learn how widely

TAM applies in other cultures around the world. Cultural differences that exist between different countries may affect a multinational organization's ability to adopt and utilize IT.

In the movement toward globalization of markets and corporate multinationalism, the value of more cross-cultural research is clear [3]. In a large survey of information systems executives conducted by Ives and Jarvenpaa, for example, more than half of the respondents felt that global IT issues were key to their firms' future success [17]. As firms grow their operations in the international arena, there is a need to exploit the power of IT to communicate among these widespread locations and to coordinate activities both within and across countries. Hence, it is important for the managers of these firms to learn, as much as they can, about the cross-cultural adoption and use of IT [6, 21]. Such knowledge can make the difference between success and failure in implementing IT.

The purpose of this research was to compare the TAM model across three different countries: Switzerland; Japan; and the United States. The paper represents an extension of Straub's [35] work in two ways. First, unlike his work, which examined the TAM constructs through bivariate relationships, this study employs multivariate statistics to test the TAM model, as articulated by Davis. Second, Straub examined two cultures, whereas the study reported here examines an additional culture in Europe.

## 2. Theoretical background and support for TAM

As originally articulated, TAM explains the behavior of computer usage. Derived from the theory of reasoned action (TRA) [2, 13], Davis' model describes the relationship between the independent variables of perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease-of-use (PEOU) and the dependent variables of user attitudes, intentions, and computer usage behavior. TAM was derived from TRA to model user acceptance of IT.

TAM posits that perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease-of-use (PEOU) affect the acceptance and use of IT. PU is the extent to which ‘people believe that the technology will help them perform their job better’ [11] while PEOU is ‘the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free of effort.’

Using different methodologies, numerous studies have found that PU and PEOU correlate well with IT acceptance across a wide variety of information systems. Table 1 lists some of the many empirical studies which have empirically tested TAM.

## 2.1. Possible effects of cultural differences on technology acceptance

While considerable research has been directed toward understanding the adoption and diffusion of IT in U.S. firms (for a review of this literature, see Ref. [27]), there have been only a handful of studies that specifically examine possible cultural effects on the adoption and diffusion of new information technologies (for example, Refs. [15, 23, 28, 30]). Research on the impact of corporate culture on IT acceptance provides a useful viewpoint [5, 22], but ethnic or national culture research undoubtedly has a special character. Nevertheless, based on the research that has been conducted to date, there is a reason to believe that connections do exist between culture and the use of certain information technologies.

## 2.2. Hofstede's cultural dimensions and their impact on technology acceptance

Hofstede's research [16] on cultural dimensions provides a theoretical foundation for exploring the impact of cultural differences on the adoption and diffusion of IT-based innovations such as E-mail. He describes four dimensions that can be used to distinguish among different cultures: power-distance; uncertainty avoidance; masculinity; and individualism. Table 2 lists the four dimensions along with a brief description of each.

## 2.2.1. Effect of uncertainty avoidance on technology acceptance

Hofstede defines uncertainty avoidance as: “the degree to which members of a society feel uncomfortable with uncertainty and ambiguity” [16]. Uncertainty avoidance could affect technology acceptance by influencing choices of computer-based media versus traditional media and cultural differences in uncertainty avoidance, specifically, could affect the way in which individuals choose media for their communication tasks. With respect to organizational communication behavior, prior research has focused specifically on uncertainty and ambiguity as principal factors in predicting media use.

Information richness theory [8, 9] posits that individuals choose a communication medium by matching the information requirements of the task to the information richness of the media. Richness in a medium is a function of the interactiveness of the feedback channel [40], the personal quality of the source, and the ability of the interface to express innuendoes. For tasks high in uncertainty and ambiguity (for example, negotiation and conflict resolution), it is thought that people will use rich channels, such as face-to-face communication. For tasks low in uncertainty and ambiguity (for example, information memos and simple information exchange), simpler channels suffice. Electronic media such as E-mail are believed to be rather simple channels because they carry only verbatim information and not the personal presence or force of the communicator.

An established theory which closely parallels this line of reasoning is social presence theory $[32]$ . Like information richness theory, this theory posits that media are chosen for specific types of interaction and for how well media ‘fit’ task information requirements. Media differ in social presence in ‘the degree to which a medium permits users to experience others as being psychologically present’ $[14]$ . Socially-present media, thus, are known for their ability to ‘transmit information about facial expression, direction of looking, posture, dress and non-verbal cues’ $[32]$ . This theory maintains that when a task involves interpersonal interaction and also is sensitive (for example, negotiation or a conflict resolution), then media with high social presence like face-to-face interaction will be selected, but that, for a less sensitive task (for example, straight-forward information exchange), the social presence of the medium is not as important $[29, 31, 33]$ .

Both information richness and social presence theories would predict that individuals choose media on the basis of how well those media reduce uncertainty. Since rich media, such as face-to-face communications, use multiple channels (for example, visual and auditory channels), these media support tasks in which uncertainty is high. Computer-based media (for example, E-mail) to date have been less socially present channels and hence do not support tasks that involve a high degree of uncertainty.

In sum, one effect on media choice is that cultures in which individuals tend to avoid uncertainty (that is, high uncertainty avoidance index, or UAI) are expected to use electronic media less often since these media are not well suited to uncertainty reduction as face-to-face and other rich channels. Knowledge workers in high UAI cultures should therefore perceive computer-based media to be less useful and harder to use than those in low UAI cultures. Moreover, for high UAI cultures, it is reasonable to assume that the TAM will be a weaker predictor of technology use than it would be in low UAI cultures.

## 2.2.2. Effect of other cultural dimensions on technology acceptance

There are reasons to believe that the other three cultural dimensions identified by Hofstede could have a specific bearing on technology acceptance, especially for communications support technologies such as groupware, E-mail, Voice-mail, and video-conferencing.

The case for the possible impact of Hofstede's other three cultural dimensions on IT use can be built by looking at the interaction between each of these dimensions and distinguishing characteristics of information richness/social presence theory. The argument stresses the role of culture in organizational communication patterns and the effect this role has on media choice. Specifically, our belief is that high cultural value placed on large power distances between managers and workers (high PDI), assertiveness in social interactions (high MAS), or achieving collectivist and group goals (low IDV) will result in a more extensive use of rich channels requiring high social presence media. By the same token, this cultural profile will incline knowledge workers in these cultures to avoid less socially present channels such as E-mail. These relationships are shown in Fig. 1.

## 2.2.3. Effect of power distance on technology acceptance

Power distance should have a marked bearing on the communication patterns of knowledge workers and their managers. In societies in which managers and workers are separated by a large power distance, the leveling effect of computer-based media is not seen or felt as a desirable feature. Ho et al. [15], for example, found that anonymity is not a desirable characteristic of communications for the cultures they studied. In high PDI cultures, individuals may show deference to authority by refraining from using media that do not allow them face-to-face contact (or, at the very least, access to a richer set of cues than simple textual messaging).

## 2.2.4. Effect of individualism on technology acceptance

Low individualism (low IDV) possibly predisposes a culture against computer-based communications because these media mute the group effect $[15]$ . Knowledge workers in collectivist cultures cannot pick up cues about the social situation as readily from computer-based media and, therefore, would be inclined, overall, toward media such as face-to-face across all communications tasks.

## 2.2.5. Effect of assertiveness on technology acceptance

Finally, assertiveness (high MAS) is highly related to interpersonal presence. Again, it can be predicted that media not conveying the social presence of the communicator, such as E-mail, would not be favored in cultures in which masculinity is a strong cultural value. In cultures which are less assertive, face-to-face media and those media with rich interactional cues are not as necessary for communicating the overall message of the communicator. Less socially present media should, therefore, be more acceptable.

## 2.2.6. Summary of hypothesized effects of cultural dimensions on TAM predictions

It has been argued that TAM predictions will not necessarily hold across cultures. Table 3 shows how the countries selected for this study differ in terms of Hofstede's cultural dimensions of: (1) uncertainty avoidance; (2) power distance; (3) individualism; and (4) assertiveness (that is, MAS) as well as an index composed of these values, the computer-based media support index (CMSI).

## 2.3. TAM predictions for culture: The computer-based media support index

We have combined Hofstede's indices to create a 'Computer-based Media Support Index' (CMSI) for selected cultures. The purpose of this straightforward, linear index is to mathematically express the simultaneous effect of all four Hofstede dimensions on the acceptance of E-mail by different cultures. It should only be viewed as a useful approximation.

To calculate this index, UAI, PDI, and MAS were added to 100-IDV, in keeping with our argument that IDV moves in the opposite direction from the other scales in its effect on perceptions and use of computer-based media (see Fig. 1).

As can be seen by the CMSI values in Table 3, the U.S. anchors one end of the scale while Japan occupies the other end. Switzerland occupies the middle position, but is closer to the U.S. than to Japan. Based on the ordinality revealed through this index and the dimensions of culture, it is possible to predict whether a given culture would support a TAM description of E-mail use.

## 3. Research method

Three countries with highly different Hofstede profiles – the U.S., Switzerland and Japan – were selected to test the TAM model across cultures. In particular, since Japan and the U.S. differ markedly on several of Hofstede's dimensions and, thus, on our CMSI index, one might expect to observe significant differences in the applicability of TAM across these two cultures. Specifically, we hypothesize that TAM would be able to predict the American experience, consistent with numerous other studies, but that it would not be able to predict well Japanese use of E-mail.

As a country in another region of the world, Switzerland represents a middle position on the continuum of cultural values. Fig. 2 shows that the U.S. and Swiss indices track relatively well together and that the CMSI for Switzerland and the U.S. are also closer (absolute value of [Swiss–U.S.: 204 – 157] = 47) than either are to the Japanese index (absolute value of [Swiss–Japanese: 204 – 287] = 83; absolute value of [U.S.–Japanese: 157 – 287] = 130).

## 3.1. Choice of technological innovation and research sites

A survey was developed and administered to explore the TAM model across different cultures. In studying the acceptance of IT innovations across different cultures, it is useful to examine technologies that markedly demonstrate different adoption patterns so that hypotheses explaining these differences can be formulated and tested. From a management perspective, it is also valuable to study technologies that are positioned to achieve major organizational impacts. An electronic communication technology such as E-mail meets both of these criteria and was therefore selected.

In the U.S. as well as in parts of Europe, E-mail has been diffused all over the workplace during the past decade and is predicted to be a significant force for change throughout the 1990s [37] and to the end of the millennium. In Japan, however, E-mail has remained in an early growth phase [18, 19]. There has been speculation, in fact, that E-mail will never diffuse widely throughout Japanese corporations. Hence,

E-mail is well suited for study, because its diffusion pattern has varied greatly across different countries.

Moreover, E-mail is suitable for study in that it streamlines organizational communications, improves coordination, and dramatically increases productivity $[7, 25]$ . Finally, previous research has shown that cultural differences between North America and Asia can be pronounced.

Knowledge workers from three major companies participated in the research. The firms were matched by industry and size to control as much non-cultural variance as possible. Moreover, all three companies had significant experience using E-mail and individuals' use of the technology in each firm was on a voluntary basis. A comparison of the firms including sample characteristics, are shown in Table 5. The sample in all cases was drawn from workers across the managerial, professional, technical, and clerical ranks.

## 3.2. Choice of dependent variable

Consistent with Davis' own practice and with most of the prior research in this area, system use was selected as the dependent variable. As Table 6 shows, the vast majority of tests conducted on TAM examine the direct effects of PU and PEOU on system use and they do not measure or test the mediating effects of attitude toward use and intention to use. Thus, following Davis and others, we have tested a parsimonious version of TAM that focuses on the constructs that are believed to be central to the model.

## 3.3. Instrument validation

This study employed the same instrument as Straub [35]. The survey instrument was pretested with 32 knowledge workers in 17 different organizations across a broad organizational spectrum in the U.S. and Japan. The pre-test was designed to refine constructs and measures. The final result was an instrument that could be said to be qualitatively valid [34]. $^{2}$ Prior to being administered in Japan and Switzerland, the survey instrument was translated into Japanese and German. In each case the translation was reviewed by a third party to insure that the meaning of the questions was preserved.

To test construct validity, a principal components factor analysis of the usefulness and ease of user items (with Varimax rotation) was performed. Two factors emerged with no cross-construct loadings above 0.50, indicating good discriminant validity. The instrument also demonstrated convergent validity with factor loadings exceeding 0.50 for each construct.

## 3.4. Scale reliabilities

Individual items measuring the constructs were combined in order to form scales. These measures were then tested for reliability to assess the internal consistency of the measurement instrument (Straub 1989). The analysis was performed across the entire database (that is, all three countries combined) and across each individual country dataset. The results of the reliability analysis for the PU and PEOU measures are shown in Table 7.

As the table indicates, reliability of the scales were generally above 0.8. All the values are acceptable by the 0.60 standard for exploratory research proposed by Nunnally [26].

Linear regression was used, with system use as the dependent variable and PU and PEOU as independent variables.

## 4.3. Limitations

Given the differences in culture that are believed to exist between the three countries, it is tempting to conclude that cultural differences alone explain the results obtained. Such a conclusion, however, would be premature. Consistent with other exploratory work in this area, the study did not directly measure cultural dimensions. Thus, it is not possible to say with certainty that a link between cultural factors and technology has been empirically established.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

Although this study has limitations, it represents a contribution to the IT literature because it is one of the first attempts to validate the TAM model outside the North America. It: (1) compares knowledge worker perceptions and use of the same technology in three countries located on three continents; (2) bases its predicted differences between these cultures on prior theoretical work; and (3) tests the TAM model in three technically similar environments.

TAM does provide explanation for IT adoption and use in two of the three countries studied (the U.S. and Switzerland). Reasoning generally from the meanings and values underlying Hofstede's indices, we predicted that TAM would be successful in explaining the U.S. and Swiss experience but not the Japanese experience. This effect may occur in spite of the rational usefulness found in the medium. In case of Japan, cultural tendencies toward more uncertainty avoidance, greater power distances between managers and workers, collectivist sentiments, and assertiveness may limit E-mail use and disassociate usefulness from use.

Although the results of the study should be interpreted with caution, they suggest that the TAM model may not hold equally well across cultures. If these results prove to be reproducible in other studies, this finding holds significant implications for both IT researchers who wish to study the adoption and diffusion of innovations and IT practitioners who want to successfully implement systems across borders.

As firms internationalize, there is a growing need to understand how cultural factors might affect a multinational organization's ability to adopt and utilize IT. Further studies may lead to knowledge that will help IT researchers and practitioners in improving the technology transfer process. Such knowledge will be valuable to practitioners, because it could spell the difference between success and failure in implementing IT.
