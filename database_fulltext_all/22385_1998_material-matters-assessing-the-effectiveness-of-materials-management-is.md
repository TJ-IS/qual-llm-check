# Material matters: Assessing the effectiveness of materials management IS

Kristi Yuthas $^{a,*}$ , Scott T. Young $^{1,b}$

$^{a}$ Anderson School of Management, University of New Mexico, Albuquerque, NM 87131, USA $^{b}$ College of Business, KDGB, University of Utah, Salt Lake City, UT 84112, USA

Accepted 17 July 1997

## Abstract

Materials management information systems (IS) are designed to enhance decision-making performance by lowering costs, increasing turnover, and improving service. Because of the large investment that these systems represent, companies have developed several ways to evaluate their effectiveness. The most direct approach is to assess the effects of the system on materials management performance outcomes such as inventory costs, turnover, and fill rates. The more common approach is to assess effectiveness via substitute measures, such as user perceptions and usage statistics. A laboratory experiment was conducted to examine the relationships between materials management performance, user satisfaction, and system usage. The three measures were recorded as subjects performed a purchasing task using an materials management IS. Correlations among the three measures suggest that although satisfaction and usage are closely associated with performance, the relationships among the measures are not sufficiently strong to warrant their use as interchangeable measures of effectiveness. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Materials management; Purchasing; IS effectiveness; IS success; IS assessment; User satisfaction; Decision-making performance; System use

## 1. Introduction

Materials managers rely extensively upon computers to help to make important inventory decisions. Information systems (IS) for materials management include a variety of software modules, depending upon the nature of the inventory. Independent demand inventory systems include inventory control, purchasing, and electronic data interchange (EDI), while dependent demand inventory systems add manufacturing resource planning (MRP II) packages that include master production scheduling, capacity management, production activity control and materials requirements planning. Implementation of these systems require major investments in labor and capital. Many inventory systems have failed due to the lack of top management support, insufficient training, unsatisfactory maintenance, etc. These problems can lead to inadequate or incorrect information and ultimately to poor performance [28].

IS play a vital role in the materials management function by providing timely and accurate information necessary for the accomplishment of decision-making goals. Materials managers use systems to monitor their stock status, to alert themselves to critical shortages, and trigger purchase orders when reorder points are reached. They rely extensively on these reports, often consulting these information sources rather than touring the warehouse. The critical nature of purchasing decisions necessitates a measurement approach to evaluate IS effectiveness. Managers must be able to determine whether and to what extent IS are helping to achieve decision-making goals, such as reduction of inventory costs.

IS researchers have used various approaches to study system effectiveness, but have not agreed upon one representative construct $[2, 3, 22]$ and at least 180 measures of effectiveness have been used $[7]$ . IS developers are concerned with how well a system supports the users. Developers therefore often measure system performance by examining user perceptions or attitudes toward the system as well as behaviors, such as system usage. For the materials manager, the most important system outcome may be decision performance – the actual quality of the decisions supported by the system. Therefore, unless perceptual measures, such as user satisfaction, and behavioral measures, such as system usage, provide good approximations of decision performance, direct measures of performance will provide more meaningful metrics of system effectiveness for the materials manager. The purpose of this paper is to examine the relationships among commonly used IS effectiveness measures to determine whether user satisfaction and system usage are appropriate substitute measures for materials management performance.

## 2. Background

IS effectiveness has been widely used as the dependent variable in IS research. This variable has generally been operationalized using three measures: decision performance; user satisfaction; and system usage [29, 30, 35]. Because IS are generally designed to provide information to support decision making, decision performance is the most direct measure of effectiveness. Satisfaction and usage are also widely used to evaluate effectiveness; they are relatively easy to gather and are thought to provide information useful to system developers and managers. Some researchers argue that IS effectiveness is multidimensional – that satisfaction, usage and performance are related but distinct constructs, and should be measured accordingly [4, 10, 19]. Others argue that user satisfaction and system usage are appropriate measurements of a system's support for decision making and can therefore be used as surrogates for decision performance [1, 8, 12].

While the appropriateness of the satisfaction and usage measures has been questioned, a large number of studies have used these measures as surrogates for decision performance. More importantly, practitioners and researchers have based conclusions about the effectiveness of systems and investment recommendations on the assumption that the surrogates are appropriate.

It has become increasingly important to develop a method for evaluating the effectiveness of materials management IS because of the large financial and labor investments in materials management systems and the high risk of implementation failure. Several approaches are available for assessing system performance in this domain. Four types of performance measurement systems are commonly found in purchasing organizations: (1) efficiency-oriented systems; (2) effectiveness-oriented systems; (3) multiple objective systems; and (4) naive systems [9]. The effectiveness-oriented system has been recommended over others because it focuses on contribution to profit, supplier relations, and customer satisfaction, and because it forces the purchasing organization to take a more holistic perspective of the firm. The effectiveness perspective is consistent with current organizational evaluation approaches that advocate linking performance measurements directly to the vision and strategy of the firm, resulting in a 'balanced scorecard [15].'

Young [33] has suggested that the four most commonly used performance measures for effectiveness-oriented systems are those designed to assess the outcomes associated with IS use. Measures such as inventory turnover, fill rates, average stock levels, and labor costs may be used to assess the cost-effectiveness of materials management systems. Such outcomes are direct measures of system quality, but they are often difficult to assess, because they are also influenced by factors outside the system, including the talents and abilities of the materials management staff.

Consequently, it has become common to evaluate materials management IS using measures of user satisfaction and system usage. These measures are easy to obtain, and provide useful information about some aspects of system performance. However, high levels of satisfaction and system use do not guarantee that the system actually increases management effectiveness. One study in a purchasing setting revealed a negative relationship between system usage and materials management performance $[33]$ . It showed that the use of materials management IS may not lead to better performance if not accompanied by a successful implementation and maintenance program. A small number of studies in other business settings using multiple performance measures have found positive correlations among the measures $[11, 21, 34]$ ; however, these relationships have not been strong.

The purpose of this study was to determine whether user satisfaction and system usage are appropriate indicators of decision-making effectiveness in a controlled experimental setting. Two hypotheses were posited:

1. User satisfaction with the IS is a valid substitute for materials management performance.

2. IS usage is a valid substitute for materials management performance.

## 3. Method

To test these hypotheses, a computerized inventory system was developed and used as the basis for a laboratory experiment involving student subjects. This methodology was deemed appropriate because it offered the maximum amount of control over factors that could potentially weaken or obscure the relationships among the three effectiveness measures. It therefore provided a strong test of the equivalence of measures. If all three measures are estimating a single construct (the system's effectiveness in supporting decision making), strong relationships among the measures should be more easily detectable in a tightly controlled setting with homogeneous subjects.

## 3.1. Subjects

Fifty-nine undergraduate business students served as the subjects for the experiment. All subjects had completed a core of business courses and had GPAs of 3.0 or higher. They were enrolled in an MIS course that is generally taken during the first term after students are admitted to the upper-division business program.

The choice of subjects limits the external validity of the study, since it is not clear whether students respond differently than their professional counterparts. The question of the validity of student responses in experimental settings has been discussed for many years. Some studies have found no differences between students and professional business managers [5, 17], while others have found differences [6]. In this study, high internal validity and control over factors that might cause the dependent variable measures to diverge were deemed critical. The decision-making task was carefully pretested and found to be sufficiently straightforward to allow subjects to complete the experiment without great difficulty. The students were also relatively homogeneous (as evidenced by demographic statistics gathered) in terms of age, educational background, and business experience – factors that could disparately affect the three dependent variables and therefore provide false support for the hypotheses. The realism of the task and the IS enhances the degree to which the results can provide the basis for meaningful conclusions.

The experiment was conducted during the normal lab hours for the MIS course. Participation was voluntary and all subjects received extra credit for completing the experiment. Prior to the experiment, students were notified that the names of the students who performed best on the task would be posted in the lab. Exit interviews indicated that students were interested in the experiment and motivated to do well.

## 3.2. Task

The experiment required subjects to assume the role of materials manager for a small catering company dealing with highly perishable inventory. The task required subjects to prepare a purchase order to meet the company's anticipated inventory needs for one day. To perform the task, subjects estimated the day's demand for each inventory item and identified the lowest-cost supplier for each required item. Poor estimation of demand resulted in stockout or holding costs. Poor supplier identification resulted in inflated inventory costs.

In order to help in performing the task, each subject received a variety of written reports and, in addition, had the option of accessing additional information via the materials management system. Written reports contained all information necessary for successful completion of the task. Written reports included: (1) a listing of inventory items that included the average daily demand for each and an estimate of whether demand was increasing, decreasing, or steady; (2) a supplier price list; and (3) a listing of special orders for items (special orders were not included in average daily demand) and the dates on which these orders were needed.

All subjects also had the option of using the materials management IS to access additional computerized reports. These reports were either more logically organized or more detailed than written reports. In the computerized reports, for example, price lists were arranged in order by item number rather than by supplier (as on the written version) so that the lowest cost supplier for an item could be more easily identified; estimated demand was presented as a listing of actual daily demand figures rather than averages, which could presumably lead to more accurate demand estimates.

## 3.3. Treatment and procedures

Subjects were randomly assigned to two experimental treatment groups: a high- and a low-complexity task group. The treatment was used to provide some assurance that the relationships among the effectiveness measures were not significantly affected by the complexity of the task. It is possible, for example, that increased complexity could result in reduced decision effectiveness, or that it could affect a subject's use of the system or satisfaction with its support [24, 25].

The basic task performed by the two groups was identical, as was the materials management IS to which the subjects had access. The optimal solution to each task was also the same for both groups. The difference between the treatments was in the written reports. All the information necessary for completing the task was provided to both groups; however, the reports contained important differences that affected the ease with which subjects could perform the task. Relative to the reports provided to the low-complexity group, reports provided to the high-complexity group were either lengthier, containing extra items not needed to perform the task, or were arranged in an order less conducive to task completion. Examples of high- and low-complexity versions of one report are shown in Appendix A, along with the computerized version of the report.

The experiment was conducted in five steps. Subjects first received instructions on how to use the materials management IS, along with information about their role as a materials manager and the requirements of the task. Subjects used these instructions to practice using the system to assist them in preparing a purchase order. The purpose of this step was to familiarize them with the system and task. The next three steps required subjects to work through the three rounds of the task, presented to them in random order. Each round required the subject to prepare a purchase order for the materials needed for a particular day's operation of the catering business, using written and computerized reports as they deemed appropriate. The first two rounds allowed subjects to learn how to perform the task, and the third was used as the basis for statistical analyses. The final step of the experiment required subjects to complete questionnaires. The questionnaires contained items about their satisfaction with the system, demographic information, and their perceived effort in performing well on the task.

## 3.4. Measures

Four measures of effectiveness were gathered during the experiment: decision-making performance; user satisfaction; system usage time; and system usage report volume. Decision performance or effectiveness is generally determined by accuracy (objective) or quality (subjective) [16]. For this task, the goal was to obtain the lowest possible cost for the inventory items needed, and an optimal cost could be objectively determined for each task. Materials management performance was determined by the subject's ability to minimize the combined product, holding, and stockout costs. Each subject's performance score (Performance) was calculated as the percentage by which the subject's costs exceeded the minimum attainable inventory cost. The sign on the performance scores was reversed, resulting in a negative number. This was done so that a higher numerical score would represent higher decision performance.

The second measure, system usage, is a behavioral variable that is often obtained unobtrusively by the IS. Measuring usage is generally straightforward, however, for usage to be a valid indicator of the system's perceived usefulness, usage must be voluntary $[26, 32]$ , as it was in this experiment. The usage definition most commonly employed in empirical research is that proposed by Robey $[26]$ – the extent and nature of use of an IS. Since that time, frequency and extent of use have been widely used as indicators of IS effectiveness in empirical studies. System usage was operationalized using two standard measures designed to ascertain the level of interaction between the user and the system. For each task iteration, the system recorded: the number of minutes a subject spent accessing computerized reports (Time) and the total number of reports accessed (Reports).

While measurement of decision performance and system use are relatively straightforward, measuring user satisfaction is more difficult. Although user satisfaction is perhaps the most widely used measure of IS effectiveness, no standard measure exists $[13, 23]$ . Measures focus on a broad range of system features, including quality of the information provided, quality of physical features of the system, and quality of the support provided by the IS department. Many measures of satisfaction used in the IS literature have been criticized for their failure to meet standards of validity and reliability $[20, 23, 31]$ . For this study, it was necessary that the satisfaction measure exhibit both relevance and rigor. Relevance is the degree to which the measure addresses the construct of interest – materials management IS effectiveness as evidenced by improved materials management decision making. Of the large number of satisfaction measures that have been developed, only a small subset focus directly on decision-making outcomes $[7, 18]$ . Two measures focus directly on the degree to which the system is perceived by users to support decision making $[14, 27]$ . Of these, the Jenkins and Ricketts MIS Satisfaction Questionnaire, shown in

Appendix B, was selected for use in this study due to its rigor.

This instrument has been widely used in previous IS effectiveness research, it is well-grounded in the theoretical literature, and has been rigorously validated. It was designed specifically for turnkey or end-user-oriented information systems, and is therefore appropriate for evaluation of the materials management system used in the experiment. Unlike like other widely used measures, the questionnaire contains only items related to satisfaction with the system and excludes items related to support provided by the IS department. Most importantly, the questions in the instrument focus directly on the degree to which the system supports or improves decision making, and the instrument could, therefore, be expected to correlate more strongly with decision performance than other satisfaction measures. The user satisfaction score (Satisfaction), was operationalized as the sum total of a subject's responses to the questionnaire's 25 Likert-scaled items on the MIS Satisfaction Questionnaire.

## 4. Results

Table 1 shows the average performance, usage (time and number of reports), and satisfaction scores for the two groups. Scores obtained for both groups were very similar, especially in the third round of the materials management task, that is, after subjects had become familiar with the system. For the task used in this experiment, the difficulty of the problem did not have a large effect on the IS outcomes, as subjects in both groups became very proficient at the task by the third round.

To answer the original research question regarding the appropriateness of satisfaction and usage as substitutes for performance, correlations among the outcome measures were calculated. Correlations among perfect substitute measures would be 1.0. In this experiment, perfect correlation could not be expected. The questionnaire shows an internal consistency score of 0.85 (obtained using Chronbach's alpha analysis); thus, 0.85 is used as the maximum likely correlation in the subsequent analysis of results.

For subjects in both difficulty groups, the measures are strongly related to one another. This finding suggests that system use and satisfaction are good indicators of materials management performance. Thus, for instance, one might expect a manager who uses the system heavily and perceives it to be useful to outperform colleagues who rely on the system to a lesser extent. Based upon the correlations found, high decision performance is associated with high user satisfaction and usage.

The findings do not, however, suggest that the measures are so closely related that they can be reliably used as substitutes. The relationships among the measures in this experiment are relatively weak. All the correlations between performance and other measures are significantly lower than the maximum expected correlation of 0.85. While the measures do tend to move in the same direction, heavy users who are satisfied with the system are not necessarily the ones who perform best when using it. Similarly, highperforming users are not necessarily satisfied with the system. Therefore, like other factors, such as education and experience which are commonly associated with high materials management performance, system usage and user satisfaction are related to performance, but not synonymous with it. These indicators are not, therefore, appropriate substitutes for measuring performance directly.

## 5. Conclusion

IS are rapidly becoming an integral part of materials management in many organizations. Evaluating these systems is consequently an important consideration for firms that invest in these systems and rely upon the information they provide. Although the most direct evaluation approach is to examine the impact of the systems on the performance of materials managers, it is more common to collect user opinions and usage statistics. Managers and researchers using these measures implicitly assume that satisfaction with a system or frequent use of a system will ultimately translate to better decision making and ultimately add value to a firm.

This experiment has shown that examining satisfaction and usage measures is not an acceptable alternative to measuring performance directly. Although the three variables are correlated, the relationships between them are not sufficiently strong to warrant their use as substitutes for one another. These results have important implications for practitioners in the field of materials management as well as for IS researchers seeking to identify factors influencing IS effectiveness.

Although measures of satisfaction and use may be useful for evaluating the IS department and past system investments, these measures should not be used as direct measures of performance. If a company wishes to determine the effect of an IS on materials management performance, direct measures such as turnover, fill-rates, and inventory costs are appropriate. If satisfaction and usage are employed as substitutes for these measures, the probable result is a set of poor decisions regarding the system's ability to support these organizational objectives. Such misinterpretations could result in the development of inappropriate or overly complex systems, or in the continued use and maintenance of unproductive ones.

These results also have important implications for IS research. If a researcher wishes to study the effects of organizational, situational, or systems characteristics on IS performance, but uses a substitute measure, the conclusions drawn from the research can be misleading or erroneous. Since effectiveness measures cannot be used interchangeably, IS effectiveness must be clearly defined before research can be conducted and reliable conclusions can be drawn. Conflicting results have been found in numerous studies regarding the factors that promote IS effectiveness. Results of our experiment suggest that a careful analysis of the measures used may provide insights into the relationships between independent variables and various facets of IS effectiveness.

## Items in Jenkins–Ricketts instrument

(All items were rated on a seven-point scale. Anchors for items #1-24 were ‘strongly disagree’ and ‘strongly agree,’ anchors for item #25 were ‘very dissatisfied’ and ‘very satisfied’.)

1. The contents of the reports from this MIS were very accurate.

2. The MIS clearly indicated when bad decisions were being made.

3. The reports from this MIS were very easy to understand.

4. The procedures and instructions for inputting data were very easy to use.

5. The reports from this MIS were very useful for identifying and defining the problem.

6. The reports delivered by the MIS were very well formatted.

7. The computer system supporting this MIS appeared to be very reliable.

8. The reports from this MIS were very useful in forming solutions to the problem.

9. The MIS clearly indicated when the real problems had been found.

10. The reports delivered by this MIS were available at the right time.

11. The procedures and instructions for inputting data prevented many errors.

12. The reports delivered by this MIS contained enough information.

13. The MIS clearly indicated when good decisions were being made.

14. The MIS always delivered reports in the best mode.

15. The procedures for inputting data were easy to understand.

16. The reports delivered by this MIS were relevant.

17. The reports from this MIS were delivered in the best sequence.

18. The MIS clearly indicated when the real problem had been missed.

19. The computer supporting this MIS was available all the time.

20. The reports from this MIS contained exactly the right type of information.

21. The reports from this MIS were very useful for selecting among alternative courses of action.

22. The instructions for inputting data were easy to understand.

23. The reports delivered by this MIS contained too little information.

24. The response time from this MIS was very good.
