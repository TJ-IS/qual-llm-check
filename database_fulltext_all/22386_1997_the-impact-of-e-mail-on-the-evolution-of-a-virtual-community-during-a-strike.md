# The impact of e-mail on the evolution of a virtual community during a strike

Nava Pliskin $^{a,b,*}$ , Celia T. Romm $^{1,c}$

$^{a}$ Ben Gurion University, Israel

$^{b}$ Thomas Henry Carroll Ford Foundation, Harvard Business School, Boston, USA

$^{c}$ Department of Management and Business Systems, University of Wollongong, Wollongong, Australia

Accepted 11 June 1997

## Abstract

This paper discusses a study of the group dynamics of a virtual community, determined through its members' communication on e-mail. The basis is an extensive case study that describes the struggle of academic staff members at all universities in Israel to gain salary increases and improved status. During the long strike, e-mail was used as a major means of communication between the strikers and their leaders. Data for the case were derived from in-depth content analysis of the e-mail messages that were found in the strikers' mail. The analysis of the e-mail messages demonstrates that during the strike this virtual community went through a series of phases that marked its evolution from a large disorganized group of dispersed individuals, into a united virtual community. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: E-mail; Evolution of a virtual community; Group development model

## 1. Introduction

This paper presents the history of the successful 1994 strike of Israeli academics. What makes this strike unique is that, for the first time, strikers established a mailing list that was used to electronically disseminate information about the strike and to air views of strike leaders and their constituents. Data for the case were derived from analysis of all e-mail messages exchanged by the strikers and their leaders. The analysis of the e-mail messages demonstrates that the strikers, who were dispersed in seven campuses all over the country, formed a virtual community that, over time, has gone through a series of phases that marked its evolution into a united goal-oriented group.

The literature at the background of this study was reviewed but the situation in the strike was unlike most of the literature, which focuses on e-mail penetration and views e-mail as the dependent variable. Here, technological adoption and diffusion were not major concerns. By the time the strike started, most faculty members were active e-mail subscribers, having embraced e-mail technology much earlier to cope with the distance that separated them from colleagues all over the world.

Our work adds to a more recent body of literature that views organizational behavior as a dependent variable which is affected by e-mail, the independent variable. More specifically, the effects of harnessing e-mail in the context of managing an industrial dispute are being explored. Through the case study, it is possible to sort the group dynamics of a virtual community, based on its members' communication on e-mail, by both activity and time.

Some of our conclusions are that the group evolution process resembles small group development models, particularly the Robbins model $[29]$ , and it is suggested that e-mail perhaps creates an illusion of closeness. As such, e-mail has the capacity of supporting group politicking, in general, and industrial relations, in particular.

## 2. Literature review

The literature on group dynamics shows that groups can have both positive and negative effects on their members. Much of the research on the positive effects of groups relates to their impact on decision making and creativity. Thus, group decision making has been shown to increase the acceptance of a solution and the legitimacy of a decision once it is made, as well as offer more complete information and increased diversity of views $[12, 16, 32]$ . Other studies demonstrated that under controlled conditions, groups can surpass individuals in terms of originality and number of ideas $[21]$ and also in terms of quality and usefulness of ideas $[20]$ . As for the negative effects of group interaction, social psychology researchers say that groups are prone to a range of negative effects. These include destructive expectations $[38]$ , pressure on individuals to conform to views that some believe are wrong $[3]$ , encouraged social loafing $[15]$ , enhanced destructive comparisons and jealousy $[9]$ , tendency to compromise on generally accepted views $[10]$ , and inclination to take unjustifiably dangerous and irresponsible decisions $[14]$ .

Research on group processes in virtual communities reveals that here, too, group processes are characterized by a mix of effects. On the positive side, Finholt and Sproull [6] reported that e-mail can facilitate group decision making and bring about unity and cohesion. A significant number of studies [1, 2, 24,

25, 26, 27, 28] demonstrated e-mail's positive effect on the group members' innovation and their attitude toward new technologies. On the negative side, other authors uncovered a series of dysfunctional attributes of computer-mediated group dynamics. These include flaming effects [19, 33], as well as dis-inhibition and de-individuation effects [13, 17]. Recent research on e-mail and gender revealed some further potentially negative effects on group dynamics. One of the pioneering studies in this area by Herring [11] concluded that gender relations on e-mail demonstrate the same patterns that characterize face-to-face communication, namely, males tend to dominate discussions in both amount and rhetorical intimidation, effectively creating a type of censorship of female communication.

Another potential effect was reported by Sproull and Kiesler [35], who were among the first to argue that e-mail enables people who are at the periphery of organizations to become more visible, facilitating communication between people at the bottom of the organizational hierarchy and those at the top. In such ways, e-mail can potentially democratize organizations.

An interesting line of research on group dynamics is the study of group change over time. The earliest group development model was proposed by Tuckman [36]. It contained five stages: forming, storming, norming, performing, and adjourning. More recently, Gersick [7, 8] introduced the notion of the punctuated-equilibrium model, because, according to her data, group development is underlined by periods of inertia punctuated by periods of change. This led Robbins [29] to use the terminology of the first model while proposing a new model which also contains forming and norming, low performing, storming, high performing, and adjourning.

More recent work has attempted to study the evolution of computer-mediated groups over time $[4, 5, 18, 31, 37]$ and the underlying organizational learning $[30]$ . One of the most ambitious attempts to document the evolution of computer-mediated groups was undertaken by Rafaeli et al. $[23]$ . They provide a historical description of the process that led to the creation of the H project group, a computer-mediated group of over 300 researchers from 15 countries. The history of that project is presented as a series of loosely defined phases in which different issues were debated at different points in time. In the following sections we are taking an approach similar to Rafaeli et al.. Using e-mail messages as the basis for the analysis, we present the history of the 1994 Israeli academics' strike. The data are presented in terms of five major phases. The emphasis in our analysis is on the content of the phases, that is, the issues that were at the center of the debate in each phase. We also discuss underlying relationships, the dynamics of the negotiations, the role of stake holders and interested parties, as well as the growth and decline of the network.

## 3. Case study

Case events occurred during a two-and-a-half month long strike and the two-and-a-half month post-strike period, leading to the signing of the final contract. Soon after the strike started, the strikers established an e-mail list called ACADEMIA. Within the first month of the strike, this list totaled over 300 subscribers. In the next few weeks, most strikers had either direct or indirect access to the list. One of the authors subscribed to ACADEMIA a couple of days after its creation and collected over 850 messages that were distributed. Data for the case study consisted of these e-mail messages which were read by both authors and served as the basis for the data analysis. In the following analysis and discussion the names of actual individuals and institutions were withheld.

A preliminary review of the e-mail messages allowed the authors to identify six major dimensions that were deemed relevant:

1. Debate issues - the major discussion topics.

2. Relationships - relations between users.

3. Negotiation status - snapshots of the opposing positions.

4. Stake holders - students, administration, retirees, etc.

5. Interested parties – politicians, newspapers, etc.

6. Network management – technical details of ACADEMIA.

These dimensions were the basis for two rounds of data analysis. In the first round, instances that reflected each of the six dimensions were identified. These, manifested as sections within e-mail messages, were highlighted in different colors, depending on their content. For example, a section suggesting that the plight of faculty members must be better presented in the media, was considered to be a ‘debate issue’ and highlighted in red, as well as relevant to ‘interested parties’ (newspapers) and highlighted in green. In the second round of analysis, all sections deemed relevant to a particular dimension (and thus highlighted with the same color) were re-considered. This time an attempt was made to identify points in the group evolution process where a phase began or ended. Once a decision was made about the timing of the major phases in the group evolution process, messages were number-coded in terms of one of the phases. Thus, the section on media coverage that was highlighted in red as a ‘debate issue’ was coded in the second round of analysis, as 1A to reflect the fact that it was an instance of dimension A (Debate Issues) of the relevant dimensions, occurring in phase 1 (Getting Organized) of the group evolution process.

Before we consider the phases of the group evolution process, a few background details about the strike are necessary. The strike started in January 1994 when the academics suspended all work related to teaching of 90,000 students. It ended two-and-a-half months later, when a preliminary agreement was drafted, even though post-strike negotiations continued for another two-and-a-half months (until the final contract was signed). The strike involved 4,500 academics in all seven Israeli universities.

The main reason for the strike was that salaries in the academic sector had eroded continually over the years to well below that of other researchers in the private and public sector [34]. A two-week-long strike declared by The Coordinating Council of university academics at the end of 1992 led The Committee of University Presidents to request the establishment of a parliamentary committee charged with the investigation of faculty reward structure. The Committee's report, published toward the end of 1993, recommended that academic salaries be increased by $30\%$ . This was unacceptable to the treasury; it was unwilling to go beyond a $20\%$ raise. The Coordinating Council was equally dissatisfied with the recommendation, insisting on a 90 to $100\%$ increase. None of the parties involved in the strike expected it to be long. Consequently, the fact that the strike lasted 75 days and resulted in a $70\%$ increase to academics' salaries was a surprise to all. ## 3.1. Getting organized

## 3.1.1. Debate issues

The debate during the first month of the strike was about its justification. Most of the messages at this stage were from the union. They included in-depth discussion of why the strike was initiated, what it aimed to achieve, and how the union intended to conduct negotiations. The few messages from non-union representatives voiced support for the leaders.

## 3.1.2. Relationships

Initially, union leaders perceived their relationship with union members as one-sided. This was reflected in the fact that the messages transmitted during the first weeks from The Coordinating Council contained no requests for strikers' input, suggestions, or opinions. It was only later, as the strike lingered, that both The Coordinating Council and the strikers realized that the network could be used for two-way transmission of information.

## 3.1.3. Negotiation status

During the first month of the strike, the two sides remained entrenched in their positions, with the strikers insisting on a significant increase in salary, and the treasury not willing to negotiate on much beyond compensation for inflation.

## 3.1.4. Stake holders

The influence of students, at this early stage, was relatively negligible. Messages that appeared on e-mail at the end of the first month of the strike reflect ambivalence of the students toward the strike.

## 3.1.5. Interested parties

Right from its start, the strike received wide coverage in the media and was the focus of public attention. Newspapers published daily reports on the strike, with headlines including a count of the days since the start. News reports also carried editorials and letters to the editor from striking faculty members, students, and impartial citizens. The public was also exposed to statements by the treasury ministry, attempting to prove that the cause of the strikers was unjustified. The strikers' response to these attacks was restrained, with leaders of the union informing the media on national radio and TV that many of the other side's accusations were groundless.

## 3.1.6. Network management

The establishment of ACADEMIA, the strikers' e-mail list, was a major achievement at the beginning of the strike. In addition to word of mouth and announcements during faculty meetings, the existence of the list became known to the strikers electronically through already-existing local e-mail networks. The new list distributed messages from The Coordinating Council, bulletins created at individual universities, and messages originating from list subscribers.

## 3.2. Starting PR campaign

## 3.2.1. Debate issues

Messages from strikers (as opposed to leaders) became common after a while. The most important issues for debate on e-mail during this phase were objectives of the struggle and methods of achieving these objectives. In particular, the issue of securing supportive public opinion nationally and internationally and the means toward this goal were discussed in detail. After a long debate on e-mail, a long message entitled “Crisis in Israeli Higher Education” was put together, detailing the state of academia in Israel in general, including salaries and faculty-to-student ratios. Academics were encouraged to share the message with colleagues overseas.

## 3.2.2. Relationships

Messages from the union, during the second phase reflected the fact that the list had transformed from a one-sided channel of communication to a multi-channel forum of discussion and debate. Gradually, it became apparent that even though the coordinating council enjoyed wide support, there was growing disagreement on how it should pursue its goals. Discussions of negotiation strategy, that is, issues that should or should not be pursued with vigor which started to appear on e-mail, reflected growing tension among strikers.

## 3.2.3. Negotiation status

The negotiations deteriorated in this phase, despite intense involvement by high-level public officials, such as the prime minister, the minister of education, the minister of finance, and members of parliament. The prime minister spoke against the strikers, backing the treasury minister, who was threatening to close the universities. Parliament members tried their hand at bringing forth a compromise solution but were unsuccessful.

## 3.2.4. Stake holders

After the first month of the strike ended, the student unions became increasingly vocal about the strike and its implications for them. Their protest demonstrations, some of which were quite violent, conveyed the message that they were innocent victims of the strike.

## 3.2.5. Interested parties

Although the academics in Israel attempted to enlist support from colleagues overseas, the e-mail traffic revealed little response. Academics overseas were either unaware that the strike was on or lacked information to take a stand on it. An important and unexpected ‘interested party’ at this stage of the strike was the prime minister, who made a series of critical comments about the academics during a party meeting. Numerous responses from angry strikers appeared on e-mail in the next few days.

## 3.2.6. Network management

During the second phase, the number of subscribers and message traffic increased dramatically, and system managers were busy consolidating the newly established e-mail network, educating newcomers on what the system is about and how they should conduct themselves as subscribers.

## 3.3. Managing conflicts

## 3.3.1. Debate issues

The debates on e-mail during this phase were about leadership. This debate was a spin-off from the previous phase's debate over goals and objectives. Thus, for much of this phase, e-mail communication continued to consist of messages discussing the objectives of the struggle and methods for achieving those objectives. The debate culminated in a proposal to conduct a referendum among faculty members in order to resolve issues of direction and leadership.

## 3.3.2. Relationships

The debate over strategic issues developed into open criticism of the leaders. The referendum was hotly debated and finally rejected by the majority of the strikers. During this electronic debate, increased tensions between strikers and their leaders were reflected by messages of support for the strike leaders and a significant number of personal attacks on leaders and their supporters.

## 3.3.3. Negotiation status

Negotiations in this phase were almost at a complete stand off. The messages transmitted by The Coordinating Council during this phase reflected their frustration with the stand-off and yet hoped that the other side would finally agree to negotiate on a deal acceptable to the strikers.

## 3.3.4. Stake holders

As the conflict with the students escalated, in the third phase, they became more vocal. Their concerns included a demand for a quick end to the strike as well as full compensation for money lost and recognition of work partially completed before the strike started.

## 3.3.5. Interested parties

Since many ACADEMIA subscribers distributed the “Crisis in Israeli Higher Education” message to their colleagues abroad, letters of support from overseas were now more prevalent. Academics from all over the world were moved by the Israeli academics’ plight and wrote supporting letters, addressing them, as requested by The Council and to leading politicians in Israel, including the prime minister.

## 3.3.6. Network management

Until this phase, network managers allowed a free flow of information on the network. This resulted in a growing number of complaints from subscribers about the quantity and content quality of postings. As the leadership debate escalated and the messages on e-mail became more vicious, some subscribers asked to be disconnected from the system.

## 3.4. Back to negotiations

## 3.4.1. Debate issues

Following the renewal of negotiations between the union and the employers, the major issue for debate was the negotiation strategy. The major controversy was over the ‘list of performance criteria,’ a proposal suggested by the government as a means for administering a ‘special salary increase.’ This list of criteria was initially opposed by the strikers, but, then, after having been assured that they would maintain control over the implementation of the criteria, an agreement was possible.

## 3.4.2. Relationships

Tensions between strikers and leaders subsided during the fourth phase, and the e-mail list served several other purposes. The most important role was probably in boosting morale. Numerous messages transmitted by The Coordinating Council were aimed at promoting and maintaining faculty solidarity and support. Many non-activists used the list to distribute messages that not only supported the union but also encouraged fellow strikers to 'keep the faith' and to believe that time was on their side, with the other side unable to take a very long strike. During this phase the list was used again to discuss the leadership of activists. This included many complaints about the leaders not doing the right thing or not doing things right.

## 3.4.3. Negotiation status

Most negotiations between the government and the strikers took place in the fourth phase. It was also the phase when the major breakthrough that enabled the drafting of an agreement occurred. The terms on which both sides were able to agree were actually proposed by two non-activist faculty members. To the dismay of The Coordinating Council, these terms were at first negotiated separately with two of the seven universities. A few days later, The Council took over, making the agreement formal.

## 3.4.4. Stake holders

When the strike entered the third month, all parties realized that if the dispute was not resolved within days, the school year would be lost. The uncertainty regarding the school year was of major concern to the students and their families. At this point, students became even more aggressive, attempting to disrupt non-teaching activities that the academics continued despite the strike, by burning tires near the entrances to the campuses. University administrative staff had also become concerned by the strike. Their unions, which at the beginning of the strike were generally supportive of the academics, were now alarmed that the imminent closure of the universities would result in loss of income to their members.

## 3.4.5. Interested parties

The Israeli president became involved during the fourth phase. He offered to summon the parties to his chambers in an effort to bridge the gap between them, but was asked by the prime minister to back off. With the president's involvement, and the rift between the prime minister and the president, the dispute was developing into a major national political crisis. Another source of pressure to end the strike was a Massacre in Hebron $^{2}$ and the fact that for a while, the Israeli–Palestinian peace process was halted. This changed the two sides' perspective. The strikers realized that they had to compromise because public opinion was moving away from them to very important issues. The government realized that with public opinion in the US going against Israel, and with the peace process being threatened, the conflict with the academics was simply not worth the trouble. It had to be resolved immediately.

## 3.4.6. Network management

Gradually, the e-mail list became an essential lifeline for strikers, gaining an unprecedented level of user awareness and appreciation. Many messages were devoted to describing how important the network had become to the users. As a matter of fact, messages exchanged at this time reflected an awareness that interruption of e-mail services could be a fatal blow to the strikers' ability to keep fighting. Another important development was the introduction of censorship to the list. Immediately following the debate on leadership in the third phase, the network organizers made an announcement on e-mail that, from now on messages would be monitored. This led to a long debate on e-mail on whether messages deemed ‘unacceptable’ should be prevented from reaching the whole list. The debate was still going on when the strike ended.

## 3.5. Toward new contract

## 3.5.1. Debate issues

The discussion at this phase was focused on the question of existence: should the group continue or cease to exist after its goals had been achieved? Some academics, keen to extend the life of this group, advocated the establishment of a National Academics Association. However, the majority of the subscribers were indifferent and no new permanent association materialized.

## 3.5.2. Relationships

Once the strike was over, some members of the group started to raise doubts as to whether the agreement that had been achieved was the one to which the academics aspired. Some subscribers went as far as to suggest that the academics had been cheated by both the government and their leaders.

## 3.5.3. Negotiation status

After a draft agreement was signed, negotiations on the final contract continued throughout this phase. The Council kept its constituency informed through e-mail on the progress of the negotiations, until the final agreement was reached.

## 3.5.4. Stake holders

The students had intensified their fight, once the end of the strike was formally declared. A heated e-mail debate between students and faculty continued, with students demanding acknowledgment of work they could not complete because of the strike. The conflict over this issue escalated to a one-day strike by the students; the situation was resolved only after the students were granted unconditional 'Pass' grades on courses discontinued because of the strike.

## 3.5.5. Interested parties

Once the conflict between the government and the academics was resolved the media focused on resolving the conflict with students and then lost interest altogether.

## 3.5.6. Network management

Two opposite themes emerged at this stage. On the one hand, there were numerous demands from subscribers to be disconnected from the network. On the other hand, other subscribers started to evaluate the role played by the network during the strike, considering it a ‘secret weapon’ that made the successful conclusion of the strike possible. Traffic on the network has been rather light since the strike ended.

## 4. Discussion and conclusions

The group that was described in the case consisted of over 300 subscribers and, yet, its five-phase evolution process, detailed in the previous section, bears striking similarities to the small group development models in the literature, particularly to Robbins' [29] combined model.

The first phase, ‘Getting organized’, is similar to Robbins’ Forming and Norming stage in which questions relating to group identity (such as why are members together? and what is it that they wish to achieve as a group?) are debated. ‘Starting PR campaign’ resembles the Low Performing stage. In the case, the major achievement was the creation and transmission on e-mail of the “Crisis in Israeli Universities” message. This was an improvement over the relative lack of activity in the previous phase, and yet it did not compare with the intensity of the fourth phase when negotiations leading to an agreement took place. ‘Managing conflicts’ resembles the Storming stage, during which members accepted the group as a functional entity but disagreed over the balance of power within the group. In our case, this was reflected in internal and external conflicts, including demands to conduct a leadership referendum which directly questioned the legitimacy of the union leadership. By the end of this phase, the leadership of the group was reaffirmed by the group members. ‘Back to negotiations’ resembles the High Performing stage, which is devoted to execution of plans perceived suitable for accomplishing the group’s mission and goals. In our case, the fourth phase was the one in which major changes to all five dimensions took place. Lasting only two weeks, it was packed with tensions, mounting public pressures, and eventually, intensive negotiations that led to the signing of the agreement and the resumption of work. Finally, 'Toward a new contract' resembles the Adjourning stage, in which task performance diminishes and the group cuts down its activities in preparation for closing down. In our case this stage was characterized by reduced communication among group members and by voluntary retirement of members from the group, symbolized by the withdrawal of many members from the list.

Thus, in this case, a group development model that was originally intended to describe face-to-face small group interaction fits the evolution process of a large group communicating on e-mail. We believe that the explanation for this phenomenon may lie in the ability of the e-mail to create an illusion of physical and emotional closeness. The sense of closeness fostered by e-mail can explain the ‘democratizing’ effect of this technology [35]. It can be argued that by creating an illusion of closeness, e-mail frees subscribers, particularly those at the bottom of the organization hierarchy, from status related constraints, allowing them to communicate directly with members at the top of the hierarchy. In our case, the illusion of closeness fostered by e-mail allowed strikers to debate strategies, disagree, and even discredit their leaders. It also allowed them to provide their leaders and other fellow strikers with continuing feedback and support. This intense communication may have led to a level of intimacy similar to small face-to-face groups, with ensuing natural group development. It remains for future research to assess the degree to which the illusion of closeness that e-mail can create leads to other similarities between large computer-mediated groups and small face-to-face ones.

Further, we believe that our study, despite its limitations of scope and context, has several other important implications to e-mail research and practice that go beyond research on computer-mediated group dynamics. First, with respect to the role of e-mail in industrial relations, our work is consistent with recent news reports. A growing number of unions have made the details of their industrial campaigns available to subscribers of the Internet, turning subscribers into ‘interested parties’ to their struggle. Some examples of organizations who took their industrial campaigns to the Internet during 1995 include: the Metropolitan Detroit Council of Newspapers, The San Francisco Free Press, the Illinois ‘Pride of the Prairie,’ and the International Commission of Co-ordination of Solidarity among Sugar Workers. Future research might address some of the intriguing questions raised by this phenomenon such as: What specific role does e-mail play in industrial relations? How does it change the balance of power between the negotiating parties? How does it change the role of the negotiating team vis-a-vis its constituency? Does the role of e-mail in industrial relations differ across industries? Does it differ across cultures?

Another research implication relates to the capacity of electronic communication media to ‘politicize’ groups. As demonstrated here, once a group of employees is linked electronically, it is capable of harnessing the electronic medium for political purposes. The political usages of the media may involve e-mail only, as was the case here, but can also extend to such technologies as video and audio conferencing. In an organizational culture that is high on competition and low on trust, as an industrial dispute is bound to be, electronic media can be used for political manipulation by both employees and management in ways that we have only started to explore. We must further investigate the ways that politics within and between organizations can be facilitated by electronic communication media in the national and international arena.

As a third implication, this research raises some intriguing ethical dilemmas for IS practitioners. Past research has demonstrated the importance of charging mechanisms to regulate e-mail use $[22]$ . For instance, a liberal charging scheme that rewards new users for using e-mail might increase diffusion. The data from this study suggest that political considerations could affect diffusion as well. As indicated in our case, e-mail can be used by employees for purposes other than the ones intended by its creators and implementers. It can be used as a vehicle for staging a revolt against the very people who established it, whether they are members of management or leaders of a striking union. What should be the role of IS practitioners when e-mail is used as a political tool by either side? Should IS practitioners advise managers about e-mail regulation in an organization that has particularly volatile industrial relations? Should IS practitioners facilitate the regulation of e-mail at the request of management? Should IS practitioners side with management when it decides to restrict employees' access or use of e-mail? or Should IS practitioners side with employees when they decide to sabotage e-mail as part of their industrial campaign? All these are ethical dilemmas that will have to be addressed by future research on the use of e-mail in organizations.

In sum, this paper sheds light on evolution patterns within computer-mediated groups. While consistent with the literature cited, this work turns the focus of research to the politics of virtual communities and encourages researchers in the future to deepen our understanding in this area. Future research might address some of the intriguing questions raised above regarding the specific role of electronic communication media within and between organizations, the effects of organizational and national cultures, the global implications at the international level, and the related ethical dilemmas faced by individuals in modern organizations.
