# The effect of EDI on market channel relationships in retailing

Leo R. Vijayasarathy $^{a,*}$ , Daniel Robey $^{b}$

$^{a}$ College of Business Administration, North Dakota State University, P.O. Box 5137, Forgo ND 58105, USA $^{b}$ Department of Computer Information Systems, Georgia State University, P.O. Box 4015, Atlanta GA 30302-4015, USA

Received 17 September 1996; revised 24 March 1997; accepted 8 July 1997

## Abstract

Many of the economic advantages of using Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) result from improved relationships between business trading partners. This study develops and tests a model explaining the effects of EDI on three structural variables of inter-organizational relationships: channel intensity, formalization and information quality. The model also includes the effects of these structural variables on cooperation and conflict between trading partners, and on satisfaction and performance in a marketing channel. The model is based on Van de Ven and Ferry's theory of inter-organizational relations and the literature on marketing channels. Responses from 97 retail companies to a mail survey indicated support for the effects of EDI use on channel intensity and formalization but not on channel information quality. Moreover, intensity and formalization were positively related to channel cooperation but not to conflict. Channel performance was positively related to channel cooperation and negatively related to channel conflict. These findings suggest that EDI use improves cooperation between trading partners and leads to greater satisfaction and performance in electronically-mediated business transactions. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

## 1. Introduction

The role of information systems in facilitating relationships among organizations is well documented [4, 24]. American Airlines' SABRE system [10], McKesson Drug Company's Economost order entry system [9], Baxter's ASAP system [27], and Levi Strauss' LeviLink are familiar examples of inter-organizational systems (IOS). Their purported economic benefits include comparative efficiency, market leadership, differentiated products and services, and increased bargaining power [34].

While most early IOS were developed as proprietary systems by large corporations, potential trading partners were often reluctant to participate in an arrangement that reduced their autonomy and independence. To address some of these concerns, industry and trade associations lobbied for standards governing the inter-organizational exchange of information. These efforts led to the development of a class of IOS commonly referred to as Electronic Data Interchange, or EDI, defined as the “inter-organizational exchange of business documentation in structured, machine-processable form” [15]. EDI enables companies to replace traditional modes of exchanging business documents, such as purchase orders, invoices, shipping notices, and so on. These docu ments can now be transmitted electronically over networks, rather than using mail, facsimile, couriers, or other conventional modes. Use of EDI is now widespread in the transportation, chemical, grocery, automotive, health care, and retail industries, and it is expanding to include financial institutions, insurance carriers, and other industries.

This study examines the effects of EDI on inter-organizational relationships. We propose and test a research model that links EDI use to partners' satisfaction with marketing channel relationships. The central premise of the model is that the perceived performance of, and satisfaction with, EDI systems can be attributed to their effects on the structural and behavioral dimensions of inter-organizational relationships.

## 2. Prior literature on inter-organizational systems

Interest in IOS can be traced to Kaufman's [25] prediction that computer networks would improve coordination between organizations and radically alter traditional billing and payment procedures. The subsequent emergence of these networks has spawned a number of conceptual frameworks for understanding IOS and their competitive impacts. For example, Porter and Millar [42] and Clemons and McFarlan [8] assessed the impacts of IOS on the activities that comprise an organization's value chain [41]. Cash and Konsynski [7] argued that IOS could support all three generic strategies in Porter's framework (low cost leadership, differentiation, and market focus). By processing information within the value chain more efficiently and effectively, such systems may help to revise established rules of competition, allowing organizations to lower both transaction and production costs. Moreover, innovative applications of IT could enhance product and service differentiation and establish tighter links with suppliers, distributors, and customers [21, 23].

The empirical literature on the consequences of IOS reveals general support for their economic advantages. Perceived economic benefits include the reduction of order-processing and inventory costs, elimination of labor-intensive tasks, enhanced speed and accuracy of communication, and increased market share $[28, 46]$ .

Tangible economic advantage is demonstrated in Venkatraman and Zaheer's [60] field experiment, which showed that electronically integrated insurance agents performed better than non-linked agents. Mukhopadhyay, Kekre and Kalathur's [38] study of nine Chrysler assembly centers showed that EDI use improved the quality of information exchanged, reduced inventory and transportation costs, and reduced administrative costs (see also [50]). They concluded that, over a ten year period, EDI use helped Chrysler to realize a benefit of over \$100 per vehicle, amounting to annual savings of \$200 million. Other recent studies support the tangible economic benefits of using EDI [22, 56].

While the reported outcomes of EDI have generally fulfilled expectations, there remains a need to explain why beneficial consequences result. Beyond the use of economic theory to explain reductions in transaction costs, relatively little research has been focused on the organizational factors. Organizational factors include the design and structure of both inter- and intra-organizational relationships. EDI clearly has the potential to transform relationships between organizations, and learning how inter-organizational relationships are altered may give additional insight into EDI's overall economic effectiveness. For example, Meier [35] showed that the use of EDI produced changes in the balance of power between trading partners. Nidumolu [39] showed that investments in inter-organizational information systems positively affected the transactional climate between trading partners in the property and casualty insurance industry. Hart and Estrin [19] found that the use of inter-organizational networks by semiconductor producers reduced production and transaction costs but increased interdependencies and associated coordination costs. The greatest benefits were achieved where electronic inter-organizational networks were more integrated with internal computer systems [55].

From this review, we conclude that the economic advantages of EDI are likely to depend on EDI's effects on the relationships among trading partners. To investigate these effects, we developed a theoretical model, drawn from research in organization science and marketing, for predicting the effects of EDI use on specific dimensions of inter-organizational relationships. The model also predicts the effects of inter-organizational structure on cooperation and conflict in marketing channels and upon the satisfaction and performance of channel participants.

## 3. Research model

In this study, we draw principally from Van de Ven and Ferry's [58] framework for studying inter-organizational relationships, which describes the situational, structural, process, and outcome dimensions of an inter-organizational relationship. Situational factors are intended to explain the conditions and motivations leading to the formation of inter-organizational relationships. Structure refers to the governing mechanisms that characterize an inter-organizational relationship. Process denotes the activities, in the form of resource and information flows, that occur within the relationship. And the outcome represents the effectiveness of the inter-organizational relationship in terms of the participants' satisfaction. Each of these elements in Van de Ven and Ferry's framework is applicable to the issues surrounding the adoption, use, and consequences of EDI. Figure 1 depicts the specific variables within the research model used here.

## 3.1. EDI use

EDI use is shown in Figure 1 as the independent variable that directly affects the three structural variables. It is expected to increase the formalization of procedures and policies, as well as the quantity and quality of information exchanged. The placement of EDI as the first variable indicates its important influence on organizational variables, but it should not be construed as a “technological imperative” [32]. EDI systems are introduced between organizations by knowledgeable actors responding to a variety of factors in the business environment. However, the situational factors affecting EDI adoption are not considered here.

## 3.2. Structural variables

The basic function of inter-organizational relationships is to facilitate the flow of resources and information. Resources include any units of value, such as products or services, and information consists of communications among organizations. Resource and information flows may be described using four structural variables: intensity – the amount of resource flows and the frequency of information flows; formalization – the extent to which the exchange of resources and information are administered through formal policies, procedures, and contracts; complexity – the number of resource flows and the number of cliques and subgroups; and centrality – the extent to which resource and information flows are dominated by one or few members. Of these structural components, complexity and centrality are most applicable to inter-organizational relationships involving more than two members. Because this study is confined to dyadic relationships, only the structural variables of intensity and formalization are examined.

Van de Ven and Ferry do not include the quality of information flow as a structural variable, but related work in the study of marketing channels emphasizes information quality [37]. Moreover, recent studies of EDI have focused on the importance of information quality in producing EDI's positive economic benefits. For these reasons, we include information quality as a third structural variable in our research model. Quality of channel information flow refers to the accuracy, timeliness, speed, completeness, and relevance of the information flows between organizations.

## 3.3. Process variables

We assume that the structural variables affect two process variables: cooperation and conflict in inter-organizational relationships. These behavioral processes have received considerable attention in market channel research $[53]$ . While relationships may be formed primarily for reasons of improving cooperation among trading partners, the potential for conflict may also increase because these relationships increase the interdependence among firms. Although some researchers view cooperation and conflict as opposite ends of a single continuum $[18]$ , others regard the two as separate constructs $[45]$ . We treat cooperation and conflict as separate constructs based on the assumption that they can co-exist in an interdependent relationship $[2]$ , and we expect an inverse relationship between them $[49]$ .

## 3.4. Outcomes variables

In order for an inter-organizational relationship to survive, participants must evaluate performance in the relationship favorably and be satisfied that it will achieve individual and collective goals. The research model includes channel performance and channel satisfaction as relevant outcome variables and predicts that EDI improves the relationships between suppliers and customers.

## 4. Research hypotheses

The research involved 14 specific hypotheses, each supported by prior research findings.

H1: EDI use is positively associated with channel information intensity.

H2: EDI use is positively associated with channel formalization.

H3: EDI use is positively associated with channel information quality.

When EDI is used for transmitting business documents, the inaccuracies and inefficiencies in manual re-keying are largely eliminated. Thus, more data can be processed and more pertinent information produced. Furthermore, the interconnection of EDI to channel members' internal systems, such as just-in-time and quick-response inventory systems, magnifies the quality of information exchanged $[54]$ . To the extent that pertinent channel-specific information is made available and accessible, we expect greater communication intensity. In addition, as EDI use increases between trading partners, the procedures governing the resource and information flows are likely to become more formal and more routine $[40]$ . Protocols governing the timing and frequency of exchange need to be established, and partners must agree on the sharing of costs and establish responsibility for transmission errors. Moreover, through the integration of EDI systems with internal IS, channel members are able to share more complete information.

H9: Channel information quality is negatively associated with channel conflict.

The greater the amount of information exchanged between partners, the greater the possibility for mutual understanding of each others' goals, which can lead to increased cooperation [1, 59]. For cooperative behavior to occur, the participants should be aware of each others' goals and objectives so that joint activities can be defined and coordinated. The greater the accuracy, speed and completeness of information, the greater will be the coordination achieved by partners in a bargaining relationship. Furthermore, formalizations of the information exchange process helps to avoid the misunderstandings and misinterpretations that can occur in more informal arrangements [47].

H10: Channel cooperation is positively associated with channel performance.

H11: Channel conflict is negatively associated with channel performance.

Both sides of a dyadic relationship seek mutual economic gain, and individual members' performance is dependent on the actions of both parties. Therefore, cooperation is vital for realizing performance gains. The establishment and functioning of a channel is dependent on negotiated settlements and compromises. Conflicts of interest in channel activities create opportunities for disagreements. If the frequency and amount of conflict persists at a high level in a channel, it is likely to influence adversely the perceptions of trading partners regarding performance [31].

H12: Channel cooperation is positively associated with channel satisfaction.

H13: Channel conflict is negatively associated with channel satisfaction.

To the extent that members of an organization perceive cooperation in a market channel, they will be satisfied with the relationship. The less tension and disagreement between channel members, the greater their satisfaction [12].

H14: Channel performance is positively associated with channel satisfaction.

Finally, the perception that channel activities are being performed effectively is expected to be associated with satisfaction [30].

## 5. Method

## 5.1. Sample

The study employed a cross-sectional field survey method, using self-administered questionnaires mailed to key informants in all of the 510 retail companies listed in EDI Yellow Pages [14] between October 1992 and September 1993. Names and addresses of contact persons were obtained from a variety of sources, including Standard and Poor's Register of Corporations: Directors and Executives [51] and the Million Dollar Directory: America's Leading Public and Private Companies [36]. For each company, one contact person was chosen from the list of executives responsible for purchasing, merchandising, information systems, or EDI.

The sampling procedure provided data about the buyer's half of a seller-buyer dyad, which was our unit of observation. It may seem preferable to obtain data from both halves of a dyadic relationship, given that an inter-organizational relationship is defined with reference to at least two partners. However, Reve and Stern [44], among others, have shown that dyad partners may perceive their relationship quite differently, reducing confidence in measures that combine perceptions of both partners. Moreover, the requirement to obtain matched responses from a dyad can reduce potential sample size considerably and limit the ability to conduct analyses of data. Our approach was to study inter-organizational relationships from the vantage point of the retailer.

In order to account for measurements errors in the form of informant bias, our initial intent was to obtain responses from two informants in each sample organization. To determine the feasibility of this, an initial mailing was sent to senior purchasing or merchandising executives in 100 of the target companies. Instructions requested the respondent to complete one of the questionnaires and to give the second copy to a qualified informant in the merchandising/purchasing or information systems department with instructions to complete the questionnaire based on the same supplier whom they had used as a reference point. After sending reminders to non-respondents, the first mailing yielded only eight matched responses, yielding an eight percent response rate. Due to this low rate, it was decided to obtain responses from only one key informant per company.

For the second mailing, the research instrument package containing a revised cover letter, questionnaire and business reply envelope was mailed to the remaining 410 target companies. As in the initial mailing, a follow-up postcard was sent to non-respondents after three weeks. Of the 410 questionnaires mailed, a total of 105 were returned for a response rate of 25.6 percent. Eight of these were incomplete or had a number of missing values for the research constructs and were discarded. An additional eight questionnaires were dropped because the respondents had answered the survey based on their EDI usage with a customer rather than a supplier. The eight responses from the first mailing were added to the 89 valid replies from the second mailing for a total of 97 responses.

To test for possible response bias, t-tests were conducted on two key measures. The annual sales and total number of employees were found to be significantly higher (p<.01) for responding companies than for those not responding. This bias is not surprising, given the relative novelty of EDI technology and the substantial capital investments required for its implementation [3]. As a consequence, results should be considered representative only of larger retail organizations.

Table 1 shows the distribution of sampled and responding companies according to industry (SIC) classification. The distributions of these companies are quite similar. Although all major categories of the retail industry are represented, the majority of responses were from general merchandise stores and grocery/food stores. Eighty-seven of the 97 responding companies reported five or less years of EDI use. On average, respondents reported 12.1 years of experience with their current employer, 5.7 years of service under the current job title, and 3.6 years of experience with EDI. The respondents' relatively long tenure with their companies increases confidence in the validity of their responses. The limited experience of the respondents with EDI is expected, given its relative novelty.

## 5.2. Measures

The questionnaire contained multiple-item scales to measure each of the research variables with single-item requests for information about the respondent company and about the vendor chosen by the respondent as the reference point for completing the questionnaire. Preliminary field interviews were conducted with EDI managers and merchandising executives in five retail companies. The participants of the interview were asked to complete the questionnaire and to assess the instrument for clarity of its content. Based on this assessment, minor modifications were made to improve the instrument. With the sample of 97 usable responses, reliability and validity analyses were conducted for each measure.

Reliability analyses: EDI use was measured using a 17-item scale, each representing a particular inter-organizational transaction that could be performed using EDI. These are listed in Table 2, which was generated from the literature on EDI [26, 57]. The internal consistency (Cronbach's coefficient alpha) of this measure was 0.79.

Channel information intensity was measured as the extent to which information is exchanged between the respondent companies and their suppliers. Twelve items derived primarily from Corey [11] were used to measure this variable. Channel formalization was measured using a five-item scale used by Dwyer and Oh [13], which measured the extent to which norms that govern the exchange of information are explicitly formulated. Channel information quality was measured with a five-item scale designed to evaluate the quality of the information exchanged between companies and their suppliers.

The 22 items used to measure the structural characteristics were subjected to factor analysis to assess the dimensionality of the structural variables. Two criteria were used for judging the strength of the factor loading of a particular item. First, it should have a primary factor loading of at least 0.50 on the scale to which it belonged. Second, the item should not have a secondary loading on any other factor greater than 0.30. Based on these criteria, one item from the channel formalization scale and two items from the channel information intensity scale were dropped. A factor analysis of the remaining items clearly identified the three factors expected to represent the structural dimensions. These three factors individually had eigenvalues greater than 1.72 and cumulatively explained 59.9 percent of the total variance.

In addition, reliability analyses were conducted for each of the structural dimension variables. The coefficient alpha for channel formalization was only 0.61, and an examination of the inter-item correlations suggested the elimination of one item. The revised alpha for the channel formalization scales thus increased to 0.66. Although this level was acceptable, it is low compared to the 0.75 alpha obtained for it by Dwyer and Oh. The standardized alphas for channel information intensity and channel information quality were 0.87 and 0.96, respectively.

Channel cooperation was measured using 11 items, derived from Sibley and Michie [48], that measure the extent to which collaborative efforts are undertaken between respondent companies and their suppliers. Channel conflict was measured with a 15-item scale designed to assess the extent to which disagreements or disputes characterize the relationship between the respondent companies and their suppliers. The 15 items were drawn from the literature on distribution channel relationships [16, 43].

Factor analysis was also conducted to assess the dimensionality of the process variables, channel cooperation and channel conflict, using the same criteria that were used for the structural variables. Based on the analysis, three items for channel cooperation and four items for channel conflict were dropped. Rerunning the factor analysis extracted the two conceptualized factors, each with eigenvalues greater than 2.94 and together explaining 50.8 percent of the total variance. Coefficient alphas for the cooperation scale and the conflict scale are 0.85 and 0.90, respectively.

Channel performance was measured using five items drawn from Stern and Kaufmann [52]. These items are used as indicators of the extent to which channel activities appear to be conducted efficiently and effectively. The measure of channel satisfaction was intended to capture the overall evaluation of the channel relationship. Five items used to measure channel satisfaction were obtained from relevant literature [13, 17]. The ten items of the outcome variables were subjected to factor analysis. One item in the channel performance scale was dropped due to low primary factor loading. When the analysis was rerun, all remaining items were loaded on the correct scales. The cumulative variance explained by the two factors was 77.3 percent, and each factor's eigenvalue was greater than 2.0. Reliability analysis produced alphas of 0.92 for channel performance and 0.91 for channel performance and 0.91 for channel satisfaction. Validity analyses. The purified measures for all variables were checked for convergent and discriminant validity. Using the multi-item measurements as a substitute for multiple (dissimilar) methods, convergent validity was assessed by examining the within-variables inter-item correlations. Strong convergent validities were evident for six of the eight research variables. The percentage of significant within-variable correlations was greater than 96 percent for channel formalization, channel information quality, channel cooperation, channel conflict, channel performance, and channel satisfaction. For EDI use and channel information intensity the percentage of significant correlations was lower (47.1% and 66.7%, respectively), but still within acceptable limit.

Discriminant validity was ascertained by comparing the within-variable correlations to the across-variable correlations. According to Campbell and Fiske [6], discriminant validity is demonstrated if the within-variable correlations are greater for more than one-half of all possible comparisons with across variable correlation. For all variables the percentage of within variable correlations that are greater than the across-variable correlations is well above the recommended 50%. Therefore, discriminant validity is established.

## 6. Results

The hypothesized relationships between the variables in the model were tested using linear correlation and regression analyses. The zero-order correlations between each pair of variables in the research model are reported in Table 4, which provides the statistics required to test hypotheses H1, H2, and H3, all of which posit a direct relationship between EDI use and the structural dimensions of inter-organizational relationships. H1 and H2 receive support because the correlations between EDI use and intensity and formalization, respectively, are positive and significant. H3, involving the relationship between EDI use and channel information quality, is not supported.

Table 5 shows the results of the regression analyses used to test H4 through H9. Based on the significance of the t-values, the relationship between channel information intensity and channel cooperation (H4) and the relationship between channel formalization and channel cooperation (H5) are supported. However, there is no support for the hypothesized association between channel cooperation and channel information quality (H6). None of the hypotheses stating a negative association between channel conflict and the structural dimension variables (H7, H8 and H9) are supported. Unexpectedly, there is a significant positive association between channel conflict and channel formalization.

H11, which proposes a negative relationship between channel performance and channel conflict, receives only qualified support; the t-value is significant only at the 0.08 level. There is no support for the positive association between channel satisfaction and channel cooperation (H12). For channel satisfaction, the negative association with channel conflict (H13) and the positive association with channel performance (H14) are both supported.

## 7. Discussion

Eight of the 14 hypotheses were supported. While the “scorecard” may not appear to support the model’s general usefulness, closer examination reveals that most of the empirical problems are associated with two variables: channel information quality and channel conflict.

## 7.1. EDI use and the structural dimensions of inter-organizational relationships

The hypothesized relationship between EDI use and two of the structural variables of inter-organizational relationships (channel information intensity and formalization) are clearly supported by the findings. As organizations use EDI for a wider range of activities, both the type of information and the medium of exchange are altered. As a result, the intensity of information exchanged between organizations is likely to increase with EDI. For retailers in particular, EDI offers the capability for exchanging more information about the availability and location of vendors' stocks and relevant shipping and billing information. Our findings suggest that EDI enhances these potential benefits through an increase in information intensity. Our findings also suggest that relationships are more formal when EDI is used. EDI requires standard protocols for data communication, which introduces the need for formal agreement between trading partners. The specific need for sharing sensitive data, such as forecasted and actual sales, mandate the need for formalizing responsibilities and schedules [29]. More formal inter-organizational relationships ensure that the procedural aspects involved in the exchange of information are performed effectively.

Our hypothesized relationship between EDI and channel information quality was not supported. EDI may still manifest enough problems to weaken its empirical association with a measure of the quality of information exchanged between organizations. While in theory, EDI enables a flow of more accurate information between partners, our data do not support the assumed relationship between EDI and information quality.

## 7.2. Structural dimensions' effects on behavioral dimensions

The portion of the research model involving the relationships between structural and behavioral dimensions of inter-organizational relationships received the weakest support from our data. Only two of six hypothesized relationships were supported. Positive associations between both channel intensity and formalization and channel cooperation were hypothesized and observed in our results. Apparently, as the amount of information exchanged between trading partners increases, the opportunity for cooperative actions in the channel in also enhanced. The willingness of companies to share hitherto closely-guarded information related to sales and inventory clearly indicates greater cooperation, and it is not surprising that partners welcome cooperative ventures in the arena of inventory requirements, market plan ning, and product warranties. The additional formalization in channel relationships also affects the level of cooperation in a channel. Formalization may reduce uncertainty and encourage channel participants to cooperate in the areas of advertising, inventory requirements, market planning, pricing policies, product warranty, and sales training.

Although channel information quality and channel cooperation are positively related, the hypothesized relationship between these two variables is not supported by the multiple regression analysis. While channel information quality may have an effect on channel cooperation, its contribution appears to be minimal in conjunction with channel information intensity and channel formalization.

None of our predictions regarding channel conflict were supported by our findings. One possible explanation for this is that conflicts among trading partners are not related to the flow of information that arises in a channel relation. Other causes of conflict may be more relevant. For example, disagreements about product quality, credit terms, pricing and sales quotas may be the basis for such conflicts, yet these areas are not likely to be affected by improvements in information exchange. The hypothesized negative relationship between channel information quality and channel cooperation was also not supported. Again, information quality may be only a minor consideration in matters of channel conflict.

The empirical association between channel formalization and channel conflict was positive, just the opposite of our hypothesis. The finding suggests that greater formalization in channel structure leads to more conflict in the channel. If supported in subsequent research, this relationship may require a revision of the role of formalization as a positive contributor to channel behavior. Formalization may, for instance, introduce too much rigidity to the channel structure, thereby reducing trading partners' freedom to bargain with each other. Formalization may also prevent informal or spontaneous resolutions to potential conflicts. With this effect, formalization in channel structure may be a double-edged sword. On one edge it helps to structure the channel relationship and promotes cooperative behavior between trading partners. The same formal procedures, however, may work against the swift resolution of disagreements that do arise between partners.

## 7.3. Determinants of channel performance and satisfaction

Channel performance was hypothesized to be positively associated with channel cooperation and negatively associated with channel conflict. Both of these hypotheses are supported by the data. Cooperation between trading partners is thus seen to affect performance favorably. As channel members pursue coordinated activities, performance measures such as order lead-time, service levels, out-of-stock situations, and inventory levels are favorably affected. The negative link between channel conflict and performance is also supported. As the level of disagreement between channel members escalates, it becomes detrimental to performance in the channel. Disagreement on issues such as product quality, minimum inventory requirements, and delivery speed translate into poor perception of performance.

As hypothesized, the level of conflict in the channel is negatively associated with the perceived satisfaction with the channel relationship. If an inter-organizational relationship is characterized by high levels of conflicts that are not resolved to the satisfaction of the channel members, the relationship is very likely to be evaluated negatively by its participants. The success of an inter-organizational relationship depends on the process by which exchange activities are negotiated and conducted to the mutual benefit of the parties involved. Strangely, the expected positive relationship between channel cooperation and channel satisfaction was not supported by the results. A possible explanation for the lack of corroboration for this link could be that the effect of channel cooperation on channel satisfaction is indirect. Channel cooperation positively influences channel performance, which in turn has a strong positive effect on channel satisfaction.

## 7.4. Relationship between channel performance and channel satisfaction

Finally, a positive relationship between the levels of channel performance and satisfaction was postulated and confirmed in this study. The respondents's perceptions of channel performance positively influences their appraisal of vendors. The evaluation of channel relationships with respect to inventory costs and service levels helps to determine whether or not the vendor gains the approval of the retailer. Better performance perceptions may also produce more positive evaluations of the rules and procedures used to guide the exchange of products and services between the channel members.

## 8. Limitations

This study is an isolated effort to understand the consequences of EDI use in the retail industry. As such, it cannot address this complex and expanding issue completely. The research model receives only partial support, so conclusions about the effects of using EDI on information quality, and the determinants of channel conflict, must be re-examined in future research. Moreover, the variance explained by the model is quite low, an indication that the model is far from being fully specified. Other variables should probably be incorporated and evaluated to understand more fully the consequences of EDI of inter-organizational relations.

The data for the study were collected from single respondents in the sample organizations. The original intention to collect data from pairs of respondents was not feasible, given the substantially lower response rate. This limitation is tempered somewhat by the fact that the titles of most of the respondents indicated that they were in positions of relative seniority and therefore well qualified to answer the questions. The study also collected data from one half of an inter-organizational dyad. Collecting data from pairs of cooperating organizations could have strengthened the study by providing the vendors, perspectives on the relationship. Such data could potentially provide a means for validating the measures obtained from the retailers and for indicating discrepancies between the perceptions of trading partners. It is also conceivable that the respondents' choices of particular vendors on which to base their answers are a source of bias. Respondents may have chosen only those vendors with whom they maintained good channel relations and neglected to report their experiences in less successful partnerships. Future research could compensate for this potential bias by examining a large number of specific relationships for each respondent.

The study measured EDI use as the extent to which different types of business documents are transmitted using EDI. This kind of measures assesses the diversity of EDI usage rather than the breadth, depth or volume of EDI use [33]. A more comprehensive measure of EDI use could tap these additional dimensions, which may produce a more complete picture of EDI use in organizations.

## 9. Conclusions

Although the findings of this study indicate only partial support for the research model, and are subject to the limitations discussed above, they provide a stimulus for thinking about the role of EDI in inter-organizational relationships. When initially formulated in the 1970s, Van de Ven and Ferry's model did not account for the emergence of electronically mediated transactions or their consequence for inter-organizational relationships. Today, it is necessary to learn as much as possible about EDI and other forms of electronic commerce. Our research suggests that EDI significantly affects the intensity and formality of relationships among organizations, and that these aspects of an inter-organizational relationship affect the degree of cooperation in marketing channels. Cooperation, moreover, is significantly related to channel performance, suggesting that the observed economic benefits of EDI are achieved through their improvement of inter-organizational relationships. Our model was not effective in explaining the role of information quality or channel conflict, and future research may be designed to examine relationships among these constructs in the model.

It would also be valuable to expand the study of EDI's relation to organizational factors by assessing the relative power of trading partners [20]. The identification of cooperation and conflict as variables in a cross-sectional survey hardly captures the complex nature of inter-organizational politics [5]. Yet, the ability of channel members to exercise power may explain much of the advantage derived from EDI use. Power and dependence in inter-organizational relations are important constructs that require observation over time in order to appreciate their roles in the use and consequences of EDI among channel members. These dynamics can be better studied by incorporating a longitudinal approach.

Understanding the effects of EDI on the relations among organizations can assist managers in improving the economic performance of their companies. We have shown that increased intensity and formalization of inter-organizational relationships, associated with EDI, account for perceived performance improvement. If those perceptions are reflected in tangible economic gains, inter-organizational relationships can logically be identified as a source of economic advantage. As with most information technologies, EDI is unlikely to produce positive effects independent of its context of implementation and use. The study of inter-organizational relationships provides the explanatory link between new technology and organizational effectiveness. Because EDI is, by definition, a shared technology, its consequences can only be understood by considering its effects on inter-organizational relationships. This study offers a theoretical basis for examining those effects and empirical results that are largely consistent with theoretical expectations.
