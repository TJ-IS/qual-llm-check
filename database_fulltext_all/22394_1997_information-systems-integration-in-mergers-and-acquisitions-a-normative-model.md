# Information systems integration in mergers and acquisitions: A normative model

Franco Giacomazzi $^{*}$ , Carlo Panella, Barbara Pernici, Marco Sansoni

Politecnico di Milano, Dipartimento di Economica e Produzione, Piazza Leonardo da Vinci, 32,20133 Milano, Italy

## Abstract

The role of Information Systems in Mergers and Acquisitions (M&A) becomes increasingly important as the need for speed of reaction and information is growing. A model for IS integration in M&A is presented; this includes the categories and strategic objectives of external growth as well as consideration of the possible choices for the hardware and software configuration after completion of the M&A. The basic variables of the model are defined and the possible IS integration strategies are classified in a matrix having, as dimensions, the software configuration and the computer architecture. The IS integration model resulting from these basic elements is divided into a descriptive model and a decision support model: the first one shows the relations among the involved variables, while the second provides a practical tool for decision-making. Both these models have been tested with a survey guided by a questionnaire; Factor Analysis was used to analyze the results. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Mergers and Acquisitions; Information Systems; IS Integration Strategies; Decision Support for IS Integration

## 1. Introduction

Recent studies have shown that companies grow more through mergers and acquisitions (M&A) (external growth) than through the expansion of their productive capacity (internal growth) [3].

Much literature has been devoted to analyzing the cause and effect that mergers and acquisitions have on the property of companies involved in the operation and on the economic value to various categories of share holders. Less attention has been dedicated to the role of the Information System (IS) in companies engaging in these activities [9].

External growth forces a change in managerial objectives (i.e., managers have to achieve new targets that reflect new production capability and distribution networks) and relies on a timetable of integration of company-cultures and backgrounds (i.e., managers have to adapt to a new internal and competitive environment where the rules of survival and victory have inevitably changed). This results in a change in traditional management policies, such as Personnel and Finance, but invariably requires a change also in Asset Specific policies such as IS.

Understanding the sources of value creation or value destruction is vital to comprehending the causes of success and failure of M&A. IS plays a key role in tactical and strategic management [4]. And its importance is growing. The increasing velocity of information diffusion creates, de facto, the emergence of global markets that rely on carefully orchestrated large-scale strategies. Competing in a global economy can place companies in an unnerving situation, where lead times are leaner and fixed costs are lower; however, business processes require greater co-ordination and sales strategies need to address new – and potentially important – markets. IS, when well designed and correctly employed, can greatly aid strategic management [2].

The goal of this paper is to propose a decision support model based on the characteristics of M&A, focusing on the manufacturing sector. The model has been validated through field analysis, and on a statistical analysis of data collected through questionnaires.

## 2. External growth: Kinds and objectives

External growth operations are generally grouped in two main classes: mergers and acquisitions.

An acquisition is defined as an act of exchange by which a company, called here a bidder company, uses money, stocks or their combination, to acquire some assets of the target company; this may be:

\- Technical: If the assets are in one department of a company and constituted by a set of material and intellectual properties in an independent operating unit focused on a certain product or some specific phase of it;

\- Financial: If the assets are stock or shares held by the target company.

There are various ways in which it is possible to make an acquisition, such as leveraged buy-out, public offering, and so on [6].

Mergers are different from acquisitions, because they always realize a combination between two or more companies by crossing their stocks, and thus concentrating on all the components of the companies involved in the operation of only one company.

There are two different ways to achieve mergers: by incorporation and of equals. The first is the most common one; it results in the absorption of the target company, which ceases to be a trade name after the operation. The second solution results in a totally new company built from the bidder and target companies.

Therefore, mergers and acquisitions from a legal point of view differ mainly in how property assets are repositioned. Since the object of our research is neither the property nor the legal final configuration, but the IS, the starting point is that two Information Systems must be integrated in some way after an external growth operation. We therefore ignore consideration of property or legal aspects.

Integration of the two IS does not necessarily imply that a single system, a software environment and architecture are chosen, but that exchange of data and organizational processes, according to the merged organization needs, are possible and efficient.

Thus this paper makes no distinction between mergers and acquisitions, but just between bidder and target company.

External growth may have four different goals: horizontal integration, if the aim of the transaction is to increase the dimensions in the market; vertical integration, if the objective is to gain other levels of the production chain; diversification, if there is a search for a broader portfolio of industrial activities to reduce market risk or; other objectives that do not depend on industrial policies, such as organizational change due to fiscal gain [7].

Mergers and acquisitions of complex companies can have legal, tax, administrative, organizational, production, technological and information implications. Information systems (IS) can be involved in this change in a strong or weak way, but, in any case, their configuration after the transaction must be considered very carefully.

As an example, important factors are: economies of scale, the wish to ‘export’ a certain company’s culture, the need to standardize performance parameters, sharing information, the adoption of new working methods that use information technology; in addition, situation variables have to be considered, such as the business in which the companies work, their relative dimensions, geographical distance, and management business lines.

Problems that can arise from integration of two previously independent IS are mainly caused by choices regarding changes to the company structure after the transaction. First of all there are technical difficulties due to physical integration of the IS components and any lack of data compatibility. These problems can sometimes be solved by expensive investment and with integration design involving the whole company. Then there are organizational problems, mostly depending on ‘company culture’; these problems are difficult to solve and are often underestimated, while their solution would need good planning, a strong firmness by bidder management and sufficient time.

Choices of company structure influence mainly the nature of problems that arise from integration, and, when there is the need to manage IS integration after M&A, it is necessary to consider both kinds of cited problems as success depends on the IS integration strategy too.

## 3. Operative choices

Immediately after an external growth operation has occurred, some problems connected with the IS of the involved companies have to be faced. Important strategic decisions, possibly involving large investments, must be made about the IS of the two companies; these involve the software components (applications that support business processes and databases), the hardware components (computers and networks), and the organizational ones (personnel and skills).

Our classification is intended to show the different choices that define the integration strategies. For applications, the possible decisions are:

\- Whether to standardize the applications of the two companies: the strategy can be more or less radical, i.e. it is possible to standardize all the applications or just those that support the same business processes; the standardization can take place through the extension of the applications of one company (usually the bidder) to the other company or just through the renewal of the applications by acquiring new packages in the market or by remaking them;

\- Who performs the operational management, including ordinary and extraordinary maintenance? (For instance, at what level are they delegated?) Either the IS department of the target company may be closed and unified with that of the bidder, or the staff may just be reduced to cut IT management costs, etc.

\- How are the computers to be organized? This involves their geographical displacement and whether centers can be centralized or distributed;

and the need for communication system between these centers.

Concerning existing databases, it is also necessary to decide about the logical and physical integration of the databases of the two companies; in addition to data integration, reports standardization in the two companies has to be considered.

## 4. Integration strategies

We classify integration strategies through a matrix (Fig. 1) that considers the final configuration of the applications (software) and the final configuration of the architecture of the new IS.

In this way, it was possible to identify six different integration strategies that may be grouped in three main classes of behavior: total integration (TI), partial integration (PI) and no integration (NI). Transition is a temporary solution that a company adopts for a certain time before deciding to adopt a deeper strategy.

The distinction between the different integration strategies is drawn by the standardization level of applications: i.e. the level of integration between IS depends especially on the number and kinds of business processes supported by the same software packages. Computer architecture only affects technical problems: it does not influence on what the system can do but rather on how it does it. Hardware physical location and differences produce technical problems rather than organizational ones; how IS supports work and the operations that it helps to carry out greatly influence the way of working. Business Specific software packages (i.e. software packages that support typical business processes) will be difficult to coordinate or standardize if the business of the companies is different.

## 4.1. Total Integration (TI)

The main characteristic of this class of integration strategy is the use of the same software packages and applications (standardization of applications) in both companies.

TI A: Business processes are unified; all applications are standardized and the target company's computer system is located in the central data processing center of the bidder company; some computers can be sold or replaced. The data processing center of the target company is closed.

The set of packages can be made up of all the bidder company's packages and applications or just a mix of the ones of both companies.

Network connections must be dimensioned to support the data flow to and from the centralized data processing center. Databases may have to be converted to new standards on new packages at this time.

TI B: This presents similar characteristics; the main difference involves computers: the architecture of the new IS is not totally centralized but some computers, that support special business processes, such as plants management and logistic, are left in their original location. In any case, the data processing center of the target company is closed and its staff reduced in order to cut fixed costs.

TI C: No case of this totally distributed architectures was observed in the studied sample.

## 4.2. Partial Integration (PI)

In Partial Integration only some of the software packages are used across the company: the ones that support the same business processes.

PI A: This results in the total centralization of computers in one data processing center and partial standardization of business processes and of the software packages.

Databases of the unified packages are translated to meet standards and new formats and are centralized. The other ones remain distributed.

PI B: This has the difference that computers here are partially decentralized, and software packages that have not been standardized run on these local computers; centralized applications run in the data processing center of the bidder company.

PI C: Involves a diffuse distributed IS architecture; the data processing center of the target company is not centralized and all software packages (standardized or not) run locally.

## 4.3. No Integration (NI)

Here the bidder company's strategy does not provide for any integration of the IS of the companies. All components are intentionally kept independent. The only linkages are those for transmission of data necessary for corporate management.

## 5. Integration model

Integration strategies consider the possible IS configurations that can be taken after mergers or acquisitions but do not explain the reasons for each choice. Here we introduce an IS integration model whose aim is to identify the drivers of each strategy, showing which variables are involved in any choice. The model has two parts: a descriptive model, showing the relations among the variables, and a decision support model, which provides a practical tool for decision making.

## 5.1. Descriptive model

The purpose of IS is to support business processes and reduce the organization's feedback time. Necessarily then, deciding how to integrate two IS is not limited to Information Technology: it involves organization, economics, and cost control factors. The relevant variables have been grouped in the following classes (see in Fig. 2): Growth Objectives, Interventions on Company's Structure, Situation Variables and Information System Requirements. Each group has a different role in the decision process:

(a) Growth objectives: While the type of industrial strategy does not affect IS configuration directly, it influences the type of organization that will emerge from the transaction, and hence the IS.

(b) The Situation variables are those whose value depends on the previous to merger ‘status quo’. They describe ‘border conditions’ of the mergers or acquisition.

Fig. 2. Graphical representation of the influence of variables on Information Systems integration decision (descriptive model).

These dimensions can be considered as constant in the short to medium term, without making significant mistakes.

(c) Effects on the company's organizational structure. This summarize the level of business and organizational integration achieved after the transaction. There are three values for this group of variables:

\- Retaining the status quo: This occurs when the level of independence between companies is high; it happens when the target company is primarily managed financially or when there is a change in top management only: organizational integration is very low, IS integration is not strictly necessary; however it experiences many difficulties.

\- Annexation: If the target company is much smaller, it may become a subsidiary. The level of interaction between the organizations is high and the operation potentially presents bigger technological and organizational obstacles during integration.

\- Mergers of equals: Here, the integration is generally the strongest, as the organizations coalesce into one new company, often with a new name. Both technical and organizational barriers are higher; not overcoming the culture gap can prejudice the success of the mergers, at least in the short term.

(d) Information system requirements: These directly influence the IS architecture and configuration, as follows:

\- Economies of scale: Due to the centralization of the computer system.

\- Operative behavior consolidation: Promotes similar company culture by standardizing business processes.

\- Report standardization/data Integration: Promote inter-company integration by standardizing Information management and data representation.

## 5.2. Decision support model

Variables do not have the same importance for all integration strategies but influence the choices in different ways. We develop a decision support model as a practical tool that suggests the integration strategy between two or more IS during M&A conditions.

First we show a hierarchy of the relevant variables (Fig. 3).

\- Level 0: Reports the strategic objectives of the transaction;

\- Level 1: Analyzes the business acquired;

\- Level 2: Considers whether the companies are located in the same country;

\- Level 3: Considers the degree of organizational intervention planned for the target company;

\- Level 4: Considers the kind of IS bidder's architectures prior to the M&A;

\- Level 5: Evaluates the important requirements of IS.

The decision support is shown in Fig. 4. Here we show some possible decision paths when choosing an integration strategy. It indicates certain possible 'short cuts': e.g., if two companies with different business in different countries are merged, the model suggests that it is better not to merge their IS, and that there is no need to consider any of the other variables.

## 6. Model validation

We evaluated the reliability of the decision support model and measured its error rate by considering real cases of mergers and acquisition. This required three steps: data collection; data analysis; and identification of model limits. Data collection was conducted after defining a set of industrial cases whose analysis could give significant insight of the phenomenon. We then prepared a questionnaire to collect relevant data in the chosen industrial cases; data was then analyzed with respect to two criteria: error calculation and factor analysis; finally model limits were studied.

The sample of companies of interest was segmented into various homogeneous clusters:

(a) Industry sector: Chemical, mechanical, alimentary, electronics, textile and material transformation (see Fig. 5);

(b) Size/Turnover: Only large bidder companies were considered (turnover over US \$ 120 million or number of workers over 500);

(c) Time frame: M&A between 1991 and 1994.

The result of the search for transactions to fit the sampling criterion showed a population in Italy of 98 cases [10]; companies that were willing to answer the questionnaire numbered 38; that is, 38.7% of the universe (the list of the cases is given in Appendix A) [5].

## 6.1. Database and questionnaire design

The first task required a definition of the type of information that was needed. The questionnaire could then be structured using a conceptual framework that allowed us to interpret the vast amount of data collected.

A conceptual modeling approach was chosen to identify the main entities and the relationships between them. We adopted the entity-relationships model [1], widely used in database design and software engineering, and, more recently, enterprise modeling. The conceptual scheme of questionnaire data is shown in Appendix B; it has been the basis for both designing the questionnaire and the repository in which the collected data was stored.

The E-R scheme includes three main portions: the central part describes the involved COMPANY and the MERGERS/ACQUISITION transaction; the INFORMATION SYSTEMS before the transaction are described on the left, and, to the lower right, the INTERVENTIONS ON INFORMATION SYSTEMS.

Starting from this scheme, a questionnaire was prepared comprising three main sections:

1. General information: This first section investigated the main reasons for the transaction. It involved:

\- Demographics: General information about the companies involved in the transaction (i.e., dimensions, industry sector, and organizational structure);

\- Transaction: Growth objectives and key situation variables (i.e., type of acquired business and geographical location);

\- IS role: The effect of IS on the bidder company's strategy;

\- IS integration requirements: Depth and breadth of reorganization needs.

2. IS status (before transaction): The IS structure and architecture in both the bidder and target company before the transaction.

\- Hardware and software: Number, type, power of computers, the functionality and flexibility of existing software packages;

\- Database: DBMS architecture (relational, index file, or other), volume of data (MB) and types of information stored to identify the relationship between the gaps in the information sets and the need to integrate the databases;

\- Network and system architecture: The global architecture of both IS to measure the gap between current systems and future requirements.

3. Reorganization analysis: Changes in IS structure required to evaluate the final configuration and probable problems to be encountered. The issues considered in this section were: Hardware, Software, Database, System architecture, IS interfacing, and User's role.

To effectively store data for further analysis, we created a relational database, starting from the E-R conceptual schema.

## 6.2. Data collection

Data collection was carried out in two steps: a test phase and the survey. The objective of the test phase was to ensure that the questionnaire provided all, and only, that information required for our study. Only bidder companies (or the ones resulting from the mergers) participated.

The first section was addressed to the financial managers; we wished to determine the strategic objectives of the transaction in non-technical terms. The second and third sections were assigned to IS managers of the same companies. We wished to analyze the IS status of both companies before the transaction and review the transformations required to adapt them to the final configuration.

## 6.3. Direct validation

Validation was obtained in two ways:

(a) Reducing the complexity of the initial questionnaire to the set of main variables mentioned in the descriptive model;

(b) Testing the decision support model by calculating the hit ratio for the cases considered in the survey: the model achieved 81.6% – in only 8 M&A cases out of the 38 examined were the conclusions derived from the decision support model different from the actual results for the companies.

Since these results were obtained by examining a sample of the universe of possible Italian cases, they must be expressed in terms of confidence limits for that universe. To calculate them we supposed a binomial distribution for the 'failure' variable, and calculated the error of the model by keeping track of the number of cases within the sample. We obtained a range of variability from 7.8 to 33.9% (assuming a level of significance of 95%). This means that hit ratio varies between 66.1 to 92.2%.

## 6.4. Factor analysis

In order to give a different key for reading the collected data, focusing on non-qualitative phenomena, the Factor Analysis - Principal Components Method [8] - was used. This method is based on the reduction of multiple variables to a limited number of macrovariables which give a new insight in data interpretation: factor analysis was therefore used to rearrange the variables into a limited number of macrovariables.

The 19 possible values of the 8 variables of the outset were reduced to 4 main factors: (Appendix C).

1. Simplicity of integration: This is easily identified. Its significance can be found observing that on its extreme ends there is the search for economies of scale, centralized architecture, same country, and the search for integration, while on the other side there are the opposite values for the same variables. This is a strong factor and its significance is the simplicity to merge the two IS;

2. Differences in management needs: This depends mainly on differences in the kind of business and the need for integrating databases. These, considered together, permit us to understand that the driver of this factor are the differences in the way companies are managed;

3. Search for similar operating logic: The only variable that matters is apparently the standardization of operational behavior;

4. Difference between companies: This primarily depends on the differences between companies and geographical location: belonging to different countries together with much smaller dimensions can be interpreted as a structural difference, because companies with different dimensions and in different countries seam to have very big structural differences.

Positioning IS integration strategies on these axes highlights the importance of any factor on each strategy. The higher the values on an axis (in absolute value) the more that axis influences strategy.

Model validation through factor analysis is qualitative and especially impacts the Decision Support Model. The links between the model and the statistical analysis can be found by comparing the paths along the decision tree that lead to a specific node involving the main variables. In fact the macrovariable that prevails strongly influences the chosen integration strategy. The prevalence of any such factor within the individual path is evidenced also by observing that the variables that create a node are the ones that characterize the factor itself and have its highest values. The influence these factors have on IS integration strategies is shown in Fig. 6: in it, values are summarized for each factorial axis (the bold ones are the highest), and the percentage of variance explained by each factor is also reported.

## 6.5. Limits of the model

The limits of the proposed model are:

\- Industry sector: The sample considered is only for the manufacturing sector. To be able to have coherent data about comparable companies, this sector was, in turn, divided into six main business lines;

\- Specificity of each transaction: Each transaction has unique characteristics that depend on a few variables;

\- Particular transactions: There are external growth operations that are particularly complex and are difficult to fit into a pre-defined framework.

## 7. Concluding remarks

The result of this study is an integration model that describes the criteria for choosing different strategies for IS integration after M&A; it defines the variables involved in the decision process and identifies the real drivers of change. It is based on the hypothesis that strategies followed by a company depend on a finite number of factors, that we term drivers.

The descriptive model explains the relationship between both external and internal elements in the final definition of IS configuration. The decision support model represents the paths that a decision maker can follow in designing the integrated IS. While these models have a practical value, statistical analysis on real cases demonstrates that the principal factors are: simplicity of integration and difference in management needs. The importance of these factors, highlighted by Factor Analysis, is that they demonstrate how IS integration in M&A depends on a mix of both technical and organizational factors that force the new asset designers to consider the impact of the strategic objectives of the transaction on the integration cost in term of financial, information, and human resources.
