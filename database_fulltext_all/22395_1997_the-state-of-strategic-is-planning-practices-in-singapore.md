# The state of strategic IS planning practices in Singapore

Thompson S.H. Teo $^{*}$ , James S.K. Ang, Francis N. Pavri

Department of Decision Sciences, Faculty of Business Administration, National University of Singapore, 10 Kent Ridge Crescent, Singapore 119260, Singapore

Received 20 September 1996; revised 12 March 1997; accepted 10 July 1997

## Abstract

Although there has been much research on information systems (IS) planning, most of this was carried out in America or Europe. There are very limited studies on IS planning in the Asian context. This paper examines IS planning in the context of Singapore, a small island of 650 square kilometers in South-East Asia. Data were collected using a questionnaire survey sent to the highest ranking IS executive in 450 firms. 92 usable responses were received; of these, 58 indicated that their firms undertook some form of IS planning. The macro-economic features of the firms, the degree of participation in IS planning by various executives, critical success factors and benefits of IS planning, and other planning parameters were examined. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Strategic IS planning; Planning practices; Methodologies

## 1. Introduction

Strategic information systems (IS) planning has become an increasingly important topic for both researchers and practitioners [5, 26]. In surveys of key IS issues over the past ten years, strategic IS planning has remained among the top ten issues facing senior executives [1, 2, 18]. Similarly, Moynihan [17] found that strategic IS planning is a top concern among chief executives. One main reason is that many companies are currently seeking new ways to leverage information technology (IT) to streamline internal operations, lower costs, and differentiate products/ services in order to compete more effectively in the global environment [20].

Although there has been many studies of strategic IS planning, very few studies have examined IS planning practices in Asian countries. Most of the studies in America and Europe examined a wide range of issues ranging from management of IS planning process, IS planning problems $[14]$ , methodologies $[11]$ , impact evaluation $[9, 21, 22]$ and alignment between business planning and IS planning $[24, 27]$ .

In a sense, this study is an extension of Pavri and Ang's [19] study of strategic IS planning in Singapore carried out in 1992. We used the same data collection technique. We wanted to compare the state of strategic IS planning in 1992 and that in 1996. Beyond that, we sought to:

1. examine the critical success factors and the benefits of strategic IS planning;

2. ascertain the sources of expertise on which firms rely when conducting strategic IS planning;

3. determine what areas should be emphasized in promoting strategic IS planning in Singapore; and

4. assess the degree of business and IS maturity.

## 2. Methodology

## 2.1. Instrument

The instrument used was adapted from studies of strategic IS planning by McLean and Soden [16], Conrath et al. [3] and our previous one. The questionnaire collected data on:

The macro-organizational features of the firm and its IS sub-unit;

The degree of participation in strategic IS planning; The critical success factors associated with strategic IS planning;

The benefits associated with strategic IS planning; and

Other relevant IS planning data (e.g., planning methodology used, alignment of business and IS plans).

Company and MIS department degree of maturity.

We defined ‘strategic IS planning’ as the process of formulating IS objectives, defining strategies and policies to achieve them, and developing detailed plans to achieve the objectives. Obviously, the strategic IS planning process also involves a long-range planning horizon for funds, human services, technical expertise, and hardware and software capabilities needed to take advantage of any opportunities that may arise.

## 2.2. Data collection

Three research assistants spent three weeks telephoning some 1400 companies picked at random from the Singapore Telephone Book (Business Listing). Only those that had a formal IS department qualified as participants. They were asked to provide the name of the highest ranking IS executive to whom a questionnaire should be mailed. This procedure generated a sample of 450 firms from many industries. A cover letter explaining the objective of the study and a stamped return envelope were enclosed. 92 usable responses were received, representing a response rate of 20%. This is comparable to similar IS planning studies.

## 3. Results

Some form of strategic IS planning was found in fifty-eight out of ninety two respondents (i.e., 63.0% compared to Pavri and Ang's 48%). Table 1 shows the results of the chi-square tests between strategic IS planning and organizational variables, such as firm size and computer configuration. It also shows whether the results of the present study are similar or different from the original study.

## 3.1. Similar findings

No significant relationships were found between strategic IS planning and the following variables: industry type, physical structure of the firm (i.e., whether business operations are carried out from one location or from various locations), IS sub-unit's computer configuration (i.e., whether it is physically concentrated, dispersed or distributed), and IS sub-unit's systems development (i.e., whether centralized or decentralized).

It seems that the conventional wisdom that strategic IS planning would be more important for service companies compared to manufacturing companies $[15]$ needs to be re-examined. One possible explanation is that, regardless of industry type, IT has proliferated throughout the economy as firms seek new ways to coordinate their operations and compete more effectively. Consequently, strategic IS planning is increasingly practised in both service and manufacturing sectors.

Significant relationships were found between strategic IS planning and two of the three components of firm size, namely: annual sales revenue [4, 7] (chi-square =16.53, df =4, p < 0.002) and number of employees [6, 23] (chi-square =9.69, df =3, p < 0.021). For our study, we added a third component, namely operating expenses; this was significantly related to strategic IS planning at p < 0.10. In addition, similar results were obtained for IS expenditure (chi-square = 13.09, df = 4, p < 0.010) and the number of IS employees (chi-square = 14.21, df = 3, p < 0.003).

Since larger firms undertaking IS planning tend to have larger IS expenditure and more IS employees, we can probably form a more embracing operational definition of firm size. ## 3.2. Dissimilar findings

The main change in findings from the original study is that we found significant relationship between strategic IS planning and the operational organizational structure of the firm (chi-square =9.87, df =3, p<0.020). From Table 1, it appears that the majority of organizations with matrix structures are more likely to carry out strategic IS planning. This could be due to the increasing complexity of responsibilities and product lines as well as information flows in a matrix structure. Consequently, there may be greater need to utilize IT to facilitate coordination and manage interdependence [25], and hence also a greater need for strategic IS planning in matrix structures.

Overall, the results show that the relationships between strategic IS planning and organizational variables did not change significantly (with the exception of operational organizational structure) compared to our study four years ago. This result is perhaps expected, either because such relationships are relatively stable or because a four year time span is too short to detect significant change.

## 3.3. IS strategic plan/corporate plan

The importance of the corporate plan in guiding the strategic IS planning process has been commonly advocated in both prescriptive $[10]$ and empirical research $[12]$ . In this study, of the 58 firms that practised strategic IS planning, 46 (i.e., 79.3% compared to Pavri and Ang's 71%) modelled these plans upon the corporate plans. Since both the previous and our present study were performed with companies in Singapore, it appears that strategic IS plans are increasingly modelled after corporate plans, as more and more companies seek to leverage IT to compete more effectively. Furthermore, in 54 firms (93.1%), those involved in the strategic IS planning process were made aware of the overall corporate objectives. Such results suggest that a significant percentage of firms realize the importance of aligning their IS plans to corporate business plans and have made corporate objectives known to IS planners.

## 3.4. Planning methodologies

Table 2 shows the planning methodologies used by respondents. We carried out a chi-square test (for the three types of planning methodologies) and found no significant differences between the results of the present study and the prior one (chi-square =1.07, df=2, p>0.05). Of the 58 firms who practised strategic IS planning, 40 (i.e., 69.0% compared to Pavri and Ang's 78%) stated that they adopted a combination of top-down and bottom-up planning methodologies. The result suggests that most firms continue to adopt a methodology that combines business vision from the top with user participation from the bottom. Similar to prior findings, top-down methodology occurs more frequently than bottom-up methodology. This is perhaps not surprising since traditional planning is usually top-down. Hence, preferences for specific planning methodologies have not changed much from the previous study four years ago.

## 3.5. Extent of participation in IS planning

Table 3 shows the extent of participation in strategic IS planning by various groups, measured on a four point Likert scale with values of (1) none, (2) low, (3) medium and (4) high. As expected, MIS managers and top management frequently participate in strategic IS planning, followed by users, systems analysts (developers) and non-MIS managers. The relatively high degree of top management's participation provides evidence of the importance of strategic IS planning. Overall, these results are consistent with our prior work, as shown by the t-test results; the exception is for non-MIS managers, who apparently participate less frequently in strategic IS planning than they did four years ago. One plausible reason is that non-MIS managers may be busy with their own area of work in this era of increased competition. Consequently, they may tend to leave participation to the users who are affected by new IT systems. Another reason may be that non-MIS managers may not feel a need or may not be invited to participate in strategic IS planning, since MIS managers and top management are already actively involved in it.

## 3.6. Critical success factors

The questionnaire listed 10 critical success factors (CSFs) for strategic IS planning, derived from the literature of the past. Respondents were asked to rate the degree of importance of each item on a Likert scale ranging from (1) low to (5) high. The results are listed in Table 4 (in descending order of importance).

The top two CSFs emphasize the importance of top management support as well as the need for a clear-cut corporate plan to guide IS planning efforts. This is in line with the existing IS planning literature which commonly emphasizes top management support and involvement in IS planning $[13]$ as well as the importance of business plans in guiding IS planning efforts $[28]$ . This may be especially true for an economy, such as Singapore, where the average size is much lower than that of Europe or the USA.

The third CSF (good user-IS relationship) is crucial for partnership between business and IS staff in leveraging IT to achieve strategic objectives. Such partnerships have been emphasized in past research and can lead to improved financial performance, operational efficiency, and quality of life for both line managers and IS staff [8].

The next CSF deals with having sufficient qualified personnel to do a proper job. In the context of Singapore, there is a shortage of skilled IS personnel due to the rapid growth in the number of businesses deploying IT to compete. Consequently, it is not surprising that having the correct IS personnel with the appropriate skills is viewed as crucial to the success of the strategic IS planning efforts. The other CSFs deal mainly with planning issues, such as anticipating environmental changes, use of proper planning procedure, appropriate planning horizon, etc.

## 3.7. Benefits from strategic IS planning

Respondents were asked to rate on a five point Likert scale ranging from (1) low to (5) high, the degree of benefits they derived from the strategic IS planning process. The results are shown in Table 5.

It is interesting to note that the top three benefits are mainly internal to the firm; these are improved productivity, improved internal coordination, and efficient and effective management of IS resources. One likely reason is that these are traditional benefits derived from computerization. Another reason is that these benefits are considered more easily attributable to strategic IS planning efforts than larger market share, etc. The remainder of the benefits are more global in nature, in the sense that improved competitive position and market share could be due to other factors than strategic IS planning.

## 3.8. Satisfaction with strategic IS plan

Respondents were asked to rate their degree of satisfaction with the strategic IS plan using a five point Likert scale ranging from (1) low to (5) high. None of the respondents gave a rating of '1'. More than 94% gave a rating of '3' or larger. It appears, therefore, that the majority of respondents are satisfied with the strategic IS plan.

## 3.10. Areas important in promoting strategic IS planning

Despite the importance of strategic IS planning, the percentage of companies practising strategic IS planning is still relatively low (only 63% in our sample). It may therefore be interesting to examine areas in which appropriate incentives may be provided to encourage more companies to carry out strategic IS planning. Respondents were asked to indicate, on a five point Likert scale ranging from (1) low to (5) high, the degree of importance of a list of items in promoting strategic IS planning,

The results (Table 7) show that education and training provided by software/hardware vendors, as well as government support in terms of grants, are the two most important areas that can promote strategic IS planning. Companies are probably interested in acquiring software tools that facilitate the strategic IS planning process. Also, government support in terms of grants and tax incentives would help to persuade companies to practise strategic IS planning. Surprisingly, low cost consultancy is ranked last among the four areas listed. Companies need to be educated and convinced of the value and need for strategic IS planning before they even think of engaging consultants to assist in the planning process.

## 3.11. Sources of expertise/information

Respondents were given a list of sources of expertise and information that they may use in order to facilitate the implementation of their strategic IS plan. They were asked to rate their degree of usage on a five point Likert scale ranging from (1) low to (5) high.

Table 8 shows that software and hardware vendors are the two most common sources of expertise. When companies acquire computer hardware and software to facilitate the strategic IS planning process, they tend to rely heavily on vendors to teach them how to use these hardware and software effectively. Furthermore, the vendors often provide free advice with their purchased product.

Sources of expertise or information from government and semi-government bodies, and university consultants are the least used by companies. Perhaps, respondents feel that approaching government bodies for assistance would entail a great deal of paperwork. Also, there may be a common perception that university staff are more concerned about teaching and research than in helping companies with their real life problems. Hence, they may feel that university consultants live in their ‘ivory tower’.

## 3.12. Evaluating IS function

Among the 58 companies which practised strategic IS planning, only 24 (25.8%) have objective measures of IS contributions to productivity. In terms of the importance of developing such objective measures, an overwhelming 57 (98.3%) firms rated it '3' or above on a five-point Likert scale ranging from (1) low to (5) high. Hence, the development of such measures is a much needed area for future research that examines the productivity impact of IT.

## 3.13. Company's degree of maturity

The company's degree of maturity in the areas of long-range business planning, capital allocation, and objective setting were assessed by asking respondents to place a check mark beside the most appropriate statement in each category. The results are shown in Table 9.

Only three companies in the table do not have formal long-range business plans. The majority of companies have either predominantly tactical or strategic business planning. This is reassuring in that the majority of companies do carry out long-range business planning. Few have no formal capital allocation criteria. It is interesting to note that companies who carry out rigorous financial analysis in their capital allocation (with or without post-audit of results) comprise about two-thirds of the sample. Hence, rigorous financial analysis appears to be an important component in most companies.

Only one company did not formally set objectives. The majority of companies have highly targeted individual objectives with strong follow-up and directly affecting compensation. Since objectives do guide future actions, it is reassuring and expected that most companies set objectives to be attained.

## 3.14. MIS department's state of maturity

The MIS department's state of maturity in the areas of computer operations, systems development, user involvement and feasibility assessment were assessed by asking respondents to place a check mark beside the most appropriate statement in each category. The results are shown in Table 10.

Few were dissatisfied with the timeliness and accuracy of computer operations. In the majority of companies, users were satisfied with their formal production control, whether feedback to users was absent or present. One possible reason that statistics such as turnaround time, percent of reruns, etc. are not communicated back to users is that the IS department may want to retain control and cover up inefficiencies in their own operations.

It is reassuring to note that in majority of companies, the users are very confident with the MIS group's ability to consistently deliver major systems approximately on time, within budget, and thus managing to meet specifications. However, this result must be viewed with caution, as our respondents are from the IS department: there may be bias in the responses.

## 4. Conclusions

We find that our latest results do not differ from the prior study in terms of the relationships between strategic IS planning and organizational variables like industry type, firm size, operating expenses, IS expenditure, number of IS employees, physical structure of the firm, IS sub-unit's computer configuration, and IS sub-unit's systems development. The major difference was the significant relationship between strategic IS planning and operational organizational structure.

The results also show that more than one third of companies in our sample do not undertake strategic IS planning. Although this percentage has decreased from 52% in the past four years, it is still relatively high, given the increasing proliferation of IT throughout the economy as firms seek new ways to compete more effectively. While the decrease in percentage is reassuring in some sense, it also indicates that there is a need to educate firms in Singapore about the benefits of strategic IS planning.

For those who undertake strategic IS planning, we find it disturbing that one-fifth do not model their IS plans after the corporate business plans. How then could such companies expect to derive benefits from their IT investments?

In terms of other results, we find that critical success factors, benefits and initiation of IS planning are more or less expected and are likely to be applicable elsewhere. However, the areas important in promoting strategic IS planning and sources of expertise are interesting in that firms in Singapore seem mainly to rely on vendors.
