# The acceptance of visual information in management

## Abstract

Developments in information and communication technologies have considerably enhanced the generation of management information. Thus, managers are confronted with an increasing flood of information, which appears to be more than managers are able or willing to process. Visualising information offers one efficient and effective way to confront this problem without limiting it's quantity. However, successful application of visualised information requires its acceptance by the manager. This paper outlines an exploratory study resulting in a theory of acceptance of visual information. It employs the results of an empirical investigation conducted amongst managers. Variables influencing acceptance have been categorised into five factors that are subsequently used to discriminate three types amongst the group concerned. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Decisions in management; Acceptance of information; Visual information; Empirical study amongst managers

## 1. Problem

Scanner technology and mobile recording instruments have simplified the gathering of information considerably, whilst modern information systems (IS) allow access to large-scale data banks. Cable and satellite television provides consumers with an abundance of advertising information that can be produced more rapidly and at much lesser costs using computerised systems. This, however results in an information ‘flood’, which carries considerable problems for both consumers and managers. The huge quantities of information, as well as its inadequate processing, leads to mental overload and possibly rejection of information by the recipient.

Possible solutions to this problem may be methods of intelligent data selection, a well-designed information management-system or visualisation of information. Relevant books and articles indicate that pictorial information is received and processed better (i.e., more quickly and completely) than, for example, text, figures, or acoustic information. By the means of visualisation, therefore, the transfer of information is made easier and thus a bottleneck in human information processing avoided.

Despite all the findings from a behavioural viewpoint, there has been little or no attempt to transform them into a managerial visualisation theory. Instead, managers have to make do with a heuristic and less scientific set of rules for the ‘adequate’ pictorial presentation of information; they can be found in Tufte and Zelasny [19, 20, 21]. These rules, however, are no substitute for a comprehensive scientific theory.

A first step towards such a theory in the recent past can be found in Meyer [10]. This study refutes the general superiority of visual information in improving the decision quality ('naive superiority hypothesis'). The choice and design of visual presentation is determined by information structure, decision environment, the decision-maker, and the task/decision. Thus, technical factors to determine the correct alternative, are linked to rules that are derived from behavioural science. The following paper does not repeat these arguments on the whole but concentrates on one aspect of the discussion surrounding visualisation, namely acceptance.

The successful use of visual information depends substantially on its acceptance by the manager and the environment. If there is little acceptance, anticipated psycho-physiological improvements in understanding information, good decision making behaviour, and high quality decisions may not occur due to the fact that the information has previously been both filtered and rejected.

The study pinpoints important factors in the acceptance of visual presentation. Information derived from studies of behaviour or acceptance in connection with IS is rare. Information behaviour studies tend to ignore the problem, whereas research on acceptance usually concentrates on the IS and not on the information quality.

The findings described in this article can only be seen as a first step in gathering ideas from everyday business life and thus in, collecting what may turn to be the side factors. This explains the mainly exploratory character of the method, namely in merely identifying the possible factors in existing management situations.

In the following the empirical study and some of its results are presented. These provide us with factors that determine the acceptance of visual information and give an idea of different clusters of manager types with regard to this acceptance.

## 2. The framework of the investigation

This paper discusses the development of a first step to a theory. As this phase is designed to generate a series of hypotheses there is no need for a theoretical concept. However the framework can be set up on the basis of the insight gained from prior research that lead to a measure of acceptance as an output variable that depends on visual presentation and other, hitherto unspecified, factors.

## 2.1. Output factor: acceptance

Acceptance has been studied in a number of different areas. Its definition and the various ways of measuring, it are not however uninformed across these areas. In our case, the definition derives from Müller–Böhling, who believes acceptance to be a measure of the positive influence an object has on its recipient, and understands it to be a phenomenon composed of two dimensions: attitude and behaviour. Attitude acceptance is a permanent cognitive and affective orientation of perception: it involves readiness-to-react to the object in question. However behavioural readiness manifests itself in reaction of the recipient, namely in the actual use (or lack of use) of the technology [12, 13].

Acceptance cannot be measured directly. It has to be determined with the help of indicators, which involve:

\- A lack or inadequate use of the technology; this can be seen as a negative for behavioural acceptance.

\- Measuring the attitude towards a certain type of presentation; this involves taking a wide range of dimensions such as the expected benefit in employing a specific method, the opportunities, and any reservations in doing so.

## 2.2. The main input factor: visualisation

Our definition of ‘visualisation’ relies on the theory of Paivio, whereby human information perception and processing are divided into pictorial and semantic levels: under this dichotomy, the information is stored as two types of knowledge. Then visualisation is the process or activity by which non-visual information is converted into a visual form $[2, 8, 15]$ .

The range of forms of visual presentation extends from simple graphics (e.g., bar charts) to three-dimensional, and thus to actual pictures and animation. Films or multimedia may also be included. A simplified and comprehensible classification of the forms of visualisation was chosen for this study (cf. Table 1).

## 2.3. Further input factors:

Research into the acceptance phenomenon primarily aims at the functional influence of visualisation measures, but it seems advisable to take further factors into account. The identification and description of the variables which influence acceptance is also the subject of empirical research $[1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 11, 12, 14, 16]$ , which proposes different theoretical frames. The variables can be summarised into five groups: the personal, situational, system - and task-related, and also implementational characteristics. Table 2 characterises the groups of variables, that were at the forefront of previous acceptance research.

Only some of these variables are relevant and useful within the framework established so far. Some may be suggested for use in exploratory studies on the acceptance of visual presentation methods in management:

\- Personal properties: motivation, knowledge of obstacles, personality (e.g., progressive vs. conservative), experience with visual presentation. Other variables are either difficult to measure (e.g., problem-solving style) or are merely indirectly relevant (e.g., education).

\- Object: one system-related variable that can be considered here is the 'form of visualisation' which takes the visual presentation actually used by the manager into account.

\- Situation and task: The questionnaire can request data that shows the extent of disturbances and time pressure, task type (routine decision vs. exceptional decision) and the maturity of the information base (too much, too little, or inadequately processed information).

\- Implementation: considerably influences on the acceptance.

## 3. The empirical study of acceptance of visualisation in management

## 3.1. Study design and execution

In 1994 an empirical study amongst managers was carried out at Berlin's Technical University. The aim was to obtain evidence on possible factors that influence manager's acceptance of different forms of visual presentation and thus to be able to classify managers into several types. Executives from 1500 German companies of all sizes and branches were surveyed by mailing them a questionnaire. A pretest with 20 students at Berlin's Technical University was previously carried out to inspect readability and comprehensiveness of the questionnaire.

The respondents were managers of the marketing departments. The response rate was 6.8%; this relatively low figure can be expected in view of the fact that managers now a days ‘suffer’ from a surfeit of questionnaires. The respondents can be characterised as given in Table 3

As with all questionnaire studies, it must be assumed that respondents have a higher than average interest in the matter than those who do not reply. In order to gain some idea of the extent of this non-response bias the variances of every item in the first 20 returned questionnaires was compared to those of the final 20, assuming that any existing non-response-bias could be indicated by a significant difference in-between these groups. Although, at a level of 5% there seemed to be no significant difference, one surely cannot totally ignore the non-response-bias effect. In any case, the study does not claim to be representative. Therefore we went without soliciting a second set for another validity test.

## 3.2. Findings

## 3.2.1. Behavioural acceptance: the use of visual presentations

Clearly the most useful indicator of behavioural acceptance is the actual use of visual presentations in decision making. All respondents use visual presentation in some way or other. This is verified by the high rating found when using these tools in preparing decisions. The only analysis here lies in comparing the actual forms of visualisation.

Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 indicate the utilisation of visual presentation methods used in management. In Fig. 1 they are assembled to groups, citing both the absolute frequency of use as well as the respective share. In Fig. 2 these frequencies are more closely investigated. As expected, static pictures (graphs, and real or realistic pictures) are clearly predominant compared to moving pictures. More comprehensive visualisation concepts are (so far) of minor importance.

3.2.2. Attitude acceptance: evaluation of forms of visual presentation

The managers were asked to give a basic evaluation of the benefits of visual presentations (The answer served as an indicator for attitude acceptance). The results are illustrated in Fig. 3.

As expected, visual information is generally considered helpful, since it simplifies problem solving and highlights the solutions. According to the managers, it helps speed up the process, thereby saving time; processes can also be visualised particularly well. Surprisingly, however, visualisation rarely reveals interrelations. Accuracy and validity are not properties associated with visual presentations.

## 3.2.3. Obstacles in using visual presentation techniques

According to the managers taking part in the study, there are several factors that make it difficult to use visual presentation; such as the over-abundance of methods, but lack of resources (e.g., data processing), that may not always be available (e.g., on business trips). Nor is it possible to be familiar with the all techniques of information presentation and there may not be sufficient training time available. Lack of routines for creating and using visual presentation, diminishes acceptance. Interestingly enough, the costs involved in preparing visual presentations seem only a minor obstacle to implementation.

Prior to this, the ‘missing cases’ were eliminated, along with one exceptional case, which was identified with the help of an agglomeration method ‘single linkage’. This method (defined by Ward) resulted in the formation of three clusters. Cluster 1 comprises 23 cases (25%), Cluster 2 contains 33 cases (35.9%) and Cluster 3 36 (39.1%) of the total 92 cases (100%). The clusters were then characterised more closely by analysing the differences brought about by the factors themselves (and the related variables, Table 6). These differences are illustrated in Fig. 4.

The managers of Cluster 1 were more sceptical in their assessment of the benefits of visualisation than other clusters. At the same time, they see the obstacles as less important. Also, by using the data available, it was possible to determine the other characteristics that describe the managers in this cluster: 50% come from top management and deal with product and price policy tasks. Of the total number of managers surveyed, only roughly 18% considered visualisation valuable and almost 26% of those who received training in their data processing facilities actually come from Cluster 1.

Only in Cluster 2 are the benefits achieved by visualisation assessed greater than average. The level of routine in dealing with software and visualisation technologies is lower here than in the case of either of the other two clusters. It was possible to determine the following additional characteristics that describe managers in this cluster: almost two thirds are middle managers and almost one third came from top management. 22% have an above-average use of visualisation concepts (this comprises 58% of all cases in the sample in which visualisation techniques were employed). 38% of all managers who used a prototype during the system's development or implementation are members of this group. Of the total number of managers surveyed, 38.5% regarded visualisation as important and necessary and 32.3% were trained in the use of their data processing facilities.

The assessment of the benefits of information visualisation by the managers of Cluster 3 was neither especially positive nor negative: possibly due to their familiarity with software and visualisation technologies they tend to see the obstacles rather than the benefits of visual information presentations. The managers of larger companies belong to this cluster. They are less prone to time pressure and disturbances than the managers of Cluster 1. Also, whilst hardly any managers from lower hierarchical levels were included in the first two clusters, 26.5% of those in Cluster 3 actually belonged to middle or junior management. The manufacturing industry is represented disproportionately high in this cluster, as are seven of the total nine companies in the ‘mixed enterprise’ category. 62% of all prototype users, and almost half of all managers, who regard visualisation of information essential, are in this cluster. The managers may be described as tending to be of a progressive while at the same time rather critical nature.

\- no experience with prototypes,

\- pay little attention to visualisation and have little training,

\- more rational types with average experience in dealing with software visualisation,

\- reserved judgement of benefits and obstacles,

• come from small companies,

\- subject to time pressure and disturbance in decision environments,

• insufficiently supplied with information.

## Cluster 2. The optimistic middle-class type

• middle to top management,

\- more communication policy tasks,

\- above average use of ‘comprehensive concepts’,

\- average implementation behaviour (prototypes, attention to visualisation, training),

\- conscious of both the benefit and obstacles, - below-average experience in dealing with software/visualisation,

• come from middle-sized companies,

\- subject to somewhat less time pressure and disturbance than Cluster 1,

\- tendency to perceive information overload.

Cluster 3. The sceptical experienced type

• middle management, works in a team,

\- on average a strong use of visualisation,

\- sole use of 'static pictures',

\- above average regarding implementation (prototypes, attention to visualisation, training),

\- classify themselves as ‘progressive’ managers,

• see more obstacles than benefits,

\- above-average experience with software/visualisation,

\- come from larger companies (disproportionately represented: manufacturing industry and mixed enterprises),

\- subject to somewhat less time pressure and disturbance than Cluster 1,

\- tendency to perceive information overload.

The results were tested with the help of discrimination analysis, with the question: how far can a combination of the five factors determine the classification? The independent variables comprise the 'factor-score-coefficients'. If all cases are classified with the aid of the discriminance functions and the result is compared to the actual grouping, 95.7% of the cases are classified correctly. The discriminance function, with the highest eigenvalue, 'Obstacle' and 'Company size' are the most influential.

It is important to note that typologies present an exaggeration of reality, since they reduce the empirically derived multitude to a minimum. Consequently the results should be compared with to findings of other information behaviour models, such as those employed in general psychology and especially in consumer behaviour theory. The typologies derived from these may also be useful for managers.

Other surveys conducted specifically amongst managers, such as the 'Kiel experiments', cannot be compared to this study because the design of the investigation procedure is somewhat different from ours. Also, a great number of parameters and procedures were investigated [5, 6]. Strothmann, for instance has developed a typology to classify purchasing agents according to their information behaviour [18].

The descriptive parameters of the three clusters are, to a certain extent, comparable to other typologies. However, whilst the latter primarily describe personal behavioural conditions and processes, the above typology, focusing on visual presentation, deals with both personal and situational parameters. It seems impossible to develop the above clusters those described in relevant books and articles. In this sense, the typology developed in this study-irrespective of its specialisation in visual information-may be viewed as a supplement to previous research.

## 4. Summary and outlook

The aim of this investigation was to analyse the acceptance of visual presentations amongst managers and to determine the acceptance factors. Both the variables investigated and the managers surveyed were grouped. The resulting five factors were:

\- The benefits obtained through visual presentation,

\- Obstacles/Requirements for implementing visual presentation,

\- The familiarity of the managers with software and the preparation and use of visual presentation,

\- The size of the companies surveyed (with respect to number of employees and turnover),

\- The decision environment (time pressure, disturbances, and information supply situation).

Furthermore three groups of managers were identified as showing significant differences with respect to these five factors:

\- Visualisation does not (yet) seem to be an important subject for the reserved rational type, despite the fact that this group of managers complains about lack of time. The benefit of and the obstacles to visual information presentations are not adequately assessed by them.

\- The optimistic middle-class type appears to choose the form of visual presentation on the basis of the task at hand.

\- The sceptical, experienced type managers are accustomed to using static pictures. Although they have recognised visual presentation as an important topic, they are sceptical of its benefits. The obstacles seem to play a particularly important role.

The investigation supports the repeated calls in books and articles for participation of staff during the implementation of (visual) information systems ([17] regarding the participation-efficiency hypotheses). Prototypes should be used at an early stage of development so that employees may understand the possibilities and limitations of visual presentation. Training schedules for fundamental data processing should also include implementation and point out the benefits of information visualisation.

## 4.1. Which of the study findings contribute further to research as well as business practice?

The clusters obtained provide a picture of an implementation scenario typical for (marketing) practice. The three types of manager offer an interesting differentiation basis for the implementation of visualisation standards within corporations. This ‘internal corporate identity’ has been demanded repeatedly in books and articles. Strategies to develop standards of visual presentation may be established on this basis, in order to increase the mutual acceptance of pictorial presentations.

\- Manager type 1: For the reserved rational type, the application of visual presentation methods would seem to be an obvious choice. Also a critical attitude and lack of experience may be expected, and thus the acceptance barriers are probably quite high. Consequently, an implementation strategy should be applied whereby the manager is gradually accustomed to the new techniques thereby highlighting the advantages and benefits of visual presentations.

\- Manager type 2: The optimistic middle-class seems to choose that form of visual presentation best for the task at hand. The managers are experienced in the use of visual presentations and have a positive attitude towards them. There seem to be no major obstacles to a swift introduction of presentation standards.

\- Manager type 3: For the sceptical experienced type the use of static pictures is a routine activity. Whilst having recognised visualisation as an important field, they are somewhat sceptical of its benefits. An effort should be made to highlight the benefits induced before introducing a standard for the particular company.

The implementation of visualisation standards depends also on the technical requirements and costs. Empirical study has established that there are sufficient data processing resources available in all companies and for all activity fields for visual presentations, at least for those which do not involve virtual reality or multimedia. Only minimal additional costs are to be expected and therefore these do not constitute an obstacle to implementation. Adequate computer facilities and sufficient basic knowledge are generally not available for more comprehensive pictorial concepts and high-quality 3D animation. This means that even if such methods were learned quickly and easily there still would be limitations to creating such presentations. Thus, the scope for implementation of virtual reality in management is limited and, due to the computer set up, office use is still infeasible. However, swift development in data processing-hardware and respective costs as well as simplified software usage will result in considerable reductions in future.

Finally, the limitations of this study and its findings must be pointed out. The effort has been a first exploratory step. A confirmatory analysis was not attempted, as this would have required further empirical studies. The test sample was comparatively small, some of the effect strengths were low and several variables showed large variances. The study does not claim to be representative. Distortions are likely, since those managers who actually responded to the questionnaire were probably more interested in visualisation than those who did not respond. Also, the experiment surveyed a large range of both forms of visualisation and types of managers. Further research, especially in the case of laboratory experiments is needed.
