# A Comparison of synchronous and virtual legislative session groups faced with an idea generation task

Milam Aiken $^{a,*}$ , Mahesh Vanjani $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Management, School of Business Administration, University of Mississippi, Mississippi MS 38677, USA $^{b}$ ISC Department School of Business, Georgia College and State University, Campus Box 12, Milledgeville GA 31061, USA

Received 26 August 1996; revised 5 February 1997; accepted 15 July 1997

## Abstract

An experiment was conducted with groups of about eight people in face-to-face and geographically-distributed electronic meeting environments. While similar studies have focused on the behavior of group members working together in a single room or working individually in different rooms (a nominal group), this research looks at a hybrid environment in which part of a group is working in one room and another part is working simultaneously in a different room that is linked via a local area network, both parts forming a virtual group. Experimental results showed that such groups generated significantly more unique, quality comments than did face-to-face groups, and that participants were significantly more satisfied with that type of meeting. These and other results indicate that groups may be able to meet effectively when distributed geographically. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Electronic meetings; Virtual meetings; Group decision support systems; Brainstorming; Brainwriting

## 1. Introduction

Group Decision Support Systems (GDSSs) and other electronic meeting technologies are helping many organizations make better, quicker decisions by providing support for the anonymous and simultaneous exchange of ideas and preferences; numerous studies have shown that group members using the systems participate more, generate more comments, and are more satisfied than when using traditional meeting techniques [15]. These computer-based systems are generally implemented in face-to-face environments on a local area network, with participants of the meetings able to see each other through a semi-circular or U-shaped seating arrangement: see [5] for two typical meeting room designs. Although the vast majority of studies using GDSSs have focused on groups meeting face-to-face in a single room, some studies have looked at group members working individually in different rooms. However, few, if any, have investigated a hybrid approach: one part of a group meeting in one room while another part meets in a different room which is linked electronically to the first.

In this paper we report on the results of an experiment involving groups of about eight people (which qualifies as a “large” group or “legislative session”) using a GDSS to aid in generating possible solutions to problems [8]. The control groups met in a single room (a synchronous legislative session) while the treatment groups were split between two different rooms. However, the two sub-groups together (we term this a “virtual legislative session”) were able to share all comments electronically [4]. In both types of groups, members were satisfied with the meeting technology, but the virtual groups were more satisfied with their sessions than the face-to-face groups. Further, virtual group members believed that the comments were “more anonymous” than did face-to-face group members. Finally, there was no significant difference between face-to-face and virtual groups in terms of the number of raw comments and the number of quality comments, but the virtual groups generated significantly more unique, quality comments. These results indicate that groups may be able to meet effectively when split into physically-separate sub-groups but linked electronically.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Verbal and manual meeting techniques

Studies of brainstorming and group problem solving date from the 1950s, when researchers began to investigate new verbal and manual, paper-based techniques to improve the productivity of meetings [17, 18]. These early studies showed that the number of ideas generated per participant in a verbal brainstorming group declined as the size of the group was expanded (with a maximum group size of about 12 people) [10]. Thus, with verbal brainstorming groups, the marginal productivity of individuals decline when the groups are larger, probably due to the restriction of allowing only one person to speak at a time. Such production blocking can nullify the assumed “synergistic effects” of brainstorming.

To overcome the limitations of verbal brainstorming, researchers investigated paper-based techniques, generally termed brainwriting. They found that, although the social benefits were reduced when moving to a silent, written form of communication, group participants were more candid in this anonymous environment and that they were able to generate more ideas, because no “waiting for a turn” was necessary [21]. Because communication in brainwriting is in the form of hand-written comments, group members may no longer need to be face-to-face or even within hearing of other participants, and physically-separated or “nominal” groups can outperform verbally-interacting groups in terms on the number of ideas generated for a problem solution [9, 20].

## 2.2. Electronic meeting techniques

In the 1970s and 1980s, researchers began to investigate electronic techniques as means of increasing collaborative group productivity: Table 1 summarizes these techniques in terms of their provisions for group memory (recordings of group communication), anonymity, and parallel communication. Many other studies have focused on electronic mail, video-conferencing, and computer conferencing: [14] for a good summary of these. We will concentrate on the form of electronic communication typically including all three characteristics, i.e. Group Decision Support Systems or GDSS (see Table 1).

## 2.3. Group decision support systems

Many researchers have criticized the term GDSS as being too restrictive: groups using the technology may be involved in other tasks than decision making and some have instead proposed other terms: e.g., Electronic Meeting System, Group Support System, Computer Supported Collaborative Work, and Groupware. However, we believe that most studies on electronic idea generation and voting can be classified as GDSS because, although a decision may not be made in the electronic meeting, the information generated in it may be used to support a decision at a later date.

Several categories have been proposed for classifying GDSS, but perhaps the most common are in terms of their: group size (small or large), geographic dispersion (one group location versus many group or individual locations), and time dispersion (synchronous or asynchronous) in a three-dimensional grid. Table 2 shows characteristics of each box in the grid. The majority of GDSS studies have concentrated on small or large face-to-face (FTF) groups representing only two of the twelve possible GDSS scenarios, and only a few studies have investigated GDSS groups in which participants work individually in separate locations (GDSS nominal groups). We have not been able to find any research conducted on GDSS groups split into subgroups that are able to communicate electronically with each other. Therefore, we believe that our research represents a step towards completing an investigation of the three-dimensional grid (see Table 2).

## 2.4. Nominal group GDSS research

Nominal groups involve participants who work individually at different locations and share their information later (for traditional nominal groups) or electronically (in the case of GDSS nominal groups) [11].

In one study, researchers found that nominal GDSS groups were more productive than face-to-face (FTF)

groups for both idea quantity and quality and more productivity per person [19]. Members of four-person nominal groups were the most productive and eight-person FTF groups were the least. The researchers attempted to explain this difference by the fact that more verbal interruptions may occur in FTF groups (laughing, talking about a written comment, etc.). There was no significant difference in satisfaction measures between the FTF and nominal groups.

In a second study involving 6- and 12-member nominal (non-communicating) and FTF (communicating) groups, researchers found that there were no differences in the 6-member groups, but the 12-member FTF groups generated more ideas [6]. These results were confirmed in a third study in which researchers found that FTF groups generated more ideas with higher quality than did groups of physically-separated individuals or sub-groups which pooled their comments after the meeting [7]. The latter results tend to contradict the earlier ones. Thus, it is not clear whether groups in which participants work individually or in sub-groups on a computer (without communication among them) are more productive than groups in which participants work in an FTF environment. Further, it is even less clear whether groups in which participants work individually or in sub-groups (with communication among them) are more productive than groups in which participants work in an FTF environment with communication.

## 3. The experiment

## 3.1. Hypotheses

Based upon prior research and theoretical considerations, we developed the following hypotheses concerning the differences between synchronous legislative session groups and virtual legislative session groups (hereafter termed face-to-face (FTF) groups and non-FTF (NFTF) groups, respectively):

H1: There will be no difference between the two types of groups in terms of the number of raw comments generated.

H2: There will be no difference between the two types of groups in terms of the number of quality comments generated.

H3: There will be no difference between the two types of groups in terms of the number of unique/quality comments generated.

H4: There will be no difference between the two types of groups in terms of their perception of comment anonymity.

H5: There will be no difference between the two types of groups in terms of their satisfaction with the system.

H6: There will be no difference between the two types of groups in terms of their satisfaction with the comments generated.

H7: There will be no difference between the two types of groups in terms of their satisfaction with their group membership.

H8: There will be no difference between the two types of groups in terms of their self-perceived participation.

## 3.2. Subjects

A total of 89 undergraduate business students took part in the experiment as a partial requirement of an MIS class having four sections. Subjects were randomly assigned to experimental conditions and groups with about eight members.

## 3.3. Task

Group members focused on an idea generation or creativity task [13], i.e., their goal was to generate novel ideas for the solution of a problem. The subjects were asked to contribute suggestions for the solution of “the parking problem on campus,” a problem which has been the focus of other GDSS experimental groups [2]. The students had a significant stake in the problem, but they obviously did not have ultimate decision-making authority for it, thus possibly limiting the external validity of the experiment. However, many studies have suggested that GDSS experiments using students rather than managers facing similar tasks do replicate findings of GDSS field experiments in organizations [16].

## 3.4. Treatments

Each group used group idea generation software based upon the gallery brainwriting technique $[21]$ . However, nearly all other GDSS idea generation software used in experimental studies is based upon the individual poolwriting technique $[1]$ . While there are significant differences between these two techniques (participants may be more satisfied with gallery brainwriting), we do not believe the choice of software affects the study $[3]$ .

## 3.5. Measures

A post-session questionnaire was given to the subjects after the GDSS sessions (see Appendix). Subjects were asked to rate their satisfaction with: their group, their proposed solutions, and the meeting software. They were also asked about their perceptions of anonymity and group participation. These questions were based upon those in earlier studies.

The comments generated by each group were also counted. In addition to the number of raw comments counted, the number of quality comments (defined here as simply comments related to the topic), and the number of unique, quality comments were counted (as evaluated by two faculty reviewers).

## 3.6. Experimental results

Table 3 shows the mean number of comments generated by the two types of groups. In general, there was a high degree of non-task behavior, e.g. just over half of the comments were related to the topic, but this is normal when students are first introduced to the anonymous environment of the GDSS. There was no significant difference between the two types of groups in terms of the total number of comments (F=1.62; d.f.=1,10; Pr>F=0.23) and the number of quality comments (F=1.69; d.f.=1,10; Pr>F=0.22). However, there was a significant difference (using alpha =0.05) in the number of unique, quality comments (F=6.54; d.f.=1,10; Pr>F=0.03); NFTF groups generated more unique, quality comments. Thus, H3 is rejected but H1 and H2 are not.

## 3.6.2. Discussion

Other researchers have noted that results may vary markedly in the field. For example, in other experiments, nominal GDSS experimental groups were placed in rooms with nothing else to do except type comments on the computer. Participants located in their own office connected via the software are likely to have many distractions, including phone calls, paperwork, visitors, etc., reducing their participation in the meeting. However, as noted earlier, there was a large amount of non-task behavior by all groups in the experiment.

Business men and women are likely to be more focused than students on the task presented to them. Although we do not believe the high degree of non-task behavior affected the results (there was an equal amount in both types of groups), it may not be possible to generalize these results outside the laboratory.

This study shows that group meetings may be conducted by splitting a large group into sub-groups linked electronically. Members of these sub-groups were more satisfied with the technology, they thought participation was more likely to be equal amongst people, and believed the comments were more anonymous. Further, non-face-to-face groups generated just as many raw, quality, and unique/quality ideas as did face-to-face groups. Therefore, we can see very few disadvantages and several advantages for these geographically-dispersed sub-groups.

If there is no significant difference between FTF and NFTF groups, more meetings may be held remotely, bringing larger groups and more expertise to a problem. With the addition of teleconferencing to GDSS, researchers may implement a “window to anywhere” to simulate the across-the-table feeling of being together when groups are physically separate $[12]$ .

## 4. Conclusions

Conducting effective meetings with sub-groups in separate locations now is possible through the use of GDSS. Earlier research has shown that the technology can improve group productivity in face-to-face environments; we determined that geographically-dispersed groups can perform just as well, if not better. Groups split between two rooms generated more comments in an idea generation task, were more satisfied with the technology, and believed the comments were more anonymous than did groups meeting in a single room.
