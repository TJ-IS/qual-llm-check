# Information systems variables and management productivity

Satya N. Prattipati $^{*}$ , Michael O. Mensah

School of Management, University of Scranton, Pa 18510, USA

## Abstract

The companies in the Premier 100 list published by ComputerWorld (CW) in 1994 were classified into groups based on the information productivity index (IPI) computed by Paul Strassmann. Discriminant analysis was used to identify the significant discriminating information systems factors between the two groups. They are: 'number of years the Chief Information Officer (CIO) has been in the company in that position,' 'proportion of software resources spent on client server applications,' and 'percentage of software budget spent on new development.' On average the CIOs in the high productivity group have been with the company for three years, as compared to eight years in the low productivity group. The companies in the high group spent $54\%$ of the software development budget on client server applications, as compared to $35\%$ for the low productivity group. Companies in the high productivity group spent $46\%$ of software budget on in-house development of new applications, as opposed to $60\%$ for the low productivity group. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Information systems productivity; Management productivity; Business value of information technology; Information systems variables; Discriminant analysis

## 1. Introduction

U.S companies invested more than a trillion dollars on information technologies (IT) in the 1980s [10]. The magnitude of this investment has prompted questions about the payoff for both the national economy and individual companies [2, 9]. However, research attempts to measure the business value of IT have been plagued by intractable measurement problems.

These problems arise primarily because researchers assume that investments in IT increase the productivity and profitability of companies and that this cause-and-effect relationship can be measured. Researchers have been looking for a model that will accomplish this and possibly provide guidance to management on

IT investment decisions. So far this approach has not succeeded [11, 21].

The objectives of this paper are to suggest another strategy for obtaining insight on IT productivity and provide exploratory evidence to prove this alternative strategy. Instead of trying to measure aspects of the IT investments (causes), and the resulting changes in productivity, profits, or other outcomes (effects), we study companies that have been relatively more successful and those that have been relatively less successful (with respect to certain productivity measures) to determine if the differences in results are associated with special information system (IS) variables. Thus, we report observed associations between indicators of productivity and certain IS factors without claiming any causal relationship. This indirect evidence can provide insight on differences between IT investment variables of more successful companies and those of less successful ones. The differential IT variables can also provide direction in the development of new approaches for studying the nature and effect of successful IT strategies.

The dependent variable in this study, IS productivity, is based on a measure of management productivity used by ComputerWorld (CW) to select and rank the Premier 100 list of companies published in 1994 [7]. We explored associations between IS productivity and several independent IS variables using discriminant analysis. Our discriminant function has isolated some variables that successfully discriminated between a sample of relatively high IS productivity firms and another sample with lower IS productivity. The significant IS variables in the model were 'the Chief Information Officer's (CIO's) years in the position,' 'the proportion of software resources spent on the development of new applications,' and 'the proportion of software development resources spent on client server applications.' The relationships of these factors to IS productivity were found to be negative for the first two factors and positive for the third.

## 2. Previous studies on IS productivity

Researchers on the benefits of IT investments have investigated the relationships between IT expenditure and a variety of corporate performance outcomes [8, 12, 17, 19, 20, 23]. Table 1 lists the results of a sample of key studies that have investigated the effect of IT expenditures on outcomes such as operating cost efficiencies, labor productivity, customer service, and market share. Others, such as Brynjolfsson and Hitt [5], have tried to determine the return on IT investments for samples of firms.

The results of these studies are mixed. For example, while Alpar and Kim [1], Bender [4], and to some extent Banker and Kauffman [3] find that various positive operational results are associated with IT expenditures, many other studies, including Loveman [13], Strassmann [22], and Pentland [18] failed to link IT investments with traditional operating performance measures. There is considerable agreement in the literature that the lack of clear evidence of the business benefits is largely caused by the researchers' inability to measure the benefits, most of which tend to be qualitative, and thus intangible. For example, a committee of the National Research Council [6] points out difficulties such as:

These difficulties are compounded by the fact that the payoff from IT investments may vary over time; for example, between periods of recession or downsizing and periods of growth, so that different research results can be reported from different sample periods. Brynjolfsson and Hitt allude to this difficulty by speculating that most of the restructuring and cost cutting necessary to realize the potential benefits of IT investments in many firms began only in the 1990s. Thus, studies of IS productivity are more likely to report positive results in the 1990s.

These difficulties and the resulting mix of reported findings notwithstanding, the need to learn more about the appropriate levels and manner of deployment of IT investments continues. Investment decisions based on hunch, intuition, and good faith may not maximize payoffs. There is a need to identify variables associated with successful IT investment and deployment in order to improve the quality of IT decision making. This paper presents preliminary findings on some IS variables that may be related to the successful deployment of IT investments.

## 3. Data and research variables

## 3.1. Source of data

Our research design required the identification of two samples of firms differentiated by a relevant outcome. The companies with relatively high scores on this operational outcome were designated as 'higher productivity companies,' while companies with relatively low scores on the same outcome were grouped into a 'lower productivity' sample. Our two samples are drawn from CW's 'Premier 100' list of companies: it considers these to be the most effective users of IT. Since 1988 CW, has been publishing an annual list of the 100 most effective users of IT. In its earlier publications, CW based its selection on a composite score for each company that reflected factors such as amounts spent on IT, amounts spent on end-user computing, and overall company profitability. In 1994, however, CW began to use a ranking procedure designed to reflect IS productivity rather than the magnitude of IT investments. We used the cross-sectional data collected by CW for its 1994 company rankings for analysis.

The operational outcome measure, which forms our basis for defining membership in the higher productivity sample versus the lower productivity sample was adopted from Paul Strassmann. Strassmann's Information Productivity Index (IPI) was used by CW to rank its 'Premier 100' list of companies. We, therefore, formed our two samples by defining our higher productivity group as those scoring in the top $33\%$ on the IPI measure, and grouping those companies in the bottom $33\%$ on the IPI score as our lower productivity sample. We recognize that using this sample of the 'top 100' firms does not allow us to make very powerful tests, since the two sub-samples may not be sufficiently different. However, we note that discriminating variables found in this study may be understatements of their importance and would therefore provide the motivation for research using more informative data.

## 4. Research variables

## 4.1. Information productivity index (IPI)

Strassmann and CW define and measure this dependent variable as an index of two management variables: Management Output, defined for the CW rankings as the Economic Value Added (EVA) of the company (net income after taxes, less cost of equity capital), and Management Input, defined as sales, general, and administrative expenses (SG&A). The ratio of Management Output to Management Input is the IPI index.

$\mathbf{IPI} = [\mathbf{Net Income After Taxes}$

-Cost of Equity Capital]/SG and A

We adopted this measure of IS productivity for a number of reasons. First, we could not isolate the effects of IT deployment on overall corporate performance. Secondly, the use of EVA as a measure of management productivity and corporate performance continues to gain wide acceptance among corporate managers (see [15, 14, 16]: these argue that EVA is a better measure of corporate and management performance than conventional approaches, such as return on investment (ROI), because it reflects, among other things, the total cost a company's capital.) Finally, the link between management productivity and IS productivity is probably strong since important reasons for IT investments include the expected improvement in the informational environment of managerial planning, coordination, control, and decision-making. If IT investments enhance the effectiveness of management in these respects, an appropriate measure of management productivity should strongly capture IT benefits.

## 4.2. Independent variables (IT variables)

In addition to the IPI published for the ‘Premier 100’ companies, CW also collected several IS deployment and management variables about each company. These variables, although not suggested by any theory of IT deployment, were developed and used by experienced IS practitioners who consider them to be relevant IT deployment factors. These, and the dependent variable, IPI, are listed and described in Table 2.

Information Systems Budget (ISBUDGET) is the amount of each company's budgeted expenditure on all IT activities in 1993 scaled by its total sales for the same year. This variable is meant to capture the relative size of IT investments in the tradition of previous research. Following hypothesized relationships in previous research, we expect the sign of this variable, if significant, to be positive. We also include a second IT size variable, IS Employees (ISEMP), the number of people working in IS as a percentage of total employees. The percentage change in ISBUDGET from the previous year (CBUD) is meant to capture growth in IT investments. Our expectation is that growth and innovation are directly related to IT productivity and therefore the sign of this factor should be positive.

Next, we define two variables showing how the IT budget is spent. The percentage spent on the internal development of new software (NEWD) measures the 'R&D' component of IT expenditures. The percentage spent on client/server applications (CSAP) is meant to capture the extent to which IT is accessible to end-users through desktops. The percentage spent outside the IS department (OUT) is meant to capture the extent of decentralization of IT decisions and therefore the extent of user orientation or user friendliness of IT within each organization. The number of desktop machines (PCS) and the percentage of desktop devices networked (NETWK) are additional employee access variables, which may contribute to labor productivity. Finally, the CIO's years in his/her position with the same company (CIOYR), and the reporting level of the CIO, (LEVEL) were included to capture the effects of CIO experience and clout on IS productivity.

## 5. Analysis and results

As indicated earlier, we formed two samples of companies from the CW 'Premier 100' list. Out of this list, only 86 were usable because of missing data. From the usable cases, all companies with an IPI score of 0.28 or higher were classified into the higher IS productivity group and those companies with a score of 0.17 or lower were classified into a lower IS productivity group. We deliberately omitted companies falling between these score cutoff points. Table 3 shows the distribution of average scores on our research variables by the 12 broad industry groups represented in our sample. Table 4 provides profile data as well as mean scores on the independent variables for the two samples. We note that there are statistically significant differences in the means of five independent variables (ISEMP, NEWD, PCS, NETWK, and CIOYR).

Correlation coefficients between the dependent variable, IPI, and the independent variables are displayed in Table 5. Table 6(A) and (B) shows correlations among the independent variables.

We performed a discriminant analysis between the higher and lower productivity samples to identify the significant IS variables that differentiate the groups. The results of this analysis are provided in Table 7. They indicate that the Wilkes Lambda of the discriminant function is statistically significant at the 0.01 level. The discriminant function was therefore effective in differentiating the two samples. We note that the function was successful in correctly classifying 82% of the cases in the samples. We tested the effectiveness of the discriminant function further, by applying the ‘leaving-one-out’ method. This involved omitting one case at a time from the function calculation and classifying the left-out case with the resulting function. We did this with 47 cases and correctly classified 33, a 70% success rate.

Three IS variables, ‘CIO’s years in position (CIOYR),’ ‘proportion of software resources spent on development of new applications (NEWD),’ and ‘the proportion of software resources spent on client/server applications (CSAP)’ were the most significant variables in the discriminant function. The average CIO’s years in position for the higher productivity sample was 3.0 years compared to 8.3 years for the lower productivity sample. The proportion of software resources spent on new applications development was an average 46% for the higher productivity sample and 60% for the lower productivity sample. For client server applications development, the higher productivity sample spent a average of 54% of their software development budget compared to an average of 35% for the lower productivity sample.

As shown in Table 3, the ISBUDGET is consistently higher for manufacturing, utilities and transportation firms than for other companies. The reverse is true for ISEMP. This suggested the need to explore the impact of industry effects. We divided firms into manufacturing (including transports and utilities) and non-manufacturing subsamples. These subsamples had statistically different means for ISBUDGET (manufacturing 1.9%; non-manufacturing 0.9%) and ISEMP (manufacturing 2.6%; non-manufacturing 7.8%). There were no other statistically different variable means.

We performed discriminant analysis for each subsample. Table 8 summarizes the results.

As shown, the independent variables CIOYR and LEVEL were significant discriminators for both manufacturing and non-manufacturing firms. For both manufacturing and non-manufacturing firms, a higher CIOYR is associated with low IS productivity. For manufacturing firms a higher reporting level for the CIO is associated with low productivity while the reverse is true for non-manufacturing firms. Finally, NEWD was replaced by ISEMP in the discriminant function for non-manufacturing firms. For non-manufacturing firms a high score on ISEMP is associated with high productivity. Although these changes in the significance of discriminating variables may indicate valid industry effects, the small sizes of our industry subsamples present possible reliability problems.

The results also indicate that while companies in the high productivity group are spending proportionately lower amounts on the development of new applications software (in-house development), they are spending proportionately more on client server applications. They may, therefore, be using outsourcing to a greater extent by making purchases from well-established developers of client server applications; this may be a risk reducing and cost saving factor.

Industry effects may be worthy of further study. When a sample of mainly manufacturing firms was analyzed, client server applications (CSAP) was replaced by the level (LEVEL) at which the CIO reports as a discriminating factor. In manufacturing firms it appears that a lower CIO reporting level is associated with higher productivity. By contrast, in non-manufacturing firms, a higher CIO reporting level is associated with higher productivity. This may be due to differences in how widespread IS functions are in organizations and therefore the appropriate level of leadership. Information systems functions in non-manufacturing firms may be more diffuse and therefore require the company-wide direction that a CEO can give. For non-manufacturing firms ISEMP also emerged as a discriminating variable. It appears that non-manufacturing firms require a higher proportion of employees in IS functions for higher productivity. This is perhaps to support greater numbers of non-technical IT users present in service industries.

Although the other IS variables were not statistically significant discriminators at conventional levels the limited contrasts inherent in our sample may have understated the importance of at least some of them. The ideal situation would be to compare companies in the Premier 100 list to a sample of companies identified at the other extreme in terms of IPI scores.
