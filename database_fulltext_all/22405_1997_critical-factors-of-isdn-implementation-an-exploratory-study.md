# Critical factors of ISDN implementation: An exploratory study

Vincent S. Lai $^{*}$

Department of Decision Sciences and Managerial Economics, Faculty of Business Administration, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shatin, N.T., Hong Kong

Received 30 November 1996; accepted 24 July 1997

## Abstract

Two hundred and forty-seven companies in the United States (US) were surveyed to determine whether various factors were associated with ISDN implementation success. Previously identified implementation factors from research in innovation adoption and diffusion, IT implementation, and IS planning were selected as likely to be important in the study. The results indicated that compatibility, relative advantage, complexity, champion, management support, openness, and formalization factors were indicative of ISDN implementation success. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Innovation; Implementation; Integrated services digital network; ISDN; Network management

## 1. Introduction

No area within the field of information systems has been more affected by technology than data communications [42]. The 1980s were deemed to be the decade of the local area network (LAN), in which intelligent workstations and peripherals were interconnected to allow sharing of computing and communication resources. Huge leaps in technology, associated with global awareness, led to the 1990s being dubbed 'the global internetwork decade'. It is expected that this worldwide internet architecture will eventually evolve into a seamless interconnection of all major networking protocols on a shared, well-managed backbone, supporting commerce, telework, information access and information distribution [6].

The integrated services digital network (ISDN) is a technology developed to realize this expectation.

ISDN, which enables the integration of voice, data, and/or images, providing end-to-end digital communications over existing twisted-pair cable, can transmit applications at data rates ranging from less than 300 bits per second (bps) to up to 2 Mbps. With this transmission bandwidth, ISDN supports services such as remote meter-reading, energy management, security management, audio, telephony, facsimile, graphics, videotext, video-conference, and high speed data networks [35]. Some of the benefits claimed for ISDN are line sharing, session multiplexing, high throughput, virtually error-free transmission, local access to packet switched networks, automatic number identification, sophisticated and automated diagnostics, and fast call setup.

Currently, there are two classes of ISDN services. They are the basic rate interface (BRI) and the primary rate interface (PRI). The BRI, also known as 2B+D, provides two bearer channels of ISDN service at 64 Kbps and one control channel at 16 Kbps. It is appropriate for residential and small business users. The PRI, also known as 23B+D, provides twenty-three 64 Kbps bearer channels and one 64 Kbps channel for control. This service is better suited for organizations with a heavier, stricter demand of data communications.

While the feasibility and implementation of ISDN has been clearly demonstrated in several European and Asian countries, awareness of this technology is not widespread among the business communities in the US $[21, 29, 41]$ . Indeed, at the moment, the major issues that slow down the acceptance of ISDN in the US are cost, standardization, competition, and regulatory matters $[22, 40]$ . Currently, some of these issues are being addressed by a large conglomerate of telecommunication companies, business corporations, and ISDN user groups. Their aim is to implement a national ISDN standard to enable different dial plan, tariff, and hardware to interconnect using a common network protocol. With these efforts, ISDN will become a more pervasive technology and its importance will become more evident.

## 2. Background

Much research has been conducted in the area of information technology (IT) implementation. Most of these studies adopted two distinct perspectives, adoption and diffusion, to evaluate IT implementation at either the individual or organizational level. Research at the individual level has focused on isolating factors that are likely to be the best predictors of an individual's adopting a specified technology. In contrast, organizational level research focuses on understanding the adoption and diffusion process of the adopting organization.

Viewed from a technological perspective, implementation is the assessment of the extent to which IT product, policy, or process was actually used, delivered, or carried into effect in accordance with the intentions of the IT's developer, inventor, or sponsor [39]. Much research has been undertaken to develop a better understanding of both IT implementation process, problems, and success factors (such as [32, 37]).

Still, IT implementation research has been quite limited in its perspectives and progress $[27]$ . Cooper and Zmud $[11]$ attribute this problem to the lack of directing and organizing framework to foster integration of implementation research streams. In response to this problem, stage-based IT implementation research models, which integrate the theories from organizational change, innovation, and technological diffusion literature, were developed $[38]$ .

The stage model essentially captures the organizational learning process at typically three stages: initiation; adoption; and implementation $[24]$ . Initiation is the scanning of opportunities and IT solutions when the organization feels the pressure for change. Adoption is the decision to commit resources to the acquisition of the innovation. Implementation is the development, installation, usage, and maintenance of the innovation to increase organizational effectiveness. Since our study is primarily concerned with the evaluation of ISDN implementation, we examine only the last stage.

## 3.1. Innovation characteristics

Extensive research has been conducted in analyzing the impact of innovation characteristics on the extent of implementation in organizations [1]. Studies have utilized distinctive variables in assessing implementation, therefore making synthesis of these studies difficult. To resolve this issue, Tornatzky and Klein [43] performed a meta-analysis on twenty-seven adoption and diffusion articles to identify common variables that motivate implementation. They concluded that only compatibility, relative advantage, and complexity are relevant to adoption–diffusion studies.

Compatibility is the degree to which an innovation is consistent with the existing values, past experiences, and needs of the potential adopters. Prior studies on innovation have found compatibility to be positively related to innovation adoption and diffusion [28]. Incompatibility could inhibit further diffusion and implementation success due to problems such as different network protocols within organizations. Hence:

H1: The greater the compatibility of ISDN, the greater the extent of ISDN implementation success.

Relative Advantage is the degree to which a new innovation is perceived by the potential adopters as being superior to the practice(s) it will supersede and any other solution that might be possible. The efficiencies and benefits derived from using ISDN for data transmission have been reported in many studies. The positive perception of the benefits of ISDN should provide significant impetus for use of the technology, thereby leading to greater implementation success $[14, 36]$ . From these viewpoints, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2: The greater the relative advantage of ISDN, the greater the extent of ISDN implementation success.

Complexity is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being difficult to understand and use. Many research studies found that complexity can function as an inhibitor to adoption and can also inhibit further diffusion and implementation success. Many large organizations' network users perceived ISDN to be a complex network and admitted that they lacked technical capability to install this technology. The negative perception of the complexity of ISDN should limit the diffusion and satisfaction level with the technology, thereby inhibiting the extent of implementation success. Hence:

H3: The lower the complexity of ISDN, the greater the extent of ISDN implementation success.

## 3.2. Vendor actions

Little work has been conducted within the IS discipline to explore the impact of vendor actions on IT implementation. Instead, most vendor action research has been conducted within the field of Marketing to evaluate vendor strategies in promoting new products to potential consumers. Assael [3] explored the relationship between consumer behavior and marketing action, suggesting that vendor involvement can significantly contribute to the rate of adoption and diffusion of new products. Brown [9] proposed that vendor actions, such as providing a supporting IT infrastructure, price reduction, and product promotion, can result in an increase in the rate of adoption.

One significant vendor involvement that could have a strong impact is the provision of effective training. Essentially, the effect of training is the enhancement of acceptance by impacting the perceived accessibility of the system $[12]$ . Since ISDN is a transient technology that represents new ways of handling communication activities to the network users, the provision and adequacy of training is deemed to be a major concern in its implementation. Among all the available approaches, vendor training is considered to be the most effective, especially when the technology is still relatively unknown. From these viewpoints, the following hypothesis was proposed:

H4: The greater the extent of vendor involvement, the greater the extent of ISDN implementation success.

## 3.3. Management characteristics

Lindsey et al. [30] identified four critical factors for implementation success. The most significant one was attributed to the presence of a responsive, visionary champion. These often play the roles of opinion leader, change agent, or top management surrogate [13] and set the right technological policy for the creation of an environment conducive to technology innovation. Beath [5] found that champions can be a key to implementation success if they are provided with (1) assistance in developing information, (2) a flexible implementation process, and (3) political support. Hence:

H5: The more the champions in an organization, the greater the extent of ISDN implementation success.

The efforts of management associated with planning, organizing, controlling, and directing IT implementation within an organization have received great attention. Much research has been undertaken to investigate the link of IT implementation and management support. Their results confirmed that top management support $[31]$ and general management support $[2, 19]$ were both significant in affecting the successful implementation of IT. Management support can encourage IT diffusion explicitly through expressed preference and mandates or implicitly through reward systems and incentives $[20]$ . Hence:

H6: The greater the support from the management, the greater the extent of ISDN implementation success.

## 3.4. Organizational characteristics

Many research studies $[7, 18]$ indicate that contextual factors in the organization have a significant impact on the IT diffusion process. These are varied, and include such characteristics as openness of the organization and the existence of norms that encourage change within the organization. Openness is the degree to which members of a system are linked to others who are external to the system. In general, an open, innovative organization with positive attitude toward change are more likely to diffuse ISDN aggressively within their internal and external communication activities, and realize greater implementation success. Hence:

H7: The greater the openness of an organization, the greater the extent of ISDN implementation success.

Others [10, 16] scrutinized the influence of organizational centralization and formalization on the extent of technology implementation and diffusion. Centralization is the degree of decision-making concentration and formalization is the degree of reliance an organization places on formal rules and procedures. Research on centralization has often found this variable to be negatively related to adoption behavior, but positively related to implementation success [24]. Studies on the effects of formalization on innovation implementation [48] also showed that formal procedures would facilitate innovation implementation. Based on these viewpoints, the following hypotheses were postulated:

H8: The greater the centralization of an organization, the greater the extent of ISDN implementation success.

H9: The greater the formalization of an organization, the greater the extent of ISDN implementation success.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Instrumentation

Most of the questions in the questionnaire were adapted from previous studies on innovation, diffusion, implementation, and information management. However, specific questionnaire items were developed to collect data relevant to the objectives of the study, as well as descriptive information. The derived questionnaire was then pilot tested in three companies with varying degrees of diffusion of ISDN in their network operations. The feedback from pilot testing was used in refining the format of the questionnaire and the addition, removal, and rephrasing of several items.

Data for this study were collected by using seven-point Likert scale items, based on the extent of agreement or disagreement with specific issues related to the variables being measured. Aggregation of scores over all questions provided the composite score of that variable. The operational measurement of each variable is shown in Table 2.

## 4.2. Data collection

The revised questionnaire was mailed to the MIS Directors of 247 organizations in the US that were using ISDN services. Names of these organizations were drawn from different sources (such as [23, 28], and phone companies). One month later, reminder letters and additional copies of the questionnaire were mailed to the surveyed organizations that had not responded. Of all the questionnaires mailed, eighty-seven responses were received, out of which eighty-six were usable. This is a 34.8% response rate, which is good considering the length, difficulty, and confidential nature of the subject matter of the questionnaire.

## 4.3. Respondent profile

Sample profiles, by industry and respondent positions of the ISDN adopters are shown in Figure 2. The largest number of respondents came from the manufacturing industry. Such a distribution is fairly representative of the distribution among businesses in the US.

A comparison of the demographic data from both mailings was performed to evaluate the possibility and extent of response bias in the survey. The largest percentage of respondents in both mailings were from the manufacturing, computer services, and finance companies. As for the respondent's organizational size, analysis indicated almost equal distribution in both mailings, with the largest percentage coming from the companies having sales over \$50 million and employees over 1,000. The analysis of respondent's current job title from both mailings indicated a high level of seniority, with 67% above manager level in the first mailing and 74% in the second mailing. As the analysis of the two mailings revealed similar background of respondent profile, the appropriateness of the sample for further analysis is justified.

## 4.4. Validity and reliability

The instrument was tested for various validity and reliability properties to ensure that true measures were being used. First, content validity was established through careful selection of previously validated instruments before adapting them to the study. Second, Cronbach's alpha was employed to analyze the variables for measurement reliability. Items with low correlations were dropped. The results of these analyses are presented in Table 3. The analysis showed that the reliability of variables were significantly higher than the 0.5 to 0.6 range suggested by Nunnally [34] for early stages of basic research. Third, the research variables were checked for discriminant and convergent validity by counting the number of intra-scale significant correlations with respect to inter-scale correlation. The result of these measures indicated satisfactory convergent and discriminant validity of the survey.

## 5. Analysis of results

## 5.1. Extent of ISDN implementation

Our survey indicated that ISDN was mostly in its initiation stage within adopting organizations. Among all adopters, the majority of them had installed less than 100 ISDN lines. Only two companies had more than 1,000 lines installed. Nearly 82 companies (95.3%) had adopted BRI service, whereas only three (3.5%) adopted PRI, and one had adopted both BRI and PRI services. However, neither services was being utilized at, or near, capacity. Only six respondents (7%) indicated that they had utilized more than 45% of their ISDN capacity. None of the respondents had utilized it to perform more than 60% of the company's local communication activities. The performance of ISDN within inter-organizational communication activities was even more disappointing. Only five companies had engaged ISDN's BRI service for more than 45% of their external communication activities. Further analysis indicated that if ISDN was used in inter-organizational communications, it was generally confined to companies within the same metropolitan area. Only three respondents used ISDN services in inter-city communications.

## 5.2.1. Innovation characteristics

Examination of the relationships between ISDN innovation characteristics and their impact on implementation indicated a statistically significant relationship $p<0.01$ , thereby supporting hypotheses 1, 2, and 3. This implies that perceived compatibility, relative advantage, and complexity of ISDN are important elements for the successful implementation of ISDN. Further investigation of ISDN characteristics revealed that:

\- Adopters reported that ISDN was most compatible with their communication platform, as opposed to the hardware and software platform indicators used in the study. In fact, 73.4% of the respondents either agreed or strongly agreed that they did not have to restructure their communication platforms to evolve to ISDN.

\- Several respondents stated that the benefits of ISDN are often conditional. If the ubiquity of ISDN and the ‘critical mass’ [25] of adoption are not attained, the proclaimed advantages of ISDN will not be fulfilled. For ISDN to reach critical mass, there must be a strong technology push phase followed by the identification of an adequate infrastructure penetration, enough cost-effective business applications, and low cost customer premises apparatus to create ISDN demand pull. All these seem to be happening now.

\- Normally, people are less interested in complex innovations. In our study, some respondents indicated discomfort with ISDN due to its perceived complexity. To overcome their problem, they had to make greater commitment to implementation to assure smoother installation.

## 5.2.2. Vendor actions

No significant relationship was found between vendor involvement and the extent of ISDN implementation $p=0.637$ . This finding contradicts previous research studies. Further evaluation indicated that:

\- There was a high standard deviation in the degree of ‘vendor involvement’ in promoting and assisting the implementation of ISDN. Additionally, the ISDN/carrier representatives spent an average of only ‘21 to 30 h’ on site, assisting with network installation. This duration, which is equivalent to less than four 8 h working days, is considered less when it comes to network installation.

\- Vendor training is a surrogate used to measure the extent of vendor involvement. According to the survey, almost all ISDN adopting companies (85 out of 86) indicated that vendor training was provided. Additionally, these companies provided additional training to their employees. The commonly used training sources included formal training programs within the company and documentation (such as ISDN news update, user manuals, and interest group flyers). Among all the training sources, the adopters expressed a feeling that vendor training was the most effective source, followed by formal training programs and informal expert consultation.

\- Around 44% (38) of ISDN adopters felt that their ISDN training had been more than adequate. However, almost 50% of the respondents reported that their vendors spent, on average, less than 10 h on training in their company.

## 5.2.3. Management characteristics

The positive relationship between champions and the extent of ISDN implementation was supported at the p<0.05 level of significance. The same study also supports significant relationship, at p<0.01, between management support and implementation success. These provide further support to the widespread agreement that managers are the key actors of implementation. Our study also observes some other interesting phenomena relevant to management:

\- On examining the champion variable, ISDN champions were found to be much more involved in the adoption decision stage than the implementation stage. Several factors could explain this. First, champions might not be competent or experienced enough to assist in ISDN implementation. Second, champions tend to be senior executives with a market vision. In almost 80% of the cases, the champions were located in the senior general management and MIS management, with a few coming from non-MIS departments. Such champions are more concerned in promoting strategies to actualize the organizational goals. Once the strategy (such as the acquisition of network facility) is determined, the champions might delegate the implementation authority to the subordinates, thereby decreasing their influence during implementation.

\- Technology awareness of MIS management, which was identified by the number of technical/trade journals or books subscribed, professional meetings attended, and external connections made, was also investigated to determine its impact on implementation. The results indicated that respondents became aware of ISDN from technical journals (81.7%), advertisements (67.4%), and professional meetings (46.1%). The vendors were found to be very ineffective in promoting the concepts of ISDN. Only 17.8% of our respondents mentioned that they first learned the existence of ISDN from their vendors.

## 5.2.4. Organizational characteristics

Evaluation of the relationship between organizational characteristics and ISDN implementation indicated that only openness and formalization lead to greater ISDN implementation success in organizations, at p<0.05 level of significance. Surprisingly, centralization is not found to have any influence on implementation success. One reason for this may be the subtle and complex effect of the organizational factors on implementation. It is perhaps more appropriate to adopt a longitudinal process method to explore the implementation process over time. Further evaluation of organizational characteristics indicates that:

\- Most organizations that exhibit an openness characteristic also exhibit some expansion actions to infuse IT. Interestingly, these organizations also have established either formal or informal procedures to guide the implementation of new IT. They indicated that these procedures stimulate employee interests in new innovations, which, in turn, reinforce the openness of their organizations.

\- A few organizations mentioned that their ISDN implementation was the result of their ISDN carrier's promotional strategy. Organizational characteristics or managerial influences have not played important roles in their ISDN implementation.

## 6. Summary

The objective of this study was to determine if there was a relationship between ISDN implementation success and nine major innovation, management, and organizational variables (compatibility, relative advantage, complexity, vendor involvement, champion, management support, openness, centralization, and formalization). The results indicate that compatibility, relative advantage, complexity, champion, management support, openness, and formalization are important determinants of ISDN implementation success.

Also implementation success, to a great extent, is determined by the characteristics of ISDN. Hence, practitioners, especially senior management, must realize that there are many investigation activities involved in analyzing the characteristics of ISDN. These activities allow the practitioners a chance to examine the compatibility of ISDN to their company's value, information base, and network infrastructure. These activities also allow the practitioners a chance to review the relative advantage of ISDN as compared to other network alternatives and the complexity of ISDN in use and installation. Execution of these activities offers the practitioners opportunities to study their current network infrastructure and projected implementation strategy, thereby improving the chance of implementation success.

Management and champion support in the planning and implementation of ISDN is also found necessary for the success of ISDN implementation. Perhaps the most important contribution from management is the provision of more accurate management expectations, learning more precisely of project plans and needs, development of mutually agreed upon project development priorities, narrowing the communication gap between management and members of the implementation team, and committing available resources to the completion of ISDN system. Additionally, management involvement would provide support to arbitrate political issues resulting from a change of power structure, normally caused by IT implementation that cuts across political boundaries. Thus, practitioners should try to include management in network projects to enhance the chance of success.

Some organizational variables are found to be more influential than others in ISDN implementation. The organizations most likely to achieve better implementation results are companies that are more open and formal. These findings have significant implications to practitioners – IT adoption is not a fad. Organizations should adopt innovations to improve their competitive edge and accomplish their organizational goals. External networks or connections will always be used to collect information to confirm their belief or disbelief in the attractiveness of IT in order to justify their costly investment.

ISDN is an evolving technology. There are still many concerns with the implementation and application of this technology. Therefore, it is important that practitioners do not grasp ISDN as the one true technology, but retain a balanced perspective. As technology development proceeds in cycles that include distinct exploratory and maturation phases, it is wise for practising managers to adopt ISDN slowly, in an evolutionary manner.
