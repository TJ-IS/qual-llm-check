# The impacts of presentation visuals on persuasion

Joline Morrison $^{a,*}$ , Doug Vogel $^{1,b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Management Information System, University of Wisconsin, Eau Claire, WI 54702, USA $^{b}$ Department of Management Information System, College of Business and Public Administration, University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ 85721, USA

Received 6 November 1996; revised 24 May 1997; accepted 21 July 1997

## Abstract

Today's microcomputer applications enable users to create and display elaborate presentation visuals that include color, clip art, and special effects such as animation and dynamic transitions. Does this impact the persuasiveness of a presentation? To explore this question, we performed two experimental studies to investigate the effect of use versus non-use, as well as the persuasive impact of visuals with different characteristics. The study provides practical guidelines for creating and using modern presentation graphics. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Persuasion; Presentation visuals

## 1. Introduction

Computer-generated visuals are commonly used to communicate information and induce an audience to accept a point of view or take a particular action. Ten years ago, these aids (usually in the form of 35 mm slides) accompanied only the most important presentations. Today, the hardware and software needed to create and display elaborate computer-generated graphics are readily available to most speakers. However, their benefits are largely based on anecdotal evidence and vendor-inspired claims. The purpose of our research is to explore how visuals impact the persuasiveness of a presentation. We also seek to develop guidelines for effective development of computergenerated visuals. This study should be of interest to software developers interested in developing and marketing-related products, managers and other individuals interested in making more effective presentations, and researchers studying the components of persuasion as related to technology.

## 2. Related work

In this study, the term ‘presentation visuals’ represents the text, charts, or images used by speakers to enhance and augment selected points of a message. Prior research has largely addressed information display options for decision support. Our work focuses on the computer-generated images that accompany oral communications for the purpose of persuasion, which can be defined as a communication process whereby the communicator seeks to influence behavior, change attitudes and beliefs, or otherwise cause acceptance of a new cognitive state in an area where the person being persuaded has some measure of freedom [3, 18, 20, 22].

The influence of presentation visuals on persuasion has received surprisingly little direct attention. Over the years, researchers have documented a generally beneficial relationship between the use of visuals and positive perceptions of a presenter. Seiler [21] noted that source credibility was positively affected. A study on the effects of the use of overhead transparencies on business meetings conducted by the Wharton Applied Research Center [24] noted that “presenters were perceived as better prepared, more professional, more persuasive, more highly credible, and more interesting when they used overhead transparencies than when they did not.” While the suggestion that presentation graphics enhance audience perceptions of a speaker is not surprising, few (if any) studies have systematically investigated their impacts on the persuasiveness of presentations.

## 3. Routes to persuasion

The communication and social psychology literature suggests two distinct routes to attitude change: central and peripheral. The first emphasizes the information that a person has about the attitude, object, or issue under consideration. This information is accumulated, assimilated, and evaluated. The receiver then integrates all this information into a coherent and reasoned position. The second approach suggests that attitude change is a result of peripheral “persuasion cues,” that is, factors or motives inherent in the setting sufficient to produce an initial attitude change without any active thinking about the attributes of the issue or object under consideration.

The impacts of visuals on presentation persuasiveness align with both routes. Speakers in a business setting normally use visuals for providing relevant information within the context of their message; persuasion, hopefully results, due to active thinking by the recipient about the relevant information presented. However, peripheral cues, such as the speaker's credibility, speaking ability, and type and quality of images are important as well. Therefore, we developed our research model based on Hovland's [12] "message learning" approach to communication, with modifications for peripheral cues.

This message-learning approach states that persuasion is communication intended to influence choice. The communication process can be analyzed in terms of who says what to whom, how, and with what effect. Communication occurs in a context or setting within the organization, and within the representative culture. The 'what' is the message content; this represents what the message has to say and includes evidence. 'Who' is the speaker, whose characteristics are an aspect of audience perceptions. 'To whom' represents the audience, composed of individuals with different characteristics. We suggest that 'how' represents the speaker medium (live and in-person, live via a remote telecommunications link, videotaped, etc.), the use versus non-use of presentation visuals, and specific characteristics of the visuals. 'Effect' is the persuasive impact of the presentation. According to the message-learning approach, persuasion is a function of attention, comprehension, agreement/yielding (degree of agreement with the presenter's position), and retention. In summary, this suggests that culture, content, context, speaker, speaker medium, audience characteristics, use of visuals, and visuals characteristics impact the persuasion components of attention, comprehension, agreement/yielding, and retention.

Our particular interest is in the area of visuals characteristics. Fig. 1 presents a taxonomy of commonly-used options that were evaluated in our experimental studies. As technology progresses, this taxonomy will, of course, evolve. Specifically, the message form of presentation visuals may include text or graphic images. These may be presented either in black and white or in color. The message may also contain animation within a single visual, or transition enhancements between visuals. Potential presentation media include overhead transparencies, 35 mm slides, and screen shows (presentations displayed using fixed projectors or projection panels in conjunction with laptop computers and overhead projectors).

## 4. Study 1: Investigation of color enhancement, imagery, and presentation medium

The first study investigates the impacts on persuasion of: (1) use versus non-use of presentation visuals; (2) color versus black and white visuals; (3) plain text versus text plus image-enhanced visuals (consisting of both 'clip-art' and graphs of numerical data); and, (4) 35 mm slides versus overhead transparencies. Previous studies have suggested that visuals provide a means to attract and hold subject attention [11], and that communicator cues, message content, and presentation visuals all contribute to subject attention. Deighton [7] observed that graphics tend to encourage viewer confidence in consumer studies, and Chaiken and Eagly [1] asserted that agreement is enhanced further by visual than verbal messages. Earlier work also suggests that using visuals in presentations increases comprehension and retention [10].

In terms of the characteristics of individual visuals, researchers have found that color positively influences attention (e.g. [14]), comprehension, and retention by providing redundant information that provides message reinforcement. The influence of graphic images is less clear. Research comparing the effectiveness of graphically-displayed data to numerical tables has been contradictory. However, 'clip-art' type images have been shown to enhance messages, specifically in the areas of changing consumer choices [19] and augmenting drug labels in gaining consumer compliance with safety messages [25]. Graphic images have also been noted to enhance learning and recall [1, 2, 6]. No literature exists to support or refute the contention of differences between $35\mathrm{mm}$ slides and overhead transparencies.

## 4.1. Preliminary study and model evolution

A preliminary study was conducted to test our experimental design. One hundred and fifty student subjects were divided into six groups and shown a 10-minute videotaped presentation attempting to persuade them to commit to attending a series of seminars on time management. Each treatment group was shown the same videotaped speaker, but each group saw different presentation visuals: black and white text transparencies, color text transparencies, black and white graphics-enhanced transparencies, color graphics-enhanced transparencies, and color, graphics-enhanced 35 mm slides. The control group saw only the speaker's portion of the presentation. The speaker was a 32-year old female deemed to be a 'typical' presenter. She had a steady, clear voice, little inflection, and few gestures or other forms of non-verbal communication. The room and associated context characteristics were identical for all treatments. The length of the presentation was approximately 10 minutes. The post-test questionnaire, given immediately at the conclusion of the presentation, measured the change in interest and propensity for commitment of time and money, as well as self-reported measures for attention, comprehension, and yielding. Additionally, information on perceived legibility of the visuals and demographics was gathered. Data on audience perceptions of the presenter were gathered for comparison with the 1981 Wharton study.

Analysis of the initial data indicated that presentation visuals had a strong impact on the audience's perception of the presenter, and a relatively weak impact on the components of persuasion. Further, consistent correlations existed between the audience's perceptions of the presenter and subject yielding, and, to a lesser extent, attention, comprehension, retention, and action. As a result, we developed a Persuasion Process Model that includes audience perception of the presenter as a mediator in the persuasion process (Fig. 2). This integration is consistent with the literature proposing an interactive relationship between messenger and message characteristics [16, 17].

Audience characteristics and 'Fixed' factors were held constant through the study, while Presentation visuals were manipulated. Potential paths of influence are indicated by the arrows between the factors. In particular, Presentation visuals were expected to affect both audience perceptions of the presenter and the components of persuasion. Perceptions of the presenter in turn were expected to impact the persuasion components, and vice versa.

## 4.2. Experimental study

In the full experimental treatment, 329 undergraduate business majors were divided into five treatment groups. Each group was shown the same videotaped speaker viewed in the preliminary study, but saw a different set of presentation visuals. Treatments were as follows: (1) black and white text transparencies; (2) color text transparencies; (3) black and white image-enhanced transparencies; (4) color image-enhanced transparencies; and (5) color image-enhanced 35 mm slides.

## 7. Study 2: Investigation of screen-show effects

The purpose of the second study was to investigate the impact of screen-show effects (animation and transitions) on presentation persuasiveness. While it is currently popular to use such effects for presentations and computer-based training, little (if any) empirical work has been done to measure their impacts. People today, particularly younger people such as our student subjects, are constantly bombarded with 'edutainment'; they expect to be entertained and stimulated by presentations, and audience attention and perception of the presenter may be negatively affected when they are not. Additionally, animation has been shown to enhance comprehension when the subject matter is used to illustrate dynamic qualities such as actions within chemical reactions $[15]$ . Therefore, we predicted that animation and transitions would enhance the attention of the listeners and their perceptions of the presenter. Additionally, we hypothesized that animation could be used to emphasize certain aspects of visuals, enabling subjects to 'zoom' in on critical aspects of the visual's message $[8]$ and thus enhance audience comprehension and retention. We could not find any existing evidence to suggest the impact of the multimedia effects on yielding.

## 7.1. Study overview

Presentation content, context, speaker, and speaker medium were identical to those used in the first study. Audience characteristics were essentially the same, with subjects consisting of 67 senior-level undergraduate business students. New visuals were created using a software application that supported animation and transitions. They were similar to the colored, image-enhanced visuals used in the original study, although some of the graphic images that were judged to be distracting were removed or replaced. Animation effects were added as follows:

\- Bar/Line/Pie Charts. Animation was used to make these charts appear to 'grow' dynamically.

\- Image-enhanced charts. Animation was used to add interest to charts containing clip art; for example, a light bulb was initially dark and then appeared to light up.

\- Bullet charts. Animation was used to make bullet items build on the visual as the speaker made new points.

Transitions between visuals consisted of different wipes, fades, blinds opening/closing, etc., and associated with the visuals in a way that the researchers felt was attractive. Subjects were randomly assigned to the following treatment groups: no visuals, non-enhanced visuals; transitions-only; animation-only; and, combined animation/transitions.

## 8. Measures

Immediately following the treatments, subjects were given a post-test questionnaire with measures identical to those used in the original study, except for the follow-up measure for retention. We felt that the initial study had confirmed a definite correlation between initial comprehension and retention. Identical audience presenter perception measures were also included, as well as subject demographic data, and information on visual acuity, color-blindness, perceived legibility, and position in the room.

An overall one-way ANOVA for all treatment groups (including the control group) revealed significant results for professionalism ( $F_{4,63}=3.2$ , p=0.019) and preparedness ( $F_{4,63}=2.0$ , p=0.10). Individual T-tests comparing each treatment group to the control group revealed that the combined animation/transitions group had the highest perceptions of the presenter, with significant results for preparedness (p=0.094), professionalism $(p=0.011)$ , clarity $(p=0.10)$ , strength $(p=0.10)$ , and quality of support data $(p=0.086)$ .

T-tests comparing individual treatment groups revealed significant results as well, with the combined animation/transitions visuals usually scoring highest. For preparedness, the combined animation/transitions visuals were superior to the plain $p=.025$ and transitions-only visuals $p=0.014$ ; for professionalism, the combined animation/transitions scored better than the plain visuals $p=0.049$ and better than the transitions-only visuals $p=0.022$ . For clarity, the combined animation/transitions visuals scored higher than the transitions-only visuals $p=0.035$ . In summary, the combined animation/transitions visuals enhanced presenter perception the most, while the plain and transitions-only visuals scored lowest. Regression analysis revealed that enhanced audience perceptions were strongly correlated to increased attention $p=0.003$ and, to a lesser degree, yielding $p=0.059$ . No correlation was found between perception and comprehension. In this particular study, the idea that enhanced audience perceptions of the speaker work as a mediator for persuasion was not proven. Path analysis indicated that although the subjects in the combined animation/transitions group had significantly higher perceptions of the speaker, this did not result in any significant improvements in persuasion.

## 10. Summary and discussion

Although previous studies suggest a strong correlation between the use of visuals and a corresponding increase in attention and yielding, this correlation did not surface in our studies. However, increased presenter perception seemed to be correlated to improved attention and yielding. One potential explanation is that no direct relationship exists between them, but that improvement occurs only when it is mediated through improved audience perception of the presenter: people may pay more attention and yield more to a presenter who, for whatever reason, is highly regarded. It is also possible that improved attention and yielding occur first, and this results in higher audience perceptions.

Although previous literature has been inconclusive, we saw a strong improvement in comprehension and retention with the visuals in both studies. Presenter perception seemed to have no direct relationship to comprehension or retention: subjects showed improved rates of comprehension and retention even when they did not have a favorable perception of the speaker.

As expected, the color transparencies were consistently superior to their black and white counterparts for comprehension, retention, and enhanced presenter perceptions. We attribute the comprehension and subsequent retention improvements to the ability of the color enhancements to help organize information. Surprisingly, the use of color did not seem to increase attention.

Previous studies have suggested that graphic images significantly enhance presentation visuals; however, we saw no significant differences. Although the image enhancements were reviewed by audio/video professionals, the imagery did not have a long history of proven successful use. We postulate that imagery only enhances plain text when it helps to clarify, organize, and/or add to the explanatory power of the visual. For example, an enhanced graphic incorporating a light bulb seemed to help subjects recognize the abstract idea of 'working smarter'; however, a stack of currency accompanying a dollar figure of organizational time loss may have only served to distract subjects. The terms 'chart-junk' and 'MacRubbish' have been used to describe the inexperienced or misguided application of imagery to visuals, and some of our imagery-enhanced visuals (as well as many of the visuals we often see in presentations) may suffer from this ailment.

In terms of presentation media, subjects perceived the presenter as being more interesting when accompanied by overhead transparencies, and as more professional when using 35 mm slides. The medium made no significant difference on the other persuasion components. We attributed the positive showing of the transparencies over the 35 mm slides for interest in part on the general activity and interaction associated with the handling of overhead transparencies compared to 35 mm slides: there may be a tendency to view a presenter using 35 mm slides as more ‘remote.’ The higher level of professionalism resulting from the 35 mm slides may have resulted from the fact that the subjects were more familiar with transparencies, and may have associated the 35 mm slides with more ‘important’ speakers or presentations.

Study 2 suggested that all of the screen-show effects improved comprehension, with the animation-only treatment appearing most effective. We believe that animation emphasized important concepts on some visuals, thus giving the subjects a mental image that enabled them to recall the messages successfully. This phenomenon was best illustrated by a 'Labeled Pie' visual which showed the percentage of time managers spend at different activities. In the animation-only version, the appropriate label for each section of the pie chart appeared as the speaker mentioned the percentage. The comprehension question corresponding to this visual asked what percentage of time managers spend in meetings; for added emphasis, the animation introduced a notable delay before this figure appeared on the screen. Subjects shown the plain visual averaged only 75% comprehension, while subjects in the animation group scored an impressive 100%. This slide contained a lot of information, and we hypothesized that the animation effectively emphasized the important points.

The combined animation/transitions treatments were substantially more effective than the other treatments for enhancing speaker perceptions. We attribute this to our original idea that subjects have come to expect ‘edutainment.’ Interestingly, our subjects reacted fairly negatively to the transitions-alone treatment. A possible explanation may have been the random nature of the transitions, which may have been interpreted as unnecessary ‘noise’ that did not add to the content of the presentation [9]. Another possibility is that the subjects were familiar with transitions within screen-show presentations so that this technology did not create any added stimulation or entertainment. Since many screen-show graphic packages enable users to create transitions between visuals, this is an area that would benefit from future studies.

## 11. Conclusions and limitations

Overall, our experimental studies indicated that the use of presentation visuals as well as specific characteristics of the visuals directly impact the components of persuasion as well as the audience perceptions of the presenter. The strongest direct effects were for comprehension and retention, while attention and yielding were correlated to improved perceptions of the presenter. This generally validates our Persuasion Process Model and provides a more detailed picture of the interrelationships among the components of persuasion for a particular scenario. However, it is important to note that these conclusions are strongly linked to the current study. It is still necessary to:

## 11.1. Investigate the impact of visuals characteristics on 'fixed' factors

The impacts of visuals need to be investigated in conjunction with other persuasion cultures, contexts, and messages to ascertain the generalizability of our results. Do ‘excellent’ speakers derive as much benefit from visuals as ‘average’ or ‘poor’ speakers? What about speakers who are remote via videotape or videoconferencing links?

## 11.2. Determine the effect of audience characteristics

When the results of these studies were presented to a group of practitioners in a major U.S. corporation one high-level manager was highly skeptical of our conclusion that combined animation/transition visuals improve presenter perceptions. He felt that if the study subjects had been older, they would have been less impressed with the ‘edutainment’ aspect of the presentation.

## 12. Implications for practice

One of the major objectives of this project was to formulate recommendations for how to use visual support technology for business presentations. We suggest the following guidelines:

## 12.1. Color vs. black and white

Color visuals are superior to their black and white counterparts. However, there is one caveat: color visuals must be sharp, clear, and legible.

## 12.2. Text vs. graphics

Graphics are useful for increasing information density, displaying information with multiple dimensions, organizing complex information, supporting abstract concepts, and illustrating trends. However, non-essential ‘clip-art’ may actually decrease presenter perceptions and the persuasive components of a presentation. When in doubt, use plain text.

## 12.3. Presentation medium

35 mm slides may inspire an image of professionalism. However, we recommend that presenters use the most convenient and reliable medium.

## 12.4. Screen-show effects

Animation seems to be effective for organizing and emphasizing information on complex visuals to increase comprehension. For presentations where the goal is enhanced audience perception of the speaker, a combination of animation and transitions would be effective. However, if the aim is high comprehension and subsequent retention, animation alone enhances complex ideas, but transitions should be avoided.

Presentation visuals can enhance persuasion and give speakers the ability to be more effective communicators. Current technology has given speakers many visuals form and display medium options. However, the most effective ways of using presentation visuals technology are not always obvious.
