# Environmental scanning and information systems in relation to success in introducing new products

Niv Ahituv $^{1,*}$ , Jehiel Zif, Isaac Machlin

Faculty of Management, Tel Aviv University, Tel Aviv, 69978, Israel

Received 30 October 1996; accepted 1 January 1998

## Abstract

CEOs in 46 firms were interviewed in regard to the pattern of the environmental scanning they performed. The results were analyzed to determine the degree of use of information systems by CEOs in their strategic decision making and to seek a link with the firm's success in introducing new products. The study indicates significant differences in the level of environmental scanning and in the use of information systems between firms that were more successful in introducing new products into the market and firms that were less successful. The differences are in the pattern and the frequency of conducting environmental scanning, in the number of computerized applications, and in the number of advanced marketing information systems. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Strategic information systems; Environmental scanning; Marketing new products; Management information systems

## 1. Introduction

In any field, the strength of a firm is strongly linked to its ability to develop and market new products (or services), in order to provide its existing and prospective customers with a continuous flow of new services and products (see, for example, $[18, 19, 24, 25, 27]$ ). Indeed, it is safe to say that a firm that does not plan new products may soon find itself in an inferior position in relation to its competitors $[15]$ .

There are many reasons why the development of new products will be ever more difficult in the future $[32]$ : capital cost will be high, competition will be tougher and more universal ('the global village'), firms will try to compete in new areas outside their traditional fields (i.e., diversify), markets will be saturated, customers' lifestyles will continue to change and be more intensively affected by the international media (satellite and cable TV), customers will be more sophisticated, the rate of technological change will be higher, product life cycles will shorten, environmental constraints will increase, and fluctuations in the costs of critical raw materials will make it even more difficult to control production costs. In addition, the 'information superhighway' provides accessibility to a vast amount of information, which, in return, increases potential competition through innovation.

Acknowledging that the environment is an endless source of opportunities and threats, top managers must identify opportunities, detect and interpret problems, implement strategic or structural adaptations, and make the link between information from customers and from the Internet, and the firm's technology that is so vital to the development of ideas for new products [23].

The firm's informational advantage about environmental opportunities and problems depends on its perceiving signals that other organizations overlook and indeed the major role of a firm's information systems (ISs) in generating strategic advantages has long been acknowledged. Porter's models [27, 28] were adapted to the area of ISs by Porter and Miller [29], Cash et al. [6], and others (see Ref. [22], for a collection of related articles).

Methodologies for planning strategic ISs have been devised by some authors (see for example, [2, 33]).

This article reports on empirical findings dealing with the use of information and ISs by firms launching new products or services. The pattern of scanning and the frequency of use of information by executives and the type of IS installed by the firm are correlated to its success in introducing new products into the market. By information scanning we meant not only the purposeful search for information but also ‘information viewing’ [1].

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses: Environmental sectors and strategic uncertainty

Research into managers' information scanning behavior suggests that the frequency of scanning indicates the amount of information obtained about the environment [17], that managers may acquire larger or smaller amounts of information on different environmental sectors and that they tend to receive information along a continuum from irregular to continuous accumulation [14]. Depending on the nature of the environment and on the perceived need for data on external events, managers may process data irregularly or continuously. Daft et al. [11] have also asked whether executives, who by the nature of their work are extremely busy people, scan all sectors equally or focus narrowly on specific sectors. These authors found that the frequency of scanning differs by sector and is related to strategic uncertainty, that is, the extent to which uncertainty surrounding a specific environmental factor affects the organizational performance and consequently the achievement of the firm's strategic goals: the strategic value of information is directly related to strategic uncertainty and managers will more frequently acquire data on strategically uncertain sectors.

Following Cooper [7], that the success with new products means success in achieving one of the strategic goals of the firm, we anticipate that positive correlation between the firm's success in launching new products and the frequency with which managers scan the more uncertain environmental sectors.

Hypothesis 1: Firms succeeding better with new products will show a higher correlation between strategic uncertainty and the frequency of scanning of environmental sectors than less successful firms.

## 2.1. Frequency and pattern of scanning

Daft et al. [11] found a broad scanning base among executives in high-performing firms; they also found that information from written sources complemented the information from personal sources in delivering a better understanding of an uncertain environment: the richness of personal sources enables subtle signals to be detected [10]; written media are important because tangible data can be gathered and communicated about discrete event. This finding is also supported by a theoretical model in decision theory assessing the value of a second opinion [3].

Adopting a multi-media approach to information gathering carries many advantages: a weak signal detected from a personal source may be supplemented by objective data; and scanning information through one medium may trigger the use of a complementary medium.

Scanning and performance of a firm may be self-reinforcing, broad scanning providing opportunities for improved performance, and improved performance providing resources for broad scanning. A characteristic of one of the dimensions of successful performance by firms is success with new products [7].

Hypothesis 2: Firms that succeed better with new products will carry out formal scanning of the task environment more frequently than less successful firms.

## 2.2. Information systems and success in new products

Certain computer-based ISs have been designed to assist the firm in processing data to support strategic decisions and improve its performance. An example is Enscan, which was designed according to environmental scanning principles $[20]$ . During recent years, almost every firm can review pertinent data through Internet and database retrieval services.

Computerized IS also include software for management and control of the process of developing new management products. Many management information systems (MISs) are based on transaction processing systems (TPSs) of the organization which, by acquiring additional features, become MISs. All ISs that support the competitive strategy of the organization become strategic information systems (SISs) [34].

Findings indicate that the process of development of a new product and its proper management make the difference between the best and the worst performers in new products $[5, 21]$ .

The information that an executive needs for arriving at decisions in the important areas is based on a broad range of IS that process data related to the organization and its environment.

Hypothesis 3: Firms that have more success in introducing new products will possess more computer applications in their ISs than firms that have less success with new products.

Cooper [8] found that the firms that succeed better with new product possess marketing information on the customers for the new products and their needs. He also identified the marketing information topics that are dealt with a significantly different level in the best-performing firms: information on customers needs and preferences, market potential, market rate of growth, marketing systems, etc.

Hypothesis 4: Firms that succeed better in introducing new products will possess more computerized marketing IS than less successful firms.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Definition of new products

The firm's existing line of business may be enriched by adding innovative features to existing products or by the development of new products (services) [16].

New products, in our research, are products which did not exist previously in the firm $[4, 31]$ . These could be products with totally new technical developments or they could be ‘revisions’ of existing products, with new packaging or a new financial service $[12, 30]$ .

The performance of a new product is measured some years after its entrance into the market $[21]$ . Morbey and Reithner $[26]$ , for example, checked the increase of sales and profits after three years.

In this study, we define a new product as one produced and presented by the firm in the three years prior to our study.

## 3.2. Evaluation of success with new products

The success of new product is evaluated by the portion of the increase in the firm's total sales and total profits attributed to the introduction of new products. Similar evaluation methods have appeared in other studies [7, 8, 9, 26].

## 3.3. Environmental sectors

The external environment of the firm is defined as all the factors and events outside the firm. It is divided into task environment and general environment.

The sectors of the task environment are:

1. The competitor sector – includes competing firms and products.

2. The customer sector – refers to firms and single customers that purchase the products of the firm.

3. The technological sector – includes newly developed production technologies, innovations in materials and products, and new trends in research and knowledge relevant to the firm.

The sectors of the general environment are:

1. The regulatory sector – includes legislation, regulations and impinging political issues at all governmental levels.

2. The economic sector – includes economic factors such as the rate of inflation, interest rates, state budgets, unemployment, and economic growth rate.

3. The socio-cultural sector – includes social values, social trends, and fashion.

## 3.4. Evaluation of the degree of environmental scanning

The degree of environmental scanning was measured by building a profile of each of the environmental sector. This entails ranking the importance, complexity, and rate of change of each environmental sector.

As already noted, firm managers may receive information regarding environmental sectors in different ways. They may scan the environment themselves or learn from others in the firm. They may increase or decrease their frequency of scanning, and they may choose among different information channels: personal or non-personal, internal or external.

## 3.5. Method of analysis of the degree of environmental scanning

The source of data for the analysis of environmental scanning was personal interviews with top executives of the firms comprising the study's sample, as in other research in the field of environmental scanning.

The methodology and major parts of the questionnaire of this paper follow the approach used by Daft et al. [11] with some modifications. The major difference, however, is that they used general performance measurements, while this study concentrates specifically on the success of new products.

To analyze the parameters of complexity, rate of change, and importance of each sector, as estimated by the executives, the persons interviewed received written and verbal descriptions of each six sectors and were asked to answer questions pertaining to these parameters on Likert scale.

Frequency of scanning was investigated by posing questions that differentiated between internal and external sources, and between personal and non-personal (written) sources. The interviewees received samples of information from each source and were asked to evaluate whether they receive information that is important to them in each of the six sectors on a daily, weekly, monthly or less frequent basis.

Strategic uncertainty was considered to be a combination of rate of change, complexity and importance of events in each sector. It was analyzed similarly to other studies, where complexity and rate of change had been combined as an indicator of environmental uncertainty $[13, 32]$ . In this research, importance of the sector is combined with uncertainty as an indicator of the need for information by the executive.

The evaluation of perceived strategic uncertainty was performed under the assumption that it is a product of importance and uncertainty. Thus, if sector importance and uncertainty are both low then the perceived uncertainty will be low, but if importance and uncertainty are both high the perceived uncertainty will be high.

The following formula was used to evaluate the strategic uncertainty for each environmental sector:

$$
S U = I ^ {*} (C + R)
$$

where SU is the perceived strategic uncertainty; I the perceived sector importance; $C+R$ the perceived sector uncertainty; C the perceived sector complexity; and R the perceived sector rate of change.

All the parameters were ranked by the respondent on Likert scales from 1 to 5.

## 3.6. The method of analysis of the success of new products

The success of new products was analyzed in two ways: by their contribution to the total sales of the firm and by their contribution to total profit in the previous year. All the firms that participated in the study were ranked according to the percentage of profit deriving from new products in total annual profit, and according to the percentage of sales from new products in total annual sales.

The upper median of the firms, by either criterion, was defined as the group of firms that succeed more with new products, and the lower median was defined as the group of firms that have less success.

## 3.7. Sample selection

Since this was an exploratory study, the selection of the sample was not random. We were initially looking for firms with strong orientation to new product initiation. After preliminary inquiry, a survey was conducted among 46 CEOs of Israeli firms who were interviewed personally and asked to fill out a questionnaire during the interview. Since Israel has a ‘Western’ economy and its firms have to compete internationally, the Israel firms could be considered a convenient sub-sample of the developed countries firms.

The results are based on 40 firms (out of the 46) that compiled with the following criteria: it had a defined task environment; performance of new products could be measured; it was large enough to have the structural and behavioral characteristics of an established organization; and its major field of business was not related to military products.

The other six firms were not considered for the following reasons: five did not have the structural characteristics of established organizations and their annual turnover was less than one million U.S. dollars; and the sixth could not provide data on its performance with new products.

In 37 of the 40 firms, the interviews were conducted with its CEO or the president. One interview was conducted with the vice-president responsible for new products and two with top executives designated by the CEO, because of their specific involvement with new products.

The firms operate in the following business sectors: food, chemical products, insurance and financial services, information technology, machinery and production equipment, electronics, textiles, pharmaceutics, footwear, printing, agriculture, medical equipment, and others.

Since the Pearson correlation between the two criteria was very high (0.88, $p < 0.001$ ), we considered it sufficient to use a single criterion. ## 4.4. Hypothesis 3

The third hypothesis predicts that firms succeeding better with new products will have more types of computer applications in their IS inventory. To test this hypothesis, a questionnaire designating 22 common IS applications was submitted to the executives who were then asked to mark those applications that were used in their firms. Table 6 displays the percentage of firms possessing each application, comparing the more successful with the less successful firms.

In order to examine which category of firms possesses more computerized IS applications, the applications were counted in each firm. The count ranged from 0 (none) to 22 (all the computerized applications exist).

The T-test analysis comparing the ‘all applications exist’ group in the more successful firms with the ‘all applications exist’ group in the less successful firms showed a statistically significant difference $p<0.05, DF=38, T=2.43$ . Moreover, the average number of computerized applications was higher in the more successful firms $M=13.32, S.D.=2.11$ than in the less successful ones $M=11.43, S.D.=2.73$ . The findings thus support the hypothesis that firms succeeding better with new products will have more computer applications in their IS inventory than do less successful firms.

## 6. Discussion and concluding remarks

Since a few studies have already been carried out on the subject dealt here, we conducted the research in an exploratory manner. So in addition to the analyses of the hypotheses, other parameters were also examined. Some of the attributes identified allowed us to crystallize a normative profile of the ISs that support top executives in firms that are more successful with new products.

We have found that the IS profile in the firms that were less successful with new products is different from the profile in firms that were more successful. Kuczmarski [21] found similar attributes among firms that succeeded with new products, and that these attributes were not related to the size of the firm, the type of industry and the competition.

We identified differences in the environmental scanning performed by top executives in the more successful firms, in the use of formal and informal sources. For the firms that are more successful with new products, our study detected a larger correlation between strategic uncertainty and the frequency of scanning in the technological sector, in the economic sector and in the socio-cultural sector compared to the less successful firms, suggesting that top executives in the more successful firms are more flexible in adapting their pattern of scanning to their environment, and that they do not rely only on fixed patterns of information.

The results for the regulatory sector are different. We found a high and significant correlation between strategic uncertainty and the frequency of scanning of that sector among the firms that are less successful with new products, compared with a nearly zero correlation for the more successful firms.

The study shows that the less successful firms rely much more on modifications of existing products (80% of total sales against 51% for the others).

In an era characterized by a universal trend in legislation toward establishing constraints on products and materials out of concern for their effect on human and environmental health, firms with older products are in a position of greater strategic uncertainty since these constraints were not in effect when their products were developed. The more successful firms, however, concentrate their efforts on launching completely new products that did not exist three years ago (49% of new products compared to 20% in the other firms). Since, these firms develop their new products according to the current regulatory constraints, they do not experience strategic uncertainty in that sector.

The more successful firms perform environmental scanning at a higher frequency in the competitor, customer, and technology sectors. They make more use of formal (written) information sources – internal and external, and of personal external sources – with personal involvement of the top executives, compared to the others. One may assume that personal external sources are in line with involvement of top executives.

The firms that are less successful with new products make significantly more use of environmental scanning of internal personal sources (informal sources), than do the more successful firms. This finding suggests that top executives that are more successful with new products look more for objective views provided by external information. This is in contrast to the top executives of the less successful firms, who are satisfied with less objective and less formal information which may be less effective for success with new products.

We have found in our study that firms that are more successful with new products possess significantly more computerized IS applications, as part of the written formal information sources than less successful firms. The more successful firms also possess significantly more computerized marketing applications than the others.

This study has some limitations, though. First, the cause and effect relationship between the variables is not always clear. There is a possibility that sometimes a successful firm has more resources to spend on IS, so the success of the firm is the cause rather than the effect. Second, the study must be considered exploratory, since the sample is small, it was not randomly selected, and was limited to Israeli firms. Its benefit is nevertheless twofold: it provides researchers with insight into the relationship between the firm's strategy and its ISs needs; on the practical level, it can help CEOs in setting priorities and goals for the design of environmental scanning and marketing ISs.
