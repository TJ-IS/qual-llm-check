## Abstract

When compared to organizational computing, end-user computing (EUC) has the tendency to be closely related to individual user tasks and motivations. This article presents the results of an empirical study designed to examine the impact of task uncertainty and end-user involvement on the use and success of EUC. A research model was built and tested with a survey of 505 end-users in five large organizations. Structural equation modeling results confirm that both EUC competence and success are determined by task complexity and the perceived importance users attribute to EUC. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: End-user computing (EUC); Task uncertainty; User involvement; User competence; User satisfaction; End-user computing impacts; End-user computing success

## 1. Introduction

From its start in the late 70s, end-user computing (EUC) has evolved tremendously, so that it is now a valuable method in a wide range of applications. End-users exhibit different computing abilities and behavior. Some develop their own applications, while others use and operate EUC applications developed by other end-users or specialists from inside or outside their organization; the applications also differ in nature. The importance of these differences appeared very early in the research conducted on EUC. Rockart and Flannery [59], for instance, identified six categories of end-users: non-programming; command level; programmers; functional support; information center support; and DP programmers. Focusing on application development by end-users, Rivard and Huff [57] proposed another taxonomy: opportunity seekers; staff analysts; and micro-IS department users.

While these authors used a single dimension on which they based their classification, more recent research proposes taxonomies that rely on several dimensions. The notion of EUC competence is a term that can be used to refer to such patterns. Cotterman and Kumar [14], for instance, identified three behavioral dimensions on which to differentiate various EUC activities: operations; development; and control. Blili, Raymond and Rivard [9] propose a measure of end-user computing competence that also considers three dimensions: user characteristics; usage characteristics; and application characteristics. Notwithstanding the label attached to the various patterns, these are seen by many researchers as being critical to the understanding of end-user computing [51].

End-user computing is closely related to user tasks. For example, Culnan [15] examined the relationship between user tasks and computing behavior of data bank users. Yaverbaum [74] analyzed the influence of task characteristics on end-user satisfaction and productivity. Igbaria's study [36] found a relationship between task structure and end-user behaviors and attitudes. More recently, Raymond and Bergeron [56] examined the relationship between task variety, task autonomy, and satisfaction with a personal DSS. While these studies strongly suggest the importance of including task-related constructs in EUC research models, other researchers have devoted relatively little attention to its role in EUC.

For task-related effects, particular attention must be paid to the concept of task uncertainty; it might have an impact on (1) how information technologies will be used by end-users, and (2) the success of end-user computing. Indeed, earlier research has demonstrated the existence of a link between uncertainty in the decision-making context and the behavior of organizational members in the area of information processing [22, 23]. Since the work of Thompson [67], information processing, and therefore information systems (IS), have been commonly accepted as the best method of reducing uncertainty. Thus, uncertainty is recognized as a key success factor in the implementation of formal IS [45]. Similarly, the growing importance of information technologies can be seen as a formal attempt to reduce uncertainty and the 'decision gap' [29]. In view of the foregoing, the first question that this study attempted to answer was:

What is the relationship between task uncertainty and (1) EUC competence and (2) EUC success?

Moreover, while end-user computing may affect, or be affected by, user-task characteristics, this also underscores the renewed importance of individual variables in explaining the complexity and success of information systems. To a large extent, the adoption of end-user computing can be seen as a form of consumer behavior; users act as consumers; and EUC is the product or service offered. In this context, the role of individual involvement in the adoption of goods or services has largely been demonstrated in the area of consumer behavior [61]. There is much literature demonstrating that the impact of individual involvement on attitudes, individual motivations, and behaviors is very important. User involvement in IS refers to the subjective psychological state of the individual, and is defined as “the importance and personal relevance that users attach to a particular system, or to IS in general, depending on the users’ focus” [5]. It is an important factor in the implementation of organizational IS [66] and can also be determinant in explaining EUC perceptions and behaviors [21]. Therefore, the second research question was:

What is the relationship between end-user involvement and (1) EUC competence and (2) EUC success?

In order to examine these two questions, a research model was first built, using IS, organization theory, and marketing as reference disciplines. To test the model, a questionnaire was sent to end-users in five large organizations. The data obtained from the 505-survey respondents were then analyzed through structural equation modeling.

## 2. Conceptual framework and research model

Our global objective is to contribute to a better understanding of the success factors of EUC: specifically, the potential consequences of task uncertainty on EUC competence and success are analyzed. In addition, in order to include behavioral nature in the model, a construct characterizing the end-user involvement in EUC was included. This research is based on the reasoned action approach $[26]$ . Other researchers have used this approach to investigate user behavior during the adoption and use of computer-based systems $[38, 68]$ . User satisfaction and impact of use are among the success indicators that have been selected for this study.

## 2.1. Task uncertainty

Task uncertainty “is the degree to which work to be performed is difficult to understand and complex” [2]. This notion has been refined by other organization theory researchers along two dimensions [24, 32, 50, 71], the first being task complexity (interdependence, autonomy, variety, structurability, intelligibility) and the second being volatility (rate of change, predictability, exceptions, controlability). In IS research, one keystone in this regard is Gorry and Scott Morton’s [31] model, linking managerial task and information characteristics [54].

It is, however, in DSS research that most efforts have been made to understand the impact of task characteristics on IS success [48]. For Sanders and Courtney [62], user satisfaction with a DSS could be affected by task variety, difficulty, newness, interdependence, standardization and authority.

## 2.2. End-user involvement

EUC, as an innovation, is driven by ‘demand-pull’ rather than ‘technology-push’ [60]. As such, user attitudes and motivations toward EUC are important usage and success factors [4, 33, 55]. These factors, along with antecedent socio-demographic variables such as age, education and experience, converge into the notion of user involvement. In the particular context of EUC, Doll and Torkzadeh [20] found that end-user involvement positively affects end-user satisfaction.

Involvement is multidimensional [40]. The first dimension is the degree to which the EUC ‘product’ (hardware, software) or ‘service’ (training, support) is perceived to be important, and thus generates expectations of the user [25]. Another dimension is the degree to which EUC is perceived to be risky, leading to computer anxiety [35]. An added facet of involvement is the degree of pleasure, from both an emotive and rational standpoint, conferred by it or its power of attraction on the individual, in terms of job enrichment, greater autonomy and freedom, and better relations with other users and IS specialists [43, 46].

Another facet is that computers are status symbols; they can increase the user's prestige and power [34], or can evoke self-image and the need for self-realization [10].

## 2.3. EUC competence

The user's decision to adopt and utilize EUC depends on a number of factors [37]. Certain individuals are more competent than others [42, 49]. In this sense, greater EUC competence makes users and their organization more productive and more effective. However, for organizations trying to deal with the EUC phenomenon, additional training helps [1, 8] and organizational policies should be added about EUC [11].

EUC competence has been characterized by the nature and purpose of the user-developed applications [64], by the manner in which users develop, operate and interact with these applications [63], and by using Rockart and Flannery's classification plus their involvement in participative applications.

## 2.4. EUC success

Evaluating the success of information systems is difficult, given the diversity of applications and usage contexts: the ‘personal’ nature of EUC dictates that it cannot be analyzed without considering the affectivity and subjectivity of users $[13]$ . This justifies employing an approach based on perception measures of EUC success $[20]$ .

User information satisfaction was chosen as a surrogate for EUC success, based on the robustness of this approach. Information satisfaction, however, is but one of the impacts of EUC on the individual. Other impacts are felt in terms of the user's productivity, decision-making capability and autonomy, leading to alternative approaches in assessing EUC.

## 2.5. Research hypotheses

The first set of three hypotheses examines the relationship between task uncertainty and the other research constructs.

Hypothesis 1 suggests that a manager perceiving ambiguity, difficulties, or complexity (uncertainty) in his/her immediate environment (his or her task) can adopt certain means to reduce the problems. Kwon and Zmud [44] note that “task difficulty is likely to motivate initiation and usage behaviors, for example information search.” Daft and McIntosh [16] indicate that when task variety is high, that is having unfamiliar, novel and unexpected events, information acquisition is needed on an ongoing basis. Gresov, Drazin and Van de Ven found that decision-makers have greater information processing demands as difficulties increase; they, therefore, attempt to increase their information processing capacity to coordinate and control interdependence. Leblibici and Salancik confirm that EUC is a more flexible, personalized alternative to organizational computing. These lead to:

H1: The greater the user's task uncertainty, the greater his/her EUC competence.

Managers who lack information feel insecure and are constrained in their decision-making ability $[72]$ . EUC is then a more effective mean of coping with uncertainty than organizational computing $[12]$ . Probably managers are more satisfied with EUC when they need to use more varied information. Raymond and Bergeron found that users with a higher level of task variety were more satisfied with ‘personal’ decision support systems. Thus:

H2: The greater the user's task uncertainty, the higher his/her level of EUC success.

Task uncertainty implies a need for information. Following the theory of reasoned action, an individual would attach importance and personal relevance to a behavior that can fulfil this need. Igbaria's [36] study showed that the task uncertainty (structure) of end-users was correlated with their attitudes toward EUC and their level of computer anxiety. Therefore:

H3: The greater the user's task uncertainty, the greater his/her involvement in EUC.

The second set of two hypotheses relates end-user involvement to EUC competence and success. Many studies have employed user attitudes and beliefs to predict IT usage behaviors [30]; for example using Triandis' [70] model of interpersonal behavior.

Thompson et al. [69] hypothesized a link between effect of PC use (interest, pleasure, appropriateness) and utilization (intensity, frequency, diversity). Testing a technology acceptance model, Davis et al. [19] found a positive relationship between effect of a PC software package and its intended behavior. Thus:

H4: The greater the user's involvement in EUC, the greater his/her EUC competence.

User attitudes have often been linked to the success of organizational information systems and DSS. In EUC research, Rivard and Huff [58] found attitudes to be positively related to global satisfaction with user-developed applications. Igbaria [36] indicates that attitudes are mostly related to the perceived effectiveness of EUC. Other empirical studies confirm this; for example Doll and Torkzadeh [21] found a significant relationship between end-user involvement and satisfaction. Thus if users confer greater importance to EUC and if EUC is seen to be pleasurable, they will experience a higher level of satisfaction and a more positive impact from EUC. Then:

H5: The greater the user's involvement in EUC, the higher his/her level of EUC success.

One hypothesis constitutes the last set. A number of researchers have examined various user, usage, and system characteristics in the context of EUC. Different user types have been identified, depicting individual EUC aptitudes, capabilities and skills [52]. Users can also be more or less autonomous [18], their access to computing can be direct or ‘chauffeured.’ User behaviors have also been studied, that is when, how often, and to what purpose individuals resort to EUC, and how they rank in terms of operating, developing and controlling EUC applications.

Some attempts have been made to relate these characteristics to EUC success. Amoroso [3] proposed a framework wherein the technical attributes of EUC applications, in terms of hardware, software, and operational environment, are assumed to have a significant impact. Characteristics such as the type of application (administrative versus transactional) [65], the origin of the application (user-developed versus acquired), the user-friendliness of the development tool, including the use of 4GLs [47] have been linked to the success of EUC. The EUC competence construct imbeds most of the previously mentioned characteristics, grouping them under the headings of user ability, usage intensity and application customization. Hence:

H6: The greater the user's EUC competence, the higher his/her level of EUC success.

## 3. Methodology

A theory of end-user computing is still in the process of being formulated. Thus, although, hypothesis testing is the central concern, measurement and data analysis instruments are important.

## 3.1. Sampling and data gathering procedures

The unit of analysis of the study is the manager's task: the activities related to the accomplishment of managerial responsibility. The term ‘manager’ is used here to mean those individuals who hold a decision-making position in an organization; thus it includes office personnel as well as top managers. However, the sample excludes individuals whose activities are essentially secretarial or clerical in nature, because of their limited decision-making capacity.

A total of 1378 questionnaires were distributed within five large Canadian organizations in the financial sector; these included three banks and two insurance companies. Their average size is 18240 employees (from 2200 to 57000); their assets average 48 billion dollars, with a minimum of 4 and a maximum of 143. All operate an information center (IC) to assist end-users. These were chosen because they concentrate a large number of end-users in the same location with similar levels of computing support. Three hundred and eighty-six questionnaires were returned prior to a follow-up letter. A further 146 questionnaires were received after this follow-up letter for a total of 532 questionnaires returned, which represents a response rate of 38.6%. Twenty-seven questionnaires were dropped due to missing values, identification errors, resignations, or job changes. The final sample therefore included 505 respondents (36.6%).

## 3.2. Measures

Task uncertainty was measured by adapting Barki, Rivard and Talbot's [6] instrument. As shown in Table 1, the validity of the construct was ascertained through a principal components factor analysis, producing a four-factor structure that includes the expected dimensions, complexity and volatility, and two additional dimensions, intelligibility and controlability. Note that volatility and controlability were apparently less reliable, with alpha values below 0.7 level [53]. The user involvement measure was adapted from Kapferer and Laurent's consumer behavior instrument. Factor analytic results shown in Table 2 indicate a three rather than four-dimensional construct, including the importance of EUC in both cognitive and affective terms, the risk perceived by the user, and the sign value (status symbol) he or she attributes to this activity, excluding the degree of pleasure. The concept and measure of EUC competence was taken from Blili, Raymond and Rivard.

Overall, these measures show an adequate level of reliability, as well as face and construct validity.

User satisfaction was measured by adapting Ives, Olson and Baroudi's [39] instrument to the EUC context. Table 3 shows the three components produced by the factor analysis: quality of information; support personnel; and user capabilities. The EUC impacts measure was adapted from a subset of Bergeron, Rivard and Raymond's instrument. The resulting four factors are shown in Table 4. All measurement items are given in Appendix A.

## 4.1. Assessing the measurement model

Structural equation modeling tests the research model and also assesses the underlying measurement model. The first measurement property to be assessed is internal consistency, involving unidimensionality and reliability. The multiple observable indicators that measure an unobservable construct or latent variable must be unidimensional if this variable is to be treated as a single value. This constraint was satisfied by using the principal components identified in the earlier factor analyses. Unidimensionality can also be assessed by examining the loadings of the items on their associated variable, using 0.5 as a cut-off point. Based on this criterion, all loadings (lambdas) were acceptable. Reliability is assessed by the rho coefficient, based on the ratio of construct variance to the sum of construct and error variance [73]. Note that the rho coefficient is more general than Cronbach's alpha because it does not assume that all scales weigh equally on their corresponding construct and it is unaffected by scale length. However, it is similar to alpha in terms of interpreting the values obtained, and Nunnally's guidelines can still be used. A rho value greater than 0.7 indicates that the construct variance accounts for at least 70% of the measurement variance.

The next required measurement property is discriminant validity; that is the degree to which all constructs in the research model are unique from each other or represent distinct concepts. It is assessed by examining the correlations between pairs of variables.

## 4.2. Assessing the structural model

The research hypotheses can be tested by examining the path coefficients that resulted from the path analysis. In terms of EUC success, the research model explains a greater percentage of variance in the information capability obtained from EUC (21%) than in the quality of information (11%). This suggests EUC impacts to be a better indicator of success than user satisfaction. Also, task complexity and the importance of EUC as perceived by users explain a significant percentage of the variance in their behavior, in terms of user ability (17%).

Hypothesis 1 is confirmed in view of the path coefficient linking task complexity to user ability. Hence, task complexity apparently leads to higher level behaviors among EUC adopters. The more users perceive the task as being complex, the more they will attempt to alleviate this complexity through EUC. User ability also highlights the prevailing importance of task uncertainty on the managers' information needs. The impact of task uncertainty on EUC competence is in line with the theory of reasoned action, which postulates that behaviors result from the context in which they take place. From this perspective, task uncertainty apparently acts as a stimulus and EUC competence as a response to that stimulus.

According to Hypothesis 2, task uncertainty is significantly and positively related to EUC success. Hypothesis 2 is, however, rejected. A high level of task complexity seems to be related to a low level of information quality and informational capability. Apparently, users who perceive a high level of complexity in their task are the least satisfied and the most demanding with respect to the support provided by EUC.

It seems that EUC competence increases users' perceptions regarding the quality of information and information capability. Hence, these may not be appropriate indicators to measure the impact of task uncertainty on EUC success. It should be noted that Igbaria also reported significant negative path coefficients linking task uncertainty (variety) to both end-user satisfaction and perceived effectiveness of EUC.

The path coefficient indicates task complexity to be significantly and positively related to the perceived importance of EUC, confirming Hypothesis 3. Those individuals who perceive the highest level of task complexity are also those who attach the greatest importance to EUC. Thus when the task is perceived as being difficult, users attach more importance to the tools (EUC) that can help them in dealing with the problems (complexity). However, while the path coefficient is highly significant, the low percentage of explained variance (3%) in user involvement indicates that the potential influence of other determinants is missing and very important.

The path coefficient also shows that the perceived importance of EUC is positively related to user ability.

Hence, Hypothesis 4 is confirmed; the individuals who are most involved in EUC are also the ones with the most ability in EUC: they are more competent in EUC solutions. Also, the impact of user involvement on EUC competence rises when users opt for it of their own volition.

The path coefficients confirm Hypothesis 5, that is the existence of a positive relation between the perceived importance of EUC, the perceived quality of information obtained, and the user's informational capability. This points to a certain degree of cognitive consonance between user attitudes about EUC and user appreciation of EUC results.

The path coefficients for the sixth hypothesis are both significant. They indicate that user ability is positively correlated with the quality of information obtained through EUC and with the informational capability provided by it. EUC success seems to be a function of the user's ability. It also shows that the perceived level of EUC success increases along with the level of user responsibility.

## 5. Limitations

There are two limitations to this study. One concerns the exclusion of all components other than those defined as principal ones. A conservative choice was made here, opting for more consistency and validity in the measurement model instead of stronger but not-so-well founded conclusions from the structural model.

The second limitation concerns the absence of potentially important constructs, such as the quality of user support in terms of training, technical assistance, availability of tools and EUC management policies and practices $[7, 51, 58]$ . While the natural tendency is to look at additional variables to obtain greater understanding, Daft [17] states: “two ingredients – a few (perhaps two, three or four) variables that form a coherent whole and depth of meaning – constitute an ideal research framework.” This results in a trade-off, with the choice being made in favor of parsimony over comprehensiveness.

In addition, there are issues of ‘levels-of-analysis’ in theory development that must be considered. Klein et al. [41] argue that the levels of theory, data collection, and statistical analysis (individual, group or organization-level) must be congruent, if conclusions drawn from testing a theoretical model are to be founded. As the present study’s focal entity is the individual end-user in terms of model, constructs, and measures, introducing organization-level constructs (such as culture and user support) could have caused difficulties. Also, the users’ computing environment was partially controlled by sampling only a few large firms in the same sector with similar IS/EUC management policies and practices.

## 6. Conclusion

This research demonstrates the applicability of the reasoned action approach in the investigation of the adoption, use and evaluation behavior of users in a EUC context. Moreover, user beliefs apparently condition their behavior and emotion toward EUC. Hence, user behavior is probably affected by its anticipated consequences.

The impact of user involvement confirms the affective and conative nature of EUC as well as the pertinence of applying this concept, borrowed from the field of consumer behavior, to the study of user behavior. These findings also confirm the importance of individual factors in the use and success of IS. Several of these hitherto unexplored variables apparently play an important role.

The results related to task uncertainty illustrate its influence on user behavior. Until now, very few studies have empirically demonstrated the impact of uncertainty on the use and success of EUC. Task uncertainty is an important factor affecting user behavior and as a vector of attitudes and perceptions. Also, results clearly indicate the presence of user involvement in the use of EUC.

The impact of task uncertainty demonstrates the importance of considering this variable in the process of allocating information processing resources: those users who have the most uncertain tasks should have access to better resources because they have more important information needs. Our results clearly show that IS managers should provide better software, hardware and support services. Appropriate training programs for end-users may play a crucial role. These programs could also be offered to managers to foster a greater level of EUC involvement. This could lead to a greater degree of autonomy and responsibility.

We have developed a way to measure EUC success; this may be applied in the evaluation of the performance and usefulness of EUC. The measures of competence may serve as an important indicator of the potential of users, while providing a valuable index of their use of computing resources. The user involvement measure may serve to achieve a better understanding of the motivations underlying the demand for informational resources. Some motivations are commendable and legitimate, while others are not necessarily so. For instance, this is the case of the 'bandwagon' effect in which resources are used in support of activities that are unrelated to the user's professional responsibilities. Finally, it should be noted that information technologies are among the most expensive resources in the organization. Hence, better knowledge of user motivations should facilitate the management and effective use of these resources, both from an individual and an organizational perspective.

His research publications have appeared in the Journal of Small Business and Entrepreneurship, the Journal of End User Computing, the International Journal of Information Management, and the European Journal of Purchasing and Supply Management. Dr. Blili's research and consulting interests are on end-user

computing, IT management for international business and in developing countries and international marketing information systems.

Louis Raymond is a professor of information systems at the Université du Québec à Trois-Rivières. He has published in various scientific journals such as the MIS Quarterly, the Journal of Management Information Systems, Data Base, and Decision Support Systems. His research interests include information systems in a small business and network enterprise context, end-user computing, and the alignment of IT with organizational strategy.
