# Developing a multidimensional measure of system-use in an organizational context

William J. Doll $^{a,*}$ , Gholamreza Torkzadeh $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Professor of MIS and Strategic Management, The University of Toledo, 2801 West Bancroft St., Toledo, Ohio 43606, USA $^{b}$ Professor of Information Systems and Decision Sciences, The University of Texas at El Paso, Texas, 79912, USA

Received 30 April 1997; accepted 23 November 1997

## Abstract

The perception of a widening gap between the potential of information technology (that is, what it is capable of being and what it can ideally achieve) and its actual use has focused attention on the need for better measures of how extensively information technology is utilized in an organizational context. Building on a taxonomy of system-use and the rich descriptive literature provided by social scientists who focus on the impact of information technology on work, this paper makes an effort to develop new multidimensional measures of how extensively information technology is utilized in an organizational context for decision support, work integration, and customer service functions.

System-use is a pivotal construct in the system-to-value chain that links upstream research on the causes of system success with downstream research on the organizational impacts of information technology. The new measures are appropriate for use as dependent variables in upstream research, or as independent or mediating variables in downstream research on the impact of information technology on work. A sample of 409 end-users enabled the researchers to provide evidence of this instrument's reliability, validity, and general applicability. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: System success; System-use; Instrument development; Computer utilization

## 1. Introduction

Increasingly, organizations are concerned with making more effective use of information technology (IT). Rapid technological change and the widespread use of IT have increased our awareness of a widening gap between its potential (that is, what it is capable of being and what it can ideally achieve) and its actual use. Effective use of IT is considered as a major determinant of competitive advantage, productivity, and even personal competency. Jonscher [38] suggests that improvements in the way that IT is used may be the principle source of future economic growth. Both academics and practitioners recognize that the potential of IT depends on how it is used.

The MIS literature has a long tradition of studying the impact of IT on decision making [33, 64] or a user's satisfaction with using an application for decision making [53, 18]. Despite these traditional concerns, the task of developing operational measures of system-use as a behavioral construct (rather than as an impact or an attitude) has lagged. For example, Sethi and King [55] propose instrument(s) for measuring the competitive impacts of IT (that is, how it provides competitive advantage). Their measures identify strategic impacts, but they do not identify the system-use behaviors necessary to achieve these consequences.

The usage (behavioral) measures actually used in MIS studies tend to be unidimensional (for example, only considering hours of usage) and ignore how technology is used in the organizational context. They do not consider the cost of usage (for example, the labor hours) and, thus, assume that more usage is always better. System-use measures are usually developed on an ad hoc basis, as a byproduct of substantive research on system implementation. As yet, no studies whose primary purpose is to report on the development of new measures of system-use have been published. Several factors have impeded the development of new multidimensional system-use measures: (1) a primarily upstream MIS research agenda, where a unidimensional system-use construct is an adequate indicator of implementation success; (2) the lack of a theory base in the MIS literature that provides a taxonomy of usage behaviors; and (3) the realization that, where use is mandatory, measures of system-use may indicate only compliance, not system success.

The potential of the system-use construct depends on how it is conceptualized and operationalized. To be applicable in required use situations, the construct would have to be rethought. Melone [49] argues for a reconceptualization of the construct to describe what she calls the ‘performance-related’ usage behaviors that reflect how IT is actually used in organizations.

The goal of this research effort is to make a preliminary effort to rethink the system-use construct and develop appropriate instrumentation. The work of social scientists who focus on the impact of technology on the nature of work, for example, $[7, 28, 56, 62, 65]$ is used as a reference theory base for this reconceptualization and operationalization. This literature provides a taxonomy of usage based on the functions of IT utilization in an organizational context $[30]$ .

In this social science literature on the nature of work, IT is viewed as being used by individuals in a work context to perform certain organizationally relevant functions (for example, to facilitate problem solving/decision making, coordinate work activities horizontally and vertically, and service customers).

How extensively IT is used to perform these functions defines how effectively it is used in the organizational context.

System-use is a complex construct and it is unlikely that any one taxonomy will be appropriate for all purposes and applications. The instruments developed here evaluate how extensively systems are used by individuals to perform certain organizationally relevant functions; they do not evaluate the application per se. Systems may be used in limited ways due to narrowly specialized jobs, user resistance, poor training, or a lack of employee empowerment. Despite this limitation, these measures of system-use in an organizational context can provide an important new research tool.

## 2. The system-use construct

Usage has been proposed as an MIS success measure in several frameworks for research $[20, 27, 37, 44]$ . System usage is also a central concept in taxonomies of success $[17, 19]$ . Based on the works of Shannon and Weaver $[57]$ and Mason $[48]$ , DeLone and McLean $[17]$ propose a sequence of six categories of success: system quality; information quality; use; user satisfaction; individual impact; and organizational impact. In this literature, system-use is viewed primarily as a dependent variable – a success measure.

Doll and Torkzadeh [19] describe a ‘system-to-value chain’ of system success constructs varying from beliefs, to attitudes, to behavior (system-use), to the social and economic impacts of IT (see Fig. 1). Most MIS research can be characterized as ‘upstream’ studies on the causes of system success (that is, implementation research). This system-to-value chain suggests an alternative role for the construct in a downstream research agenda – a role as an independent variable. System-use depends on system quality and is a major factor in determining the social and economic impacts of technology. Thus, system-use can be viewed as both a success measure in upstream research and as a complex causal agent that explains/predicts the downstream impacts of IT.

## 2.1. System-use measures in MIS research

The literature has long recognized the importance of system-use as a measure of technology's acceptance [36]. An upstream research agenda focusing on system implementation has shaped the MIS community's thinking about the system-use construct. System-use has been considered as a dependent variable (that is, a success measure) that is used to study the effects of various causal factors (for example, functional and interface characteristics, system development methods) on end-user acceptance. For this research agenda, a unidimensional concept of system-use was adequate and more usage was always considered desirable.

In lab studies, system-use is operationalized as use of computer-based decision aids $[1]$ , use of numerical vs. nonnumerical information $[5]$ , hours of usage $[22]$ , frequency of requests for specific reports $[6]$ , use of data sets $[15]$ , and the number of DSS features used $[25]$ . In field studies, system-use is operationalized as the number of messages sent or received on an average day $[58]$ , percentage of time DSS are used in decision making situations $[4]$ , number of requests for information $[11]$ , frequency of use $[12]$ , frequency of past or intended usage $[21]$ , frequency of general use and specific use $[23]$ , frequency of microcomputer use $[16]$ , number of minutes, sessions, and functions used $[24]$ , frequency of voluntary use $[31]$ , percentage of total work time spent using computers and average weekly hours of use $[32]$ , hours of usage and number of different types of applications used $[43]$ , expenditures/charges for computing use $[26]$ , frequency and hours of usage $[34]$ , and minutes of job-related PC use per day, frequency of PC use, and diversity of software packages $[61]$ . Igbaria et al.'s $[35]$ level of sophistication of usage and Cooper and Zmud's $[10]$ levels of usage of MRP systems are notable exceptions that characterize the extent of use rather than just the amount. However, it is questionable whether these measures are more appropriately viewed as indicators of skill.

The MIS literature has devoted little effort in developing a multidimensional concept of system-use (that is, a taxonomy of performance-related behaviors) that recognizes the organizational functions for which IT is utilized in the post-implementation context. After systems have become operational, system improvement efforts often focus on reducing the cost and improving the effectiveness of computer usage. The goals are to do work in less time while utilizing IT more effectively to perform organizationally relevant functions. The number of hours systems used is a determinant of administrative labor costs. In this post-implementation context, hours of use may be a success measure, but less rather than more hours is desirable.

A new multidimensional concept of system-use that identifies key performance-related usage behaviors could provide a set of independent or mediating variables to predict the downstream social and economic impacts of IT on work. A unidimensional concept of system-use is not likely to provide sufficient explanatory power to facilitate a rich downstream research agenda. IT has a plethora of social and economic impacts at the level of the individual, the work group, and the organization. A multidimensional system-use construct has inherent advantages in explaining and predicting these diverse impacts.

## 2.2. Technology's impact on the nature of work

The social science literature on technology's impact on the nature of work describes, in some detail, how IT is used in a post-implementation context, how it shapes the nature of work, and how it impacts task performance at the level of the individual end-user [7, 28, 29, 30, 62, 65]. This literature is characterized by a transition from an industrial paradigm to a post-industrial paradigm. Early writers such as Braverman [7] saw IT as automating and deskilling work, much as machinery was used in the industrial revolution. Later writers saw a post-industrial labor process where work is primarily an intellectual rather than a manual activity, where IT enriches work, and where workers require enhanced intellectual skills to make full use of the new computer and communication technologies available to them.

In Labor and Monopoly Capital, Braverman argues that the widespread introduction of IT in manufacturing, services, and white collar work has improved productivity and management control, but has not changed the labor process itself. Guided by Taylorist practice of dividing up tasks, deskilling labor, and separating task conception from execution, Braverman suggests that there is no need for new theories to explain how IT can be used or to describe its impact on the nature of work. He sees IT being used to facilitate the vertical specialization of work with management/staff planning the employee's work, monitoring performance, and taking corrective action.

Using a post-industrial perspective, Hirschhorn [28] contends that IT is used in new ways that have decisive implications on the general texture of life. Worker skill is not the skill of executing, but the skill of problem solving and the ability to learn and innovate. Work becomes heavily dependent on information processing, increasingly abstract, and mediated by sensing mechanisms. Workers feel responsible even if they act infrequently. Thus, in studying the impact of IT, we cannot just study what workers do, but rather how prepared they are to do what they might have to do. He argues that the overall impact of technology is to diffuse the management functions. Empowered workers can use IT to solve problems in innovative ways, plan their own work, and monitor/control their own performance. To make full use of the new technologies, Hirschhorn contends that we must confront the constraints imposed by management systems, capitalistic interests, and old organizational designs.

Zuboff [65] distinguishes between the deskilling impact of ‘automating’ and enhanced cognitive skills (sense making, inferential reasoning, systemic thinking) needed in an ‘informed’ environment. She describes the use of IT for identifying and testing task innovations that improve productivity and management control. Weick argues that IT is equivocal (that is, open to several interpretations, subject to misunderstandings, complex, and recondite). IT utilization requires ongoing structuring and sense making. Work is largely cognitive and time alone is not a good measure of either the amount of work done or the extent of computer usage.

Hirschhorn and Farduhar [30] identify three functions of IT utilization: (1) decision support; (2) customer service; and (3) work integration. They also identify five components (see Fig. 2). The computer allows personnel to make decision-making more explicit, and augments and amplifies creative judgment in problem solving by providing access to models and data bases. Decision rationalization includes both explaining decisions and improving the decision process. They also describe how IT is used to create value for customers by improving customer service.

Work integration includes both the vertical and horizontal integration of job tasks. IT shapes the extent of the division of labor within the flow of work (horizontal) and between the managers and the managed (vertical). IT facilitates communication among the members of a work group, enabling the organization to utilize the specialized expertise of a number of individuals. The level of vertical integration is determined by the degree to which IT is used by workers to plan their own work, monitor their own activity, and communicate vertically.

Hirschhorn contends that industrial and post-industrial organizations use IT differently. Industrial systems emphasize the use of IT for decision rationalization and vertical integration (for example, command and control). In many post-industrial organizations, the users of IT will emphasize problem solving (for example, amplifying information or sense making) and the horizontal integration of work. Customer service involves using IT to provide more differentiated and customized service to internal customers (that is, internal people who are serviced by an application) or external clients.

## 3. Research methods

To measure this multidimensional concept of system-use, a literature review was conducted to ensure that a comprehensive list of items was generated. The works of previous researchers on system usage $[40, 41, 45, 46, 47, 50, 51, 54, 59, 60]$ and the social and economic impact of IT $[7, 13, 14, 29, 30, 42, 52, 56, 62, 65]$ were reviewed. These writings provide a rich description of how IT is used in the work context. Illustrations and examples in this literature were used to generate measures for system-use behavior. The researchers generated 62 items (see Appendix A) to measure the five components of system-use defined in Fig. 2. A five-point Likert-type scale was used where 1 stands for ‘not at all’, 2 for ‘a little’, 3 for ‘moderately’, 4 for ‘much’, and 5 for ‘a great deal’.

## 3.1. Pilot study

A pilot sample of 89 usable interviews were obtained using a structured interview procedure (2 h per interview) followed by the self-administered questionnaire. To make the results more generalizable, the researchers gathered data from 30 different firms and sampled a variety of applications and situations (for example, individuals engaged in computer intensive work as well as more casual users). Qualitative comments from the structured interviews were compared with the responses to items measuring each of the five components. This enabled the researchers to verify that the respondents understood the questions.

The users were asked to demonstrate their application, provide sample output reports, and describe how they used IT in their work. The research assistants recorded qualitative interview comments for each dimension, enabling the researchers to make observations concerning how extensively each application was being used for problem solving, decision rationalization, horizontal integration, vertical integration, and customer service. These observer scores, using a 1 to 5 scale for each component, were used as a criterion for eliminating items.

The researchers analyzed this database with several objectives in mind: purification; unidimensionality; reliability; brevity; and simplicity of factor structure. First, as suggested by Churchill [8], the researchers purified the items (that is, eliminated 'garbage items'). Two criteria were used to eliminate items. First, they were removed if their corrected-item total correlation was <0.50. Second, items were also eliminated if their correlation with the corresponding observer criterion score was <0.50.

After this, an exploratory factor analysis of the remaining items was conducted to identify items that were not factorially pure [63]. Items that loaded on more than one factor at 0.40 or above were eliminated. These pilot study methods resulted in a tentative 30-item instrument that measures the three dimensions and five components of system-use defined in Fig. 2. Table 1 illustrates these 30 items and their reference code; corrected item-total correlations and correlations with the appropriate observer criterion are reported for the pilot sample.

## 3.2. Survey methods

To further explore this 30-item instrument, a sample of 409 computer end-users was gathered from 18 organizations. This was half of the firms contacted. The MIS directors were asked to identify their major applications. The MIS directors then consulted with the heads of user departments to identify major end-users and applications.

Responses were obtained from 63 percent of those surveyed.

## 3.2.1. Sample characteristics

The organizations surveyed include eight manufacturing firms, one retail firm, two government agencies, two utilities, two hospitals, two educational institutions, and one ‘other’. The 409 respondents represent 139 different applications, including accounts payable, accounts receivable, budgeting, CAD, CAD-CAM, customer service, service dispatching, engineering analysis, process control, work order control, general ledger, manpower planning, financial planning, inventory, order entry, payroll, personnel, production planning, purchasing, quality, sales analysis, student data, and profit planning. The large number of organizations and the variety of applications help support the generalizability of the findings.

Respondents were asked to identify their position within the organization; they included 20 top level managers, 80 middle managers, 76 first level supervisors, 143 professional employees without supervisory responsibility, and 90 operating personnel. Forty-seven percent of the respondents stated that their application was a personal computer application. Thirty-six percent of the computer applications were developed primarily by end-users but only 12 percent of the respondents developed the applications themselves. Fifty percent of the applications provided data analysis capabilities (spreadsheet, modeling, simulation, optimization or statistical routines). Forty-six percent of the applications provided a data base with flexible inquiry capabilities.

## 3.2.2. Data-analysis methods

After the pilot, the researchers were still unsure of the dimensionality of the system-use construct (that is, three functions versus five components) and where individual items would load. Thus, the 30-item instrument was not considered a hypothesized measurement model, permitting the use of confirmatory factor analysis. Traditional exploratory factor analysis was used to further explore factor structure. The researchers used principal component as the means of extraction and oblimin as the method of rotation. The ratio of sample size to number of items (13.6:1) was above the 10:1 ratio suggested for factor analysis by Kerlinger [39].

A chi-square test described by Bagozzi and Phillips $[3]$ was used to assess discriminant validity between pairs of constructs. Using LISREL, models of pairs of latent constructs and their observable variables were run with the correlation between the latent constructs fixed at 1.0 and also with the correlation between the latent constructs free to assume any value. The differences in the chi-square values for the fixed and free solutions indicate whether a unidimensional rather than a two-dimensional model could account for the intercorrelations among the observed items in each pair.

To assess predictive validity, the researchers examined correlations among the three dimensions of system-use (decisions support, work integration, and customer service) and a 12-item predictive criterion scale measuring the application's perceived impact on work. This scale, illustrated in Appendix B, measures four aspects of the impact of IT on work: task productivity; task innovation; customer satisfaction; and management control. A five-point Likert-type scale was used to measure perceived impact (that is, 1 stands for ‘not at all’ . . . to . . . 5 for ‘a great deal’). In this sample, the perceived impact scale had a reliability ( $\alpha$ ) of 0.94. Finally, the instruments' reliability ( $\alpha$ ) and predictive validity were examined in the overall sample and across alternative contexts or subgroup of applicability.

## 4. Results

Without specifying the number of factors, an exploratory factor analysis yielded four factors with eigen values >1.0 (13.665, 2.935, 2.519, and 1.230). This four-factor solution was not easily interpreted. Factor analyzes were run for 3 through 10 factors to identify the simplest structure and the most interpretable factors. The three-factor solution, reported in Table 2, represents the simplest structure (fewest cross loadings) and the most interpretable solution. Cross loadings $\geq$ 0.30 are reported. Of the eight solutions examined, the three-factor solution was the only one with zero cross loadings. The items are grouped by their highest (primary) factor loading and listed in descending order. The three factors explained 63.7% of the variation in the 30 items. The factors were easily interpreted as decision support, work integration, and customer service, respectively.

## 4.1. Discriminant validity results

To test discriminant validity, the researchers used LISREL on three- and five-factor models. For the three-factor model, the differences between the chi-square values (one degree of freedom) for the fixed and free solutions for the three pairs of constructs were: 21.86 for decision support versus customer service; 13.14 for customer service versus work integration;

and 9.88 for decision support versus work integration. For one test, a chi-square value $\geq6.63$ for one degree of freedom is significant at p<0.01; for three tests, the chi-square value for any pair must be approximately $\geq8.50$ for significance at the p<0.01 level ([9], p. 167). Thus, in the three-factor model, all three pairs of construct show adequate discriminant validity at the p<0.01 level.

In this data set, a five-factor model (problem solving, decision rationalization, horizontal integration, vertical integration, and customer service) failed to achieve discriminant validity. Problem solving (six items) and decision rationalization (seven items) have a chi-square difference score of only 0.61, strongly suggesting only one dimension. The horizontal (four items) and vertical integration (seven items) have a chi-square difference score of 3.90, indicating that this pair of constructs does not achieve discriminant validity at the p<0.01 level.

Reliability ( $\alpha$ ) and predictive validity scores for the system-use scales are adequate (see Table 3). The 30-item overall usage scale is more highly correlated with the predictive criterion (r=0.702) than any of its components, suggesting that the three usage patterns capture somewhat different variances and that the combined scale may benefit from these differences. To assess applicability across subgroup, reliability and predictive validity are also reported for different hardware platforms, types of applications, positions of respondents, modes of application development, and required usage conditions. All $\alpha$ scores are high (0.87–0.97), indicating good reliability across all contexts and subgroups.

In each subgroup or condition, correlations with the predictive criterion are significant $p<0.05$ , suggesting that the utilization of computer applications for decision support, work integration, and customer service are adequate predictors of perceived impact on work. ## 4.3. Hypothesized measurement model and parameter estimates

Once a hypothesized measurement model was determined, structural equation modeling was used to state this model with precision. The researchers recommend a hypothesized measurement model consisting of three correlated first-order latent constructs (decision support, work integration, and customer service) with 30 observable items. The large number of items per latent factor suggests that a partial aggregate model (that is, where composites based on subscales are used as indicators of latent variables) would be appropriate [2].

Except for the customer service items, where the literature suggested only one interpretation, subscales were formed by adding together items with similar interpretations. This method of creating subscales enables one to examine how well each loads on its corresponding construct. ## 5. Discussion and conclusions

The MIS literature and the social science literature on the impact of IT on the nature of work have developed independently. Multidimensional measures of system-use can facilitate research on the link between upstream causal factors of interest to MIS professionals (for example, design features or development methods) and their downstream consequences (that is, the social and economic impacts of IT). Measuring system-use in terms of decision support, work integration, and customer service functions has several advantages. First, it enables us to investigate the patterns and extent of system-use along organizationally relevant dimensions. Thus, it sheds light on how fully or effectively organizations are using IT. Second, it is meaningful and relevant whether usage is voluntary or required. Third, it enables us to measure system-use at the application or individual level, yet put this usage in an organizational or work context. Fourth, it is appropriate for studies where the unit of analysis is the application, the individual, or the work group.

Some words of caution are appropriate. First, one must distinguish between evaluating applications and evaluating how extensively IT is used to perform certain organizationally relevant functions. These instruments should be used cautiously, if at all, to evaluate applications. Second, the number of factors (3 or 5) may depend on the nature of the sample. For example, light or moderate administrative users may not distinguish between problem solving and decision rationalization, but this distinction may be more apparent to users engaged in intensely scientific (that is, analytical) work. Third, the evidence that the system-use dimensions predict the perceived impact of IT on work does not imply that these usage variables can be directly manipulated; system-use may make more theoretical sense as a mediating variable with the directly manipulable causal factors being such variables as end-user skill or training.

When we evaluate how people use technology, we are evaluating a complex socio-technical phenomenon defined by the interaction of people and technology in an organizational context. It is precisely this unpredictable nature of system-use that enhances its theoretical significance as a mediating variable. Nevertheless, IT studies that assume an emergent perspective are rare. By enabling research designs that incorporate system-use as an independent (mediating) variable, these system-use measures may provide an important tool for advancing research on the emergent perspective of causal agency.

In the emerging post-industrial setting, scholars contend that IT is used in new ways that have substantial implications for the nature of work, productivity and economic growth. Increasingly, work has become heavily dependent on information processing, abstract, cognitive, and a fusion of doing and learning. IT can empower workers. Thus, it has the potential to diffuse management functions throughout the work force. By creating a more flexible and responsive organization with a flatter structure and lower overhead costs, this diffusion can have strategic implications. These system-use measures provide a tool for assessing whether IT is, indeed, being ‘used in new ways’.
