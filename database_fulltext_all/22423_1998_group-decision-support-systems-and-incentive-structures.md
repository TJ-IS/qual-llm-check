# Group decision support systems and incentive structures

Ashraf Shirani $^{1,a}$ , Milam Aiken $^{b,*}$ , Joseph G.P. Paolillo $^{2,b}$

$^{a}$ BCIS/QM 211 Weller Hall, Hofstra University, Hempstead, NY 11549-1090, USA

$^{b}$ Department of Management and Marketing, School of Business Administration, University of Mississippi, University, MS 38677, USA

Received 17 March 1997; accepted 14 December 1997

## Abstract

Many studies have compared the advantages and disadvantages of meetings using Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS), but relatively little research has been conducted on the role of incentives in these meetings. An experiment was conducted with 40 eight-person groups to determine the influence of incentive distribution mode (group or individual) and incentive type (positive or negative) on GDSS outcomes. Results indicate that group performance, including task performance, consensus, process satisfaction, and participation, is higher if there are group-based incentives, and that negative incentives or extinction result in more participation and less satisfaction. No interaction effects were found between incentive distribution mode and incentive type. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Group support systems; Incentives; Electronic meetings; Brainwriting

## 1. Introduction

The emerging phenomena of virtual corporations, virtual products, corporate alliances, distributed workplaces, and telecommuting require employees, customers, suppliers, and other stakeholders to meet, but not necessarily in one place at the same time. As a result, Group Decision Support Systems (GDSSs) and teleconferencing systems may become increasingly important for these requisite meetings.

Substantial evidence has indicated that GDSSs can enhance creativity among group members $[28]$ , support negotiation $[17]$ , improve productivity $[27]$ , and increase group effectiveness and satisfaction [11, 25]. By making the decision-making process more structured and by reducing evaluation apprehension through anonymous communication, GDSSs improve individual contributions resulting in enhanced group decision quality. Further, parallel communication afforded through a GDSS reduces production blocking and may help expedite decision making [1, 12].

However, GDSSs may foster a decision-making environment which is centered upon the individual, and thereby create a potential danger that overall group or organizational interests may not be well-served unless participants have a incentive to do so. The role of incentives in electronic meetings should, therefore, be of interest to all organizations that currently use or will use this technology in the future.

If incentives influence an individuals's motivation to participate, it may be possible to devise reward systems (incentives) to boost group performance or individual productivity in electronic meetings.

## 2. Previous research

What motivates individuals in a meeting to withhold information, contribute misinformation, participate as passive observers, or free load? Several perspectives from social psychology and economic agency theory help to explain such behaviors.

## 2.1. The social psychology perspective

A basic understanding of individual motivation is provided by need-based theories of motivation, such as those offered by Maslow, Alderfer, McClelland, and Herzberg (see for example [6]). These theories assert that human behavior, including its intensity and duration, is influenced by a person's unfulfilled needs. Process theories of motivation, on the other hand, consider motivation as highly dependent on subjective and dynamic individual assessments of each situation with respect to effort and reward. Behavior modification and reinforcement theories recommend eliciting and sustaining desired behavioral traits through appropriate rewards and punishment. In almost all the theories of motivation, rewards or incentives are of central concern. In the case of collaborative decision making, a problem may arise when incentives for an individual group member are not properly aligned with those of the group. The asymmetry of individual and group interests may result in unproductive individual behavior unless appropriate reward systems (incentives) are developed and implemented.

Increasingly, organizations are developing and utilizing reward systems administered on a group basis to stimulate greater employee effort and productivity. In the traditional, face-to-face decision environment, group-based and organization-wide incentives may result in a high degree of employee participation. In GDSS-supported environments, however, we expect the opposite to be true.

## 2.2. The economic agency perspective

If we view the benefits from an organizational or group-based decision as ‘public’ or ‘quasi-public’ goods for the employees and group members, the theory of public choice then provides a formal explanation for individualistic behavior of the employees in organizational decision making. A public good is characterized by a lack of restriction on its supply and impossibility or inefficiency in attempts to exclude others from consumption once it has been supplied to any members of the community. According to Mueller [26], “the jointness of supply is the carrot, making cooperative-collective decisions beneficial to all.” The non-exclusion principle in group decision making could mean that all group members receive equal benefits from the group decision, irrespective of their effort and contribution. In order for the group to maximize its welfare, therefore, the decision-making process must be monitored for individual member efforts towards the common good. A GDSS-supported environment with anonymity may encourage free-riding if group members are not excluded from group benefits. A group which does not provide incentives for individual effort must, therefore, either incur monitoring costs or suffer process losses through lack of participation in decision making and consequent lower decision quality.

Agency theory also attempts to explain why individual managers' behaviors may need to be monitored if the managers' and the firm's interests are not aligned. In their seminal article on theory of the firm, Jensen and Meckling [19] argue that the agency problem may be addressed from the perspective of "contractual relation (including compensation incentives) between the principal and the agent to provide appropriate incentives for the agent to make choices which will maximize the principal's welfare." They define the agency relationship as a contract under which a person (the principal) engages another person (the agent) to perform some service, thereby delegating decision-making authority to the agent. As Alchian and Demsetz [4] and Jensen and Meckling emphasize, contractual relationships among owners, employees, customers, and suppliers of the firm are the essence of the firm. Conflicting objectives of individuals are brought into equilibrium within a framework of these contractual relations. They argue that managerial behavior may need to be monitored in order to enforce the explicit and implicit contracts, thus incurring monitoring/agency costs. As argued earlier, the GDSS meeting, in general, lacks mechanisms to do this and consequently is vulnerable to process losses.

## 2.3. The group decision support system perspective

McGrath [23] viewed task performance by groups as a consequence of communication patterns and interpersonal relations. McGrath's model describes group interaction and decision making as a three-mode or three-stage process. First, two or more persons communicate to solve a problem or discuss an issue. The communication process, channel, and modalities used constitute the communication pattern. Second, the communication behavior may be considered in terms of its content, which will have a task-oriented as well as an interpersonal behavioral component. The third stage of the overall interaction and decision-making process concerns impact, involving the outcomes or consequences of the interaction for the task as well as the relationship of the members to one another. Group outcomes in McGrath's model are thus a function of communication channels, modalities, the task, and interpersonal relationship patterns. A GDSS and decision-making environment substantially affect these determinants by providing structure and support for the task and meeting process, allowing anonymous communication, and eliminating, or significantly reducing verbal, face-to-face communication and social interaction. Behavioral theories and empirical findings established for the traditional verbal meeting, therefore, may or may not hold true in the GDSS-supported decision-making environment.

Verbal communication provides social context cues which regulate behavior. When such cues are weak or absent, as in electronic meetings, people tend to produce self-centered and unregulated behavior. Both the static and dynamic cues present in face-to-face meetings are important regulators of participants' behaviors. Static cues emanate from appearances of objects, people, and their environment; dynamic cues emanate from behavior (for example, nodding approval, hesitating before replying, and frowning) [15, 29]. Through social context cues in face-to-face meetings, people “adjust their targets of communication, the tone and content of their communication, and their conformity to social norms” [20]. Text-based communication, on the other hand, frees people from conforming to social norms and they are less concerned about making a good appearance [8]; their behavior may become more extreme, more impulsive, and less socially differentiated [34]. Sproull and Kiesler [36] reported increased self-absorption and uninhibited behavior in electronic mail messages. Electronic meeting environments promote self-interest by freeing individuals from group conformity and scrutiny.

A GDSS masks most non-verbal behaviors and sometimes eliminates verbal ones. Anonymity of group members in communicating ideas and contributing towards group goals serves as another mask. The organizational supervision of individual behavior is temporarily removed and work groups and task teams tend to behave as ‘individuized’ groups. ‘Individuation’, in Worchel et al.’s [39] model, is a stage where group focus breaks down and attention shifts to individuals. Group members no longer contribute towards group performance or receive rewards equally or equitably. In this stage of group development, group structure is altered or relaxed and individuals begin to pursue personal goals. Although Worchel et al.’s group-stage model is essentially a life cycle approach with a sequence of stages, “movement from stage-to-stage is not exclusively dependent on temporal factors.” One method in which the individuation stage may be triggered is through altered communication modalities, which according to McGrath would result in ‘quasi’ groups. These are characterized by restricted or structured communication networks as found in brainstorming, nominal group, and Delphi techniques. By restricting communication to the written medium and by supporting anonymity, a GDSS may trigger individuation and formation of quasi-groups, thereby shifting the focus from organizational/group goals to individual goals [16].

Most research has compared the performance of groups in oral, face-to-face meetings to GDSS-supported, face-to-face meetings. The dependent measures have almost always been grouped into one factor and variously termed group outcomes $[9, 13]$ , group performance $[24]$ , quality of group outcomes $[31]$ , meeting effectiveness $[18]$ , and decision quality $[40]$ . The most commonly used dependent measures of ‘group outcomes’ in these studies include consensus, participation, satisfaction, and some measure of task performance. Most of these studies were conducted in a ‘decision room’ setting as defined by

Dennis et al. with small group size, members together in the same room, and synchronous exchange. Some other, infrequently used, dependent measures in the comparative GDSS research have included time to decision $[33]$ , number of non-redundant ideas, confidence in recommendations, equality of participation, and influence behavior. Our research uses the four most-frequently-used dependent measures of group performance: task performance, consensus, satisfaction, and participation. Furthermore, the effort considers group performance as a comprehensive concept involving meeting outcome as well as decision implementation.

## 2.4. Hypotheses formulation

It was postulated that participation in electronic meetings may be higher in groups that reward their members for individual effort rather than in those which do not.

This study postulates a relationship between incentive structures and group performance in GDSS-supported meetings. Three research hypotheses representing main and interaction effects were formulated.

H1: In GDSS-supported meetings, the mode of the incentive distribution (group vs. individual) has no effect on group performance measures, including (a) task performance, (b) consensus, (c) participation, and (d) satisfaction. It was anticipated that group-based incentives would have a positive effect on all group performance measures except participation. Some group members may free-ride because the GDSS environment provides anonymity and reduced social interaction, thereby eliminating the social pressure to participate. Moreover, the non-exclusively principle of group-based incentives guarantees free-riders equal benefits. Consequently, it was expected that there would be more free-riding behavior and less total task-related participation in groups with group-based incentives than in groups which reward individual performance.

H2: In GDSS-supported meetings, the type of incentives (positive or negative) have no effect on group performance including (a) task performance, (b) consensus, (c) participation, and (d) satisfaction. It is generally accepted that rewards are more effective than punishment [7]. Negative incentives tend to increase hostility, resentment and withdrawal, and consequently we expected a reduction in satisfaction. However, a short-term behavioral response to negative incentives was expected to increase participation in group decision making. Previous research did not indicate any relationship between incentive type and task performance or consensus.

H3: In GDSS-supported meetings, there is no interaction effect between incentive types and incentive distribution modes on group performance measures, including task performance, consensus, participation, and satisfaction. Since group-based incentives provide a motive for cooperation and mutual support, it was expected that in group-based treatments, the resentment effects of punishment would be somewhat diffused.

## 3. The GDSS experiment

## 3.1. Subjects

Subjects for the experiment were recruited on a voluntary basis from several sections of an undergraduate MIS course. An incentive to participate in the experiment and to perform well was offered in the form of extra credit in the subjects' classes. The participants were randomly assigned to the various treatment groups, and a pre-meeting questionnaire was used to verify that differences among groups were not statistically significant. The group size was kept at eight persons, and a total of 40 groups (ten for each of the four treatments) were used in the experiment. Thus, 320 subjects participated.

## 3.2. Task

Task selection for group problem solving has a profound effect on information search and problem-solving strategies, and a particular task confronting a group must be a driving force in electronic meeting software design $[10]$ . According to Poole et al. $[30]$ , “the general variable ‘group task type’ is emerging as an especially important variable, often accounting for as much as 50% of the variance in group performance.”

Using McGrath's taxonomy, the task selected for the experiment was intellective (a task requiring selection of the correct alternative). The task was a combined Mathematics and English language test (developed from a Scholastic Aptitude Test preparation guide) with one correct answer for each of the ten questions [5].

## 3.3. Independent variables

A $2 \times 2$ factorial design was utilized in this experiment. The two factors, incentive types and incentive distribution mode, were each studied at two levels. Under the distribution mode, subjects received rewards and penalties based on either their individual achievement or on performance of their group. Two types of incentives were administered: positive incentives to reward the subjects with bonus points, and negative incentives to deny subjects some of the bonus points. ‘Negative’ incentives in this research actually followed the principle known as ‘extinction’ in the behavioral reinforcement literature. Extinction involves reduction in, withdrawal of, or the absence of a positive reinforcer [32]. Here, negative incentives meant withholding of bonus points.

## 3.4. Dependent variables

## 3.4.1. Task performance

Task performance was measured at two levels, individual and group. On a scale of 0–10, test scores were directly proportional to the number of correct answers. Group scores were computed on the ‘majority wins’ basis. Individual tests within a group were used as votes; an answer with the largest number of votes was considered the group answer. Such voting schemes often are used in objective performance evaluations [38]. In the case of a tie between two incorrect answers, groups were to score zero on that question; a tie between one correct and one incorrect answer yielded a 1/2 point for the group; a three-way tie between one correct and two incorrect answers yielded 1/3 point; and a four-way tie between one correct and three incorrect answers yielded a 1/4 point score.

## 3.4.2. Consensus

A number of instruments and procedures for the measurement of consensus have been suggested in the literature. Some previous studies have used the concept of distance from consensus to measure consensus [21, 22]. Distance from consensus was determined ‘by the total number of positions members were away from the most agreed-upon policy’ [35]. Here, the concept of distance was used iteratively to compute total ‘dissonance’ level within a group.

Thus, there were four possible alternatives for each of the test questions, and each group consisted of eight members. Adopting the consensus definition as ‘unanimous agreement’, a consensus level of one was assigned if all of the eight members of a group opted for the same alternative. This also resulted in a dissonance level of zero using the above formula. On the other hand, if all eight group members equally disagreed by casting two votes for each of the four alternatives, consensus was said to be zero, and the dissonance level was the maximum (12 by using the dissonance formula). Given eight group members and four alternatives in this study, all possible voting configurations (15 in all) were determined and dissonance levels computed. In assigning consensus levels to various configurations, an equal distance was assumed between two successive voting configurations with one being complete consensus and zero as no consensus.

## 3.4.3. Participation

Gallupe et al. measured participation by the number of non-redundant ideas generated by groups. Here, participation was measured by the number of ‘relevant’ comments generated by a group. Comments related to the task were considered relevant and all other comments including social communication were considered irrelevant. Two objective faculty members independently judged the relevancy of comments through a blind-review.

## 3.4.4. Process satisfaction

Satisfaction has almost always been measured on a Likert-type scale, on a self-reported basis $[14]$ . Here, we used a slightly-modified version of the scale developed by Gallupe et al. The post-session questionnaire asked: (1) were you satisfied with the decision-making process used, and (2) would you recommend this process for decision making and problem solving? Responses were measured on a five-point scale.

## 3.5. Statistical analyses

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) were employed to assess the effect of the treatments on the dependent variables. Appropriate statistical analyses were first conducted to assess whether MANOVA assumptions were satisfied. This included intra-class correlation for the independence of the observations, Box's M to check for homogeneity of variance-covariance matrices, and tests for normality using skewness coefficients [37]. All the necessary assumptions were met.

## 3.6. Experimental setting

An electronic meeting room consisting of 55 workstations networked together in a U-shaped configuration was used for the experiments [2]. The locally-developed GDSS electronic brainstorming software allowed group members anonymously and simultaneously to type and view comments on their individual workstations while all comments were recorded [3].

## 3.7. Procedures

Subjects were randomly assigned to groups and to the four experimental conditions. An analysis of variance was conducted on subjects responses to their gender, major area of study, educational level (freshman, sophomore, junior, senior), years of computing experience, and whether they liked working in groups or not, and the subjects in the four treatment groups were not found to be significantly different.

Before the experiment, the subjects were given a 10-min introduction to GDSS meetings by letting them practice on a sample problem. Subjects were then given verbal as well as written instructions on how their performance on the test would be translated into bonus points. A test was then administered. Subjects had 30 min to answer the 10-question test. They were free to ‘discuss’ answers using the GDSS system; verbal communication and calculators were not allowed. Individual group members were asked to turn in the completed test. One group’s answer to the test was computed on a ‘majority-wins’ basis, each individual’s turned-in answer counting as one vote. Finally, the subjects were asked to answer two satisfaction rating questions.

The test result for H1 was highly significant in supporting the postulated assertion. This implies that group-based incentives do result in a significantly different performance: in this case better task performance, higher consensus, more participation, and more satisfaction.

## 4.2. Univariate results

H1: Group performance consisted of four dependent measures: task performance, consensus, participation, and satisfaction. A review of the literature suggested that group-based incentives (GBI) would increase group performance in terms of all of these measures except participation, which was expected to decline for GBI treatments, since anonymity in GDSS-supported meetings would encourage free-riding. As shown in Table 2, univariate results for all the four dependent measures were highly significant. Groups with group-based incentives (GBI treatment) had better task performance, higher consensus, more participation, and higher satisfaction than those with individual-based incentives (IBI treatment). The fourth sub-hypothesis pertaining to participation was therefore not supported. This suggests that either participation is not a good surrogate for not-free-riding behavior or group-based incentives do not really promote free-riding.

H2: On the basis of previous research, it was expected that negative incentives would result in less satisfaction but more participation than positive incentives. Sub-hypotheses relating to task performance and consensus were not specified due to a lack of theoretical or empirical support for a link between these variables and group performance. The ANOVA results supported the two hypothesized relationships as shown in Table 3.

H3: The third hypothesis had suggested one interaction effect, among the many possible: between incentive modes and incentive types. Table 4 shows the ANOVA results for H3. This hypothesis postulated that although satisfaction decreases with negative incentives, the decrease would be more with individual-based incentives than it would be with the group-based incentives. This interaction hypothesis was not supported.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. The role of individual and group incentives

Based on organizational behavior and economic theory, we anticipated that group-based incentives would reduce member participation in brainstorming. This assertion was made on the basis of findings that the anonymity offered by electronic brainstorming encourages free-riding behavior unless members are encouraged to participate by linking rewards to their performance. However, experimental results indicated the opposite behavior: group-based incentives resulted in more participation than individual-based incentives. Possibly free-riding by some group members does not mean less overall group participation; that is, group-based incentives may induce some free-riding behavior, but the increased group cohesion and consequent participation more than compensate for the lack of participation by free riders.

## 5.2. Positive and negative incentives

Previous research suggests that negative incentives or penalties may result in an immediate increase in participation or even overall performance, but that their long-term effects may include resentment and other harmful effects. In a GDSS-supported meeting environment, negative incentives increased task performance, consensus, participation, and dissatisfaction, although the results were significant only for dissatisfaction and consensus.

Also anticipated was a significant interaction between negative incentives and the incentives distribution mode. Based on the assumption that group-based incentives provide a motive for cooperation and mutual support, satisfaction was expected to be significantly less in groups with individual-based treatments than those with group-based treatments. The test result was not significant, although the dependent variable means pointed in the anticipated direction. One interpretation may be that electronic meetings lack the mechanisms for mutual social and emotional support among group members.

## 5.3. Limitations of the research

One obvious limitation of this research is in generalizing its findings to subjects and tasks much different from those used in the study. The subjects were undergraduate students, mostly business majors. It was assumed that the business students would retain their decision-making characteristics as they encounter real-life business and managerial situations. Further, an ‘intellective’ task with one correct answer was used; this allowed accurate measurement of the effect and increased the sensitivity of the treatments. Although intellective tasks are frequently performed, other task types with no one correct answer are often encountered in organizational decision making. In addition, most organizations do not have dichotomous incentive plans (that is, only positive or negative) as in this study. Rather, complex schemes with graduated incentives are often employed.

A second limitation of this study is the restrictiveness of variables used in the model of group dynamics. Incentives are only one of a number of possible variables that influence group dynamics and behavior. Other variables include the type of electronic brainstorming software, group size, and meeting environment.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper has explored the role of incentives on group performance. Experimental results strongly supported the hypothesis that group-based incentives result in superior performance in GDSS-supported decision-making groups.
