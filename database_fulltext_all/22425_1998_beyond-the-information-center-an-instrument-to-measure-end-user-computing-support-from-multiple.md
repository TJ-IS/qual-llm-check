# Beyond the information center: An instrument to measure end-user computing support from multiple sources

Chittibabu Govindarajulu $^{a,*}$ , Brian J. Reithel $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Computer Information System, COBA, Southwest Missouri State University, Springfield, MO 65807, USA $^{b}$ Department of Management and Marketing, School of Business Administration, University of Mississippi, University, MS 38677, USA

Received 30 December 1996; revised 11 August 1997; accepted 21 December 1997

## Abstract

Recent studies have shown that end-users may be dissatisfied with support received from the centralized information center. While this has been the predominant source of formal support in the past, contemporary studies have identified a more decentralized form of support: local MIS staff. A third popular support source for end-users is the informal network of friends and colleagues. The relative degree of support that end-users receive from each of these sources is not evident. The lack of a reliable instrument to measure end-user support may have contributed to this absence of evidence. This paper presents an instrument that was designed to measure support from a set of diverse sources, such as the information center, local MIS staff, and informal sources. The instrument was pre-tested and refined prior to collecting data from 108 middle-level managers. The construct and convergent validities of the instrument appear to be strong. This instrument will be useful to researchers and practitioners who would like to identify support functions best provided by a specific support source. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: EUC support; EUC support instrument; Instrument validity; Local MIS staff; Centralized and decentralized EUC support

## 1. Introduction

End-user computing (EUC) is the use and/or development of information systems (IS) by the principal users of the systems' output or their staff [34]. Management of EUC is still receiving much attention from the community. Because EUC has both advantages and disadvantages, its effective management is required to minimize risks and to maximize gains. EUC support has received considerable attention in recent years [3,

5, 8, 14, 15, 18, 19, 22, 23, 25, 32]. Many researchers have consistently pointed out users' dissatisfaction with the services provided by Information Centers (ICs) [1, 29]. At the same time, users tend to seek help from other support sources, such as Informal Support (mainly from colleagues and Lead Users) and Localized MIS staff. However, the relative degree of support provided by these sources on various areas of support (such as hardware support, functional support) has not been fully studied. This may be due to the absence of a common instrument to measure such support. The paper discusses the development of one such instrument.

## 2. Sources of support for EUC

Since end-users lack technical knowledge regarding software and hardware, they require adequate support to realize the productivity gains of EUC $[7]$ . The use of another more recent form of support, local MIS staff appears to be an emerging trend in EUC support.

## 2.1. The Information Center

The basic goal of an IC is to help users help themselves. Hammond [16] has provided prescriptions for IC support services: staffing ratios, IC job descriptions, control policies/procedures, and organization design guidelines. One of its most important tasks is to maintain the balance between support and control. After its introduction in IBM, Canada in 1976, the IC concept became popular and spread to other firms. The services offered by the ICs can be grouped into six support categories: Hardware Support, Software Support, Data Support, Functional Support, Training and Education, and Miscellaneous Support (such as the provision of hot-line telephone assistance, publication of a newsletter, etc.).

## 2.2. Local MIS staff

A review of the literature reveals another form of formal support – Local MIS staff [30]. These staff members exclusively support the users of a specific department. They typically report to the functional department manager and not to the IS manager. A study conducted by the American Management Association found that, of the 250 organizations examined, most routine IC functions are now assigned to user departments [12]. The ICs were mainly responsible for communications technology, training tasks, and data backup and security. In addition, this study reported that 30% of large companies have MIS specialists on departmental budget lines to support users in that department, and ten firms with ICs announced plans to phase them out while three firms that once had ICs have since phased them out. Another study noted that some 9% of the IC managers report to a user department [10]. Carr [9] suggests that ICs could indeed be under the direction of user departments. Though this indicates that the percentage of IC managers reporting to user departments and the percentage of user departments having MIS staff is low, this may indicate changes in EUC support.

## 2.3. Informal support

Informal support mechanisms have received consistent attention $[2, 11, 21, 24]$ . The main sources of informal support are peers, friends, lead users, and other users. Users generally seem to maintain an informal network for their support needs. Brancheau et al. $[6]$ found that only 13% of the users relied exclusively on the IC. Another study showed that it was not seen as a particularly useful source of training and expertise $[28]$ ; indeed users gained most of their experience by learning on their own or from discussion with peers.

The main sources of informal support are Lead Users and Friends/Colleagues. Lead users have more experience and knowledge of information technology than other end-users. They are, in many ways, ideal for providing support to others, since they have the requisite business and computer knowledge. Rockart and Flannery's [31] functional support personnel would closely match the description of a lead user (For additional reading, Govindarajulu [13] presents a detailed review of informal support for EUC).

## 3. Need for a common instrument to measure support

There are mixed findings with respect to user satisfaction and the support sources. Bergeron and Berube $[2]$ reported that users were more satisfied when an IC existed. In addition, it has been rated as the most important source of support by end-users. However, more recent studies reveal an interesting pattern of results. One reported that services expected by users and services provided to users by the ICs were mismatched. Mirani and King $[23]$ also reported differences in the support expectations of different types of end-users and the support they received through an IC. Consistent with these findings, Nord and Nord $[25]$ found that a significant percentage of end-users were less than pleased with the support provided by the IC. It appears that later studies reveal the less enthusiastic support for IC. Also, researchers report that users prefer informal sources over formal sources $[5]$ .

Because local MIS staff is an emerging form of support, the literature contains no systematic studies about user satisfaction with it.

End-users require support in various areas, such as data and functional support; it is not clear what categories of support are best provided by the existing support sources. In order to provide effective support, it is vital, as a first step, to identify the areas of support best provided by these support sources.

A common instrument to measure support from different support sources is needed. Mirani and King proposed a nine-factor instrument to measure the support provided by ICs. This is specific to measurement of IC support, and thus may not be suitable for measuring support from other sources.

## 4. Development of a support instrument

Based on earlier work by Wetherbe and Leitheiser [34] and Vijayaraman [33], Carr and Rainer produced a list of 35 IC support services grouped into six support categories: Hardware, Software, Data, Functional, Training and Education and Miscellaneous. Later, based on a survey of 126 ICs, Rainer and Carr reported a mismatch between the services requested by users and those offered by ICs. The authors ranked the services in two ways: based on the percentage of ICs offering the service and on the percentage of end-users requesting the service. Of the 35 services, differences in rankings were found for 33 services. End-users placed high importance on data related support, while ICs ranking of services placed consulting on user problems at the top of the list. In the current study, a comprehensive list of support services was produced using the following guidelines:

1. User needs were given top priority. Many tasks that are considered to be under the control of corporate computing are now performed by end-users. This makes it important to assess the current support needs. Because present day end-users are more knowledgeable than in the past, the changing needs must be taken into consideration. To address these emerging service needs, the current study included services that had a ‘ranking gap’ of five or greater in the 1992 Rainer and Carr study. Ranking gap refers to the difference between the rank of the percentage of users requesting a specific service and the rank of the percentage of ICs offering that same service.

## 5. Data collection and analysis

For pre-testing, the survey was administered to 400 faculty and staff members from 20 departments of a southern university in the United States. This was an ideal source for a pretest, since the university employed both centralized (help desk) and decentra-

lized (local network administrators) forms of support. The sample population was divided into three equal groups, one for each of the three support sources (Help desk, Friends/Colleagues, and Local MIS staff). A structured questionnaire was used for data collection. A seven-point scale was used to measure support received by a user when compared to the support required/desired by the user. The questionnaire had two parts. The first part was used to collect demographic information, such as user computer experience and types of application the user had developed. The second part collected data about the support provided to users. Of the 80 responses received, only 57 were usable (response rate 16.75%). Table 2 shows the breakdown of mailings and the responses. This relatively low rate for each group caused some difficulty in drawing definitive conclusions from the pretest data factor analysis. For factor analysis to yield useful results, it is recommended that the ratio of items to data points should be 1:4 [20]. Thus, at least 68 responses are required for 23 items.

Given the data limitation, a factor analysis was conducted to study the factor loading pattern. This, followed by varimax rotation, was coded to yield five factors. Table 3 presents the set of factors that emerged. These pretest results should be treated with some caution and in a purely exploratory manner. The field survey was designed to overcome this data limitation problem.

All the functional support items (except FUNC2: Helping you to design applications) and all data support items formed factor 1. Two hardware support items (HW3: Help to list approved hardware vendors and HW5: Help in telecommunications hardware) and all software support items formed factor 2. All the training items and one functional support item (FUNC2) formed factor 3. Two hardware support items (HW1: Help in demonstrating hardware and HW2: Help in standardization of hardware) formed factor 4. Finally, a single item of hardware support (HW4: Help to outline formal procedures for getting hardware purchases approved) formed factor 5.

Since the wordings of items for hardware and software support seemed clear, the factor loading pattern of software and hardware support items suggested a possibility of a missing or more general support construct. However, the different factor loading of one functional support item may have been due to its misinterpretation by the respondents. This suggested the need to reword a few items. The changes were incorporated before the final mailing.

## 5.1. Modifications in the instrument

Pretest showed that the following help items loaded differently:

(a) to outline formal procedures for getting hardware purchases approved; and

(b) to list approved hardware vendors.

A careful review of them reveals that these are closely related to purchasing rather than technical support. Thus, a new factor of support was added with purchase related items grouped together. This was termed ‘Purchasing Support’. Mirani and King’s instrument contained a similar factor (Purchase Related Support). Certain items in the support factor ‘Functional Support’ were modified to minimize ambiguity. After these necessary modifications, the final instrument contained six primary constructs.

## 5.2. Final data collection

Respondents for the survey were a randomly selected group of 1000 non-IS middle-level managers across industry throughout the US. A survey conducted by Nolan and Norton found that more than half (55%) of all such employees use a PC or workstation [27]. Given the growth in the sales of personal computers, one can expect that most of the white collar workforce use computers. The main reason for selecting middle-level managers is that over 50% of the end user population is at the middle management level [4]. Only firms that have more than 150 employees were selected for the study. It is assumed that such a firm will have some formal support (IC or local MIS staff). This is important because one cannot expect a manager to complete a survey containing items about an IC or about local MIS staff if no formal support exists in the firm.

The addresses of the managers were purchased from a commercial mailing list firm based in California. The sample population was divided into three equal parts. The first sub-sample (333) received instruments designed to collect data about either the IC or the local MIS staff. The respondent was given the opportunity to pick one of the two support sources listed. This was intended to deal with the case where one of the support sources was absent in the respondents' firm and, thus, the recipient would not be able to complete the survey. Simultaneously, surveys were mailed to two other groups. One sub-sample was mailed an instrument designed to collect data about the use of IC or friends/colleagues, while the other was sent an instrument designed to collect data about local MIS staff or friends/colleagues. In order to improve the response rate, a reminder post card was mailed to the sample population five days after mailing the survey.

A total of 115 responses were received (11.5% response rate). Out of these, seven were ignored, since responses were incomplete in many respects, giving a final response rate of 10.8% (108 usable responses). Because of incorrect addresses, 17 mailings were returned ‘undeliverable’ (1.7%). Hence, the adjusted final response rate was close to 11%. Researchers have suggested that nonresponse bias should not be considered serious unless there is a good reason to believe that the respondents do infact significantly differ from the nonrespondents [17]. Here, with a sample composed only of middle-level managers, there is no strong a-priori reason to believe that the respondents would differ from nonrespondents.

## 6. Instrument validation

The survey instrument was tested for construct validity and convergent validity. Two primary approaches were utilized: factor analysis, and correlation/alpha analysis.

## 6.1. Construct validity

Construct validity refers to how well the instrument measures the concept or construct. Since the proposed factors of support had a total of 23 items, a minimum of 92 observations are required for proper analysis. Thus, the total of 108 usable responses exceeds the prescribed ratio. Prior to pooling the responses from the three sub-samples, the means of the computer experience of the respondents were compared. A one-way ANOVA was conducted to test the similarity of the three means. The results (presented in Table 8) confirm that the means of computer experience of respondents are not unequal, thus allowing the responses to be pooled for factor analytic purposes.

Exploratory factor analysis yielded a total of four factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0. The support factors can be characterized as: Factor-1: software, hardware and training; Factor-2: functional; Factor-3: data; and Factor-4: purchase. These four factors account for 75.4% of the variance. The loadings are presented in Table 9.

## 6.2. Convergent validity

Convergent validity measures how closely the items in a single construct are correlated with each other. High correlations indicate strong convergent validity.

Correlation analysis was used to test the validity of the items in the support instrument. With the exception of items of hardware support, all other item correlations were above 0.6, with most above 0.8. For hardware items the minimum value of the correlation coefficient was 0.53. All the correlations were statistically significant. Table 10 presents the results of reliability analysis (Cronbach's Alpha) for the six original constructs. The alpha values lend support to the strong scale reliability of the instrument. All the Alpha values exceed 0.78, with four of the six exceeding 0.90; this level of internal consistency is generally considered acceptable [26].

## 7. Discussion of results and limitations

A significant percentage (62%) of respondents reported the presence of departmental IS staff to support user computing needs. This has not been reported by earlier studies. Spreadsheets, word processing, and database applications still appear to be popular among end-users.

The results, in general, indicate that the instrument is strong. However, hardware support, software support and training did not load on three separate factors. Instead, they all loaded on a single factor (Factor 1). A closer look at the factors suggests that while factors 2, 3, and 4 denote specific support categories, Factor 1 represents a ‘generic’ form of basic support that includes software, hardware, and training: General Computing Support. Thus, the final instrument has the following four factors:

1. General Computing Support;

2. Purchasing Support;

3. Data Support; and

4. Functional Support.

The emergence of factors 2, 3 and 4 as proposed indicates the careful selection of items. Since these factors emerged based on the data collected from responses from three different sources, these factors are generalizable. It should be noted that factors 2, 3, and 4 were present in Mirani and King's instrument for IC only. This consistency adds strength to the general validity of the instrument. The high correlations among the items of the support instrument and the high values of Cronbach's alpha for the originally proposed factors indicate that the convergent validity and scale reliability of the instrument are strong.

This empirical study is subject to certain limitations. The number of responses for EUC relying on friends/colleagues was low (25 of 108 respondents). A higher response rate would have been more desirable. Further studies should be conducted to retest the validity of the instrument with alternative support sources and different types of end-users (other than middle managers). The level of support did not differ significantly (Table 11) across support sources except for data support by the IC, which was higher and significantly different from the data support by friends/colleagues (of course, IC staff members probably have more knowledge of corporate databases and data transfer techniques). Insignificant differences in level of support may have been due to the method of data collection. The sampling method only allowed a between-subjects rather than a within-subject comparison. If each respondent had completed the instrument twice for two different support sources, a clear distinction between the levels of support could have been obtained. Despite these limitations, the results of the survey support and extend prior research findings.

## 8. Conclusions

The widespread presence of local MIS staff may be an indication of an emerging trend in EUC support. While the rating of the support provided by local MIS staff was not significantly higher than other sources, the values for local MIS staff support was higher than the other two sources. The instrument developed to measure EUC support was found to have adequate construct validity and convergent validity. Though the instrument was designed to measure support from only three support sources, it may be extended to measure support from other support sources, such as User groups and Vendors.

Organizations regularly face decisions about EUC support in terms of the desired size or continuation of the IC, the level of support provided by the informal network, and the placement of local MIS staff. Also, IS researchers in EUC regularly face the difficulty of collecting information regarding the nature and degree of end-user support and its impact on effectiveness. The instrument and factor structure developed here should help both practitioners and researchers address issues associated with the management of EUC.
