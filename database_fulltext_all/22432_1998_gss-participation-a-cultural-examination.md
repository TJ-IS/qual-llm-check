# GSS participation: A cultural examination

Barry P. Robichaux $^{a}$ , Randolph B. Cooper $^{b,*}$

$^{a}$ Holland and Davis, 3355 West Alabama, Suite 1050, Houston, TX 77098-1718, USA $^{b}$ University of Houston, Decision and Information Sciences, Houston, TX 77024-6282, USA

Received 20 September 1997; revised 13 November 1997; accepted 2 January 1998

## Abstract

It has been suggested that group support systems (GSS) reduce many meeting participation barriers. GSS impact may interact with culture, thereby affecting GSS efficacy in a given context. However, since the majority of GSS research is based on studies in the United States, implications of this interaction for different cultural settings are not obvious.

A research model is developed to help identify effects of cultural settings. Hofstede's cultural dimensions of power distance, individualism–collectivism, and masculinity–femininity are employed as example settings. Parallel and anonymous entry are employed as example GSS functionalities. The research model's usefulness is then illustrated by generating research propositions focusing on interactions of these settings and functionalities. Further examples employing other cultural settings and GSS functionalities are then suggested as evidence that the model is robust as well as useful. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: GSS; GDSS; Group support; Culture; Participation barriers; Power distance; Individualism; Collectivism; Masculinity; Femininity

## 1. Introduction

Solving unstructured problems often requires face-to-face meetings, which can increase decision quality by introducing diverse and useful perspectives, promoting consideration of several aspects, and correcting participants' errors $[1, 10, 59]$ . The participation of group members provides a free flow of ideas and comments and helps in achieving increased decision quality. Although face-to-face meetings can increase participation, barriers that decrease participation (process losses: $[48]$ ) also exist. For example, if group members must share speaking time, they may have to wait for a turn to address the group. This can lead members to forget ideas or not listen to others $[16]$ , which can decrease decision quality by reducing the number of relevant ideas.

Level 1 group support systems (GSS) [15], which combine computer, communication, and decision technologies to support problem formulation and solution in group settings, have been said to reduce many participation barriers [35]. Group participation is also affected by social norms, which may differ across cultural settings. Therefore, the ability of GSS to reduce participation barriers may depend on culture in a given context. This interaction becomes more important with the increasing globalization of markets, which provides greater opportunities for the diffusion of technologies across cultures [33, 50]. Different cultures require different kinds of information [55], process information differently [54], and have different degrees of satisfaction with information systems [27]. Therefore, there may be culturally-based resistance and consequent technology failure [9, 40]. This paper develops a research model that can highlight potential problems when diffusing GSS across cultures.

Since the majority of GSS research is based on studies in the United States, the impacts of culture on GSS are not yet clear $[34]$ . As stated by Tan, et al. $[52]$ , “existing IS theories and research findings need to be examined to assess their cultural robustness. IS researchers need to address a fundamental issue: Can knowledge gained in a North American cultural setting be applied in other cultural settings?” They further suggest that examining the relationship between culture and GSS is especially important, since the intent of a GSS is to change group behavior, which is a reflection of culture. The model in this paper is an important step in focusing empirical GSS research on a well recognized, though neglected, domain.

The majority of GSS research has focused on divergent tasks, such as idea generation, where group members can work with relative independence. In order to take advantage of the depth of understanding provided by this research, we also focus on idea generation.

For our purposes, group members participate when they distribute and also when they receive ideas. Through the features of parallel entry, anonymous entry, and group memory, GSS is expected to increase participation by reducing common barriers (such as shyness, laziness, low status, etc.). Parallel entry allows group members to enter ideas simultaneously: there is no need to take turns. GSS typically provides each member with a computer keyboard to enter comments, which are stored in data files (the group memory) that is available to all. Anonymous entry enables ideas to be exchanged without identifying the contributors. Although anonymity is typical, some GSS allow users to attach their name to their comments (e.g., GroupSystems: [19]). Members can also make oral contributions that forfeit their anonymity.

## 2. Technology acceptance model for GSS

Our research model reflects a Western cultural bias, focusing on an individual's decisions to participate based on cognitive evaluations of his or her beliefs and perceptions. Employing this approach to study cross-cultural phenomena can be questioned because, for example emotion rather than rational reasoning can predominate in some cultures. However, as a first approximation, we assume that typically Western research notions concerning (1) rational (or boundedly rational) decision making, (2) important GSS behaviors (such as participation), and (3) important relationships among GSS attributes are applicable across cultures.

GSS participation is an individual-level behavior which can be examined in terms of the technology acceptance model (TAM) [13]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, an individual's attitude and intention toward using IS can be affected by perceived ease of use and usefulness. Individual group members will participate in GSS meetings to the degree that they find it easy to use and to the degree that they believe participating to be useful. The application of TAM to GSS, illustrated in Fig. 2, focuses on the determinants of ease of use and usefulness, which are external variables in TAM.

## 2.1. Determinants of the ease of participating

Perceived ease of use is affected by accessibility (convenience, unrestrictedness, unobstructedness: [51]) of the technology and the cognitive, social, and behavioral skills [3] required to use it. The less accessible the GSS, or the more required skills one lacks, the more difficult it is to participate.

## 2.1.1. Accessibility

Personal factors, such as abilities, affect information channel access. For example, in face-to-face settings, this may require shouting or pounding on a desk to gain and hold the attention of the group. An information channel may be dominated by members possessing superior contention skills at the expense of others. This has been called Time Domination [16]

Status and power relationships and behavioral norms operating within the group can also influence accessibility [45]. For example, a person's status (prestige or importance) or power (ability to reward or punish) may lead others to contend less. Similarly, norms may dictate that less experienced members defer to more experienced members, thereby making accessibility a function of experience. Cohesiveness may serve to reinforce such norms, which also results in time domination.

Parallel entry reduces time domination by eliminating the ability of any member to restrict the participation of others. Anonymous entry reduces time domination by superiors, since it makes it difficult to identify the contributor.

## 2.1.2. Cognitive, social, and behavioral skills

Cognitive, social, and behavioral group participation skills are affected by personal factors, such as an individual's ability to use a given media. This includes motor skills, such as the ability to speak, write, or type, and expression skills, such as the ability to communicate persuasively. For example, when a group must select one of many competing alternatives, advocates of one may need to persuade, not merely inform, others of its benefits. This may require eloquence. Though not typically noted as a face-to-face participation barrier, this Media Incompatibility influence has been noted as a potential barrier [35]. For example, older users may find it difficult to express ideas using a keyboard.

## 2.2. Determinants of the usefulness of participating

Perceived usefulness generally results from a mental ad hoc cost/benefit analysis of the value of using a technology [12, 14]. The less consistent GSS participation is with individuals' goals, the less useful it is to participate. Personal outcome goals include an individual's need for recognition and advancement as well as for reducing negative feedback. Task outcome goals relate to the decision being made, such as organizational efficiency. Group outcome goals relate to group maintenance concerns, such as conflict reduction to increase group cohesion. Five goal-oriented issues can affect the value of participation [16, 45]:

\- Indifference free riding occurs when a member believes there is no personal value in participating, and thus does not participate.

\- Dispensive free riding occurs when a member believes that other members have the same or better ideas and therefore does not participate.

\- Evaluation apprehension occurs when a member who wants to avoid negative evaluation does not participate.

\- Conformance pressure occurs when a member who wants to avoid punishment from powerful members does not participate.

\- Conflict avoidance occurs when a member does not participate in order to avoid conflict among members, and thereby enhance group maintenance.

Group structure variables can affect the perceived usefulness of participating. For example, (1) values emphasizing group goals over personal goals may reduce indifference free riding; (2) values which relate status to knowledge may affect dispensive free riding; (3) values emphasizing status or power differences among members may increase evaluation apprehension and conformance pressure; and (4) norms may dictate that critical remarks should not be made, thereby increasing conflict avoidance.

Parallel entry may increase indifference free riding by reducing the ability to be assertive, thereby reducing the benefits of participating for some people. Parallel entry may also increase dispensive free riding by increasing the likelihood that a member would view his or her own ideas as dispensable because more members are able to contribute at one time.

Indifference free riding may increase under anonymity since, for example, the potential for personal credit is removed. Anonymous entry may reduce evaluation apprehension and conformance pressure, since members are disassociated from their ideas thus eliminating the potential for criticism by others.

Anonymity may also reduce conflict avoidance since ideas, not senders, are criticized [4].

## 3. Adding cultural context to the research model

Culture can be thought of as “the collective programming of the mind which distinguishes the members of one human group from another” [20]. Many types of culture affect an individual; this paper focuses on societal culture because of its resistance to change [21] and its systematic control in most GSS research results, which employ subjects from North American firms and universities [52]. Societal culture consists of norms, values, etc. resulting from socialization during the early years of an individual’s life.

Culture can be described in terms of language, ritual, belief, and ideology that serve to codify meaning, instill normative patterns, and promote purpose $[37]$ . There are many different frameworks that have been suggested as constituting important cultural dimensions. Nevertheless, as Kumar et al. $[29]$ note, the following are significant theoretical and empirical reasons to employ the framework proposed by Hofstede. It:

\- resulted from research exploring thousands of respondents in over sixty countries [26],

\- has been validated by further theoretical and empirical evidence (e.g., [22, 41, 44]),

\- consists of dimensions that are based on value orientations considered important and shared across cultures [5], and

\- has been used successfully by researchers in a variety of disciplines (e.g., [26, 28, 47, 56]).

In addition, Hofstede's framework is being increasingly employed in information systems research (e.g., [50, 58]).

Hofstede [21] identified five cultural dimensions: power distance, individualism–collectivism, masculinity–femininity, uncertainty avoidance, and Confucian dynamism. We employ Hofstede's power distance, individualism–collectivism, and masculinity–femininity dimensions as examples of how our model can be used to derive cultural-GSS research propositions.

## 3.1. Power distance and GSS participation

Power distance is a measure of the extent to which the less powerful members within a culture expect and accept that power is distributed unequally. Within high-power distance cultures, superiors and subordinates consider each other as existentially unequal. Superiors are entitled to privileges, and contacts between superiors and subordinates are to be initiated only by superiors. Subordinates expect to be told what to do. The ideal boss in the subordinates' eyes is a benevolent autocrat or 'good father'. Relationships between subordinates and superiors are frequently loaded with emotions (adored or despised with equal intensity). There is no assumption of redress for victims of power abuse. Higher distances are found in many Latin (e.g., in France, Spain, Latin America), Asian, and African groups.

Within low-power distance cultures, subordinates and superiors consider each other as existentially equal. Organizations tend to be decentralized with flat hierarchies. Superiors are accessible to subordinates, and the ideal boss is a resourceful (and therefore respected) democrat. Subordinates expect to be consulted before a decision is made that affects their work, but they accept the boss as the one who finally decides. Status symbols are suspect. Lower distances are found for many groups in the United States and Great Britain and its former empire.

## 3.1.1. Parallel entry

The ability of parallel entry to reduce time domination due to power differences is expected to increase from low- to high-power distance cultures. High-power members of high-power distance cultures may dominate speaking time in meetings, reducing the degree to which low-power members participate. Parallel entry reduces the ability of members to prevent others from participating.

P1: Within high-power distance cultures, parallel entry will reduce time domination by high-power members; this will increase low-power members' participation.

Social power can be derived from many sources, including attraction, reward, coercive, legitimate, and expertise $[17]$ . As noted by Shaw $[45]$ , “the powerful group member is seen as more likable than the less powerful group member, is treated deferentially, is the target of a disproportionate share of intragroup communications, and others are more likely to comply with his or her wishes”. High-power members have been found to communicate more frequently and receive more communication than low-power members (e.g., $[24, 30]$ ).

This proposition is based on the assumptions that (1) given the opportunity, members will freely participate (they will not withhold ideas) and (2) members are willing and able to type ideas. Failure to meet the first condition is expected to increase from low- to high-power distance cultures, since low-power members of high-power distance cultures typically do not expect to be involved in decisions. They may therefore defer to high-power members.

P1a: Within high-power distance cultures, dispensive free riding by low-power members will limit the ability of parallel entry to increase their participation.

Collaros and Anderson [6] manipulated expert power and found dispensive free riding to be higher with greater perceived expertise of other members in the group. Individual productivity was highest when no other members were believed to be experts and lowest when all others were believed to be experts.

Failure to meet the second assumption is also expected to increase from low- to high-power distance cultures, where high-power members typically believe typing to be low status clerical work (e.g., [31]) and thus may be relatively unwilling and less able to type.

P1b: Within high-power distance cultures, parallel entry will increase media incompatibility for high-power members; this will decrease their participation.

Proposition P1b is related to skills expected for powerful members of high-power distance groups. Lyytinen, et al. [31, 32] have found that different backgrounds and skills of GSS users interferes with their participation. Individuals may shun activities that are inappropriate given their power and status in a group.

## 3.1.2. Anonymous entry

The ability of anonymous entry to reduce time domination, evaluation apprehension, and conformance pressure is expected to increase from low- to high-power distance cultures. High-power members of high-power distance cultures may use their power to discourage others from participating. Also, low-power members typically feel their ideas are less important or informed than those of high-power members, leading them to withhold ideas for fear of criticism or punishment. Anonymous entry reduces the ability to determine the sender of an idea and thus the sender's power.

P2: Within high-power distance cultures, anonymous entry will reduce time domination by high-power members and evaluation apprehension and conformance pressure of low-power members; this will increase low-power members' participation.

High-power members gain compliance of low-power members when they control the ability to administer rewards or punishments. This desire to comply causes evaluation apprehension and conformance pressure in low-power members. Since anonymous entry reduces the ability to reward or punish behavior, proposition P2 reflects expectations that feelings such as evaluation apprehension and conformance pressure will also diminish.

This proposition assumes that members are willing to participate anonymously. Failure to meet this condition is expected to increase from low to high-power distance cultures, since low-power members of high-power distance cultures are typically not involved in decisions. Therefore, they may defer to the views of high-power members.

P2a: Within high-power distance cultures, dispensive free riding by low-power members will limit the ability of anonymous entry to increase their participation.

Collaros and Anderson [6] manipulated the level of expert power by stating how many experts were in the group. The perceived presence an expert was sufficient to limit contributions of group members.

## 3.2. Individualism–collectivism and GSS participation

Individualism and collectivism depends on the difference between the interests of the individual and the group. Within individualist cultures, ties between individuals are loose: everyone is expected to look after him or herself and his or her immediate family. Speaking one's mind is a virtue and telling the truth about how one feels is the characteristic of a sincere and honest person. A difference of opinions is believed to lead to a higher truth, and coping with conflict is normal. Many groups in the United States, Australia, and Great Britain tend to be individualist.

Within collectivist cultures, people are integrated into strong, cohesive in-groups which continue to protect them in exchange for unquestioning loyalty. Intense and continuous social contact in collective groups result in a focus on harmony. Direct confrontation is rude and undesirable. Personal opinions do not exist; they are predetermined by the group. Many groups in Guatemala, Ecuador, and Panama tend to be collectivist.

## 3.2.1. Parallel entry

The ability of parallel entry to reduce time domination due to in-group membership is expected to increase from individualist-to-collectivist cultures. In-groups in collectivist cultures may dominate speaking time in meetings. Parallel entry reduces the ability of members to prevent others from participating. Such in-groups are less likely within individualist cultures.

P3: Within collectivist cultures, parallel entry will reduce time domination; this will increase non-members' participation.

Collectivist in-groups can be viewed as coalitions within a larger group. In-group members act jointly to affect the outcomes of the entire group, sharing and controlling resources (such as the opportunity to address the group) and improving the results for each member over that which could have been obtained individually [53].

This proposition assumes that, given the opportunity, members will freely participate. Failure to meet this condition is expected to increase from individualist-to-collectivist cultures, whose members may withhold ideas that oppose those of others to help promote group maintenance.

P3a: Within collectivist cultures, conflict avoidance will limit the ability of parallel entry to increase participation.

In-groups are cohesive: members are attracted to, and coordinate efforts on behalf of, the group. They are characterized by friendliness, cooperation, and pressures toward uniformity $[45]$ .

## 3.2.2. Anonymous entry

The ability of anonymous entry to reduce time domination due to in-group membership and conflict avoidance due to group maintenance concerns is expected to increase from individualist-to-collectivist cultures. In-group membership may be used as a basis for allowing members to participate within collectivist cultures. Anonymous entry reduces the ability to determine the sender of an idea and thereby in-group membership. In addition, members may withhold comments or ideas that oppose those of others to promote group maintenance. Anonymous entry reduces the possibility of feeling threatened by disassociating ideas from contributors.

P4: Within collectivist cultures, anonymous entry will reduce (a) time domination and thereby increase non-members' participation, and (b) conflict avoidance and thereby increase overall participation.

A coalition unable to control necessary resources (such as meeting time) is less able to influence the group. Criticism of ideas from unidentified senders is often considered to be constructive rather than personal [4, 7]. This reduces the likelihood of conflict avoidance.

This proposition assumes that members are willing to participate anonymously. Since anonymity reduces the potential for individual rewards and recognition, it may lead members of individualist cultures to withhold ideas.

P4a: Within individualist cultures, anonymous entry will increase indifference free riding; this will decrease participation.

The consequences of group interaction can be described in terms of rewards and costs associated with outcomes, and group participation extracts some cost. Members who do not anticipate a reward to compensate for this cost will refrain from participating.

## 3.3. Masculinity–femininity and GSS participation

Masculinity pertains to cultures in which members' values include being assertive, tough, and focused on material success. From this perspective, the humanization of work refers to job enrichment that provides opportunities for individual recognition, advancement, and challenge. Masculine management hero types are assertive, decisive, and aggressive. Many groups in Japan, Austria, and Venezuela tend to be more masculine.

Femininity refers to cultures where members tend to be more modest, tender, and concerned with the quality of life. Being non-ambitious and average (rather than best) is valued. Compared to masculine cultures, assertiveness and the need for individual recognition are less likely and concern for group maintenance is more likely. The humanization of work should provide more opportunities for mutual help and social contacts. Feminine management hero types are less visible, intuitive rather than decisive, and seek consensus. Groups in Sweden, Norway, and the Netherlands tend to be more feminine.

## 3.3.1. Parallel entry

The ability of parallel entry to reduce time domination and free riding due to assertiveness differences is expected to increase from feminine-to-masculine cultures. Greater assertiveness may lead to the domination of speaking time in meetings in masculine cultures. Parallel entry reduces the ability of members to prevent others from participating.

P5: Within masculine cultures, parallel entry will reduce time domination; this will increase participation.

This proposition assumes that members are willing and able to type. Failure to meet this condition is expected to increase from feminine-to-masculine cultures, since members may find that typing reduces the ability to be assertive due to the exclusion of non-verbal cues (body language, raised voice, etc.) and interruptions. This may reduce their incentives and thus reduce their participation.

P5a: Within masculine cultures, parallel entry will increase indifference free riding; this will decrease participation.

Typewritten media conveys less social cues [10], making it more difficult to be assertive and aggressive.

## 3.3.2. Anonymous entry

The ability of anonymous entry to reduce conflict avoidance due to group maintenance concerns is expected to increase from masculine-to-feminine cultures. Anonymous entry reduces the potential for conflict by disassociating ideas from individuals, and thereby reducing the possibility that individuals will feel personally threatened in feminine cultures.

P6: Within feminine cultures, anonymous entry will reduce conflict avoidance; this will increase participation.

Criticism of ideas from unidentified senders is often considered to be constructive rather than personal $[4]$ . Thus anonymity will lead to further critical analysis of ideas (less conflict avoidance) in groups in feminine cultures.

This proposition assumes that members are willing to participate anonymously. Failure to meet this assumption is not expected within feminine cultures. However, since anonymity reduces the potential for individual rewards and recognition it may lead members of masculine cultures to withhold ideas.

P6a: Within masculine cultures, anonymous entry will increase indifference free riding; this will decrease participation.

This proposition reflects the removal of benefits for members of masculine cultures and identifies their expected action in light of an anticipated negative outcome (not participating).

## 4. The propositions and the GSS literature

GSS research which has not specifically addressed culture is now re-examined in light of the propositions. This is followed by a review of the GSS research that has highlighted culture as an important variable.

## 4.1. Non-cultural GSS research

GSS research performed in North America typically does not examine the impact of culture, and has determined that GSS groups have more participation, more egalitarian participation, and are less inclined toward domination by a few members $[19, 34, 57]$ . However, these results are taken largely from studies employing subjective recall by GSS members. GSS effects on participation are less consistent in studies employing objective measures. GSS has been found to (1) lead to more solution alternatives [18] and have no impact on the number of solution alternatives [25, 39] and (2) lead to more equal participation [19, 46] and have no effect on the equality of participation [25, 39].

In a meta-analysis, Pinsonneault and Kraemer [38] suggest that the ability of GSS to influence participation may depend on established patterns of communication and power within a group. If no such patterns exist, GSS may help prevent their emergence; if such patterns exist, GSS may be unable to overcome them. This agrees with our propositions surrounding power distance. Propositions P1a and P2a indicate the potential inability of GSS to affect participation when power distances are large and result in significant patterns of communication and power.

Jarvenpaa, et al. [25] studied the impact of GSS on participation of software designers. Groups were formed and members with leadership experience were assigned to be leader for each group; in no case was the leader's authority challenged. This created status differences (leaders and non-leaders) which can inhibit participation and which GSS may be able to overcome. However, GSS use did not lead to improved participation. This result can be explained in light of culture. Based on propositions P1 and P2, GSS will be effective in overcoming status differences in high-power distance cultures while having little impact in lower power distance cultures. Since the Jarvenpaa, et al. study occurred in the United States, and since United States groups typically exhibit low-power distance, the lack of GSS efficacy is reasonable.

In describing their (primarily United States) research, Nunamaker, et al. [36] state that “one can easily envision situations in which the participants would be reluctant to suggest ideas without being recognized as the author.” Since the United States culture tends to be individualistic and masculine, propositions P4a and P6a are in accord with this view: members in individualistic and/or masculine cultures may not participate due to a lack of individual recognition.

## 4.2. Cultural GSS research

Watson and his colleagues [58] investigated the impact of culture on the change in consensus resulting from GSS use. Singaporean groups were found to have higher pre-meeting consensus and less change in consensus than United States groups. This is consistent from a cultural perspective because: Singaporean groups are more likely to be collectivists than United States groups and collectivists more highly value and strive for consensus independent of GSS use. Therefore, the ability of GSS to increase consensus will be diminished when employed by collectivists. Similarly, Mejias, et al. [33] found that non-GSS groups obtained higher consensus than GSS-supported groups in Mexico, though this was not the case in the United States. This finding is reasonable given the more collectivist culture of Mexico. Though these works are consistent with our cultural view, they are not in accord with any of our propositions because we do not address consensus.

Studies focusing only on Singaporean groups $[23, 52]$ found that GSS use resulted in a decreased impact of status and normative influences on decision making. This is consistent with a cultural perspective since Singaporean groups tend to have high-power distance. As indicated by proposition P1, GSS can overcome these influences in high-power distance cultures.

In a non-GSS study, Straub [50] examined the Japanese use of e-mail and fax. Compared to fax use, Straub found e-mail use in Japan to be very low. Straub suggests that this finding could be due to the Japanese tendency toward uncertainty avoidance (a Hofstede cultural dimension not considered here). This leads them to favor richer communication media which can reduce uncertainty and ambiguity (fax is richer – it can easily incorporate pictures, special characters, etc.). Though these communications media are not GSS, e-mail has some important similarities to GSS: it requires computer keyboard input, allows parallel entry, and can include anonymous entry. This study may therefore have important implications for GSS in Japan.

## 5. Concluding remarks

Though GSS can improve the quality of decision making in North American groups, there have been few tests of its ability in other cultural settings. Based on the TAM, three cultural dimensions suggested by Hofstede were employed to generate research propositions. We found that the level at which a group lies on

Hofstede's power distance, individualism–collectivism, and masculine–feminine cultural dimensions can significantly impact the ability of GSS to increase participation.

Due to the Western bias of TAM, the propositions generated should be viewed as highly tentative, and regarded as opportunities for further theoretical and empirical investigation. In addition, there are other important behaviors that can impact GSS effectiveness and therefore should be examined in light of culture. For example, low- and high-power distance individuals may generate an equal amount of messages (equal participation), but the low-power individuals may alter their messages' content.

Though tentative, it is interesting to speculate on potential benefits that can be derived if these propositions are supported by future investigations. To illustrate this potential for managers, we consider two GSS diffusion scenarios. A manager transferring from the United States to Mexico may be tempted to transfer GSS use as well. However, as verified by Stephens and Greer [49], these cultures differ more than they resemble each other. On Hofstede's scales which rank various countries and regions, Mexico exhibits high-power distance and collectivism. In contrast, the United States exhibits low-power distance and high individualism. The two countries are close in terms of masculinity. Propositions P1, P2, P3, and P4 suggest that the high-power distance and collectivist nature of Mexican culture may increase GSS efficacy, largely due to the reduction in time domination. This increase may be partially offset, however, by dispensive free riding (P1a and P2a), media incompatibility (P1b), and conflict avoidance (P3a). A challenge to managers implementing GSS in Mexican cultures is to reduce these 'offsets' to further increase GSS ability to increase participation. For example, if ideas are desired from low-power members specifically, meetings of only low-power members could be held to reduce dispensive free riding. Since media incompatibility may prevent high-power members from typing ideas, managers could provide typists or other kinds of data entry to enter and send their ideas. Since members may withhold comments or ideas that oppose those of others to avoid conflict, managers could have members initially generate but not exchange ideas. This may reduce the knowledge that members have of one another's ideas and thus reduce instances of their withholding ideas that they feel are in conflict.

Guatemala is even more dissimilar to the United States than Mexico. Guatemala exhibits very high-power distance, is very collective, and very feminine. A manager considering diffusing GSS into this culture would accrue most of the benefits and challenges associated with GSS use in Mexico. In addition, due to the reduction in conflict avoidance in feminine cultures (P6), GSS has the potential to be even more effective than in Mexico or the United States.

Our application of TAM provides researchers with guidance for determining potentially fruitful avenues of research regarding the impact of culture on GSS participation. The model's value does not lie merely in its ability to generate propositions based on three of Hofstede's cultural dimensions. Rather, the model can help develop research propositions based on a wide variety of cultural influences, as they affect TAM components. For example, hedonism, a societal culture dimension offered by Schwartz et al. [42, 43], suggests that personal motives include pleasure and enjoying life. GSS would therefore be perceived as more useful if they fulfill the personal goal of having fun during the GSS process. In addition, avoidance, an organizational culture dimension offered by Cook and Rousseau [8], suggests that group norms focusing on punishment rather than rewards may lead to personal goals that make participation less useful. GSS anonymity can reduce this negative influence on participation.

This paper offered propositions based on an assumption that individuals of a single culture were employing GSS. Also of importance with the growth of multinational corporations, is the impact of GSS when participants represent multiple cultures $[2, 11]$ . There are also other aspects of culture that can be important in affecting GSS participation. Such aspects can be captured by our model: cultural differences in terms of language affect the interaction between personal abilities and the supporting technologies, which result in a differentiation of behavioral skills and lead to differences in the ease with which participation in communication can occur.

Though the demonstration of our model focused on two technical support factors (parallel entry and anonymity), the model is not limited to these. The key is to examine the technical support functionality of a GSS and relate it to potential interactions with personal and group factors that can lead to differences in accessibility, cognitive, social, and behavioral skills, and personal, task and group outcome goals. For example, restricted keyboard character sets can interact with Japanese language requirements [50] thereby reducing communication accessibility and participation.

In summary, though our application of TAM-to-GSS participation research model has a decidedly Western bias, it has allowed us to generate potentially useful research propositions based on the effect of culture on personal and group factors and their interaction with technical support functions provided by various types of GSS.
