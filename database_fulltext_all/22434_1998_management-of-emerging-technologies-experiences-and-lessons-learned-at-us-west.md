# Management of emerging technologies: Experiences and lessons learned at US West

Anol Bhattacherjee\*

Information Systems, College of Business and Administration, University of Colorado at Denver, Campus Box 165, P.O. Box 173364, Denver, CO 80217-3364, USA

Received 5 April 1997; accepted 9 March 1998

## Abstract

Successful management of emerging technologies is critical to the survival of business organizations today. However, given the risky and often unproven nature of these technologies, considerable confusion exists on how to manage their implementation. This paper recounts the experiences of US West Communications with their intranet project, and uses it to understand problems faced in implementing emerging technologies and ways of overcoming such problems. By providing a longitudinal view of the company's intranet experience over a period of 3 years, it identifies key organizational factors that facilitated or impeded acceptance of this emerging technology. Lessons learned at US West are used to recommend guidelines that can help practitioners implement emerging technologies in their organizations more effectively. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Technology diffusion; Management of technology; IS implementation; IS management; Emerging technologies; Intranet

## 1. Introduction

Business organizations are currently faced with more dramatic changes in their business environment than ever before $[1, 3, 5]$ . This change is manifested in constantly changing customer needs, changing work patterns, and increasing global competition. Many organizations are increasingly turning to emerging information technologies (IT) as a means of coping with this change $[3]$ . However, such technologies are often unknown, unproven, and risky, and are therefore difficult to manage. IT organizations are faced with the task of not only identifying relevant emerging technologies, but also developing their organizational awareness and motivating users to utilize them. This study analyzes 3 years of experience with the Global Village (intranet) project at US West Communications (a major North American telephone service provider), with the goal of understanding organizational elements impacting the adoption of emerging technologies. The observations are used to suggest managerial strategies that can help manage these technologies more effectively.

The Global Village project at US West was started in early 1994 on an experimental basis with three employees and a modest budget; it was intended to explore the potential of web-based technologies to generate new products or services for the company. By the end of 1997, the project had captured the attention of over 40 000 US West employees scattered across 14 US states and was widely recognized as a means of reducing the company's costs of internal operations and improving customer service.

The experience at US West suggests that a carefully designed, structured approach aids in managing the implementation of emerging technologies; this should address such issues as developing a technology vision, conducting pilot projects, leveraging existing resources, sheltering the project from potential cost-reduction initiatives, adopting a grassroots strategy for diffusing interest about the technology, and fostering user involvement and ownership in the technology. While some of these strategies has been suggested in prior IT implementation literature (e.g., [2]), the US West example demonstrates how these strategies can be orchestrated within the overall context of an emerging technology (the intranet) that has generated considerable interest among business organizations in recent years.

## 2. The Global Village project

Founded on January 1, 1984 as a result of AT&T's divestiture into seven Regional Bell Operating Companies, US West is a leading provider of telecommunication services within the United States and worldwide. In its starting year, the company had \$16 billion in assets, \$7 billion in annual revenue, and 73000 employees. By the end of 1997, assets increased to \$23 billion, revenue expanded to \$11.7 billion, and number of employees decreased to 61500.

Headquartered in Englewood, Colorado, US West's operations are currently managed by two major groups: US West Communications, which provides basic telephone services to 25 million customers, long distance services within specified calling areas, and high-speed networking equipment and services for business customers and government agencies in 14 western and midwestern states within the continental US, and US West Media Group, which operates the company's telephony, wireless, cable, directory, and information services in 17 countries worldwide. The two groups were formally separated on October 31, 1995, when two separate classes of common stock started trading on the New York Stock Exchange.

In early 1994, US West Communications became one of the pioneers of internal company-owned computer networks with the initiation of its Global Village project, well before the term ‘intranet’ was coined as a popular term. The project started on an experimental basis, when the Finance organization within US West was exploring ways to make their billing system more flexible to accommodate new products and services. Margaret Tumey, Vice President of Finance operations, hired Sherman Woo as a ‘technological visionary,’ to help her identify potential new, technology-based products and services that this billing system should accommodate in future. The subject of Internet was frequently raised in brainstorming sessions, and the idea of testing Internet-based technologies on US West’s internal network emerged.

Based on extensive project planning, Woo requested \$4 million and 40 employees for the project, but the request was turned down by the management as being extremely expensive for a thought experiment. The resulting project team consisted of a small budget and only three employees, Sherman Woo, Patricia Hursh, and Suzanne Mullison, all of whom were assigned to the strategies group within the Finance organization.

By initiating a move order within the company, Woo acquired an underutilized SUN 490 server with 4 MB of memory and purchased three Macintosh computers and three Infocus projection televisions for approximately \$30 000. US West's existing 10-year old, SNA-based backbone network, called USWnet, was reconfigured to carry TCP/IP-based intranet traffic on top of the company's existing SNA-based data and application traffic. Woo set up the SUN server and transformed a conference room into a multimedia facility to demonstrate intranet possibilities to interested individuals within the company.

Once established, the demonstration room became the cornerstone for a grassroots effort to build company-wide interest in intranet technology. Woo gave tours of the Global Village and the browser software to any interested employee. This tour included one internal web site, the external Internet, and a computerized demonstration of potential business applications that could run on the intranet. Each employee who attended a tour or received the browser software was asked to show at least two colleagues how the web worked. This grassroots effort, coupled with increasing awareness of web technologies, generated strong interest in the project among US West employees.

Since its inception, the Global Village has been redesigned three times to serve the information and communication needs of US West employees better and more effectively. The last of these revisions was completed in September 1996, when US West licensed the entire line of Netscape's client and server software for Global Village users. Netscape's SuiteSpot server was chosen as the software platform for creating, managing, and distributing web applications and 50 000 copies of Netscape Navigator client software were licensed to serve as a common user interface across the company. Thirteen different electronic mail systems existing within US West prior to this time were consolidated into two systems. Employees with specific business reasons to use Lotus Notes were allowed to continue using its e-mail application, while all other employees were asked to migrate to Netscape's NetMail.

US West Communications employees could access the Global Village from any corporate office within the company's 14-state operating region by entering the appropriate uniform resource locator address (http://www.gv.usWest.com) in their web browser. This displayed the Global Village home page on the users' screen and provided hyperlinks to various internal corporate sites or even the external Internet. Four types of activities were supported, as shown in Table 1.

## 2.1. Information sharing

Individual employees, groups, and departments could create web sites to share personal and professional information with the company. A directory of internal web sites was maintained with information about each department and projects for interested employees to locate information about specific projects. Contact information (e.g., telephone number and e-mail address) of all US West employees could be retrieved directly from an online employee directory. The Headline News section on the home page provided links to corporate news and information about US West and the telecommunications industry (this was updated daily by the Public Relations organization), while employee-specific information, such as changes in company benefit plans, daily news advisories, internal job openings, and departmental announcements could be obtained from the employees news network (ENN). Employees also had access to US West Media Group's home page and the public Internet, in accordance with company policy.

## 2.2. Communication and collaboration

A combination of e-mail, workflow software, and bulletin board systems (BBS) were used to facilitate communication and collaboration among employees and project teams. Lotus Notes was the primary workflow software used for collaborative projects, while Notes and NetMail were the two e-mail systems. Rumor Mill, a BBS managed by the IT organization, allowed individual employees to voice their questions and concerns by name or anonymously on the intranet; corporate executives normally responded to these within a few days. In addition, a Web Developers Coalition home page served as a forum to help individuals, groups, and departments build and maintain web-based applications, exchange ideas, and register for an internal listserver of web-related issues.

## 2.3. Employee transactions

These were interactive processes that allow employees to request specific information (e.g., benefits, project status) using on-line forms. These forms authenticated the requester, queried appropriate databases, and displayed the response on the users' screen. For example, the Human Resources department maintained an online benefits form, where US West employees could enter a life situation they were approaching (e.g., getting married, having a baby, or planning for retirement); this form then queried a life situation database and displayed all corresponding changes in benefits, taxes, and so forth, along with direct links to online forms that needed to be completed and submitted to appropriate departments. Employees whose paycheck were electronically deposited to their bank accounts could use a paystub application to receive it via e-mail instead of receiving a paper copy mailed to their home address. These paystubs were stored electronically and could be easily retrieved when required (e.g., during preparation of tax returns).

## 2.4. Business computing

Core business applications interfacing with US West's legacy applications (e.g., payroll, time reporting, billing, and inventory management) were available using intranet technology. One such application was a facilities check application, which allowed US West service representatives to check the availability of facilities before making a service commitment to a customer. Prior to this application, customers requesting a service received commitments based on the average time for that service; these services were sometimes late because of the lack of required facilities at the promised time. The facilities check application significantly improved utilization of US West resources and reduced missed commitments and customer complaints.

In 1994, the Global Village started with only one site, designed by US West's Advanced Technologies division. The number of web sites grew rapidly as more employees became interested in the technology and started setting up personal web pages. As of late 1997, the intranet consisted of 230 sites located on web servers throughout the company's 14-state operating region, with an average of five new sites being added every week. Additional servers were also added to the network and a comprehensive security plan implemented using firewall (two-tiered proxy server) technologies.

## 2.5. Increased employee productivity

The Global Village provided US West employees with easy and convenient access to important corporate data via hierarchical directory structures and fully integrated search engines, helping them make quicker and better decisions. The electronic data was more accurate and current because the information was updated more frequently (typically, daily) than was possible with the print media. Widespread awareness and popularity of web technologies made it easier for employees to learn how to use the intranet quickly.

## 2.6. Reduced operational costs

Availability of information in electronic form led to cost savings via reduction in the processing, printing, and distribution of paper documents. For instance, the paystub application described earlier was projected to save \$300 000 annually if all US West employees participated in this program.

## 2.7. Improved customer service

The ready and timely access to up-to-date information provided by Global Village led to more effective business decision making by US West employees and hence better customer service. For example, the facilities check application helped reduce missed service commitments; and improved customer satisfaction.

## 2.8. Generated new business

By virtue of early and successful deployment of the Global Village, US West was able to leverage its knowledge and experience with intranet technologies to offer value-added consulting services to business customers. One example is !NTERACT, which offered a full range of Internet connectivity solutions for business-to-business communication, including desktop computer networking, site hosting, directory services, and integration with Lotus Notes applications.

## 2.9. Provided an exemplar on using new technologies

The Global Village project operationalized US West's philosophy of using leading edge technology to further internal operations and customer service and demonstrated how this can be accomplished. This experience can prove invaluable in managing other emerging technologies as the company prepares for a deregulated and highly competitive US telecommunications industry.

Patricia Hursh, manager of the Global Village, summarized the benefits from the intranet project as,

"At US West, we are in the information industry. Over 70 percent of our workers are knowledge workers, the only things they move around are ideas. We have to come out with an effective and inexpensive means of doing that, which is what the Global Village attempts to do. At the same time, it has been a great tool to help employees serve customers better, while reduce internal operating costs at the same time".

## 3. Enablers and barriers to intranet diffusion

For the first two-and-a-half years, use of the Global Village was mostly limited to technology enthusiasts and select user groups. However, since the final version of the Global Village was implemented in September 1996, the number of accesses to intranet servers increased dramatically from a modest 14000 to over 100000 per month. Woo explained this increase as a transitioning from early adopters to mainstream users. This diffusion pattern was typical of most innovations, which according to Rogers [6], follows an S-shaped curve, with diffusion starting out slowly among a few early adopters, followed by a ‘take-off’ as adoptions breed other adoptions, finally leveling out as the adopter population reaches saturation. In late 1997, about 75 percent of US West Communications’ 50 000 employees were using the Global Village, generating as many as 200 000 requests per month. Regular intranet access was however difficult for some employees, such as field technicians or mobile employees, with no permanent office and/or computer.

## 3.1. Technological vision

The Global Village project was initiated as part of a long-term plan intended to examine how new and emerging technologies could impact the company's product and service offerings. Following a year-long study of potential changes in customer demographics, lifestyles, marketplace, and public policy, project leader Sherman Woo concluded that the telecommunication industry was headed toward increasing globalization, digital convergence of communication, information, and entertainment services, and innovative use of new technologies. NCSA Mosaic, the first web-browser application, had been released a few months back and the idea of using the Internet as an electronic means of doing business was beginning to emerge. Woo felt that the web would be a very valuable resource for the telecommunications industry and proposed an internal web for facilitating US West's operations. This vision led to the development of the Global Village project. Woo recalled his role at the start as,

“I felt like a person who was looking at the right part of the sky and saw the comet before anyone else did. There was no intellectual magic, I simply happened to look at the right place at the right time and saw what was coming. I took a risk and proceeded to do it. Maybe I deserve credit for the fact that I acted on it rather than just observing it”.

## 3.2. Low startup costs

The initial project team consisted of three individuals with a modest budget of \$30 000. Utilization of US West's existing network, unused servers, and office space, helped reduce the initial capital costs for this project. In retrospect, Woo considered the low startup costs to be ‘fortunate’, because it helped the project escape the long and arduous process of cost justification. Being a small team with a small budget, the Global Village also did not present any threat to other organizational employee or group. The approach that was employed to building the intranet was therefore one of ‘personal evangelism’ than of a formal organizational project.

## 3.3. Management sponsor

As intranet technology was not well understood in 1994, the Global Village project failed to evoke a lot of interest among the company's senior executives. However, the project had a strong ally in Peggy Tumey, the VP of Finance. Tumey was instrumental in sponsoring the project, securing initial capital and human resources for the project, and protecting it from premature demise. According to Suzanne Mullison,

“Peggy’s enthusiasm about the project and desire to see it through was the glue that made the Global Village a reality”.

As the project evolved and captured the interests of user organizations, the senior management became more receptive toward the project. Funds were allocated to support the project, the Global Village team was formally recognized, and new job responsibilities were created to sustain the implementation effort. By 1997, the Global Village staff consisted of 10 technical support people and seven content managers, and had its own dedicated office space in Denver, Colorado.

## 3.4. Sheltering within the Finance organization

Since IT projects are difficult to cost-justify, they tend to be early targets for corporate reorganization and cost reduction initiatives. This was not however the case with the Global Village, which was sheltered and protected within a user department (the Finance organization). Many IT professionals at US West believe that had this project been under the auspices of the IT organization, it might have faced a premature demise even before it got started.

## 3.5. Grassroots effort to establish a support network

Every employee who attended Woo's intranet demonstration or received the browser software was asked to demonstrate the use of the technology to at least two colleagues. The purpose was to develop a ‘support network’ of organizational employees, who could then self-sustain subsequent diffusion of the intranet without the direct involvement of the Global Village staff. This grassroots effort was immensely successful in diffusing awareness and enthusiasm among potential users and in developing a ‘critical mass’ of users, while relieving limited IT staff for more pressing needs. As noted by Peggy Tumey,

“These efforts have provided a powerful foundation on which we can build. Sherman did a fantastic job of getting the people engaged, helping them see the potential of this technology, and spreading the word”.

## 3.6. Disciplined approach

Despite utilizing a 10-year old network and an increasing volume of accesses to Global Village servers, the intranet has not yet faced any severe capacity limitations. This was partially because of the disciplined and professional way in which the network was managed. To reduce network congestion, no large graphic, movie, or audio files were allowed on the intranet. Instead, efforts were focused on maintaining the accuracy and currency of its information content, and making it widely accessible. As stated by Woo,

“Part of that is due to the discipline we exercise in not using large graphics, movie files, and sound files. We try to keep away from all the media hype and instead focus more on the business content. I think, it is a very professional approach in managing a common facility".

## 3.7. User involvement and ownership

To stimulate user interest and involvement in the project, the Global Village organization distributed the ownership of the intranet's information content to different user organizations throughout US West. Over 2000 individuals were responsible for maintaining the accuracy and currency of the intranet's content, while the central Global Village organization managed the ‘server farm’ and ensured that the network was up and running. Woo described this shared management of the Global Village as,

“We have separated publishing from the operations of the machines. We deal with server access, disk, CPU, and so forth; content is managed by people in each organization. It is a collaborative effort, and so far it has been a pretty good collaboration. We do all of the technology acquisition, so that users naïve about the technology do not have to worry about stretching the dollar, and instead can focus on managing the content. A good thing about the web concept is that it allows distributed management, and that is exactly what we are doing. The analogy will be that of a newspaper where subscribers can write and print to, as opposed to just reading in the morning”.

Despite the above enablers, the Global Village project also faced a few barriers that limited its utilization among certain user groups. These barriers are listed in Table 4.

## 3.8. Lack of documentation

Being an experimental project with a small project team, the initial Global Village staff was more concerned with making the technology work than documenting information resources throughout the intranet. Lack of adequate documentation had somewhat inhibited the intranet's use among certain users. However, this was not a major problem, because most users had a general understanding of web technologies and were able to locate their required information via appropriate directories and search engines.

## 3.9. Lack of supported business applications

Much of the value of the Global Village project was expected to stem from web deployment of the company's legacy applications. However, as of the end of 1996, only about 10 percent of these applications had been migrated to the web platform. Some applications used data from multiple departments and therefore required resolution of data ownership and access privileges prior to deployment. In addition, the new Application Development Group, headed by Woo, was attempting to identify additional business applications that could benefit from the intranet.

## 3.10. Behavioral resistance to change

The Global Village was expected to replace all paper-based messages with e-mail and web-based communication, and was resisted by some employees used to traditional forms of messaging. To overcome this resistance, in many cases, the intranet has duplicated rather than replaced paper-based messaging. For instance, the Employee Directory is still printed and distributed yearly on paper. Expected cost savings from the project will greatly depend on change management strategies employed to overcome users' behavioral resistance toward electronic communication.

IT has clearly become a major driver for furthering organizations' strategic initiatives. However, identifying the right IT is the first step toward ensuring its effective utilization. IT managers must have a technology vision of the company over the next 5–10 years in order to match appropriate technologies to organizational goals and priorities. This will act as a lens with which to evaluate emerging technologies and provide a headstart in implementing strategic efforts. At US West, though the management were somewhat unaware of technological trends, they hired a person who could provide them with that vision. It was this vision that eventually led US West expand their service offerings (e.g., !NTERACT), reduce costs, and improve customer satisfaction.

## 4.2. Conduct a pilot project

Emerging technologies, such as intranets, are difficult to manage because many of their benefits are ‘soft’ and intangible, and their costs are often not known in advance. Furthermore, their unproven nature makes them particularly risky to implement in organizational settings. In order to mitigate risks of these technologies and to obtain more realistic estimates of their costs and benefits, it is advisable to ‘start small’ and implement them piecemeal as a series of pilot projects. Knowledge gained from these projects can be transferred to subsequent full-scale projects as and when needed. The Global Village was one pilot project intended to examine the feasibility and usability of web-based technologies in the telecommunications industry.

## 4.3. Select the right person(s) for managing the project

The outcome of any IT project depends greatly on the person managing the project. This is more so in the case of emerging technologies, where potential outcomes are unknown and there is very little prior experience to build on. This person is responsible for orchestrating the course of the project, retaining its visibility, and providing leadership to the project team. As stated by a Global Village staff member, had it not been for Woo, this project might not have seen the light of day.

## 4.4. Identify a management sponsor

It is difficult for any major IT project to survive without the direct or indirect support of senior management. The role of the management sponsor is to mobilize capital and human resources for the project, protect it from budget cuts, and involve all stakeholder groups into the project. Peggy Tumey, VP of Finance, served as the management sponsor in this project. Her interest and personal involvement was a key ingredient in the project's success.

## 4.5. Leverage existing resources to the greatest extent possible

IT projects are often blamed for being late, over-budget, and not delivering the promised outcomes. In order to reduce costs and ensure timely completion, the project team must make the best possible utilization of all existing resources both internal and external to the organization. The Global Village team utilized existing underutilized servers and US West's existing SNA-based network, to reduce the initial capital outlay of the intranet project.

## 4.6. Instill discipline in project planning

Strict discipline is required to ensure that IT projects are completed within budget and time and without disruption of any mainstream business operations. This discipline was reflected in this project by preventing the use of huge graphic, audio, and video files that could lead to a network bottleneck.

## 4.7. Shelter project from possible cost-reduction drives

IT projects are often difficult to cost-justify and are hence easy targets for organizational cost reduction initiatives. Many emerging technologies involve a direct upfront cost, while their benefits may be long-term, indirect, and unquantifiable. The Global Village project was sheltered within US West's Finance organization to prevent its premature mortality.

4.8. Adopt a grassroots strategy for diffusing interest about the technology

New technologies typically require behavioral changes on the part of potential users, and are therefore resisted [4]. Achieving the necessary cultural change is one of the most difficult challenge confronting today's IT managers. Rather than force a new IT on unwilling employees, a more effective strategy may be to employ their friends and colleagues as ‘change agents’ to diffuse interest about the IT and indirectly influence their behavior. This grassroots strategy was very successful at US West, with about 75 percent of the employees accepting the intranet within 3 years.

## 4.9. Foster user involvement and ownership at all levels

A behavioral change can be accomplished only when the potential users are knowledgeable about and actively involved in the change process. Users are generally more committed to an IT project if they perceive themselves to be ‘owners’ of the system [5]. At US West, user involvement and ownership were fostered by making user organizations responsible for the accuracy and currency of the information content on their web site.

## 4.10. Continue experimenting with the technology even after it is implemented

Emerging technologies require close monitoring and careful management even well after its installation and subsequent adjustments may be required to ensure its effective utilization. The impacts of these technologies on organizations often ‘emerge’ unpredictably from complex social interactions between technological and organizational elements, and cannot be predicted in advance [4]. Ongoing efforts by the Global Village team to discover new ways of utilizing the intranet (e.g., identifying new business applications that can be web-enabled) reflects this philosophy at US West.

## 5. Conclusions

The purpose of this paper was to understand key organizational factors affecting the implementation of emerging technologies. Toward this goal, intranet implementation at US West was examined over a period of 3 years. Organizational enablers and barriers to intranet adoption were identified. Ten lessons learned from this case study were prescribed as guidelines that managers can employ to manage emerging technologies within their organizations.

In conclusion, the Global Village project demonstrated that organizations can diffuse emerging technologies, despite their inherent risks and unproven nature. However, their successful implementation may present difficult challenges for IT management (e.g., institutionalizing a cultural change). Adequate efforts and resources must be devoted to understanding and managing these challenges.
