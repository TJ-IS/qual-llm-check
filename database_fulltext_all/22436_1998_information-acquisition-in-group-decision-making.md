# Information acquisition in group decision making

Carol Saunders $^{1,a,*}$ , Shaila Miranda $^{2,b}$

$^{a}$ Michael J. Price College of Business, University of Oklahoma, Norman, OK 73019-4006, USA $^{b}$ Department of Decision and Information Systems, Florida Atlantic University, Boca Raton, FL 33431, USA

Received 2 August 1996; revised 11 June 1997; accepted 15 January 1998

## Abstract

This study focused on accessing information during group decision making across traditional face-to-face and group support systems (GSS) environments for two different types of tasks. It also explored the impact of varying access patterns on the quality of the group decisions in a laboratory experiment. Findings indicate that individuals in GSS groups accessed information significantly more often; however, access patterns varied across meeting conditions and tasks. Contrary to expectations, GSS groups accessed information later in the decision-making process, while traditional groups accessed it earlier. When working on a task with a best answer (i.e. an intellective task), groups sought information later. On a judgmental task (i.e. cognitive conflict task), groups tended to seek information earlier in the decision-making process. Finally, information accessed late in the decision-making process appeared detrimental to decision quality for the cognitive conflict task. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Group support systems; Electronic meeting systems; Information access; Decision-making processes

## 1. Introduction

Information acquisition and use is critical to the decision-making process, yet research about the acquisition and use of information in the decision-making process has been lacking $[29]$ . There are gaps on two important issues: patterns of acquisition in decision-making groups across meeting environments and task types, and the relative effectiveness of various patterns of access. Related research is limited and has focused primarily on individuals. Although researchers of collaborative decision making acknowledge the importance of information acquisition as a determinant of decision quality (e.g. [16]), very little empirical work has been performed.

With the increasing popularity of group support systems (GSS), it is also necessary to explore information-acquisition behavior across meeting environments. Observers of advanced information technologies posit enhanced information access with the use of such technologies (e.g. [14, 15]). However, again, little empirical evidence supports or denies this.

Decision-making groups undertake different types of tasks, ranging from planning to selecting from among alternatives. Researchers believe that task type is an important determinant of group behavior and task differences recently have emerged in empirical work. For example, Straus and McGrath [33] found the efficacy of computer-support for problem-solving groups to be mediated by task type. Kaplan and Miller [17] found that task type influenced group members' responsiveness to normative and informational peer pressure. So we should expect different information acquisition patterns to emerge in different task situations. However, there is no evidence to support this premise, much less indicate how task type affects information acquisition or whether the difference is important. Moreover, we have yet to ascertain the relative effectiveness of different patterns of information acquisition: the timing of the access may affect the efficiency and effectiveness of the final decision. Acquiring critical information early in the decision-making process may help to define the problem correctly and to search for a good solution. It may encourage developing or exploring alternatives that otherwise may not have been considered. Further, timely information acquisition may make the decision-making process more efficient. If critical information is obtained later in the process, the decision maker may have spent unnecessary time analyzing an inappropriate solution. Other factors, such as meeting environment and task type, may therefore moderate the effectiveness of information acquisition.

## 2. Exploring information acquisition

Dean and Sharfman [8] found that following a rational procedure had a strong positive impact on the effectiveness of strategic decision making. They stated that information acquisition beforehand was critical in effective decision making.

Our research model is based on the work of Saunders and Jones [31]. They proposed that acquisition is influenced by decisional stages and contextual variables, including task type and meeting environment. We examine information acquisition for two different types of tasks in traditional and electronic meeting environments and explore the extent to which group information acquisition patterns influence decision quality.

## 2.1. Information acquisition across stages of decision making

A frequently cited model of the decision-making process is Simon's [32] three-stage model of intelligence, design and choice. In this model, information is accessed primarily at the first stage, when the problem is studied and identified. A large proportion of subsequent decision-making research has followed Simon's linear approach [19]. At the other end of the spectrum of decision models is the apparent irrationality of organized anarchy theories exemplified by that of March and Colleagues [3, 6, 21]. These focus on decision making as a social-interaction process and do not emphasize sequential activities for diagnosing problems and generating solutions. Instead, researchers with this perspective are concerned about how the different organizational components interface with one another to solve the problem.

The decision-making process is probably a blend of the rational and anarchistic perspectives $[22]$ . Mintzberg et al. $[25]$ synthesize these two perspectives by introducing interruptions, delays, and deviations into the rational, linear decision-making model. Decision makers can loop back and forth among three decision-making stages, namely identification, development, and selection.

Saunders and Jones base the decisional component of their model on the stages proposed by Mintzberg et al. In addition, Saunders and Jones propose information acquisition and contextual components. They argue that information acquisition can occur in all stages of decision making. Their third component, the decision-making context, includes factors such as the perceived importance of the decision to the organization or manager, problem/task characteristics, time pressures, the decision arrival time, the number of decisions the manager is working on simultaneously, established interpersonal behavior patterns involving the manager, and the manager's decision role. In order to fully understand information acquisition during decision making, the decisional, information acquisition, and contextual components must be integrated. Each of these components asserts a dynamic impact on information acquisition during decision making.

## 2.2. Meeting environments in group information acquisition and exchange

Advanced information technologies using electronic media may affect the interaction of decisional, information acquisition and contextual components. Huber $[15]$ argued that advanced information technologies, including computer-assisted communication and decision support technologies, increase the breadth of decision making. Individuals who are separated in time or physical proximity can use the technology to exchange information more readily (e.g. $[5, 13]$ ). Advanced information technologies also enable groups to obtain information from sources outside the meeting group via electronic databases or electronic communication with human experts. They increase the speed and power with which information can be acquired, processed, and presented for use.

A variety of electronic technologies are available to provide decision-making groups with varying levels of support. Related research falls under the broad classifications of group support systems (GSS), group decision support systems (GDSS), group communication support systems (GCSS), computer-supported collaborative work (CSCW), electronic meeting systems (EMS), and computer-mediated communications (CMC), and under the specific technologies of electronic mail, computer conferencing, and groupware. The lowest level of group support can occur with a single stand-alone computer to support the group's decision-making process. More advanced support exists in the form of networked keypads or computers for each individual decision maker, with linkages to electronic data and information systems outside the conference facilities. This research focuses on more advanced GSS that provide electronic support to individuals and groups via computers and database linkages. The advanced group support system that we used allows a sequenced agenda to be displayed on a public screen. GSS of this type offer visually persistent form of passive guidance and restrictiveness of selection options through the on-screen agenda that may be viewed by group members. In their study of structured group decision making, Wheeler and Valacich [36] suggested that such a procedural focus for a group's activities increased the effectiveness in applying linear decision models.

GSS affect the extent and patterns of information acquisition in decision-making groups. They possess several attributes that facilitate information acquisition and sharing. These systems can provide immediate access to electronic databases and information systems outside the meeting room $[14, 18, 26]$ . Electronic recording makes it easier for group members to share information with each other and provides a permanent record of all meeting transactions for future reference $[1]$ . In addition to the public screens and permanent records, some, but not all, GSS environments allow participants to talk with one another on a face-to-face basis. Face-to-face discussions are not possible when the GSS participants are geographically dispersed or if the intent is to allow privacy to participants.

Some decisions involve several managers, each with his or her own interpretation of the same event. These decisions are ambiguous, or equivocal, and require exchange of subjective information and convergence on similar interpretations. Daft and Lengel [7] argue that media that can convey rich information must be used. On the other hand, well-defined problems which only need more information to be solved, can use media low in information richness. GSS apply new media to highly equivocal situations [14]. With GSS, each member may think and work individually with existing databases, while exchanging ideas, anonymously or openly, through discussions and a public display screen. Feedback among members is fast. Information exchange in the GSS context is ideal for decisions requiring equivocality reduction. Members of the group have direct access to different sources of information. Theoretically, a group member may indirectly access information that is unknown to any one of the others and share it with fellow group members. However, sharing information among group members has costs in terms of time and effort. GSS may facilitate information sharing and reduce these costs. Few researchers have examined such information acquisition or sharing. Dennis' [9] work on information exchange is an exception. In his study, groups of ten student subjects each made decisions about admission of students to the university. Seven subjects received a subset of information favoring the same sub-optimal alternative, while three received different subsets of information favoring the optimal alternative. Groups using GSS exchanged more information than traditional face-to-face groups, but did not make better decisions. In addition, GSS groups remembered less shared information and were less likely to use the same. They perceived the shared information to be less credible. In contrast, Hightower and Sayeed [12] found GSS groups exchanged information less efficiently than face-to-face groups. Conflicting prediscussion preferences improved information exchange dramatically in face-to-face groups but not in groups using computer-mediated communication systems.

## 2.3. Task type and information access

The Saunders and Jones model suggests that task type, a contextual factor, may impact the acquisition and use of information over time (i.e. different decision-making stages). Similar interactions of temporal and contextual components in information acquisition have been proposed by many researchers. For example, Hightower and Sayeed suggest that information exchanges vary as a group's dynamics change over time. McGrath and Hollingshead [24] were concerned with another aspect of the relationship between information exchange, time and task type: information sharing. They thought that group members probably either suffer serious information overload or ignore each other's information inputs. For them, the problem of overload is a temporal issue which implies having too many things to do at once, even though the tasks do not have a critical time component. With more time, the overload problem changes into a problem of prioritizing and sequencing. McGrath and Hollingshead suggest that temporal and task features ought to be given more attention than has been given to-date.

Poole [30] proposed that task-related variables influence decision maker's need for information. A number of researchers related task type with Daft and Lengel's information richness. In particular, McGrath and Hollingshead and Straus and McGrath suggested that idea generation tasks (e.g. brainstorming) may require the transmission of specific ideas with low information richness. On the other hand, tasks in which groups negotiate and resolve conflicts of view or interests may require not only ‘facts’ but also values, attitudes, emotions, and affective messages that are richer in information. Tasks involved with solving problems with a correct answer, or intellective tasks, may lie between these two extremes, but nearer the idea-generation tasks. On the other hand, cognitive conflict, or judgmental, tasks can have multiple solutions. These tasks require groups to reach decisions on issues for which there is no right answer, but only an agreed-upon consensus. They lie between the two extremes, but closer to negotiation tasks in terms of information richness. Straus and McGrath found that face-to-face groups were more effective than computer-mediated groups on cognitive conflict, but not intellective or idea-generation tasks.

## 2.4. Information acquisition patterns and decision quality

Knowledge of appropriate information acquisition patterns can be used to improve decision quality. While research linking information acquisition to the quality of group decisions is limited, some previous research hints at such a linkage. Simon's model of decision making embodies an implied prescription that decision makers begin the decision-making process with a search for information related to the problem at hand. Janis' [16] treatise on faulty group decision making appears to support this position. Based on anecdotal evidence, Janis concludes that decision-making groups have a tendency to accept only information that supports their preferred position. Information that fails to support the solution preferred by the majority of group members is likely to be discarded or refuted. This indicates that decision-making groups need to examine and assimilate relevant information early in the decision-making process before forming a strong preference for a solution.

## 3. Research hypotheses

GSS can speedily and easily provide electronic access to databases outside the conference facilities. Group members may electronically obtain information from individuals outside the group. Further, through GSS use, individual group members can obtain information without impinging on the ability of other group members to concurrently retrieve information from the same source; however, traditional groups often need to take turns. Also, GSS may provide more information and the means of effectively structuring information to reduce information overload.

Other GSS characteristics can lead to more informal information acquisition. Umanath [34] found that when groups were overloaded with information, they tended to recall the incidental information that they stumbled over more frequently than information acquired via a directed search. When GSS settings provide decision makers with a way to browse through data, group members may serendipitously discover needed information. Hence the relationship between GSS use and information access is expected to be positive.

## H1: Groups using GSS will more frequently access information than will traditional meeting groups.

Previous research has repeatedly suggested that the search for information starts with the most accessible sources. For example, Gerstberger and Allen [10] found that engineers first used the more readily accessible channels in their information searches. Their findings support the law of least effort (i.e. choice based on the least average rate of probable work). They used sources that were more accessible, even when they provided lower-quality information. The same preference for accessible information was found by O'Reilly [28] in his study of welfare workers and Culnan [4] in her study of college students. Based on these research findings, Saunders and Jones proposed that information sources external to the organization are used less during the identification stage of decision making. Instead, the most accessible sources are used. This implies that decision-making groups exchange information among group members first. However, the Saunders and Jones model does not take into account the use of GSS. In fact, no previous research has explored this issue. It could be argued that GSS technology makes the information from sources external to the group more accessible. A counter-argument is that users find the GSS technology more foreign and, hence, it creates technological impediments for information access. However, training in the use of the technology can facilitate assimilation of this software. We pose the following hypothesis:

H2: GSS groups will access information more frequently in the identification stage than will traditional meeting groups.

While it has not been demonstrated in previous research, task type may also play an important role in information access over time. For example, with intellective tasks, problem identification may be easier than with cognitive conflict tasks. More emphasis may be placed on gathering information for developing and choosing alternatives to solve an intellective task than in defining the problem. Because cognitive conflict problems are harder to define, more information may be accessed during the identification stage. To solve cognitive conflict problems, it is often necessary to uncover members' conflicting opinions or to understand societal values. Often, however, the members are not fully cognizant of societal values, the reasons for their opinions, or subtleties differentiating their opinions from those held by other group members. Thus, it may be easier to identify the problems in intellective tasks than in cognitive conflict tasks, since there is less need to gather information when defining intellective problems.

Differences in the nature of the cognitive conflict problem may affect information access, which may be especially important for problems that focus on moral issues or societal values. Further, cognitive conflict problems arising from differing opinions and biases of the group members may provoke group members to bolster their own position by soliciting information from sources outside the group. Thus, a greater need may exist for information access in problem identification in cognitive conflict tasks. We hypothesize:

H3: Groups solving intellective tasks will access less information in the identification stage than will groups solving cognitive conflict tasks associated with moral issues.

Finally, along with Simon and Janis, we believe that information acquisition early in the decision-making process improves the likelihood of a high-quality decision because it is more likely to be assimilated and processed without bias. We believe that this relationship between patterns of information acquisition should hold across meeting environments and task types.

H4: Groups that access information early in the decision-making process will make better decisions than those that access information later in the process.

## 4. Setting, procedures and measures

The study sample consists of 33 four-, five- and six-member groups drawn from undergraduate introductory information systems classes at a Southeastern University in the US. Six students signed up for a group meeting at a designated time. However, some of these decided not to participate, leaving their groups with only four or five members. $^{3}$ To motivate them, all participants received extra credit for the class. In addition, they were told that each member of the group suggesting the best solution for each problem would receive a money order for use at the campus bookstore. The groups were instructed to propose a solution that reflected a consensus of the group members.

Prior to the study, participants received training in structured problem solving and an introduction to, and hands-on training with, the GSS to be used – VisionQuest. Groups then met for two, 2-h sessions to solve specified problems; these were spaced a week apart. During one meeting, groups used the GSS in face-to-face synchronous meetings; the other session was a traditional face-to-face meeting that did not use a computer-based medium. During each meeting, groups solved a different problem. The problems were related to topics covered in the class. The order of problems and technology use were rotated across groups to control for order effects. Of the initial GSS groups, half were randomly assigned the intellective task; the other half were assigned the cognitive conflict task. The face-to-face traditional groups were assigned tasks in a similar fashion.

Both GSS and traditional face-to-face groups were given a written agenda that suggested an approach to solving the problem. The problems were not complex and the groups could move in the straightforward, linear fashion that they were taught.

In the GSS groups, the agenda was also reflected in the displayed VisionQuest agenda and was closely followed by the process facilitator. Both groups had equal opportunity to share information internally. The GSS groups communicated primarily through electronic means. Group members had access to VisionQuest through their computers. While they could talk to all group members, they were not easily able to see everyone in the group because equipment blocked their view. However, groups frequently found ways around these obstructions, by moving around the room or standing so they were more visible.

This research employed decisional, contextual and information acquisition components. Specifically, the research was designed to control for two factors in the contextual component: tasks and meeting environments. It measured the number of information accesses (information acquisition component) in each stage of decision making (decisional component). The dependent variable was decision quality, as assessed by expert raters' knowledge about the task.

## 4.1. Task (contextual factor)

This research used two decision problems. One, vendor selection, is an intellective problem with a deterministic answer that was developed for this study. (See Appendix A.) It required groups to choose one of three software vendors based on specified criteria. The second one, an ethical dilemma, is a cognitive conflict, or judgmental, task. (See Appendix B.) Groups processed different biases and moral judgments and generated a non-deterministic, creative solution. The ethical dilemma concerning faulty software is a modification of a problem by Weiss [35]. These tasks and supporting materials were designed to supplement students' classroom learning in their introductory information systems course.

## 4.2. Electronic vs. traditional meeting environment (contextual factor)

GSS meetings in our research used VisionQuest, version 2.11, a software product that supports decision making and planning groups. This study used three of its tools on each task: brainstorming, comment cards, and scoring. The agenda was displayed on the participants' screens and on the public screen.

The control condition used traditional face-to-face meeting techniques; groups used paper and pencil techniques and had access to flip charts. They followed a printed agenda similar to the agenda in the GSS condition. Appendices C and D show the agenda and supporting notes for meeting groups.

## 4.3. Stage of decision making (decisional factor)

We used the Mintzberg et al. stages of decision making. During the identification stage, students read and gathered information about the problem and discussed its nature. In the development stage, alternatives were presented and analyzed. Selection involved evaluating the alternatives, making a choice, recording the decision and justifying their choice. The researcher determined the decision-making stage of an information access based on the activities that were being performed when a request for information was made.

## 4.4. Information access (information acquisition factor)

Information access was operationalized as information about the decision situation that was not provided to the group members in the problem statement. At the start of the meeting, the experimenter informed groups of the availability of additional information to assist in making a decision. The experimenter provided the groups with a list of topics available and described how groups could access this information. The additional information for the intellective task described executive information systems and their critical success factors, the fictitious company, and each of the vendors being considered. Existing law and related court cases buttressed the information provided in the cognitive conflict task. Some hold-out information was helpful in making a good decision, such as the information about vendors in the intellective task. Other information, such as background information about the fictional company, provided a context, but did not have a direct bearing on the groups' choices. Eight pieces of additional information (sheets or screens) were available for each task. This hold-out information is presented in Appendices E and F. Each information sheet or screen could be viewed several times in the course of the meeting session.

For GSS users, the additional information could be accessed using a VisionQuest agenda item that invoked a menu program to allow users to select various types of information. A program specifically developed for this study counted each access of additional information. The experimenter ran the program at the end of each stage of the decision-making process and recorded the number of times information was accessed during that stage.

The same information was available to traditional decision-making groups via a study assistant's notebook. The study assistant had two copies of each information sheet. Group members were allowed to check these out during the course of the meeting, one sheet at a time. The assistant logged each request and indicated the decision-making stage when the request was made. The assistant did not otherwise interact with the subjects.

To calculate the number of information accesses per group, the number of accesses by individual group members was aggregated at the group level. This number was then divided by the group size to adjust for differences in size.

## 4.5. Decision quality

Decision quality was assessed using a report written by each group. Each decision was evaluated by two raters using a 7-point scale (a '7' representing an excellent decision, and a '1' a poor decision). The raters were professionals who were chosen based on their familiarity with the content of each of the two tasks. The raters then met to discuss their ratings and reconcile major differences. The ratings of the two raters on each decision were summed for statistical analysis. The inter-rater reliability of 0.68 for the intellective task and 0.74 for the cognitive conflict task were both significant at $\alpha = 0.0001$ .

## 5. Results

A total of 179 subjects, 74 females, 101 males and four undeclared, participated in the study. Participants' average age and work experience was 23.8 and 3.8 years, respectively. Participants were third-or fourth-year undergraduates from nine different business majors.

## 5.1. Meeting environment, task type, and the frequency of information access

Hypothesis 1 is supported. GSS groups are significantly different from traditional groups in the extent to which they accessed information (F=23.47, p=0.0001). Overall, GSS groups accessed information a mean of 29 times during a meeting. Traditional face-to-face groups only accessed information a mean of 17 times. There is no significant effect for task, or for the interaction of task with type of meeting environment on number of accesses. As can be seen, there are significant main and interaction effects involving the stage of decision making on the amount of information accessed by groups.

## 5.2. Patterns of information acquisition

Fig. 1 shows different information access patterns for GSS groups and manual groups. However, the direction of the effect is in a direction opposite from that predicted by Hypothesis 2. Most (65 percent) information accesses for the traditional groups were at the identification stage. By contrast, only 31 percent of the information accesses were made by the GSS groups in the identification stage. GSS groups tended to access information primarily at the selection stage. Three-way interactions shown in Figs. 2 and 3 indicate that this effect was consistent across task types, but more pronounced on the intellective task.

In Fig. 4, we see that groups accessed information significantly less frequently at the initial stages on the intellective task, but accessed significantly more information at the final phase of the decision-making process. In fact, most accesses (i.e. 69 percent) for the intellective task were made during the selection stage. The remainder were made in the identification stage. In contrast, only 11 percent of the information accesses for the cognitive conflict problem were made in the selection stage. Over half (57 percent) of the accesses for cognitive conflict tasks were made in the identification stage, while 32 percent were in the development stage. Figs. 5 and 6 indicate that these information access patterns are consistent across meeting environments. This supports Hypothesis 3.

The primary stage of information access was operationalized as the stage at which groups accessed the most amount of information. A separate ANOVA was conducted for each task type, since the criteria used for evaluating decision quality differed across tasks. ## 6. Discussion

Study findings, summarized in Table 4, indicate that information acquisition patterns in GSS and traditional environments vary across decision tasks and stages. As predicted, GSS groups access information significantly more than do traditional face-to-face groups (Hypothesis 1). Thus, GSS use does facilitate information access.

GSS researchers have noticed an initial lag in the ability of groups to be effective in appropriating the technology $[2]$ . This process, or any other social artifact, requires that users not only possess technical skills, but use them in a productive manner, modifying the technology and the manner in which it is conventionally used, if necessary $[11]$ . Lags were found to persist despite training $[27]$ . This may explain the tendency of GSS groups to seek information later in the decision-making process: their heightened information acquisition behavior toward the end of the meeting probably occurred after they became more comfortable with the technology.

Further analysis of information acquisition patterns for the cognitive conflict and intellective tasks reveals striking differences (Hypothesis 3). As anticipated, groups faced with cognitive conflict tasks focused their information gathering efforts early in the decision-making process during the identification stage. On the other hand, groups solving intellective tasks gathered their information primarily in the selection stage of decision making. Significant differences in information access patterns persisted across different stages for different tasks during meetings of groups in electronic versus traditional face-to-face meetings.

Another explanation for the pronounced differences in acquisition patterns lies in the way groups approach different types of decisions when using electronic media. Groups faced with more ambiguous, equivocal decision-making tasks may gather as much information as possible in identifying the problem. When problem identification is more straightforward, as in intellective tasks, most information may be needed to buttress alternative evaluation.

Accessing information late in the decision-making process resulted in significantly worse decisions for the cognitive conflict task. However, the time at which information was accessed had no significant effect on decision quality in regard to the intellective task. Since cognitive conflict tasks are more likely to evoke personal biases and positions than intellective tasks, it follows that information accessed later on cognitive conflict tasks is more likely to be detrimentally used to bolster individual positions than to modify the group's solution.

## 7. Conclusion

This paper considered the contextual, information acquisition, and decisional components as a group decision unfolds. Major findings suggest the following: the utility of GSS in promoting information access, albeit later in the decision-making process; differences in access patterns across task types; and the effectiveness of early information access on cognitive conflict tasks. It provides one of the first explorations of the decision-making process in GSS environments. Further, it delves into the process across two decision tasks.

The artificiality of the highly controlled laboratory environment $[20]$ and zero history groups $[23]$ poses questions regarding the generalizability of study results. Student groups differ from real-life groups on several dimensions: maturity, abilities, and motivation. While the average age and work experience of students in this sample indicates acceptable maturity and generalizable abilities, motivation to provide a good solution may have been a problem. Miranda and Bostrom $[27]$ discuss the source of these motivational problems and indicate that group motivation can be expected to impact the results of the study and reduce its generalizability. To increase the generalizability of the results, future subjects should be practising managers with experience in making similar decision and with a history of working together. The decisions should be of a complex nature that typifies problems found in the work setting.

Previous research that has focused on intellective, creative and decision-making tasks may have yielded findings that are not applicable to other types of tasks, such as cognitive conflict tasks. This study's finding about differences across task types have implications for process structuring and choice of GSS tools. In all probability, different ideal problem-solving processes exist for different task types. For example, in developing GSS agendas, comment cards to identify opinions and moral values may be more helpful early in the agenda for cognitive conflict tasks. Intellective tasks may benefit more heavily from the use of brainstorming tools in the development stage and analysis tools in the selection stage of the decision-making process. The findings of this study suggest the value of future research to discover ideal process structures and appropriate matches of GSS tools to different tasks. GSS tools may need to be used differently when supporting different types of tasks. Knowing how and why may improve GSS use.

## E.1. General information about executive support systems

Executive support systems (ESS), often known as executive information systems, attempt to provide managers with immediate data access in a user-friendly fashion. ESS vary widely in the kinds of features they provide. However, some common ESS features include:

\- on-line, context-dependent help screens

\- customized user setups

\- interfaces to other software

\- integration of data from various sources

\- security for data, screens, and systems

\- access to external databases (e.g. Dow Jones)

\- flexible data presentation styles – graphical, tabular, or textual

Most ESS also provide some level of support to executives to expedite office management and communication activities. These features are called office support tools.

A number of companies have successfully adopted and continue to use ESS. Lockheed, GA, a pioneer in ESS, has recently instituted ESS at other Lockheed locations. Other notable users of ESS include Hertz, Metropolitan Life, Motorola, Quaker Oats, and Duracell.

## E.2. Ess development issues

## E.2.1. Development characteristics leading to successful ESS

A number of issues at the developmental stage can influence the later success of an ESS. Primary among these is the need for an executive sponsor – a senior executive, typically a CEO or vice president – who provides the motivation for the development of the system. ESS initiatives by IS groups, in the absence of an executive sponsor, have rarely been successful. It is also critical to have the right mix of people involved in the ESS development process. The development team should include people with strong technical skills as well as a sound understanding of the business.

## E.2.2. Cost issues

Initial cost for an ESS is reported to average \$365 000. Annual maintenance costs average \$208 000. These figures are based on the study of large companies who used ESS. Smaller firms may develop more limited and, therefore, less expensive ESS.

## E.2.3. Other success-related issues

User-friendliness is essential with these systems since the targeted user is often less familiar with computers, and always short of time. Similarly, immediate data access is critical. Slow or cumbersome system response has resulted in the demise of many systems.

## E.3. Office support tools

\- Wordprocessing – enables executives to create and format their own documents.

## F.1. Software background information

Computers are used in every walk of life today. Software applications can be found in a number of areas: in business, in product design, in scientific research, in publishing, in education, and in medicine.

Software applications are available in various forms. Standard application packages such as word-processors, spreadsheets, and databases are available in stores. Customized software may be developed at the request of a user. Such software is developed when users do not find off-the-shelf software that meets their specific needs.

## F.2. The software testing process

Major software producers, such as MicroSoft, are usually fairly conscientious about testing software. The software testing process typically involves a number of steps.

The firm usually starts with extensive testing of the product in-house. This testing begins with members of the development team who test each of the product modules. Once the product modules are put together, the software product is tested some more. It is then distributed to in-house users for further testing.

The software is then given to knowledgeable users outside the firm, with the stipulation that they report all problems back to the company. This is the alpha-test phase. At the next phase, the firm distributes the software to a wider clientele, perhaps at a discounted price, or with other inducements to participate in the product testing. This is the beta-test phase. User reports of software problems are used for further updates of the product. Following product modifications after this stage, the firm begins commercial distribution of the product.

## F.3. Overview of law

The legal system is divided into Criminal Law and Civil Law.

## F.3.1. Criminal law

This area of the law relates to public wrongs that are prohibited by the state. Criminal prosecution is initiated by an agent of the state (the prosecutor). In such cases, the defendant's guilt needs to be proved beyond a reasonable doubt. In addition to formal punishment, criminal convictions carry the penalty of social stigma. Crimes are categorized as felonies, serious crimes, or misdemeanors, lesser crimes.

## F.3.2. Civil law

Civil law is applied when one party sues another party because of the other's failure to meet some legal duty owed to the first party. In civil proceedings, the burden of proof rests with the plaintiff, who must demonstrate that the preponderance of the evidence favors him.

Civil law includes an area of the law, called TORTS, and contract law.

## F.4. Torts and contract law

## F.4.1. Torts

These are private or civil wrongs against a person or their property. They represent a breach of duty resulting in loss or harm to another person. If there is a such a breach of duty, it could be intentional, or the result of unintentional but negligent actions.

## F.4.2. Contract law

Contracts are agreements that are voluntarily created by persons with the capacity to contract. Contracts consist of an offer, made and accepted. The agreement must be supported by some consideration (e.g. an agreement to buy a house for a certain dollar amount).

## F.5. Strict products liability

Strict products liability holds a “seller liable for negligence in the manufacture or sale of a product which may reasonably be expected to cause harm if it is defective. The rule applies to all products, which if negligently [designed or] manufactured, could cause physical harm or property damage” (Birnbaum, 1988).

The plaintiff does not have to prove negligence under this provision of the law. Strict products liability requires that the plaintiff demonstrate that:

1. the product was defective when it was bought;

2. the product was used in an intended or reasonably foreseeable manner; and

3. the product was the proximate (immediate) cause of injury.

Under strict products liability, product disclaimers do not free the manufacturer or seller from responsibility for the consequences of a faulty product.

## F.6. Uniform commercial code (UCC)

The uniform commercial code (UCC) applies to anything characterized as a good, rather than a service. The code defines a good as “all things (including specially manufactured goods) which are movable.”

The UCC provides for explicit and implicit warranties. The following are the two types of implicit warranties:

1. Warranty of merchantability – the product should work as it is suppose to.

2. Warranty for particular purpose – the customer imparts information to sales person about needs. Sales person is liable to ensure needs are met.

These warranties can be disclaimed.

## F.7. Therac 25

Therac 25 is a computerized therapeutic radiation machine. During 1985 and 1986, three people were administered a higher dosage of radiation than was intended. This resulted in the deaths of two of the three victims, and the disfigurement and injury of the third.

Closer examination of the equipment revealed a bug in the software that ran the radiation machine. This bug resulted in the administration of higher intensity radiation if the administering technicians edited their input in a particular fashion.

Atomic Energy of Canada Ltd., the company that manufactured Therac 25, settled claims in all three cases out of court. All parties involved were prohibited from discussing the terms of the settlement.

## F.8. RRX Industries, inc. vs. LAB-CON, Inc.

Thomas E. Kelly Associates (TEKA) contracted with RRX laboratories to supply RRX with a software system for use in its laboratories. The contract obligated TEKA to correct any malfunctions or ‘bugs’ in the system, but limited TEKA’s liability to the contract price. TEKA then formed LAB-CON to market the software system, and assigned the RRX contract to LAB-CON.

Bugs appeared in the system soon after installation. TEKA attempted to fix the bugs. However, the system remained unreliable because bugs continued to exist. RRX sued TEKA and LAB-CON for breach of contract. The court concluded that since TEKA and LAB-CON were either unwilling or unable to provide a system that worked as represented. The court found that TEKA and LAB-CON had breached the contract, and awarded RRX damages.
