# Examining the conflicting results of GDSS research

Ki Jeong Chun, Hung Kook Park $^{*}$

School of Information Technology and Telecommunications, Sangmyung University, 7 Hongji-Dong, Chongro-Ku, Seoul 110-743, South Korea

Received 25 June 1996; revised 21 December 1996; accepted 12 March 1998

## Abstract

The growing interest in group decision support systems (GDSS) was supported by, and gave rise to, a burgeoning academic literature on GDSS during the 1980s. However, GDSS research is now rare. One possible reason is the difference between field experience and many experimental studies. Another reason is that GDSS research over the past years mainly focused on decision rooms. The important question posed is: what are the reasons for the conflicting results of GDSS research? The contradictory findings among GDSS studies are indeed a problem if academic research is to be applied effectively in business settings. Now that group collaborative support is becoming more widespread, it is natural to wish to achieve a better understanding of the implications for organizations in their adoption of GDSS. Thus, this study systematically reviews existing GDSS studies and explores the probable reasons for inconsistent findings. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Group decision support system (GDSS); GDSS experiments; Group decision-making; Decision room; Group support systems; Computer-supported cooperative work

## 1. Introduction

Previous research reveals that, even within a decision-room type group decision support system (GDSS), there are considerable variations across system configurations and the way that they are used. Basic attributes like software design and functionality, room configuration, method of facilitation, and group-process technique differ remarkably from one GDSS to the other.

Decisions made in the design of the decision room, even within the multiple workstation type GDSS, are also different from one to another. The Claremont room (formerly the Southern Methodist University room) [34, 35] is based on the assumption that most executives do not type or do not like to type; therefore, the individual terminals are touch screens and are menu driven. However, the Minnesota [28] and Arizona [54] rooms are based on a different assumption, namely that previous experience or skill in keyboarding has little to do with actual GDSS usage. While some GDSSs (e.g. decision conferencing) believe in the importance of the facilitator, others (e.g., SAMM: Software-aided meeting management) do not.

The experiments designed to test the effects of a GDSS also differ from one another along many dimensions. For example, to compare the results of using one software in one experiment with a different software in a different environment, etc. can be misleading. While some research compares the effects of a GDSS to a manually structured process, other efforts compare GDSS groups to baseline (no-support-at-all) groups. The comparison of no-support-at-all groups to manually structured groups permits determination of simple structural effects; the comparison of manually structured groups to GDSS groups permit determination of 'pure' computer system effects, and the comparison of no-support-at-all groups to GDSS groups permits determination of effects due to support versus no support [20, 83]. Thus, comparing the results between studies, without considering the differences between the phenomena being investigated with each research design, is essentially impossible to interpret.

Given these differences, general statements about a GDSS also can be misleading. All the findings must be interpreted carefully because these are often the answers to different research questions $[16, 30]$ .

Contradictory results, however, suggest the need for comparison of how different technologies are used in different circumstances, and exactly what features of a GDSS then provide the most effective and efficient support $[40, 44]$ .

Our current study thus aims at providing possible reasons for the inconsistencies in the results of existing studies. The focus is intended to identify and summarize findings rather than criticize any one.

## 2. Previous GDSS research review

Prior GDSS research has primarily concentrated on finding out its effect on performance, user attitudes, participation, and conflict.

## 2.1. GDSS effects on performance

Many GDSS experiments have examined the effects of a GDSS versus no GDSS support on user performance. Typical variables are the quality of group decision and decision time. Results show a somewhat conflicting picture with regard to decision quality and decision time. Results are inconclusive. A summary is provided in Table 1.

## 2.1.1. Experimental studies

## 2.1.1.1. Decision quality

GDSS versus no-support-at-all treatments: Seven studies have shown that use of a GDSS improves decision quality compared to no-support-at-all treatment. While two studies have shown the use of a GDSS to have no effect on the decision quality, one study reports that the decision quality of GDSS groups was lower than that of no-support-at-all groups because users suffered from problems due to their lack of familiarity with the technology; it then concludes that the group's cognitive struggle and procedural orientation due to the unfamiliar technology may imply that help will be necessary in GDSS settings, or repeated use of a GDSS will reduce these problems. In fact, Sharda et al. [69] have shown that, in earlier stages of the use of a GDSS, the decision quality of GDSS groups was worse or the same with no-support groups; however, in later stages of GDSS use, GDSS groups outperformed no-support groups. Therefore, some researchers [10, 40, 69, 78] believe that learning through repeated use of the GDSS may be required before improved performance occurs.

GDSS versus manual structured treatments: Results are inconclusive. Four studies found the use of a GDSS to improve decision quality, while seven studies showed the use of a GDSS to have no difference in decision quality compared to a manually structured method. Two studies have shown that the impact of a GDSS on decision quality is more visible when dealing with more difficult tasks. However, Watson et al. [84] found that the decision quality of GDSS groups was lower than that of manually structured groups because users suffered from problems of technical unfamiliarity.

The majority of the research reviewed here shows that there is no distinct difference in decision quality between GDSS and manually structured groups. However, the results also imply that we cannot ignore the possibility of improved decision quality from the use of a GDSS over manual treatment when dealing with particularly complex tasks, or from the repeated use of a GDSS.

## 2.1.1.2. Decision time

GDSS versus no-support-at-all treatments: The use of GDSS compared to no-support treatments has been shown to increase the time required to make a decision or to have no effect on time required. Sharda et al. [69] found that GDSS groups compared to no-support groups took more time in the first 3-week period, but showed no difference in the decision time taken in the last 5-week period. Despite the differences in system configurations, all of the research reviewed here reveals that there is no benefit from the use of a GDSS compared to a no-support treatment in terms of the decision time saved.

GDSS versus manual structured treatments: The use of a GDSS compared to a manually structured process has been shown to increase the time to reach a decision or to have no effect on time required. Lewis [46] found that a GDSS took a longer time than a manually structured or a no-support process, but the structured process group took longer than the no-support group. While Gallupe [26] found that GDSS groups took the same time with no-support groups, irrespective of the task difficulty, Bui and Sivasankaran [7] reported that GDSS groups compared to manually structured groups took a longer time in dealing with low-difficulty tasks and took the same time when handling high-difficulty tasks. Watson et al. [84] argue that the electronic medium associated with GDSS should allow the structure to be invoked and applied more quickly than is possible with a manual system. However, research reviewed here again shows that there is no benefit from the use of a GDSS compared to a manual treatment in terms of the decision time.

## 2.1.2. Field studies

It is interesting to note that the research results of all field studies show both, enhanced decision quality and shortened meeting time with the use of a GDSS compared to a similar conventional meeting. Nunamaker et al. [55] report that 55% of time saving (in terms of project duration, number of meetings, and person-hours) has been achieved with the use of a GDSS at IBM sites. Phillips [60] also notes that agreement is reached much more quickly with the use of a GDSS than could be achieved by a conventional procedure.

## 2.2. GDSS effects on user attitudes

While performance is admittedly the primary dependent variable of interest in information system research, user attitudes (users' perceptions of their performance, their satisfaction with the decision process and outcome, and their intrinsic interest in the system) are also key issues $[47, 72]$ . In the case of a GDSS, user attitudes have been recognized as critical to acceptance and adoption of the technology, especially in organizational settings $[15, 29]$ . However, like the results of performance variables, previous GDSS studies in user satisfaction and decision confidence show somewhat conflicting results (see Table 2).

## 2.2.1. Experimental studies

## 2.2.1.1. User satisfaction

GDSS versus no-support-at-all treatments: While four studies reported that GDSS groups exhibited less satisfaction compared to no-support groups, three studies reported a higher level of satisfaction from GDSS use. However, five studies found no difference in the level of satisfaction between GDSS and no support groups. Jessup et al. [41] interpreted the enhanced satisfaction with the outcome of GDSS groups as due to the anonymous function inherent in their system. Aiken et al. [2] reported that electronic brainstorming in GDSS was superior to verbal communication in large groups while there was no significant difference between small electronic brainstorming groups and small, verbal groups. They concluded that satisfaction increased with group size for the GDSS groups.

Overall, the majority of the surveyed research show that user satisfaction with the use of GDSS is not higher than that with no support treatments. It is, however, interesting to note that participants' satisfaction with GDSS use varies according to the system configuration. All of the five studies which reported no difference in the level of satisfaction are facilitator-driven GDSS. But, three out of four studies which found decreased satisfaction from the use of a GDSS are user-driven GDSS.

GDSS versus manual structured treatments: Results are contradictory. Four studies found that use of a GDSS results in enhanced satisfaction with the decision process compared to a manual structured treatment. However, five studies showed the use of a GDSS to dampen participants' satisfaction with the decision process. Beauclair [4] reported that there was no difference in the perceived satisfaction with the outcome between GDSS and manually structured groups. Bui and Sivasankaran [7] reported that satisfaction with the outcome of GDSS groups particularly increased when dealing with high-difficulty tasks.

Participants' satisfaction with GDSS use is strongly affected by the system configuration. Three out of four studies, which reported improved satisfaction with the use of GDSS compared to a manual support, are facilitator-driven GDSSs. However, all of the five studies that found decreased satisfaction are user-driven GDSS.

User-driven GDSS research suggests that participants suffer from problems of technical unfamiliarity in their initial use of a GDSS. They propose that the group's cognitive struggle to deal with technology can cause dissatisfaction and, sometimes, impede the group's performance.

However, according to one type of facilitator-driven GDSS research $[20]$ , the enhanced satisfaction of GDSS groups compared to manually structured groups could be attributed to the idea that computer groups thought a GDSS should help them produce a better decision or that the computer might provide an easier mechanism to record and analyze the data than the use of flip charts.

## 2.2.1.2. Decision confidence

Four studies found that the use of a GDSS deteriorates users' confidence about their decisions. Gallupe et al. [28] interpreted this unexpected finding as follows: "Groups supported by the GDSS tended to generate more possible decision alternatives. They also considered those alternatives in more detail. Because of this condition, these groups had a more difficult choice to make, and once they made a decision, they were possibly less confident because of the number and quality of the choices" [28]. However, Steeb and Johnston [73] reported that GDSS groups generated more options and evaluated those options with more criteria in a very systematic manner, so that enhanced decision confidence of the GDSS groups over manual groups was observed. These different results may be due to the type of system being used in each study, while DECAID and SAMM, which the four studies [26, 27, 28, 84] used, basically incorporate a rational problem-solving agenda [17]. Perceptronics' Group Decision Aid that Steeb and Johnston [73] used is a multi-attribute utility theory based decision analytic model building system, and it is facilitated by a specially trained system operator. Decision analytic computer support models might increase users' confidence about their decision.

## 2.2.2. Field studies

All but one of the field studies examined here $[53]$ have shown that GDSS use produces high satisfaction and enhanced decision confidence without regard to prior experience with a GDSS. All of these used facilitators.

## 2.3. GDSS effects on participation and conflict

Numerous GDSS studies (e.g. [38, 19, 80]) argue that the main goals of a GDSS are to make meetings more productive through improving group communication activities by encouraging equality of participation and increasing the efficiency of that participation.

Researchers explain enhanced participation as the effect of the individual workstation. Many multi-workstation GDSS researchers (e.g. [71]) claim that an individual workstation provides a framework within which group members who are reluctant to contribute are encouraged to participate. Researchers have continuously found the anonymity of electronic communications to increase the number of interpersonal exchanges and reduce the probability of any one member dominating the meeting. However, the anonymity function inherent in multi-workstation GDSS was found to heighten conflict as members tend to communicate more aggressively: members tend to express themselves more forcefully and are seldom as polite when interfacing through the system rather than in person (e.g. [42]). Others (e.g. [25, 43, 45, 76]) also found that the use of electronic media (public display and multi-workstation) reduced the social cues and social interaction of a meeting, and therefore increasing conflicts.

## 2.3.1. Experimental studies

GDSS versus no-support-at-all treatments: As shown in Table 3, five studies report enhanced participation of GDSS groups compared to no-support groups, while three studies ([26, 40, 84]) found no difference in participation between the two treatments.

GDSS versus manual structured treatments: Six studies found the use of a GDSS in increasing participation among members. However, three studies showed no difference in participation between GDSS and manually structured groups.

White et al. [86] suggest that the key effect of structured procedures is to increase member participation. Overall results of the comparison between GDSS and no-support groups substantially support this finding. Of greater interest is the finding that more research leads to enhanced participation from the use of a GDSS compared to a manually structured support.

Technology (e.g. electronic communication channels, individual assured input, anonymity) clearly acts to increase participation among members. However, it has a cost factor. Previous research consistently reports increased conflicts among participants from the use of GDSS compared to manually structured supports and no-support-at-all treatments.

## 3. Interpretations of different results in GDSS research

Many authors $[33, 61, 62, 77]$ have suggested clues for the inconsistencies prevalent in GDSS research results.

## 3.1. Contextual pressures

Decision makers in actual organizational settings may be subject to a variety of pressures and constraints not easily investigated in laboratory settings.

O'Reilly et al. [56] argue that studies need to account for the contextual pressures that affect decision makers' willingness and ability to search for and use information in the actual performance of their duties. This is an important consideration, since a number of studies ([3, 22, 74]) suggest that decisions made under the artificiality of the laboratory situations may not correspond with the same decisions made in the living world. O'Reilly et al. [56] concluded that, unless contextual or situational influences on the decision makers' propensities to seek out and use information are investigated, findings about information processing and decision-making behavior may not possess the external validity to be useful in understanding organizational decision-making. This conclusion can be applied to our GDSS study.

Contextual pressures may be one of the important reasons for the differences in the findings between experimental and field GDSS studies. GDSS do not exist apart from the group and organizational contexts or the external environment of organizations $[39, 49]$ .

## 3.2. Tasks

Virtually all group researchers agree that group performance cannot be studied generically without regard to task, and that an individual's performance is, without question, affected by the type and characteristics of the task [51, 63]. In some research, the nature of the task has accounted for as much as $50\%$ of the variance in group performance [64]. Task determines the need for information and the consequent communication practices of the decision-making group. GDSS researchers have attempted to investigate the effect of tasks on how computer support is viewed and used. Here, we examine the effects of task differences on GDSS results.

## 3.2.1. Task difficulties

A number of researchers in group decision-making have shown that task difficulty affects group performance in terms of outcome quality and group process $[24, 67, 70]$ . There is much evidence that using a GDSS for problems of low difficulty is not worthwhile due to the ‘overhead cost’ involved in using the sophisticated tool $[14, 87]$ . Some research has shown that decision quality was particularly improved with GDSS use for more difficult problems. Most field GDSS studies report improved decision quality and efficiency from the use of a GDSS compared to a similar conventional meeting. Thus, it seems clear that task difficulty is one of the important variables.

## 3.2.2. Structure of tasks

The task may be ill- or well-structured. To measure the effectiveness of a system in a limited time period, laboratory GDSS research generally uses a task with right or wrong answers for their experiments. Subjects (mostly students) also share similar perspectives and data on the task. However, the nature of the ill-structured tasks in the real world precludes knowing a ‘correct’ solution.

As Mason and Mitroff [50] have commented, with ill-structured problems there is no criterion to determine a solution to be correct or false: solutions are, at best, judged as good, bad, or reasonable. Watson et al. [84] also argue that many organizational meetings occur without prior or post-knowledge of the ‘correct’ outcome. Managers also bring a different set of data and their own role-defined perspective to the issues. In such situations, groups must often resolve competing personal preferences and maximize agreement on a solution to a problem. Thus, it is not surprising to see different results from experimental and field GDSS research.

## 3.2.3. Task characteristics associated with the decision phase

Some researchers $[48, 52, 65]$ have described the decision process as involving a number of distinct phases in which a particular type of decisional activity dominates all the others. Based on this, many researchers have investigated the effect of GDSS use on the group performance by varying the task characteristics inherent in each decision-making phase. The following is the task characteristics used in GDSS research:

\- problem finding task;

\- idea generation task;

• consensus reaching task; and

\- choice task [8, 88].

In problem-finding tasks, GDSS groups show enhanced decision quality, but increased dissatisfaction with the process over no-support groups. In consensus reaching tasks, while two studies show no difference in decision quality between GDSS and no-support groups, one study $[83]$ shows decreased decision quality in GDSS groups compared to no-support groups due to the technical intrusion problem. But, all of them report less satisfaction with GDSS use compared to no-support treatments. In choice tasks, GDSS groups show no advantage in decision quality over manual groups, but their degree of satisfaction with the process is higher than manual groups.

According to Zigurs et al. [88], the effectiveness of a group's adaptation to support technology is partially a function of the match between the group's view of task demands and its view of the technology. That is, the key to increasing computer effectiveness in support of groups may be in both, the adaptability of the system to the task and the adaptability of the group to their view of the system's utility and meaning; i.e. one particular type of GDSS may be more effective in dealing with one particular type of task.

The findings from the field studies also show a somewhat different picture from the experimental studies; most field studies reported improved decision quality, efficiency, and satisfaction from the use of a GDSS compared to a similar conventional meeting. Real-world issues always require managers to exercise all of the major task characteristics at the same time.

## 3.3. Group characteristics

Individuals usually belong to many groups, but these are not just any aggregation of people. Depending on a person's perspective, most definitions stress the ideas of common perception, common goal, interdependence and interaction, history, size, and organizational structure (particular norms and procedures in a certain group).

## 3.3.1. Subjects

Many of the past experimental GDSS studies used groups of students formed solely to address a task created for an experiment. Experimental groups were not created because they had the desire to solve a particular problem, but to conduct an experiment $[5]$ . Therefore, they rarely have a vested interest and responsibility in the outcome of the studies and are likely to be less enthusiastic than field study groups $[75]$ .

## 3.3.2. Group development stage

Relationships and groups do develop and grow from the early stages of getting acquainted to the mature stages of effective, smooth functioning, and stable states and stagnation $[68]$ . Group dynamics research $[6]$ has pointed out the drawback of using zero-history groups to study group behavior. GDSS researchers (e.g. $[23]$ ) also point out the importance of using groups with a meaningful history and future. However, most GDSS studies have examined the effect of computer support during the first (and, in fact, the only) meeting of ad-hoc groups. Although it is difficult to pinpoint the developmental stage of a group at any specific time, the group development model, at least, implies that newly formed groups can show quite different behavior patterns from mature ones. Thus, GDSS research results may also be affected by the stage of development of a group used in the study.

In their laboratory GDSS research, Dennis et al. [13] show that, in general, there are differences between established and ad-hoc groups. Established groups are less afraid to be openly critical of their peers or of being impolite. Group members do not participate equally in meetings: some people dominate the discussion. They talk more about how the process should go and make more uninhibited comments.

However, the results of most field studies do not confirm this expectation. Most field studies have reported very positive findings from the use of a GDSS compared to a conventional method. Specifically, Vogel and Nunamaker $[79]$ report that the GDSS can be successfully used both, by mature groups where the members are familiar with each other and by groups specially formed to address a specific problem or question.

## 3.3.3. Norms

Norms are rules of behavior or proper ways of acting that have been accepted as appropriate by members of a group. Given a set of goals, norms define the kind of behavior which is (believed to be) necessary for or consistent with the realization of those goals $[36]$ . For example, Jarvenpaa et al. $[40]$ tested for differences in how each of the three software designer groups used the technology. They reported significant differences in performance and interaction measures among the groups examined under identical technological supports. Zigurs et al. $[88]$ observed that the norms of one experimental group were such that it chose not to use the GDSS during the experiment. Dennis et al. $[16]$ reported that group meetings can become more or less electronic, more or less conventional, depending on the preference of the subjects. They also showed a great deal of variance in the performance from the use of a GDSS between the established groups as compared to the ad-hoc groups.

## 3.4. GDSS configurations

The results paint a mixed picture of the utility of meeting support technologies. All components of the GDSS environment have been shown to impact the process and outcomes of GDSS research. For example, the facility has an effect on the outcome of meetings: hardware speed is important, structured group work procedures show generally positive effects, the role of group facilitation is also crucial $[18, 58, 59]$ , etc. Literature on the effects of computer-mediated communication also indicates that the use of technology will change the group process. For example, it influences interpersonal relations, roles, organizational work, job performance, group productivity, and decision-making activities $[9, 11, 37]$ . Thus, it is natural to expect that different GDSS technologies can affect meeting outcomes and, therefore, research findings.

## 4. Concluding remarks

Recently, organizations in the global business environment are much more interested in groupware systems that include group activities not necessarily in the same place or at the same time.

There is, and will be, a large number of decision-making problems that no one person can handle. In the global society, it is becoming more important to coordinate conflicts and collaborate in work with others in the distributed environment. This also amplifies the need for GDSS in geographically separated group.

The important question posed by this review of prior research is: what are the reasons for inconsistent results in this research, and even for experimental tests? Is a GDSS in face-to-face meetings simply not functionally advantageous or is the lack of positive effects from using a GDSS a result of serious limitations in the existing laboratory research?

Some technical configurations also cause negative effects: e.g. it becomes clear that conflicts are increased in a multi-workstation-based GDSS. Unlike a facilitator-driven GDSS, participants in a user-driven GDSS show less satisfaction with the initial use of the system.

The distinctive difference in the results between field and laboratory research may imply that the real effectiveness of a GDSS can only be recognized when real managers use it to tackle their live and formidable tasks. Of course, we cannot rule out the possibility that the results of field studies may be affected by the investigator's subjective optimism: results are mainly based on anecdotal evidence, rather than on rigorous measurement. However, results from all field studies reviewed here consistently show the positive effect of a GDSS, regardless of different technical design configurations, softwares, and tasks.
