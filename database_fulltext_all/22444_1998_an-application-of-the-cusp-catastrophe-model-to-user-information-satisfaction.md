# An application of the cusp catastrophe model to user information satisfaction

Vikram Sethi $^{a,*}$ , Ruth C. King $^{b}$

$^{a}$ CIS Department, Southwest Missouri State University, Springfield, Missouri 65804, USA $^{b}$ Department of Business Administration, College of Commerce and Business Administration, University of Illinois at Urbana Champagne, 350 Commerce West Building, 1206 South Sixth Street, Champagne, IL 61820, USA

Received 23 May 1997; accepted 15 February 1998

## Abstract

This study shows that there is a nonlinear relationship between overall user information satisfaction (UIS) and an index created by combining satisfaction responses to various information systems components/attributes. Several instruments have been developed in the IS literature to measure UIS. These measure UIS by asking respondents to rate their satisfaction/dissatisfaction with a variety of IS attributes; for example, EDP services, EDP staff, information products, and involvement in IS development. Tests of predictive validity, in the past, have shown only a 55% correlation between global UIS and UIS scores. Here, we show that the global UIS measure and IS factor scores follow a nonlinear relationship and are, in fact, cusp-distributed. The extent of use of IS is shown to be a splitting factor in this distribution and produces abrupt, catastrophic changes. Responses from faculty members at an academic institution were used to test for the distribution using IS component scores as the normal independent variable and the extent of use of IS as the splitting variable. The cusp model was a more efficient predictor than the linear model or a control equation using bimodal transformation. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: User information satisfaction; Catastrophe theory; Measurement

## 1. Introduction

Many of the phenomena observed in information systems (IS) studies, including behavioral responses such as user information satisfaction (UIS), have been mathematically modeled assuming smooth and continuous change in criterion values or, if not, as step functions. However, many psychological and attitudinal measures are not continuous or do not meet the assumptions of simple step functions, such as stationarity and independence of path.

In a nonlinear model, a sudden change in a behavior or criterion variable is not accompanied by abrupt or large changes in presumed causes. Such events are readily observable in society. 'A jury's perception of evidence, the productivity of a manufacturing organization, an industrial accident or disease, urban renewal, fatigue, and overwork' [31] are some examples of nonlinear change. One classical example of nonlinear behavior was presented by Zeeman [60]. According to him, two variables – anger and fear – cause an animal to fight or flee. Anger alone causes it to attack and fear alone causes it to flee. However, when the influence of both anger and fear increases simultaneously, unpredictable behavior may result. A slight preponderance of one influence over the other may cause discontinuous behavior. When a threshold level of causal variables is reached, behavior readily changes from one state to another. Similar examples of nonlinearity have been proposed in areas, such as the behavior of large groups, economic theory, inflation and expectations, and politics [35].

We suggest that UIS, a response to a variety of factors related to the delivery of information products and services, is also amenable to nonlinear modeling. Previous UIS research has modeled the construct as a linear combination of an individual user's responses to IS attributes, such as information delivery and the information product. Such a model assumes that a change of perception regarding any IS attribute is accompanied by a proportionate change in a user's global UIS evaluation. In this study, we test this assumption and propose an alternate formulation involving a nonlinear relationship.

A nonlinear model of UIS is suggested by past studies that have found attitudes to be amenable to catastrophic modeling $[9, 52]$ . According to Flay $[23]$ , there are five properties common to nonlinear phenomena:

\- the behavior of the phenomenon is bimodal for some values of the control factors;

\- abrupt, catastrophic changes are observed between one mode of behavior and another;

\- there is hysteresis; that is, the abrupt change from one mode of behavior to another takes place at different values of the control factors, depending on the direction of change;

\- there is an inaccessible zone of behavior for some values of the control factors; and

\- the possibility of divergent behavior is implied.

When these properties are found in a phenomenon, a condition known as singularity is present and a cusp catastrophe is implied [42].

Global UIS measures exhibit the above five properties. The relationship is controlled by the degree of use of the system. At lower levels of IS use, global UIS changes linearly with IS factor scores. However, at higher levels, small changes in factor scores can translate into catastrophic changes in global UIS.

The model, thus, accounts for differential judgments by two users interacting with the same set of IS resources, evaluating different IS attributes in much the same way but giving a much different judgment of their overall satisfaction with IS resources.

## 2. Analytical treatment of UIS

The analytical treatment of UIS has followed the Bailey and Pearson [5] formulation of UIS wherein:

$$
S _ {i} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} R _ {i j} W _ {i j}\tag{1}
$$

where $R_{ij}$ is the reaction to factor j by user i and $W_{ij}$ the importance of factor j to user i.

These models postulate that a user's attitude (in this case, UIS or $S_i$ ) can be determined by his/her evaluation of a system based on a set of relevant attributes $R_{ij}$ weighted by his/her perceptions of the importance of these attributes $W_{ij}$ . Alternate models have dropped the importance scale to use a model where UIS is a summation of attribute evaluations only (see e.g., Doll and Torkzadeh [17], Joshi [34] and Miller and Doyle [38]). In these cases, the UIS is measured as:

$$
S _ {i} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} R _ {i j}\tag{2}
$$

The critical elements of both models are the components employed and the compositional rules assumed; that is, the manner in which the model assumes that individuals combine the various pieces of information measured. The UIS model assumes the following:

\- the contribution to the effect for each attribute is multiplicative;

\- the powers of the terms in the product for each attribute are assumed to be 1; and

\- the product terms add over attributes to form an overall effect measure.

No studies have been performed to assess this model. In the absence of these assumptions, no measures may adequately reflect an individual's overall attitude.

These models are based on the theory of cognitive integration: how people derive an overall attitudinal disposition from an array of knowledge and beliefs that they hold about the attitude object. Anderson's information integration theory [4, 20, 43] provides the framework for cognitive integration in the form of two concepts: valuation and integration.

Valuation refers to the process of determining the evaluative scale value and the weights assigned to each item of information that contributes to the attitudinal judgment. The scale value varies from positive to negative and is considered independent of context and other cognitive elements. The weight parameter reflects the influence of the cognitive element in determining the overall attitude; it can vary from zero to one, and its value is influenced by context.

Integration refers to the process of combining the information units into an overall attitudinal judgment. In Anderson's theory, the integration process is one of algebraically combining the weight and scale value parameters into a single judgment. The algebraic rule that appears to best describe evaluative judgments such as person impressions is averaging: the numerator contains the sum of the products of weight times scale value (across all cognitive components) and the denominator is simply the sum of all weights. Information theory, however, allows for the possibility that other rules may be used for different judgment domains (e.g., summation might describe the value of a stock portfolio, and multiplication may be best suited for job performance evaluation).

There are several assumptions implied. For example, Devine et al. [16] note that the theory, as stated, postulates that: (1) there exists a memory store in which weight and scale value parameters are stored for each cognition; (2) the parameters for each relevant cognition are retrieved; (3) the algebraic operations are performed on those parameters; and (4) the resulting implicit evaluation response is used as the basis for overt response. They then raise a variety of questions related to the above assumptions. Where do the weights and scale values come from? How can there be a store large enough to contain all the weights and scale values for all cognitive elements, especially given that each cognitive element must have two parameters for each possible judgment dimension? How accurate is the 'synaptic calculator' that conducts the needed algebraic manipulations on the parameter values? Is the implicit judgment discarded upon overt response, or is a decision made whether or not to store it for future use?

In response to the above questions, they suggest that there are other mechanisms used for attitude formation, derived from the broader set of cognitive activities potentially involved in producing attitude responses. For example, Stone $[51]$ suggests a random walk model, under which individuals randomly sample knowledge and beliefs about the attitude object, extracting a value from each sampling. A positive response is generated when the positive count reaches a specified threshold or a negative response is generated when a specified negative count is reached. The first of these two thresholds to be attained will trigger a response.

## 3. Review of impression and attitude formation

Other studies in social psychology provide alternative viewpoints to understand the processes used by individuals to form impressions. These studies focus on social information processing and the principle of cognitive economy $[39]$ , which proposes that people seek to simplify external complexity by organizing information into categories and allowing a similar response to all occurrences of a category $[37]$ .

Fiske and Neuberg [22] provide one such model – the continuum model of impression formation – to explain how individuals process information to form impressions about others. According to the model, people form impressions through a variety of processes which lie on a continuum that reflects the extent to which a person utilizes a target individual's attributes. Toward one end of the continuum are category-based processes that use a target's category membership ignoring its particular attributes. Toward the other end of the continuum are individuating processes that use a target's particular attributes, while ignoring its category membership.

There are several components to this model: (1) perceivers initially categorize others, and their immediate responses may be triggered by this assigned category; (2) if the target is at least minimally relevant, perceivers may give further attention to attribute information based on informational and motivational factors; (3) attention to attributes mediates use of the processes along the continuum; (4) when possible, perceivers will confirm the initial category; (5) when an initial category cannot be confirmed, perceivers will recategorize; and (6) when recategorization fails, individuals will undertake an attribute-by-attribute integration to form a new assessment.

This model is not as much an alternative to the information integration theory as it is an extension. Information integration occurs but not all the time. When it occurs depends on the relevance and motivation to the perceiver. The model suggests that the rapid ‘categorization’ process requires no attention and is given priority over attribute-based processes. Similar observations have been made by Bruner $[6]$ and Smith $[50]$ . Initial categorization is a necessary response to information overload $[21]$ and needed by the perceiver to make simple inferences $[3]$ . Another characteristic of category-based responses is that they are accessible immediately upon formation. Once activated, these responses are potentially more accessible than attribute-based information and have priority over attribute information.

Only when perceivers are confronted with target attributes in the absence of any category label and these attributes do not elicit an implied category label, must they undertake attribute evaluation. A key factor in the model is why perceivers choose to undertake the additional processing necessary to absorb all available attribute information. The probability of a perceiver doing so increases when the perceiver is highly dependent on the outcome. For example, when a new boss steps in, subordinates acquire substantial quantities of information to generate individuated judgments about the new person.

Motivation and involvement have also been noted to increase issue relevant thinking $[8]$ . In experiments conducted by Petty $[41]$ , subjects who were led to believe that the topic of a message will affect their lives were less persuaded by weak messages but more persuaded by strong ones. Recently, Howard-Pitney et al. $[32]$ found that higher personal relevance increased systematic processing and this processing tended to be biased. These studies have led to suggestions that at more extreme levels of issue involvement and motivation, processing may produce curvilinear effects on persuasion and attitudes $[7, 14]$ .

The term ‘issue involvement’ in this context has been used in a variety of different ways, such as personal relevance [49] or commitment to one side of an issue [1]. The term ‘content involvement’ has also been used to describe the idea that some messages stimulate greater mental processing than others [15]. In all cases, the underlying theme is that the self-interest of the perceiver guides the decision of when to undertake extensive attribute evaluation.

The issue of involvement is also a key factor in the attitude-behavior consistency theories proposed by Fazio and Zana [18]. In Fazio's model of attitude accessibility, an attitude is viewed as an association in memory between a given object and a perceiver's evaluation of that object. The strength of the attitude can vary and determines if that attitude is activated when the perceiver encounters the object. Only if there is a strong association with the object is it likely that the evaluation is activated upon its observation. In various priming experiments conducted by Fazio [19], subjects who had strong evaluative associations with objects showed automatic activation of the evaluation at the presentation of the object. These and other experiments by Schneider [44] and Shiffrin [48] have shown that with strong memory association, automotive activation of attitude occurs. Once activated, the attitude will serve as a 'filter' through which the object is perceived. These selective perceptions may cause a further accentuation of the existing attitude. There is no deliberate reflection or reasoning such a sequence.

This does not rule out the existence of deliberate reasoning as proposed by Ajzen [2]. The question is: when do individuals choose to be involved in deliberate reasoning? Effortful or deliberate reasoning here refers to the consideration of specific attributes of the attitude object. In cases where resultant outcomes or behavior are important to the individual, deliberate reasoning is employed. Sherman and Fazio [47] provide examples of deciding which college to attend or which job to accept as cases of attribute evaluation. Kruglanski's [36] general theory of epistemology and Fazio's MODE model of attitude-behavior relation both refer to differential processing based on motivation and attitude relevance to the perceiver.

## 4. Review of catastrophe theory

Catastrophe theory has its roots in mathematics [53, 54, 56, 57], physics [24, 45], and biology [59]. The theory seeks to characterize phenomena where small variations in independent variables may cause large variations in a dependent variable, or where ‘cata-strophic change’ may occur.

A catastrophe, in general, is any discontinuous transition that occurs when a system can have more than one stable state or can follow more than one stable path of change. The catastrophe is the ‘jump’ from one stable state to another. The transition is discontinuous because intervening states are not stable.

Based on Thom [55], seven elementary forms of catastrophe can be identified for such transitions to occur. These catastrophes can be illustrated by graphs that show stable states in a ‘behavior space’ and are the basic models for many different processes. All forms result from control factors and behavior axes which together define the behavior space, when every possible equilibrium state of the system is represented by a single point, and the points form smooth surfaces.

The seven forms are: the fold; cusp; swallowtail; butterfly; wave crest; hair; and the mushroom.

The forms are hierarchical and vary in the complexity of the behavioral spectrum they encompass. In these forms, the fold is a function of one control variable; the cusp surface is a three-dimensional surface formed as a function of two control variables; the swallowtail, wave crest, and hair have three controls; and the butterfly and mushroom are functions of four control variables.

Of these elementary forms, the cusp catastrophe has been frequently used to model attitude formation and change. The general form of the cusp is shown in Fig. 1.

The cusp catastrophe occurs in systems whose behavior is dependent on two control factors. Its graph is three-dimensional with every point on the surface representing an equilibrium state. All points on the underside of the pleat (shown shaded) are unstable, points along the fold line are semi-stable, and all other points are stable. For certain conditions of the two control factors, there are two possible stable states, one on the upper surface of the pleat and one on the lower.

Fig. 1. The general form of the cusp catastrophe.

Consider two points at the far edge of the surface (near the axis of control factor 2). They represent systems with the same value of control factor 1, but slightly different values of control factor 2. If the value of control factor 1 increases, the points move forward toward the front of the surface, tracing parallel paths. If both paths are on one side of the pleat, the behavior of both systems is the same. If, however, one travels to the upper surface and the other to the bottom surface, the behavior of the two systems is divergent. Thus, two points start together, undergo the same transition but, at the end of the change, are at very different stable states.

The figure also shows discontinuous change when a point moves from the left to the right of the surface. At the far edge of the surface, a point can pass smoothly from left to right or vice versa. But if the point is at the front of the surface (high factor 1), a discontinuous jump will occur at the pleat. This is called hysteresis and is found to occur in many dynamic systems, from electro-magnetic phenomena to manic-depressive psychoses.

## 5. An alternative formulation of UIS

The literature on information processing and impression formation suggests two categories of information processing: deliberate and automatic. Theories related to deliberate processing suggest that people expend cognitive effort to evaluate information and integrate it with prior information in forming an attitude or judgment. This occurs if the information is important or relevant to the person. When this happens, Anderson's information integration theory is applicable. Studies suggest that prior categorization and simple decision rules are the means by which people arrive at an evaluation or judgment when the attitude object is less important or relevant to the perceiver.

We propose that the above categories of processing are also applicable to IS users. Our formulation of UIS is based on two factors: (a) an evaluation by a user of different IS attributes; and (b) the extent of use of IS. In this formulation, we use the non-weighted model of

Eq. 2. We propose that UIS, factor scores, and extent of use together produce a response surface of the cusp catastrophe, as shown in Fig. 2.

The control variables in the model are (a) the normal factor which represents the model scores (the normal factor), and (b) the splitting factor which is the extent of use of the system.

The normal factor is so called because, at the back of the surface where the splitting factor (b) is small, the behavioral variable UIS is dependent only on its magnitude and increases continuously with it. The control factor (b) is called the splitting factor because increases in its magnitude lead to a progressively larger divergence between the top and the bottom of the response surface.

One of the key concepts in attitude research is that the formation of attitudes involves selective information processing, which involves the variety of forms in which information can be organized.

Information could be ignored as irrelevant, recognized immediately, or become an object of thought. Tversky and Kahneman [58] have shown that there are general rules that are followed in everyday contexts regarding information absorption. When individuals deal with information concerning a topic or issue in which they are involved, there is more thorough processing.

Therefore, the way things are interpreted depends on the perspective from which they are viewed. To the extent that an individual is involved in an issue, his/her outlook serves as an anchor for attitude formation. Thus, high involvement may well produce exaggerated responses. This has been shown to be the general case by Sherif [46].

It seems likely that the extent of use of an IS provides similar information filtering through UIS. For highly involved users, a perception change regarding an IS factor may produce an exaggerated response in UIS evaluation.

## 5.1. Model dynamics

Fig. 2 can now be explained in low, moderate, and high use cases. When the extent of use is small, the range of UIS is relatively constrained. Individuals tend to be neutral toward UIS. This is reflected by movement between points A and B in the low use region. The vertical distance represents the range of UIS relative to the level of satisfaction/dissatisfaction toward individual components of IS. To the extent that UIS is present, it is directly related to the level of satisfaction with individual IS attributes.

![](/api/attachments/7UVQ3SE8/fulltext/images/0d8b2c39b84b38293850fc5c52bea41a360d0fe475ab85059f09bb83ca4513a9.jpg)  
Fig. 2. The UIS cusp catastrophe.

Changes in satisfaction levels with IS attributes thus translate relatively directly to differences in UIS, but at a slow rate and within modest levels of UIS. Thus, if an uninvolved user were to become dissatisfied with any IS component, a fairly large increase in dissatisfaction would be necessary to move the user from moderate UIS to low UIS. The reverse is also true as shown in the movement between points A and B.

When the extent of use is moderate, individual responses become more wide-ranging. Users are less likely to be UIS neutral over a wide range of IS component satisfaction/dissatisfaction levels. Users may exhibit higher levels of satisfaction/dissatisfaction than they would at low extent of use levels. Because IS has a greater impact on these users, stronger attitudinal responses are associated with any change of IS components. Small changes in component satisfaction can result in fairly substantial changes in UIS. The transition in UIS, however, is smooth. There is no discontinuous shift from dissatisfaction to high satisfaction.

If the extent of use increases beyond moderate levels, behavioral responses move out to points C, D, and E, as represented by the fold. UIS response in this case can be bimodal – users are either dissatisfied with IS or are highly satisfied. They are not neutral in their evaluations. Users resist changes in UIS levels – a highly satisfied user will be satisfied even when moderately dissatisfied with IS components.

Consider the case of a user highly involved with IS but not reporting satisfaction with IS attributes. For this individual to report a high UIS score, satisfaction levels on all IS attributes must be high. Only this situation will produce a shift from C to D. The reverse is also true. A highly-involved user who reports a high UIS rating will not become dissatisfied until he/she reaches a threshold value of dissatisfaction on all attributes and makes a switch from point D to point E.

## 6. Study design

Data to test the cusp UIS model was collected using a mail questionnaire sent to faculty members at a midwestern US academic institution. The questionnaire was mailed to 75 faculty members at the College of Business Administration. Fifty-five questionnaires were returned and used for data analysis. All respondents were assured confidentiality and anonymity.

The questionnaire was designed to elicit responses related to:

1. overall UIS with the IS function at the university;

2. extent of use of computer resources; and

3. factors or components of IS that are important to the respondents and the degree of satisfaction/dissatisfaction of the respondents with each of these factors.

Prior to questionnaire development, interviews were conducted with fifteen faculty members. In addition, interviews were conducted with six department chairs and four deans. Each interview lasted about a half hour and was recorded and transcribed. Based on these interviews, sixteen factors were identified as being relevant to the measurement of UIS: these are shown in Appendix A and relate to satisfaction with hardware, computer labs, networks, support staff skills and responsiveness, and training programs available to faculty.

During the course of the interviews, questions relating to the use of IS resources were also asked to judge their extent of use of IS. Applications identified in these interviews were grouped into seven items which were included in the questionnaire (Appendix B).

Finally, all respondents were asked for an overall evaluation of computer resources at the university. This question appeared on the first page of the questionnaire and was designed to measure overall UIS on a 1–5 scale, with 1 being extremely dissatisfied and 5 being extremely satisfied.

## 7. Model estimation

In a cusp model, changes between the two equilibrium states of behavior are governed by two control parameters - asymmetry, $a$ , and bifurcation, $b$ . The response surface is then a set of points for which

$$
y ^ {3} - b y - a = 0\tag{3}
$$

Cobb [10-12] determined that the cusp catastrophe can be transformed into a probability density function (pdf) through the application of the Wright-Ito formulation. The cusp pdf is dimensional. At low values of $b$ , it is unimodal, resembling a normal distribution, and at higher values, the pdf becomes increasingly bimodal.

Guastello [25] has shown that the cusp hypothesis may be evaluated through the use of the polynomial regression equation:

$$
z _ {2} - z _ {1} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} z _ {1} ^ {3} + \beta_ {2} z _ {1} ^ {2} + \beta_ {3} b z _ {1} + \beta_ {4} a\tag{4}
$$

where a and b are control parameters and $z_{1}$ and $z_{2}$ the standardizations of the criteria $y_{i}$ such that:

$$
z _ {i} = \frac {(y _ {i} - \lambda)}{s},
$$

where $\lambda$ and s are the lower limits (estimate of location) and sample standard deviation (estimation of scale), respectively, of y. Control variables are also normalized. Values of $\lambda$ may be arbitrarily set equal to 0 or sample minimum values may be used. The $R^{2}$ coefficient for Eq. 4 indicates the degree to which the data fit the topological model. In practice, the variables hypothesized to correspond to a and b can be singular or plural.

The purpose of the location parameter, $\lambda$ , is to fix a zero point on the criterion measurement. Either the lowest possible value of y or the observed value of y for the sample under study may be chosen. The scale parameter represents dispersion around a mode rather than dispersion around a mean. The sample standard deviation is a good estimate of dispersion around the mode of bifurcation and asymmetry variables since these would be normally distributed. In the case of the criterion variable (y or UIS), the standard deviation becomes a less perfect measure of scale. The discrepancy is dependent upon the amount of unfolding in the surface that underlies the observed data distribution. As a result, the difference between the standard deviation and scale is reflected in $\beta_{1}$ , which is associated with the cubic potential.

The terms of Eq. 4 are entered in the order in which they are written. If the cubic term is significant, the model is in fact classified as a cusp. If the quadratic term is also significant, one threshold of the cusp is better represented than the other, and/or the distances from the cusp point to the modes are unequal. The significance of the bz and a terms indicates that those variables contribute to the latent bifurcation and asymmetry parameters, respectively. In this application, the cubic term is more important in order to show the presence of a cusp.

A significant $R^{2}$ coefficient is not sufficient to accept the model. It is necessary to determine the $R^{2}$ coefficient for a next-of-kin model which gives the change in the dependent measure in a linear function of the experimental variables a and b:

$$
y _ {2} - y _ {1} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} b + \beta_ {2} a\tag{5}
$$

Note that no pre-estimation of location or scale is used here. The surface of the cusp has a smooth (low b) and an unfolded (high b) region. It is now possible to ask if the empirical cusp is smooth by testing a variant of Eq. 5:

$$
y _ {2} = \beta_ {0} ^ {\prime} + \beta_ {1} ^ {\prime} y _ {1} + \beta_ {2} ^ {\prime} b + \beta_ {3} ^ {\prime} a\tag{6}
$$

Eq. 6 is similar to Eq. 5, with $y_{1}$ moved from the left to the right side of the equal sign and multiplied by a new coefficient.

Only an inspection of the coefficients is necessary, since previous applications of catastrophe theory have demonstrated that such differences are dramatic $[13, 27, 28, 29, 30]$ . Thus, the following comparisons are made:

1. If $R^{2}$ for the cusp model is greater than or equal to $R^{2}$ for the linear change model, the change is better explained by the cusp than the linear model;

2. If $R^{2}$ for the cusp is greater than $R^{2}$ for the pre-post model (Eq. 6), the change is more catastrophic than smooth; and

3. If the first rule holds but the second rule does not, the pre-post model may be adopted as the best predictive model, but the cusp is still accepted as a better explanation of change that the linear model.

## 7.1. Critical points

Implicit discriminant functions (IDFs) and bimodal transformation are further investigations into the nature of the catastrophic activity. It is possible that the cusp point and the unfolding may not be located in the center of the data distribution but in the upper or lower tail of the criterion distribution [26]. The problem is managed by data recoding rules, which provide utility transformations of the criterion [40] and are called implicit discriminant functions.

A cusp surface may be restricted in range at the top or bottom of the scale or in the unfolding. These restrictions may be corrected by using a bimodal transformation, which, with IDFs, are data recoding rules that effectively rotate the surface around the data to see if a catastrophic shift is more likely at the top or the lower end of the scale. If the data transformations are successful in capturing a discontinuity in the data distribution, the $R^{2}$ coefficient for Eq. 4 will increase.

For the bimodal transformation, criterion scores less than -1s are recoded to 0.00. For the upper IDF, criterion scores less than +1s are recoded to 0.00. For the lower IDF, scores above -1s are recoded to 0.00. The lower IDF employs a retrograde transformation, such that resulting positive difference scores reflect positive changes. The absolute value of the difference score increases as it approaches the -1s point.

The basic cusp model is superior to both the linear change model and the constant change (pre-post) model. The best catastrophe model was the lower IDF, which was superior to all control equations.

## 9. Conclusion

We have shown that there is a nonlinear relationship between a user's overall evaluation of UIS and an index created by averaging the user's satisfaction responses to different IS-related attributes. We have also shown that the extent of use of IS is a control factor affecting the nonlinear relationship. Using a catastrophe model, we have confirmed that UIS is cusp-distributed with two control variables – extent of use and IS factor scores.

The analysis in this study is motivated by several observations. First, tests of predictive validity of UIS have ranged from 0.55 [33] to 0.79 [5]. This variance suggests that the relationship between global UIS and factor scores is more complex than previously assumed. A second motivating factor for the study is to understand why different users who are exposed to the same IT environment evaluate the environment differently. A review of the attitude formation and change literature suggests that user attitudes and user information processing leading to attitude formation is not always attribute-based.

In this study, we have used two streams of literature to propose that the extent of use of IS provides relevance to the user for attitude formation. When the extent of use is low, global UIS reported by users does not change linearly with a change in IS attributes. Prior beliefs are never updated by fresh attribute information since attribute processing does not occur. However, in the case of high use, attribute information affects global UIS and can cause nonlinear change in global UIS reports.

We hope that future research will use the catastrophe model to examine changes in UIS due to an evolving user environment. It should be noted, however, that the cusp model is a broad framework that can well be adapted to attitude changes other than UIS.
