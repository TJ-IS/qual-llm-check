# Electronic data interchange: a multi-industry investigation using grounded theory

Connie W. Crook $^{*}$ , Ram L. Kumar $^{1}$

The Department of Information and Operations Management (IOM), The Belk College of Business Administration, The University of North Carolina at Charlotte, 9201 University City Boulevard, Charlotte, NC 28223, USA

Accepted 22 February 1998

## Abstract

Interorganizational systems (IOS) based on electronic data interchange (EDI) are being increasingly used. However, trading partners in EDI-based IOS vary in terms of their extent of EDI use. This paper presents a grounded-theory based study of EDI use in four organizations in different industries. The methodology allows the study of EDI use from perspectives of causal conditions, types of EDI use, strategies for encouraging EDI use, consequences of EDI use, and contextual factors. Such a qualitative study facilitates an understanding of how and why different factors impact EDI use. Under-researched issues, such as internal use of EDI, impact of EDI on different types of employees, and use of EDI for collective benefit of multiple organizations are discussed. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Interorganizational systems; Grounded theory; Electronic data interchange

## 1. Introduction

Information systems (IS) professionals face many obstacles in managing the use of technology in the firm. With the introduction of computer technologies that transcend traditional company boundaries, firms must determine new methods for successfully diffusing this technology. IS that are developed and used by two or more networked organizations are referred to as interorganizational systems (IOS) [2]. An example of an IOS is electronic data interchange (EDI).

Individual organizations first adopt EDI technology and then attempt to increase its use in order to derive financial benefits $[9]$ or competitive advantage $[10, 16, 17, 21]$ . However, some firms are coerced into using EDI.

Stages of diffusion have been proposed by many researchers $[14]$ . Cooper and Zmud $[4]$ proposed a model of technology diffusion that begins with adoption and then progresses from adaptation of the technology to an infusion stage that includes organizational perspectives. Massetti and Zmud $[12]$ also found that EDI adoption and diffusion could be classified into volume (number of document exchanges using EDI), diversity (number of distinct document types a company handles with its trading partners), breadth (number of established connections with external trading partners), and depth (degree of electronic consolidation between two or more trading partners). While these two perspectives have some similarities, Massetti and Zmud concluded that the measures identified for each classification included both functional and organizational components. Cooper and Zmud in their study of material requirements planning diffusion evaluated organizational issues only in their infusion stage. Our study will use Massetti and Zmud's suggestion that organizational issues must be evaluated at each stage of technology diffusion.

Trading partner considerations are important. Each partner involved in EDI normally has different standards, technology, and ways of conducting business. Hart and Saunders [7] studied EDI adoption and use between organizations and identified several power and trust related factors that impact electronic interrelationships between organizations.

Other studies have examined the phenomenon of EDI use. Case-based $[19, 20]$ and survey-based $[1, 8, 15]$ approaches have been used to explore issues of EDI adoption and diffusion. This paper examines the phenomenon of EDI use in greater detail, using Strauss and Corbin's $[18]$ grounded theory approach. According to Yin $[22]$ , this approach uses “analytic generalization” to develop a generalization of patterns and theory. It allows theory to emerge and be inductively developed from field study insights. In contrast, quantitative studies must recognize the factors that exist before the study is initiated.

## 2. Research methodology

The goal of this research is to generate a descriptive and explanatory theory of EDI use based on grounded theory $[13, 18]$ . The process (as outlined in $[5]$ ) includes

1. iterative data collection and analysis,

2. generation of concepts (example: customer-initiated EDI is a concept),

3. development of categories and relating them to concepts (Example: industry, organizational, and individual consequences are concepts in the category consequences of EDI use),

4. theoretical sampling (Example: sampling for different types of consequences of EDI use), and

5. use of the coding paradigm of conditions, context, strategies, and consequences to discuss the phenomenon of interest (EDI use).

## 2.1. Coding

The grounded theory approach uses the views of participants to develop a theory by analyzing their responses using three types of coding; open, axial, and selective. In open coding, the researcher examines the qualitative information gathered through individual interviews and secondary information sources to develop categories that relate to the information collected. Often, subcategories called properties emerge in this process. The researcher then examines the categories for any interrelationships that may exist. This portion of the process is called axial coding, where the researchers examine the relationships between the central phenomenon of interest, the causal conditions that relate to the phenomenon, the context in which the phenomena exists, and any strategies or consequences of the emerging phenomena. In this study, EDI use is the phenomenon of interest. Causal conditions refer to the reasons why organizations use EDI. Organizations can apply different strategies to increase EDI use. Consequences of adopting these strategies could impact individuals, organizations, or groups of organizations (within an industry). The context is a collection of organizational and environmental conditions that moderate the interactions among the causal conditions, phenomenon, strategies, and consequences. Open coding identifies several categories of causal conditions, phenomenon, strategies, and consequences. After open and axial coding, the researcher develops a narrative (selective coding) that integrates the results of axial coding $[3]$ .

The researcher in grounded theory methodology uses a process approach that seeks to constantly compare additional information with established categories. The researcher continues to look for instances of that category until no more information can be found. This approach is called constant comparative analysis. Grounded theory also seeks to begin with participants that are selected based on common attributes and then diversifies to seek participants that are heterogeneous. This theoretical sampling looks for initial conditions that support the phenomena and then for conditions that are not consistent with the initial categories. In this study, the researchers enhanced theoretical sensitivity by beginning with the literature on EDI and innovation diffusion and then attempted to find empirical evidence relating to factors identified by the literature.

## 2.2. Site selection

Major criteria for selecting the study participants were that (a) the organizations should be in different industrial segments, (b) the organizations should differ in terms of EDI use, and (c) the organizations should be willing to share their experiences with the researchers. Diversity among organizations in terms of industry and degree of EDI implementation facilitated “theoretical replication” [22]. In other words, it is reasonable to expect differences between sites. Personal and institutional contacts were used to identify four organizations in the banking, chemical, tobacco, and textile industries. All organizations were promised anonymity and a formal non-disclosure agreement was signed in one case.

The tobacco company is part of a large diversified corporation with over 70 000 employees and annual sales of US\$ 15 billion. The bank employs over 61 000 personnel and has assets of US\$ 170 billion. The chemical company is part of a large, diversified, multinational corporation with several manufacturing plants worldwide; it has over 29 000 employees and sales of US\$ 8 billion. The textile company has sales of approximately US\$ 1.1 billion and over 13 000 employees.

The four organizations also differ in their degree of EDI use. The tobacco company has managed to persuade almost 100% of its suppliers to use EDI and has aggressively encouraged its use by small suppliers. The chemical company conducts EDI transactions with only its larger business partners, but has greater depth and diversity of EDI applications. The level of EDI use at the textile company is somewhere between the other two. It has a larger number of its customers using EDI when compared with the chemical company (breadth), but has less depth and diversity.

The bank is using EDI as a method of obtaining additional business. The main use of EDI is a comprehensive payable service for its customers. This cash management service is marketed to replace the customers accounts payable function and provides funds transfer and check writing for a fee. The bank has recognized the importance of EDI as the basis for innovative products and is presently evaluating other uses internal to the bank.

Multiple information gathering methods were employed, with open-ended interviews as the major method. Twelve participants from a variety of backgrounds were interviewed as shown in Table 1.

Each interview lasted from one and a half to three hours and involved two researchers. In three cases, the interviews were taped and transcribed while, in the other case, both researchers took notes. A variety of documents, such as outlines of EDI applications, memos relating to standards, and outlines of presentations made, provided valuable additional information. Archival records, such as company financial reports, were used to obtain background information.

Eisenhardt [6] discusses the fact that this high degree of interdependence between theory and evidence results in an empirically valid theory.

## 3. Research results

Opening and axial coding were used to develop a classification of concepts into context, causal conditions, phenomenon, strategies, and consequences.

## 3.1. Context for EDI use

The context for EDI use (Table 2) consisted of organizational factors and environmental factors.

## 3.1.1. Organizational factors

Three major organizational factors were organizational size, information technology (IT) capability, and senior management commitment.

Organization size: In all four organizations interviewed, diversity of EDI applications was more likely between large companies; large companies had more IS resources available for EDI application development. Although the bank participated in EDI transactions primarily with other large banks, it also sought smaller companies as customers for its EDI-related products.

Information technology capability: This is an aggregate term used to describe state-of-the-art technology and its use for productive business purposes. Organizations that have a high degree of IT knowledge integrate technology, people, and organizational factors in order to achieve business goals $[11]$ . The textile company was outsourcing much of its new development. Although they had successfully completed several EDI projects, downsizing had lead to the use of skilled outsourced IS personnel. The chemical company also experienced a lack of IS personnel due to recent downsizing and the loss of key EDI personnel to other companies. This lack of IT capability lead the company to seek specialists outside the company to fulfill IS requirements and hindered efforts to increase the number of trading partners using EDI and to develop new EDI applications.

Both the bank and the tobacco company were knowledgeable users of IT (had high IT capability). The tobacco company was a leader in EDI implementation, providing high quality resources to its development. It justified EDI development by showing that it could cut purchasing costs. The bank was a very effective user of IT with a variety of diverse IS products and services, making large IT investments and maintaining leading edge technology as well as skilled staff.

Senior management commitment: This factor is always critical to the successful IS use. The bank and the tobacco company were highly committed to EDI. Senior management in the tobacco company viewed it as needed to cut costs and gave customers deadlines to meet EDI implementation requirements. They sent a letter to all suppliers to set deadlines for becoming EDI-compatible and detailed support that would be provided.

The bank viewed EDI as a way of developing new growth-oriented electronic products. The textile company felt pressure from customers to implement EDI, while the chemical company felt that management was just beginning to understand the significance of EDI use. Each company stated that senior management commitment was key to the successful use of EDI.

All organizations felt that problems with EDI were often very visible to senior management. One participant from the chemical company stated that when things go wrong with EDI (e.g. “lost” Automatic Shipment Notifications (ASN)), top management is informed. While we were at the textile company, a call from a customer with an EDI complaint was received during our interview. One researcher asked if this happened frequently and the participant responded, “this customer always needs assurance and ackowledgment that the transaction has completed successfully.” She further stated, “top management knows when problems occur and puts pressure on us [the IS department] to hold the problems to a minimum.”

## 3.1.2. Environmental factors

Within the context of EDI use, industry experience, the nature of the relationship of the company with its suppliers, and the relationship with the customers are all key factors. Each of the firms had differences due to the nature of the industry. However, many of the factors that affect the environmental context are common across industries.

Industry experience with EDI: Several firms within each industry studied are effective users of EDI. The textile company views EDI as a method to aid competitive advantage. The threat of low cost foreign competitors has required the textile company to examine use of new technologies to make the domestic industry strong, e.g., Quick Response (QR) is a means of combating cost advantages of foreign textile firms.

The chemical and tobacco industry have many users of EDI, but differences exist between small and large companies. While links between the customer and the tobacco industry are strong, EDI has not diffused in supplier transactions. The banking industry has recognized the need for EDI. Several companies have joined the Financial Services Technology Consortium to develop products and strategies that can be used to strengthen the entire industry. This consortium has members from academic, banking, and other financial companies that follow new technologies.

Nature of the suppliers: This refers to size, management style (family-owned versus corporation), and the industry category. The chemical and tobacco companies both have a variety of suppliers. Different standards have to be reconciled and translations made to complete transactions.

The tobacco and the textile companies have a mix of both small and large suppliers. The bank has a variety of suppliers that range from individuals, other members of the financial industry, and the government. Such differences have an impact on the degree of EDI use, since not all suppliers are equally EDI-compatible.

Nature of the customers: The textile and tobacco companies mainly have retail customers, but the textile company also serves other industrial customers, e.g., it produces goods that are used in medical supplies. The chemical company serves other industries, e.g., it provides dyes to the textile industry. The bank customers can be individuals or a variety of small and large commercial organizations.

## 3.2. Causal conditions for EDI use

The conditions that enable the use of EDI included three major sub-categories, as shown in Table 3: (1) customer-initiated, (2) organization-initiated, and (3) reaction to competition. It is possible to find a combination of all of these three conditions in some organizations.

## 3.2.1. Customer-initiated EDI use

Often customers force a firm to increase use of EDI. This may be due to their need for improved information and customer service.

Loss of market share: Three companies exhibited customer-initiated EDI use to combat loss of market share: large customers of the textile company, grocery chains of the tobacco industry, and reengineering outbound logistics processes in the chemical industry using ASN.

Improved customer service: Vendor managed replenishment (VMR) along with Just-in-Time (JIT) enables the supplier to electronically inquire into the customer's inventory to determine whether supplies should be ordered. VMR requires a direct link into trading partner's inventory system to check the availability of supplies. If supplies are at a critical level, the vendor/supplier restocks the company inventory automatically, and an electronic bill is issued for payment. EDI, along with JIT inventory management, eliminates costly inventory and valuable, timely information concerning supply needs.

The chemical company provided improved customer service by warehousing inventory in a trailer for an important customer and allowing the customer to pay for goods as they were removed. Developing a cash management program based on EDI, allowed the bank's customers to monitor business cash flow; the bank could also monitor cash needs to determine reserve flows or investment opportunities. The textile and tobacco companies were considering VMR but had not implemented it.

## 3.2.2. Organization-initiated EDI use

Cost savings: Most of the companies recognized benefits of the move to EDI. The tobacco industry was able to reduce inventory. The chemical company recognized cost savings due to a time reduction in the supply chain. Reduced paperwork and data entry and reduced errors in rekeying were advantages in both the tobacco company and the bank.

## 3.2.3. Reaction to competition

Organizational level: EDI use has been common in the textile industry for 10 years. The tobacco company and the bank felt pressure from competitors to adopt EDI to maintain market share. Because customers of the chemical company used EDI as a means to measure their vendor's performance, it was necessary to reduce the error rate and provide faster error free maintenance, otherwise the number of orders would have been reduced. EDI was initiated in the bank to compete with other financial services companies in providing electronic cash management for their customers.

Industry level: To combat foreign competition, EDI is being implemented to provide QR throughout the textile supply chain. JIT inventory provides a competitive advantage for domestic firms.

Because US governmental regulations now permit other industries to provide financial services, banks have used EDI as a way to provide additional service to commercial customers, thereby providing an additional relationship to counter competitors.

## 3.3. Phenomena of EDI use

Table 4 shows the EDI usage by industry.

## 3.3.1. Increased customer transactions

All companies were concerned with increasing the number of EDI-based customer transactions. The textile and chemical companies have a fairly low percentage of trading partners on EDI. The bank has a vice president in charge of marketing EDI use.

## 3.3.2. Additional EDI transactions

The textile and tobacco companies initially introduced EDI to handle purchase orders; over time this has been extended to include Vendor Managed Replenishment (VMR). The use of ASN by the chemical and tobacco companies allows each party to manage the supply chain more effectively. The bank increased the types of electronic transactions available to customers. One application, comprehensive payables, provided customers with multiple payment options including check or electronic funds transfer.

## 3.3.3. Integration of EDI with other applications

Comprehensive payables is integrated with the bank's processes for checking/processing of electronic payments. The bank views EDI as a way for individual companies to either integrate or replace their cash management functions, thus providing more customer service.

## 3.3.4. Internal use of EDI

The use of EDI internally is limited in all four companies (see Table 4).

## 3.4. Strategies for EDI use

Strategies can be classified into three broad subcategories: coercion, support, and building long-term relationships (see Table 5).

## 3.4.1. Coercion

Threat of business loss: This threat was common to all, except the bank. In the case of the textile company, large powerful customers required EDI transaction with their small suppliers. The chemical company had large customers who provided annual contracts that were dependent on EDI performance (as measured by percentage of erroneous transactions, missing ASNs etc.). The tobacco company experienced coercion by large grocery stores who required compatible EDI in order to transact business. The tobacco company in turn issued deadlines to its suppliers for becoming EDI compatible.

## 3.4.2. Support

Technical support: This support usually took the form of assistance in selection of hardware, software, and value-added network (VAN). The tobacco company contracted with a EDI clearinghouse to help its suppliers; initially, it converted EDI transactions from the tobacco company into faxes and vice versa, but later provided a migration path to real EDI transactions. The bank set up the EDI infrastructure for its customers.

Training: This was provided to trading partners by all four organizations. Types of training included classes and on-site consultation.

Senior non-IS management support: Managers expressed strong support for increasing EDI transactions in all four organizations.

Incentives: All companies provided some level of incentives to encourage EDI use among trading partners. In the case of the chemical company its customers provided financial incentives based on EDI performance. The tobacco company helped its suppliers identify other business opportunities that were possible once they started using EDI.

## 3.4.3. Collaboration

Building long-term relationships: Some organizations exhibited a strong commitment to collaborating with trading partners to resolve any problems as well as explore other EDI applications. In the case of the textile company, efforts were underway to implement VMR with key customers. However, it was wrestling with several issues regarding integrating production and demand and thus VMR implied a high degree of trust, sharing of information, and intent to build a long term relationship. The textile company has developed simple contracts and relies on the strength of its established relationships. The tobacco company had already implemented VMR with some key customers, while the chemical company had not.

## 3.5. Consequences

Several consequences are illustrated in Table 6.

## 3.5.1. Industry consequences

Competitive advantage: The textile industry major industry associations actively encourages EDI use by forming technology groups to discuss common issues. In addition industry associations have formed partnerships with universities and research laboratories to explore ways to enhance competitiveness.

The financial services technology consortium (FSTC) was formed with members from major banking companies and universities. It was charged with development of electronic banking and related issues such as secure electronic transactions: the industry perceived electronic banking as a future growth area with considerable potential. The chemical and tobacco companies perceived EDI as something that major customers wanted and felt that it could provide financial benefits.

Conflicts: In the textile and tobacco industry, there were significant conflicts between small “mom and pop” organizations, who did not understand the need for EDI, and large organizations which are run by professional managers. The chemical company encountered conflicts as a result of supplying to diverse industries having different EDI standards.

## 3.5.2. Organizational consequences

Financial performance: All four organization reported that increased EDI use resulted in cost reduction. The textile company however pointed out that not all companies in their supply chain reported cost savings as a result of inventory reductions; some organizations felt that inventory was merely being pushed down the pipeline as a result of EDI and JIT. Some other sources of cost reduction were personnel and paperwork reduction. The tobacco company reported that they were able to reduce the staff in the purchasing department from 129 to 43 as a result of the use of EDI.

New products and services: The textile company was in the process of implementing VMR, the tobacco company had implemented VMR, and the chemical company was able to offer ASNs and JIT inventory to select customers. The bank began offering several EDI related products and services to its customers.

## 3.5.3. Individual consequences

IS Personnel had to learn new technologies (such as managing interfaces with value added networks) and integration of applications with EDI transactions. All four organizations reported that their IS employees experienced increased interaction with the organization's customers. For the chemical and banking companies, IS personnel accompanied sales personnel on key customer calls. The textiles and chemical companies reported that IS personnel felt that their work was more visible to senior management, especially if things went wrong.

## 4. Discussion

This research has studied the phenomenon of EDI use using four organizations from different industries.

Using grounded theory to conceptualize the qualitative data obtained from these organizations has helped to increase our understanding of the complex EDI use process.

Increasing the volume of existing EDI transactions received the most attention in the organizations studied. This was achieved by encouraging new trading partners to implement an existing set of EDI transactions. Organizations that had invested in the hardware, software, and operating procedures for a certain set of EDI transactions stand to gain by leveraging this investment over as many similar transactions as possible. Hence, increased use of EDI is often organization-initiated. However, success in increasing the volume of EDI transactions is likely to be impacted by environmental factors, such as the nature of customers and suppliers, industry experience with EDI, and organizational factors pertaining to customers and suppliers. Coercion and support were both used to increase the volume of EDI transactions. These strategies are focused externally on trading partners.

The IS department had a major role to play in increasing the volume of EDI. Its employees had to deal with greater customer interaction and enhanced visibility. The purchasing department also played a major role in the case of the tobacco company which had over 90% of its suppliers on EDI.

Integration of EDI with other applications could be organization-initiated or customer-initiated and involve process reengineering for the organization. Perceived benefits and costs of integration, as well as contextual factors such as organization size, IT capability, management support, and use of EDI in the industry are likely to be important determinants of EDI integration. Integration could lead to organizational benefits such as improved financial performance, improved customer service, and development of new products and services.

Internal EDI is typically organization-driven and use may be relatively slow due the absence of stimuli from customers or competition. Contextual factors such as organization size, IT capability, and senior management support are possibly more important than the nature of customers and suppliers and industry experience with EDI. We encountered limited internal use of EDI in our discussion with companies but many recognize the need to use internal EDI along with corporate intranets. Strategies to develop and use internal EDI could include technical and managerial support and incentives. Internal use of EDI could involve significant process changes and potentially impact a wider range of employees than use of EDI for external transactions.

## 5. Conclusions

This research has presented a grounded theory based framework for understanding the use of EDI based on data from multiple industries. Contextual factors, causal conditions, phenomena, strategies and consequences relating to EDI use have been analyzed using real-world data. A qualitative theory of EDI use grounded in real-world experience has been developed.

It has illustrated the use of the grounded theory methodology for understanding EDI use. Use of grounded theory adds to earlier research by providing a rich descriptive theory of the phenomenon of EDI use that is grounded in practice and generates interesting insight into the nature and management of EDI use.
