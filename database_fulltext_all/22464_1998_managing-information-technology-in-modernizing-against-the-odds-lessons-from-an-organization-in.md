Case Study

# Managing information technology in modernizing ‘against the odds’: Lessons from an organization in a less-developed country $^{1}$

Ramiro Montealegre\*

University of Colorado, Boulder College of Business and Administration, Campus Box 419, Boulder CO 80309-0419, USA

Received 7 April 1997; accepted 11 May 1998

## Abstract

This case study of a Guatemalan sugar company focuses on management's role in implementing information technology (IT) in less-developed countries. It shows that even where the intellectual skills required to use IT are very scarce, in the long run the company may gain more from ‘informating’ its environment than from merely ‘automating’ its production processes. But the study also shows that managers must consciously and actively develop these intellectual skills in workers. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Change management; Organizational change; IT management; IT implementation; Computing in developing countries

## 1. Introduction

In developed countries, the implementation of information technology (IT) requires broad and deep organizational changes  \( [18, 29, 42] \) . The greater the functionality of IT systems, the more learning and adjustment are required to use them, ranging from operator skills through organizational procedures and structures to cultural fabrics  \( [44, 47] \) . In less-developed countries (LDCs), technology-transfer efforts often fail because they do not take into account the distinctive nature of the business environment  \( [5, 38,

41]. In particular, ITs face one stumbling block right from the start: although they permeate the economic and production systems of the world rapidly and seemingly without effort, these technologies are not available ‘off the shelf.’ They have to be absorbed, mastered, and controlled. Their application calls for a pre-existing capability to insert new ideas, new practices, and new elements into a flexible system. They require ‘new’ intellectual skills – abstract thinking, problem-solving, and inference [47] – that are very scarce in the vast majority of LDCs [1, 13]. This paper analyzes the successful implementation of IT over two decades in an agroindustrial organization, Azucar, S.A., in an LDC, Guatemala. (The identities of the company and its managers have been disguised.)

During the time frame covered by this study (1973–1993), Guatemala suffered from political instability, low gross national product per person, lack of capital, high inflation, and a staggering illiteracy rate. In general, Guatemalan workers were less educated, less literate, more culturally and ethnically diverse, and less likely to share a common mother tongue than their counterparts in developed countries. In computer-related fields, there was a mismatch between the nation's needs and the types of professionals trained at educational institutions. Hence, one might have expected that, compared to those in any developed country, Guatemalan workers would be less likely to adapt to new technology and less able to communicate and cooperate in closely coordinated work teams. In the same period of time, however, Azucar, S.A., had a rich history of technological innovation, production improvement, and organizational change. First introduced in 1975, IT rapidly became a critical tool for managing the company's core business and management processes. In 1990, at the World Sugar Conference, Azucar was recognized for its excellence, receiving the “Best Technical Work” award for the implementation of its agricultural IS. This case study in modernization ‘against the odds’ analyzes was why Azucar successful.

The research reported here employed historical and longitudinal data, gathered from contact with managers and workers, site visits, and archival reviews, to document organizational change initiatives. These data provide

1. a thorough description of how IT influenced and was influenced by the social setting in which it was embedded; and

2. a historical analysis of the various phases of IT implementation.

Two questions guided this exploration:

\- How can management extract value from implementing IT in an LDC organization?

\- What role does management play in aligning the implementation of IT and the available resources (especially human skills) in an LDC environment?

The findings suggest that the impact of IT in LDCs depends on adapting to local circumstances. At the company studied, management was faced with the daunting task of managing the implementation of IT while simultaneously managing the development of the IT resources that were not provided in the national context.

## 2. Research issues for LDCs

Managers in LDCs face many of the same problems that affect organizations in developed countries, but they also have distinctive difficulties. In LDCs, government often exerts more influence over industries and organizations, controlling, for example, access to key resources and setting costs and prices. Innovation is limited by scarcity of managerial and technical personnel and capital, inadequate physical and information infrastructure, social and cultural diversity, and political barriers that modulate and distort market and competitive forces. A few companies, frequently with strong ties to the government or influential business groups, exercise disproportionate power, leading to implicit or explicit market-sharing arrangements and/or ruthless actions against competitors. The rules of the competitive game are often unclear and unstable, reflecting the underlying instability of the political and economic environments and the information imperfection of the markets. Finally, LDCs are generally more vulnerable to external economic shocks. Shifts in international prices of key exports or imports or access to primary export markets can dramatically affect competitive dynamics.

Austin suggests that, given the fragile national contexts in which LDCs organizations operate, managers who plan to introduce a new technology must systematically analyze the business environment in which that technology will be implemented. Existing research on IT transfer to LDCs has recognized, in particular, the need to develop skilled manpower $[4, 16, 33, 35]$ , to learn from the mistakes of other countries $[28]$ , to develop national IT policies $[23, 27]$ , to use proven technologies (i.e. established or blending edge vs. leading edge) $[6, 8, 9, 14]$ , and to employ consultants or develop international partnerships to import expertise along with the technology $[31, 32]$ . But most of these studies have focused on conditions rather than actions and behaviors, and on weaknesses rather than on ways of overcoming them. Furthermore, most studies have paid little attention to how the interaction between IT and the organization evolves over time $[17, 24, 39, 43]$ . Given the widespread prescription of IT for Third World countries, the urgency of their needs, and the paucity of their economic resources, it seems useful to understand the state of IT absorption in different countries and the effect of different technology levels, cultures, and priorities on IT implementation.

For many LDCs, such as Guatemala, the agroindustrial sector is a primary source of gross domestic product and economic stability. Unique industry characteristics include: seasonality of raw materials and production processes; perishability of raw materials and product which necessitates speed and care during procurement, processing, and transportation; variability in quantity and quality of raw materials due to weather, diseases, or pests; and stringent standards and regulations governing production processes and product quality, that may differ from country to country $[2]$ . Therefore, it is very information-intensive industry. Usually located in rural areas, agroindustrial organizations must deal with the urban–rural gap that characterizes LDCs. Rural areas in LDCs have less access to education, health care, communications, food and medicine distribution, and recreation systems and, therefore, are less attractive to skilled individuals $[13]$ . Guatemalan agroindustries, however, may be favored by the relative abundance of the resources required for conventional agricultural practices and manufacturing methods, including a large pool of unskilled labor and a pool of people trained in monitoring and controlling production, accounting, and financial analysis.

## 3. Research propositions

This research draws upon three streams of research (the implementation of IT in organizations, the transfer of IT to LDCs, and agroindustrial organizations in LDCs) to derive two hypotheses concerning the value that the organization extracts from IT, and the alignment of IT and the IT resources available to the company.

## 3.1. The value extracted from IT

Effective implementation of IT depends on the organization's vision of change, either by deliberate design or as an emergent phenomenon. Zuboff differentiates between ‘automating’ and ‘informating’ roles of IT. Automating means applying technology in ways that increase the self-acting and self-regulating capacities of machine systems, thus minimizing human intervention. Informating, on the other hand, means using IT to translate existing organizational processes, objects, behaviors, and events into explicit information. This latter environment requires the development of a new kind of human knowledge, which is relatively analytical, abstract, and conceptual. It focuses on people, rather than bypassing them.

Given that IT can be used to reduce human intervention in production or administration, it can help where skilled individuals are scarce. And given the scarcity of technical and managerial skills in LDCs like Guatemala, one may hypothesize that agroindustrial organizations there would derive a larger value from exploiting IT's automating capability rather than its informing capability. Reducing human intervention in production and administration, and lowering the skill levels to operate the new technology, would cut costs and increase flexibility. Such a proposition is in accord with Gordon Davis' suggestion that in an LDC “initial applications should focus on organizational functions with a pre-existing procedural discipline that make them amenable to computerization and avoid the violation or clash with organizational or national culture” [7]. Therefore, we propose

Hypothesis 1: Given the scarcity of technical and managerial skills in LDCs, information-intensive organizations would maximize value by using IT to automate the organization.

## 3.2. The alignment of IT and the availability of resources

Researchers studying IT transfer to LDCs have particularly deplored the lack of personnel skilled in IT management, analysis, design, and use $[3, 45]$ . Strategies that have been prescribed to supply this lack include:

1. capital investments in infrastructure;

2. education and the development of human capital;

3. technology transfer; and

4. integrated user-technical development [4].

In addition, the role that LDC governments can, and should, play in redressing the shortage through national IT planning and policies has been widely stressed. We argue here that managers are critical.

They must be able to change the technology to fit the organization and vice versa, as Leonard-Barton [20] claims, but also identify and develop or co-opt IT resources that are not as readily available as they are in a developed country. Therefore, we propose

Hypothesis 2: Given the imperfection and scarcities of the LDC environment, effective strategies for change will include developing and acquiring resources required by IT systems that are not readily provided by the national context.

## 4. Research strategy

Research on the implementation of IT in organizations and the transfer of IT to LDCs suggest that the introduction and assimilation of IT is a complex, 'messy,' rich process full of coercion and shared struggle, alignment and misalignment between the technology and environment, and uncertain payoffs. There is a growing recognition that IT implementation must be viewed within a broader social and organizational context, as a process of organizational change $[19, 22, 30, 34]$ . This study, therefore, was designed as a longitudinal, embedded, single case study $[46]$ . The design choice is consistent with the exploratory nature of the research questions, the researchers' inability to control the interactions among the constructs of interest, and the focus on a contemporary situation in which the major actors were still working with the company studied and were thus available for interview $[21]$ .

The strategy for data collection can be described as triangulation $[40]$ ; it involving multiple methods for collecting historical and longitudinal data. At no time did the author intervene in or manipulate the events unfolding within the company, country, or industry $[36]$ . The key method used for historical reconstruction was documentation review: the analysis, coding, and classification of annual reports, industry and government documents, trade magazines, organizational charts, organization meeting agendas and minutes, IT system design documents, manuals of various IT systems, and internal correspondence and memos. The historical reconstruction was complemented by data compiled from interviews with the complete group of middle and top managers for the episode (18 people), the leaders of the IT system implementations (7 people), systems users (42 users), and sugar industry experts (15 people). Every interview was tape-recorded. Transcription and initial coding of interviews were done within 36 h to ensure that interpretations elicited were preserved accurately and made available for planning subsequent interviews. However, it is important to note that the actors were interviewed about events that had transpired several years earlier, so there may have been some failures in memory, and, more importantly, self-justification may have changed the interpretation with the passage of time. In addition, the researcher lived among ‘those who are the data’ [37], residing on-site with the workers for 112 days solely to realize this investigation, and both spending nearly full time on the working site during official hours, as well as socializing with organization members outside work (eating, drinking, and sleeping in the same community). During this time, the researcher tried to learn the subjects’ rules for organizational life and to interact with them frequently to understand how and why they construct their social world. The researcher had no previous connection with the company and contemplates no future work with it. A case study database was used to manage the voluminous data.

The data collection, coding, and analysis proceeded iteratively $[11]$ ; that is, the early stages of the research were more open-ended, while the later stages were directed by the emerging concepts and, hence, involved more strategic selection of archival data and informants and more structured interview protocols. Data collection focused on context, technology, key players, and change processes. Among other things, the interviewer asked about: the environment, mission, structure, and culture of the firm and IT department; the history of systems development in the firm (including configurations, policies, and procedures); the rationale for acquiring the tools; the nature and methodology of the tools acquired; the IT implementation strategy and schedule; managerial commitment; user involvement; training; individual and team experiences with the technology; and changes associated with the use of IT systems in areas such as skills, knowledge, work, performance, and interaction with peers, superiors, and users. The data analysis was performed on site, and the transcripts were reviewed weekly. The data collected and the various analyses were shared with the key informants within Azucar in order to verify accuracy.

## 5. Case study: Azucar, S.A.

The history of IT at Azucar suggests that the changes occurred episodically, in bursts preceded and followed by longer periods of stability. This is consistent with Gersick's [10] punctuated equilibrium theory (periods of revolutionary change are triggered by a key event and interrupt periods of relative stability) and with Greiner's [12] revolutionary/evolutionary change perspective (all organizations pass through cycles of revolutionary and evolutionary change as a normal part of organizational growth and development). Our analysis clusters events into episodes to describe sequences of organizational change having a specifiable opening, trend of events, and outcome. The term ‘era’ is defined here to comprise related episodes of IT change. Fig. 1 portrays the three IT eras and six episodes that characterize change at Azucar between 1973 and 1993.

## 5.1. Company background

Azucar, S.A., produced sugar on the Guatemalan Republic's Pacific coast, a fertile region planted with sugar cane, cotton, and coffee. The Heredia family had acquired Azucar in the seventeenth century to offset production variability in its grain export business. From 1923 to 1973, Azucar was managed by Don Carlos Heredia, who retained overall responsibility for strategic direction and profitability. Don Carlos delegated management of day-to-day operations to a factory supervisor brought from Cuba and four farm administrators brought from Spain because of their expertise in sugar-cane administration. Each farm administrator had his own machinery, purchased his own supplies and materials, and managed his workers and farm according to his own style. $^{2}$ Before 1973, only isolated efforts had been made to maintain manual records of Azucar's diverse activities. Management was informal; written records and procedures were few; managers for the most part gathered information, gave orders, and coordinated operations orally.

## 5.2. The early years of information technology (1973–1979)

In 1973, after Don Carlos had been ill for a number of years, another family member, Don Jorge Heredia, assumed control of the company. Don Jorge had spent most of his life studying in the United States and had returned to Guatemala after receiving an MBA from Columbia University. Eager to put his new management theories into practice, he quickly restructured the company from a loosely run family business to a stock corporation in which he assumed the role of CEO and chairman of the board. Manuel Figueroa, a 29-year-old, college-educated civil engineer who had originally worked at Azucar as a consultant, subsequently assumed control of production and operations. A second important new hire, Roberto Duran, had recently completed an MBA in finance at the University of Southern California and was working for Azucar's external auditing company, Price Waterhouse.

The introduction of IT, led by Don Jorge, Duran, and Figueroa, was part of the initial vision for restructuring the business. When Azucar pioneered the adoption of IT in Guatemala, $^{3}$ management had neither a central IT plan nor any coherent view of how information technology could be used within the organization – only some general knowledge concerning the potential capabilities of computers ‘to mechanize difficult manual procedures.’ Azucar, at that time, was what Austin would call a ‘low technological capacity’ organization. In 1974, recognizing that Guatemala had few computer professionals with the needed technical skills, Azucar contracted with a local service bureau, Telectro S.A., to help select a computer system and to program a sugar-cane control application.

Azucar's strategy throughout the early era of IT, as is clear from the company's internal documentation and from interviews with its managers, was to become a ‘low-cost provider by improving operational efficiency’ [25] (see Fig. 2). Accordingly, IT was used to support core operational processes, such as sugar-cane quality control (mainly in the redesign of factory processes) and payroll, inventory control, materials control and agricultural processes (centralizing the farms administration). The implementation of IT systems involved changes such as the transfer of the sugar-cane control function from the factory to the administrative division, and centralizing the payroll operations to accommodate the computer situated within the administrative division.

Initially, management did not associate IT systems design efforts with the redesign of business processes nor did managers realize that the technology could be used to transform processes and streamline and integrate across functional boundaries. For example, management failed to recognize conflicts that were created between the factory and the administrative division when the sugar cane control function was transferred to the factory. “When I arrived in Azucar in November 1978,” remarked one laboratory manager,

The factory personnel had perfected a scheme of data gathering. The operators were supposed to collect a sample of the sugar cane every so often and send it to the laboratory. The supervisors, however, would estimate what they believed the sugar cane quality should be and report their estimate rather than the real number. Sometimes the number was off by a factor of three or more.

These and other comments by employees suggest that the new system was viewed by most employees as a hindrance. As management experimented with the technology, however, learning occurred. In 1975, for example, management began to explore payroll automation. “We decided to pilot the payroll system initially for workers in one farm,” a manager commented.

A devastating earthquake that struck Guatemala during the sugar harvest in 1976 destroyed much of Guatemala City and totally demolished many outlying towns, killing 23000 people and injuring 75000. Family emergencies took many sugar-cane cutters from the fields. Shortly after the harvest resumed, Azucar experienced a second major catastrophe: its workers, organization-wide, struck. After these events, Azucar's management was ready to support the expansion of the computerized payroll system to include all agricultural workers, even though this would require additional investment in computerization. An agricultural manager explained,

We began by asking the habilitadores [outside consultants, who received and distributed pay to the workers hired to harvest the sugar cane] to provide information about the workers: name, address, social security number, names of wife and children. Next, we gave each worker a notebook with an individual contract that explained his rights and obligations. He was trained in the ways of cutting and harvesting the cane and was given the opportunity to make more money than before, but pay was now directly tied to output and efficiency, which would be logged daily by the foreman in the notebook. On payday, each worker went to the pay window with his notebook and received his money, and the amount of pay was noted next to the total output. At the end of the harvest, efficient employees were given a letter of recommendation, which was considered a work guarantee for the following year's harvest.

Direct recruiting and management of farm workers was pilot tested at one farm during the 1978–1979 harvest [26]. Workers were given the choice of contracting with Azucar's Labor Relations Department or contracting through the habilitadores. The pilot was intended to demonstrate the virtues of the new system, and to work out IT and organizational problems. Not all workers offered glowing reviews of the new system, but on the whole it seemed to be an improvement. A field supervisor recalled,

When the change was introduced, cane cutters even slept in the fields to make sure that the sugar cane was properly weighted by the sugar-cane lifting teams that worked twenty-four hours of the day. As time went by, however, they began to trust the new system, especially since it was very agile and was providing them with a higher payment than ever before. We had to begin paying weekly instead of semi-monthly.

Discussion: By the end of this era, Azucar integrated its database to include information on the workers' performance, their salaries, etc. This database was used first to automate the sugar-cane control and payroll processes; the system provided better data integrity, increased efficiency, and tighter security and control. Next, the database was used to show the managers how to strengthen their link with the workers and to regain control over agricultural processes that had been passed to habilitadores and farm administrators. Both of these changes were important to the company, but management concluded that “bypassing the habilitadores was more important than the efficiency and control gained with the implementation of the initial IT systems.”

Initially, Azucar's managers had no previous exposure to IT implementations but did have a basic understanding of IT from their business administration education. Concerned that the company did not have the experience, skills, or resources to implement a full system even within an extended time scale, they took action to assess the firm's technological capacity, and transfer expertise into the firm, providing formal and informal education on the technology, and increasing the level of technical skills in the firm.

## 5.3. The centralization of IT (1980–87)

The early 1980s were marked by drastic changes in Guatemalan political and economic factors, as well as changes in the international sugar market and political instability in the Central American region. At Azucar, the strategy became to produce efficiently at low cost, while coping with external forces (see Fig. 3). IT was used to improve and centralize the monitoring and control of organization-wide processes and resources.

Alfonso Jordan, a public auditor and accountant, was named Chief of the Computer and Systems

The top management's lack of control over costs was seen as an economic threat, and IT systems were viewed as a way to gain control or as a way to make middle managers aware of the need for control. Jordan's plan called for ‘totally integrated systems’ to be built by obtaining detailed data from day-to-day operational activities, filtering out data that presumably would be of little interest to higher echelons of management, and summarizing and condensing the remainder in reports. Computer printouts – and later, direct access to data via computer terminals – were supposed to enable top managers to omnisciently control all organizational activities.

The ‘Computer and Systems’ Department changed its name to ‘Data Processing Center,’ and the design was dominated by computer specialists, who developed systems to maximize the utilization of the computer. According to Jordan, this centralization made it “easy to control business processes and rationalize the use of resources.” New systems were implemented but, mainly because of the lack of involvement of managers and workers outside the IT unit, they failed to achieve adequate data quality to fulfill their purpose of monitoring, projecting, and controlling. From the perspective of the users, the approach was an ‘imposed strategy’ of the IT unit.

Discussion: Management was still able to derive value from IT by providing more sophisticated automation, and by stressing instrumental rationality in the use of IT at the organizational level. However, no attempt was made to derive value from the processes of designing and using IT. These processes were delegated to technologists. At the end of this era, middle and upper managers – many of them engineers by training – were distancing themselves from the IT unit. They, therefore, were driven to experiment with PCs – not as organizational systems but merely as individual efforts.

During this era, most efforts to develop and acquire IT resources were directed toward improving the availability of technical skills:

1. hiring IT trained staff;

2. using technical consultants;

3. educating to develop improved IT skills; and

4. subscribing to technical U.S. magazines to keep up with state-of-the-art techniques.

These efforts were often centered on Alfonso Jordan, who acted as an intermediary between the IT resources and the organization.

5.4. The decentralization of information technology (1988–93)

By mid-1987, top management felt the need for more and better information to support decentralization and goal setting. Most of the problems with IT systems were now widely recognized, and real efforts were made to resolve them. The Data Processing

Center had seldom delivered on its promises and its technical people seldom understood business decisions. Though users often did not know what they really wanted, top management did not make the tough decisions needed to provide for long-term infrastructure requirements. Azucar, therefore, worked on opening the dialogues that helped end the problems (see Fig. 4).

Top management contracted Alfredo Mendez, who had spent the previous ten years developing and implementing information systems for various organizations in different countries to be chief of IT Systems Development. A new Corporate IT Committee, constituting Don Jorge Heredia, the finance/administrative manager, the operations manager, Jordan, Mendez, and an external MIS consultant, was “crucial in reviewing the logical design of each new system that was developed.” At the end of 1988, Alfonso Jordan left the company to become an independent IT consultant, and Mendez was named IT Division General Manager. He recognized that, as it moved into a user-oriented era, the IT Division would need new infrastructure to support decentralization. Specialized IT training companies were contracted to provide training for users, and IT experts were contracted to advise analyst/programmers on optimizing IT applications. Formal analysis and design techniques began to be applied.

Discussion: The third era of IT at Azucar exhibits a considerable increase in autonomy of action on the part of personnel in the business units. Top managers had become aware that IT could enable deep organizational change, and policies and formal procedures were aimed at generating a greater shared sense of purpose. Crucial initiatives included management education and ‘hands-on’ use of technology, direct management involvement with key stakeholders early in the process, iterative design techniques and pilot testing, and the transfer of IT expertise into the firm. ‘User-friendly’ IT systems were developed jointly by middle managers and technologists.

The shift enabled management to make business procedures and practices more transparent than ever before, to discern operational patterns, and to anticipate opportunities and problems. For example, a database containing information provided by several business units was used by the agricultural division managers in 1992 to design a new compensation strategy. The computerized systems that enabled this relatively autonomous action also provided data which was used (1) to improve managerial processes (such as the managerial processes of the agricultural, transport, laboratory, and purchasing and material systems); and (2) to provide better information for central monitoring (e.g. it allowed better budgeting and organizational strategic planning).

## 6. Analysis of the strategy for change

## 6.1. The value extracted from IT

On the basis of the literature on the implementation of IT in organizations, transfer of IT to LDCs, and agroindustrial organizations in LDCs, this paper proposed that an LDC agroindustrial organization would derive a larger value by using IT to automate the organization than by using it to informate. Azucar's experience during its first two IT eras supports this proposition. Over time, however, Azucar's strategy shifted, and the company was able to extract a larger value from IT by ‘informating’ the organization.

Before 1973, Azucar's business data was stored in people's heads. This data was codified and rationalized during the initial two IT eras at Azucar to control production control and minimize cost: to automate clerical activities. The essential logic was to constantly remove manual record keeping and calculation. The general manager of the factory division said:

When I came [to Azucar], just by injecting human effort, motivation and supervision, we were able to increase the factory efficiency by 25%. Then, we kept seeding these principles in the workers. In the factory, the basic focus was in the production processes, and the main issue was direct supervision... Furthermore, a new employee was forced to work in close contact with the factory workers, instead of reviewing papers. And we believe that was one of the factory's most positive aspects.

As managers at Azucar were automating clerical activities, they were also creating their informing environment. They were learning and trying to improve the business processes. The learning was restricted, however, to a few employees within the administrative division. During the second episode, the company began to use IT to automate organization-wide processes. The data and information about these processes immediately became more visible.

During the third IT era, the data were made accessible to anyone who could use them. In particular, upper and middle managers developed a vision of how the IT systems and the data could be exploited. As business units began to take charge of their new IT design and implementation, the information base of the organization became more open, first in the business units, and then in the organization as a whole. An increase in the explicit information content of tasks revealed the dynamics of the company's processes more clearly and comprehensively than ever before. This is illustrated in the words of the general manager of the Agricultural Division:

Today [1993], I ask the agricultural engineers to analyze the data, and to delegate their functions to agricultural technicians. I tell them, “you must review the data, in order to understand if what has been done in the field is appropriate...” I am convinced that the only way to be efficient is by understanding the information generated by the various tasks... Every Thursday, during the sugar crop, we [all the agricultural engineers of the division] meet to analyze and discuss the activity.

However contra-intuitive this move may appear in an environment where the intellectual skills required are very scarce $[15]$ , it is in accordance with the IT implementation research in organizations of developed countries. Thus the ‘informating’ value of IT is not restricted to developed countries, and IT itself can create a demand for a new set of skills, altering the grounds of task-related knowledge and the basis for competent participation in the life of the organization.

## 6.2. The alignment of IT and the availability of resources

At Azucar, managers were able to develop or acquire IT resources that were not readily provided in the national context. When IT was first introduced, the company was a near-passive receiver of IT systems developed by a service bureau. Notwithstanding, management was able to gain experience to clarify and redirect its change initiatives.

Most of the managers who were enthusiastic and supportive of IT systems were those who were IT literate: they understood the nature and benefits of computers and had the skills required to manage the systems. The general managers of the administration, transport and factory divisions played a primary role in the development of the first IT systems, which were then used as prototypes for subsequent systems. Even when IT resources are lacking and government policies do not spur the introduction of IT, managers can develop and acquire the resources they need. Good managers learn over time and they plan ahead to develop the necessary human, capital, technical, and organizational infrastructure for future IT applications.

## 7. Summary and conclusions

Although top management usually has a relatively clear business strategy, it often lacks anything that could pass for a conscious and coherent IT or organization strategy. This was true at Azucar. But, management's growth in vision is central to the effective implementation of IT. The ability to develop an effective strategy for IT and organization change requires a superior competence in understanding the forces shaping the environment and the interactions that exist among the business, organization, and IT strategies. At Azucar, this ability included the competence to align limited IT resources, particularly in terms of IT skills, with the strategy for change. The alignment was a key ingredient in assuring that every new IT system supported the attainment of the company's objectives within each IT era.

This study hypothesized that (1) an LDC organization would derive a larger value from IT by automating the organization than by informing it, and (2) to implement IT, LDC managers would have to develop/ acquire the IT resources that were needed by the new systems and that were not readily provided by the national context. The first hypothesis was supported only for the first two IT eras (1973–1979 and 1980–1986). During the third era, management exploited IT not only as an automating technology, but also as an informing technology. LDC organizations may be able to compress the time required for this shift by learning from the experience of organizations in developed countries, as Azucar did.

The study supported the second proposition for the whole twenty-year period investigated. This finding is consistent with McKenney's accounts of IT implementations in U.S. companies in the 1950s, but because intellectual skills are so scarce in LDCs, managers there must consciously and actively develop the skilled workers they will need.

Change strategies in LDC organizations are inevitably ambiguous, because of the need to ensure agreement among diverse parties, the uncertainty in the organization's technology and environment, the multiple perspectives of different individuals and groups, and the lack of information that characterizes LDC contexts. No one manager is likely to be able to evaluate the organizational components that will be affected by the implementation of a new IT, but the responsibility for strategy can easily fall into a gap between IT developers and strategic managers. The role of top management, therefore, is to track historical context and to help align multiple strategies.

Managers need to emphasize creative autonomy in their roles and actions, involving a relatively loose coupling between policy and implementation actions, and the establishment of their own independent credibility with important stakeholder groups. Past events and circumstances can be consciously selected and presented in ways that facilitate present action. Management also needs to consider the norms and values that underpin the strategy, its possible impact on power relations, and the question of organizational involvement in the generation of the strategy.
