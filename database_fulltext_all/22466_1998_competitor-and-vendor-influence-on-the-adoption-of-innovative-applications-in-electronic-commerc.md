# Competitor and vendor influence on the adoption of innovative applications in electronic commerce

Brian L. Dos Santos $^{a,*}$ , Ken Peffers $^{b}$

$^{a}$ College of Business and Public Administration, University of Louisville, Louisville KY 40292, USA $^{b}$ School of Business, Rutgers University, Camden NJ 08102, USA

Accepted 20 May 1998

## Abstract

The rapid growth of the Internet over the past 5 years has greatly increased interest in its potential use of the Internet for electronic commerce (EC), particularly as a vehicle for transactions between customers and suppliers. Today, Internet-based EC applications are in their infancy and investments in innovative information technology (IT) applications are risky and expensive. The managerial decision, to adopt these EC applications early or wait for the technology to become more established, is not trivial. Early adoption poses significant demand and technological risks, but the rewards could be great. We report on an empirical investigation of the influences on the adoption decision for one of the earliest EC applications, the automated teller machine (ATM) system, to determine whether marketing efforts by hardware and software vendors, imitation of competitors or a mixture of influences affected these decisions among bank managers. We develop a time series of cumulative ATM adoption estimates for 1971–1992 and use these data to fit three models from the diffusion of innovation literature, the external, internal and mixed influence models. The results suggest that imitation and communication among industry competitors was most important, but marketing efforts by the technology vendors were also important in the first few years after the introduction of the technology. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Electronic commerce; World wide web; Automated teller machine; ATM; Diffusion of innovation; Information technology adoption; Information systems

## 1. Introduction

The rapid growth of the Internet over the past 5 years, since the introduction of the first world wide web (WWW) browser in 1993, and the attention paid to it in the business press has greatly increased interest in electronic commerce (EC). One of the major types of EC activity is the use of computer-based electronic media to conduct transactions between a firm and its suppliers or customers. Firms in two industries have successfully used this type of EC activity for over 25 years, the customer reservation systems in the airline industry and the automated teller machine (ATM) systems in the banking industry. Today, few firms in these industries can compete without these systems. With the explosive growth in the use of the Internet by consumers and firms, there appears to be a tremendous potential new market, ideally suited to the development of innovative EC applications in many industries.

Such applications are, however, innovative and their ultimate potential is difficult to gauge when the applications first appear. What is clear is that the value of these applications is dependent upon the adoption and acceptance by the relevant parties. For firms, investments in innovative information technology (IT) applications are expensive and risky $[9]$ . The decision, to adopt such innovative applications early or to wait until they have been proven successful, is extremely important for the firm. Early adopters may be able to gain long-term advantages over competitors, as did early adopters of airline reservation systems $[8]$ and ATMs $[10]$ . Early adopters, however, incur very high costs and great uncertainty as to the payoffs, compared with firms that follow. Moreover, the benefits to early adopters are affected by the adoption timing decisions of firms that follow. If other firms in an industry are slow to imitate an innovation, benefits to the early adopters can be large.

What determines the adoption of new IT applications by firms in an industry? While the adoption of new IT applications has been studied (e.g. [14, 18]), previous studies have not sought to determine how different factors affect adoption decisions. IT adoption decisions among firms affect the deployment of enormous resources, estimated to average 0.2–8.9% of revenue, depending on industry [28] or 5% or more of firm capital stock [4]). In addition, adoption decisions determine whether technologies become industry standards or are eclipsed by substitute technologies. Some IT applications are adopted rapidly while others require many years to be adopted by the majority of firms in an industry, even in cases where benefits are generally available to all adopters beginning with technology introduction. What factors affect the timing of technology adoption when benefits may accrue to all adopters?

In order to understand this adoption phenomenon, we investigated the adoption of one of the first EC applications to be widely adopted by U.S. firms, ATM systems. The adoption of ATM technology in the financial services industry closely parallels merchant adoption of Internet technology for electronic commerce, now in its early stages. In both cases, industry members widely assumed, at the time that the technology was first introduced, that it would eventually be widely adopted $[1, 5]$ . In both cases, however, the benefits of merchant adoption were not immediately realized because customers shied away from actually using the technology because of concerns about security and benefits $[10, 17]$ . As a consequence, 9 years after the first ATMs appeared in 1971, less than 20% of all banks had installed ATMs and 16 years elapsed before a majority of U.S. banks had installed any ATMs. Likewise, 5 years after the introduction of the first WWW, browser and in spite of extremely rapid growth in the number of consumers with access to the WWW the number of merchants who actually accept electronic transactions via the Internet remains small $[31]$ . Most customers appear to be reluctant to send sensitive personal information across the Internet and to make legally binding transactions in this environment $[22]$ . For example, although travel services produce more revenue than any other Internet-based EC product, only about 5% of customers browsing the largest online travel services site actually purchase tickets online and the service is not yet profitable $[23]$ . What will it take for firms to rapidly adopt Internet-based EC applications? Will the rate of adoption of this technology be driven by the actions taken by providers of the technology used in these applications? Answers to these questions should be of interest to many firms contemplating the deployment of Internet-based EC applications and to potential providers of the technology that drives these applications. We may be able to help address these questions by looking at what happened with ATM technology: a technology that faced many of the same hurdles when it was introduced.

This paper presents the results of a study of ATM adoption between 1971 and 1992. Adoption of the technology by banks was a key prerequisite for convincing customers to use the technology. But what persuaded banks to adopt the technology? We attempt to determine whether the adoption of ATMs by U.S. banks was influenced by actions taken by firms within the industry, by the marketing efforts of underlying hardware and software vendors, or a combination of these influences. Our analysis suggests that ATM adoption was influenced by a combination of factors; adoption of the technology by competitors and efforts by technology providers influenced ATM adoption. The results have implications for merchant adoption of new electronic commerce applications and for manufacturers of new technologies.

## 2. The ATM adoption decision

## 2.1. Factors in the adoption decision

What factors influenced the decision by bank managers to adopt the technology? Development of ATM applications resulted from a duel sponsorship by large innovative banks and hardware and software vendors. Some large retail banks saw a potential in these systems to use advantages they already had in existing systems and expertise to achieve competitive advantage. They made efforts in the early 1970s to develop such systems [12]. Saloner and Shepard [30] found a positive relationship between bank size, as represented by deposits and number of branches, and early ATM adoption (between 1971 and 1979). They attribute this to the existence of network externalities for proprietary ATM networks, that is, larger banks could create greater value for customers by developing larger proprietary ATM networks.

The development of innovative proprietary systems threatened other banks, encouraging them to adopt their own systems. Thus, information obtained regarding adoptions by rivals may have helped managers make adoption decisions. Hannan and McDowell [15, 16], using a similar sample, found evidence that banks were more likely to adopt if they operated in markets where competitors had already adopted the technology or where local market concentration was the highest, that is, where the largest banks in the local market had more market share. Banks may have responded to avoid being left behind and losing market share.

Investors seem to believe, however, that investments in information technology systematically increase the value of the firm only when those investments are innovative $[11]$ . This is consistent with the economic theory that exceptional returns accrue only to those that quickly seize opportunities arising from innovation. In the case of ATMs, Dos Santos and Peffers $[10]$ found that there were systematic benefits to banks that adopted ATMs early, at the expense of those who adopted later or not at all. This suggests that it was best for banks to have adopted early rather than late.

However, Peffers and Dos Santos's [26] findings indicate that, even though the earliest adopters benefited more, few benefits actually accrued to even the earliest adopting banks until after 1979. This, they concluded, was because banks required several years to learn how to use the technology to their best advantage. Industry reports suggest that there was evidence of the technology's benefits to banks at the time of the technology's introduction. For example, $81.8\%$ of respondents to a 1972 survey of bank executives [1] indicated their belief that ATMs would eventually be adopted industry-wide.

The other sponsors of ATMs were the producers of the underlying hardware and software. These vendors promoted the use of the technology by demonstrating its feasibility and disseminating information about its benefits. Banks, especially those with few technical resources, may have responded to information from vendors to address concerns about technical and operational feasibility issues. Consequently, the vendors' marketing efforts may have influenced managers' adoption decisions.

Early adoption was in the interest of banks generally and evidence existed of these benefits; hence, any bank manager with perfect insight would have opted to adopt early. However, the evidence may not have been persuasive because banks did not actually obtain benefits for a number of years after introducing the technology. In addition, bank executives have found it to be very difficult to determine the value of strategic benefits from innovative information systems [27]. As a result, investment in ATMs may have occurred when managers at each bank finally were convinced of the technology's value, that is, the timing of adoption was a question of persuasion or influence. Were bankers persuaded to adopt this technology through the influence of competition and imitation among peer firms or were they convinced by the marketing efforts of hardware and software vendors? The answer to this question may have implications for the marketing of innovative EC applications for merchants to conduct transactions on the WWW.

## 3. Innovation adoption: Influence models

Researchers interested in the diffusion of innovation have tried to answer the question: how are users of innovative technology persuaded of its value? The diffusion of innovation is the process by which an innovation is spread through a population of potential adopters $[29]$ . A number of models has been used to model diffusion, particularly in the consumer behavior and marketing science literature $[3, 20]$ . Three conceptual models that have received strong empirical support are the external influence model, the internal influence model and the mixed influence model $[3, 4]$ .

The external influence model assumes that adoption is driven by information from a source external to the social system and only by information from such sources. It has been found to describe situations where the adoption influence is exclusively from outside the adoption community, that is, there is little or no communication, which would lead to effective imitation among the members of the system. For example, it was found to describe the adoption of a new drug among physicians in towns in the midwestern United States $[7]$ . Much of the underlying hardware and software for ATMs was developed by firms outside the banking industry. Consequently, these firms promoted the adoption of ATMs $[10]$ .

In this model, adoption at any time is related to the number of members in the social system that has yet to adopt the innovation $[7, 32]$ . The number of adopters at any point in time is only related to the number of remaining potential adopters; prior adopters do not influence potential adopters. Consequently, adoption begins rapidly and slows down as the number of adopters increases. The number of adopters in any time interval can be described by the following equation:

$$
\frac {\mathrm{d} N _ {t}}{\mathrm{d} t} = p (m - N _ {t})\tag{1}
$$

where $N_{t}$ is the cumulative number of adopters in the social system at time t, m is the number of potential adopters, and p is the coefficient of external influence. p and m must be positive in this model. Integrating Eq. 1 yields the adoption function.

$$
N _ {t} = m \left[ 1 - \mathrm{e} ^ {- p t} \right]\tag{2}
$$

This model results in an adoption function where the number of adoptions increases as t increases, but at a decreasing rate and is bounded by m.

The internal influence model assumes that adoption is driven by communication within a specific community or social system. The assumption here is that adoption within the community is based entirely on communication within the community, leading to imitation. This model was used to model the adoption of seed corn among farmers in 31 U.S. states [13]. Among the approximately 12,000 U.S. banks, managers keep abreast of the technology used by ‘peer’ institutions [25] and much, for example, has been written in industry association publications [1]. In this model, the expected number of adopters in any period is related to the number of banks that have already adopted as well as the number of potential adopters [21, 32]. The number of adopters at any time interval can be described by the following equation:

$$
\frac {\mathrm{d} N _ {t}}{\mathrm{d} t} = q N _ {t} (m - N _ {t})\tag{3}
$$

where q, which is expected to be positive, is the coefficient of internal influence. The adoption function, obtained by integration, is an S-shaped curve, with m as the limiting value. Integrating Eq. 3 yields the adoption function.

$$
N _ {t} = \frac {m}{1 + \frac {(m - m _ {o})}{m _ {o}} \mathrm{e} ^ {- q m t}}\tag{4}
$$

The mixed influence model assumes that both internal and external sources influence the adoption decision. The mixed model is the most widely used influence model. For example, Bass [2] used it to predict sales of consumer durables, such as television sets and room air conditioners. In the banking industry, it is not unreasonable to expect that both external influence (vendor marketing efforts) and internal influence (competitor communication and imitation) influenced adoption. In this model, the rate of adoption at t is given by:

$$
\frac {\mathrm{d} N _ {t}}{\mathrm{d} t} = (p + q N _ {t}) (m - N _ {t})\tag{5}
$$

Integration of the mixed influence model yields an asymmetrical S-shaped adoption function shown in Eq. 6; external influence results in more rapid early adoption than with imitation alone.

$$
N _ {t} = \frac {m \frac {p (m - m _ {o})}{(p + q m _ {o})} \mathbf {e} [ - (p + q m) t ]}{1 + \frac {q (m - m _ {o})}{p + q m _ {o}} \mathbf {e} [ - (p + q m) t ]}\tag{6}
$$

Parameters Influence model

In this study, we try to determine which of the above functions Eqs. 2,4 and 6 best describe the ATM adoption data.

## 4. Data

The data, used in this study, are a time series of population estimates of the number of individual U.S. banks, which adopted ATM technology. Specifically, the data estimate the cumulative number of banks that had installed at least one ATM in each of 13 years from 1971 through 1992. We developed these data from three sources. For the years 1971–1976 and 1979, the FDIC conducted a bank survey to determine whether the banks had installed at least one ATM each. Hannan and McDowell [16] of the U.S. Federal Reserve subsequently surveyed some of the same banks to determine whether they had installed an ATM in 1977 or 1978. This resulted in an adoption sample for 1971–1979 of more than 3000 (about one-fourth of the total number) banks.

The American Bankers Association (ABA) surveyed member banks about the status of ATM adoption in 1972, 1975, 1978, 1981, 1986, 1989, and 1992. The number of respondents ranged from 1657 (1975) to 373 (1989); the variation in the number of respondents was due primarily to budgetary constraints.

We used the data from the U.S. Federal Reserve and the ABA surveys to develop 15 population estimates of cumulative first-time ATM installations. The surveys did not represent banks of different sizes in proportion to their numbers in the population and the adoption of ATMs differed for banks of different sizes. To correct for this bank size bias in the surveys, we employed a piecewise linear adjustment, using population data pertaining to the characteristics of U.S. banks from the FDIC Report of Condition and Income. Specifically, for each year in the data we multiplied the proportion of adopters in each size class by the number of banks in the population for that size class to produce an unbiased estimate of the number of adopters in the population. Table 1 shows the resulting estimates. We were unable to develop a credible estimate from the 1975 ABA survey data.

## 5. Analysis and results

To determine which influence model best explains ATM adoption, we fit each of the three models described earlier to the combined adoption data, using an iterative non-linear least squares procedure. To avoid accepting a local minimum, we repeated the procedure with a number of different starting points. The estimated coefficients for the three models are shown in Table 2. All coefficients are statistically significant at the 0.05 level. All three models fit the data points with very high adjusted $R^{2}$ , making it be difficult to easily determine which of the models best fit the data. There are, however, interesting qualitative differences in the way that the models fit the data.

## 6. Discussion

The fact that all three models fit the data so well (aside from the negative parameters for the fitted external influence model) and the fact that the mixed influence model is the best statistical and visual fit, suggests that the mixed influence model may best explain what influenced adoption decisions. This interpretation is consistent with that of Hannan and McDowell [16] who argue that the internal influence model alone is not adequate to describe ATM adoption because, using an ATM adoption sample from 1971–1979, they find evidence that the likelihood of adoption changes over time.

The good fit for the internal influence model suggests that communication within the banking industry and observation of competitors' success with the technology were an important source of influence in the adoption decision.

The internal influence model is believed to be the best fit when an innovation is socially visible and not adopting it places social system members at a disadvantage $[19]$ . Such was clearly the case with ATMs;

it was widely believed by industry experts that ATMs would eventually be universally adopted $[1]$ . However, in this study, the internal influence model clearly underestimates both early adoption and the number of eventual adopters.

The external influence model is believed to be the best fit when adoption decisions are not affected by the adoption of the technology by other firms in the industry. In this case, it appears that the fact that other banks adopted ATMs played an important role in a bank's decision to adopt the technology. Clearly the external influence model alone does not describe ATM adoptions well. However, there is good reason to believe that external influence also contributed substantially to adoption decisions during the first few years. Vendor sales efforts may have had an impact on decisions to invest in the innovative technology early in this period, before there was good information available on the experiences of the first adopters.

The mixed influence model incorporates the influence of internal and external change agents on the adoption decision $[19]$ . It has been used to forecast sales of consumer durables, where the effects of sales efforts by vendors as well as imitation of other consumers play a role in the adoption decision.

Fig. 4 shows the fitted internal and mixed influence models plotted against actual adoption estimates for 1970–1982. It suggests that internal influence alone does not account for many of the adoptions during the first few years. Through 1979, the mixed influence model ‘accounts for’ 46% more of the actual cumulative adoptions by that date than the internal influence model alone.

This suggests that external influence was very important during the first few years after the technology's introduction. Once the number of adopting banks began to grow, internal influence predominated, as competitors watched for indications that the adopters had succeeded in adapting the technology to the industry and customer were drawn to it.

These results could have significant implications for adoption of electronic commerce applications on the Internet. Like the early environment for ATM adoption, experts now believe that the Internet will eventually be an extremely important vehicle for commercial and consumer transactions. To date, however, firms have been slow to adopt the technology. While most firms now have an Internet presence, in the form of a corporate website, few use it to conduct transactions with customers and suppliers. Moreover, the early adopters have found that customers have been slow to accept the technology and therefore, with few exceptions, the benefits of early adoption have not been realized. Technology vendors and competitors both potentially influence merchant adoption of EC applications on the Internet. The internal influence model suggests that merchants will adopt EC technology as they observe competitors' success in using this channel to attract customers and generate revenue and profits $[6]$ . The external model suggests that technology vendors such as ClearLogic, CheckFree, and Open Market, will drive adoption by persuading potential EC merchants to adopt the technology. They may do so by actions that improve security, make customers feel more secure, and by raising expectations that, for example, electronic retailing will soon “sure...be a trillion-dollar industry” $[24]$ .

The factors influencing merchant adoption of EC applications are very similar to those that drove ATM adoption. If the parallels continue, and we feel they will, EC adoption will be influenced by internal and external influences. Vendor influence appears to be very important in this, the early period, as merchants await the introduction of technology that has more robust security. The influence of competitors will be felt strongly only after merchants can observe that the early adopters have succeeded in persuading customers to conduct transactions on the Internet, just as bankers scurried to adopt ATMs after customers begin to use ATMs in greater numbers.

One limitation of this study is that we did not include decision variables, other than influence, that might have affected individual adoption decisions. Other studies have considered such variables as prior adoption by a competitor in the local banking market $[16]$ , local market concentration $[15]$ , number of bank branches $[30]$ , and wage rates $[16]$ . We were unable to include these variables because, unlike the 1971–1979 data used in earlier studies, our data did not include the identities of individual adopters. Bank identities were not available from the ABA data. In addition, our procedure to adjust for survey bias by bank size precluded the use of bank identities. Moreover, it was necessary to use the post-1979 data because ATM adoption was still in its early stages in 1979 and we sought to learn from what happened with ATM technology, rather that predict how ATM adoption would proceed; the objective in the studies cited above.

Another limitation is that we do not account for the effect of price changes in the rate of adoption. It would have been useful to have had price data for this period, but these data were not available. However, prior research has shown that banks that adopted ATMs early benefited $[10]$ . First mover benefits greatly exceeded the benefits to later adopters, even though later adopters incurred lower technology costs. Consequently, price decreases may not have had a big impact on the rate of adoption.

In addition, prior research has shown that the introduction of price as a variable in these models can present technical problems. The Bass et al. [3] study showed that when the mixed influence model is extended to incorporate a price variable, the resulting model, when estimated using non-linear least squares, 'gets confused,' that is, the parameters are interchangeable. Also, when price decreases over the product life cycle are continuous, they are accounted for satisfactorily by the mixed influence model [3].

As the above discussion suggests, our use of the ‘adoption of innovation’ models implicitly assumes that such models can ‘account’ for adoption decisions without the use of decision variables, such as those mentioned above. Indeed, the widespread acceptance of such models in the marketing literature and their successful use to predict the adoption rates of innovative products suggests that this is a reasonable assumption $[3]$ . In addition, we did not include a decision variable for acceptance of the technology by the ultimate end-users, the banking customers. Use of the adoption of innovations models implicitly assumes that this factor is included in the internal influence component, that is, part of the process of observing adoption of the technology among on's neighbors includes observing their success in obtaining customer acceptance. It also includes competitor observance of the process of technology adaptation for its best use.

## 7. Summary and conclusions

This paper reports on an empirical study of the factors that influence adoption of a very successful EC application, the ATM. Three models of influence were fitted to estimate cumulative ATM adoptions from 1971 through 1992 to test whether hardware and software vendors, industry competitors, or a mixture of influences affected such adoptions. The results suggest that both internal (competing banks) and external factors (hardware and software vendors) influenced the adoption decision. They also suggest that although competitor influence predominated, vendor influence may have been particularly important to 'jump start' adoption of the innovation in the first few years after the technology was introduced.

These results have implications for the adoption of other EC applications. Today, many firms are considering EC applications on the Internet and some firms have already introduced new EC applications in this new frontier. The success of these new applications will depend upon many factors, including the introduction of similar applications by competitors and the efforts of technology vendors. Will adoption be affected by internal or external factors? Producers of technology that enable these new applications make every effort to get firms to introduce these new applications. Should they market their products to all potential customers or should they market their products to the potential customers most likely to adopt the applications very early? If the internal influence model best describes adoption decisions in an industry, these firms should focus their marketing efforts on making the products available to influential early adopters. To the extent that the external influence model best describes adoption decisions for a new application, they may best be served by marketing to all potential customers. The results of this study suggest a third alternative. External influence was important to speed adoption in the first few years after introduction, until a substantial number of adoptions had occurred, in this case about 20–25% of the potential adopters. After that, competitor influence appears to be more important to firms still considering adoption. This suggests that firms marketing new EC technology should focus on market penetration at the present time. Once a substantial customer base is established, they can rely on communication within the potential EC community to help with their efforts to increase market size.
