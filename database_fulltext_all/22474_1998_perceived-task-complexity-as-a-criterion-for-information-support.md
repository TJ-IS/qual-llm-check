# Perceived task complexity as a criterion for information support

Thomas E. Marshall $^{*}$ , Terry A. Byrd

Department of Management, College of Business, Auburn University, Auburn, AL 36849, USA

Accepted 23 February 1998

## Abstract

Here, the conceptual model of perceived task complexity, which incorporates the user, task, and system into a comprehensive unit of analysis, is used to investigate how, when, and why computer-assisted instruction (CAI) systems provide effective support. The work is based on data provided by 156 experimental subjects; information system (IS) use was found to vary by user groups depending on task performance. In addition, the effects of task presentation on task behavior and performance were found to vary between groups, based on their performance. The findings imply that user, task, and system dynamically interact in providing effective IS support. A comprehensive model of perceived task complexity that includes a user's domain knowledge is developed. This should help in further investigating into how, when, and why IS are effective. © 1998 Published by Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Cognitive processing; Information processing; Information load; Information acquisition; Computer assisted instruction; Task domain knowledge; Task complexity; Decision behavior

## 1. Introduction

In 1982, Robey and Taggart wrote of the necessity of recognizing “the appropriate division of labor between the electronic computer and the human bio-computer” [34]. They stressed the importance of designing the information system (IS) to support the type of human information processing required by the task. As they said, most of the work tries “to characterize the nature of human information processing so that the role of computer systems that support human decisions might be better understood”. Kozar and Zigurs extended this theme by investigating how process should be designed to achieve the maximum benefit of technology use [22].

In the past, this IS research stream has almost exclusively concentrated on static models of input/output analysis $[32, 38]$ . Todd and Benbasat stressed that IS research must address issues of how and why IS are effective. In their view, “research techniques are needed that permit us to examine the decisional behavior that takes place when groups and individuals use these systems” $[43]$ . Libby $[23]$ considered the development of a general descriptive framework of how decisions are made to be the first step towards the goal of improving decision-making. More recently, Libby and Luft $[24]$ , Bonner $[4]$ , and Brown and Solomon $[5]$ have suggested research models for investigating decision performance effects that include aspects of subjects' abilities and knowledge. In a related manner, Gerlach and Kuo stressed the importance of the behavioral needs of users as a means of better understanding how IS support the decision process.

Previous work addressing human information-processing needs during task accomplishment have yielded valuable insights into task performance $[29]$ . Card, Moran, and Newell $[7]$ applied cognitive psychology principles in human performance and task analysis to derive IS that are better for the user. Ericsson and Simon $[15]$ , using verbal protocol analysis, proposed that human cognitive processing should be the basis of analysis for further developing human information-processing theory. Newell and Simon $[30]$ analyzed subjects' decision strategies using verbal protocol analysis as a means of explaining high levels of task performance. Miller $[27]$ suggested that task analysis focusing on cognitive behavior provides a better way of determining task structure. He elaborated on this theme by stating:

The behavior structure of tasks is offered as a guide for organizing a way of looking at task and task information that is compatible with a way of organizing knowledge in experimental psychology according to task settings.

He believes that one of the benefits of behavior-based task analysis is the reduction in randomness in determining task information requirements.

Mathieson investigated variations in users' definitions of an IS and found that the complexity of the system definition (number of elements) of the IS varied with IS related experience. In essence, he found that experts and novices have different perspectives of an IS as it relates to system definition. He stressed the importance of recognizing user characteristics, such as experience and ability, as critical factors that system analysts should address in IS design [25]. Gerlach and Kuo support this theme by stating that “software developers need to expand their focus beyond functional requirements to include the behavioral needs of users [19]”.

In an effort to understand the decision process, Payne [31] viewed contingent decision-making behavior as consisting of three phases: information acquisition, evaluation/action, and feedback/learning. His model is similar to Simon's [40] decision stages of intelligence, design, and choice. According to both, this description of decision making allows the researcher to focus on criteria that influence the decision maker's strategy. Strategy selection criteria are often evaluated by decision makers using cost/benefit paradigms [1, 28, 41]. Major factors affecting strategy selection have been broadly categorized as context and information influences. Context influences are closely associated with the implicit values of the task, such as similarity or attractiveness of the alternatives [6]. Information influences deal with the structure of the task; those associated with the user's cognitive processing capabilities and IS presentation are the main focus of our research.

## 2. Related literature

Much of the research addressing information presentation has been directed towards the effects of presentation mode (e.g., graphic versus tabular) [21]. Another significant stream of research addresses the effects of information cue ordering on decision behavior and performance. The issue of simultaneous versus serial presentation ordering has been addressed in the past, where individual characteristics, as moderators of presentation effects, have been identified as critical factors in determining the optimum information presentation [13]. It has also been found to have a dramatic effect on decision behavior [13]. Einhorn and Hogarth stated that caution should be exercised when simultaneous information presentations may have dysfunctional effects. Gardner [18] proposed that, for some individuals, increases in mental activation (e.g., simultaneous presentations) may have good performance effects. However, Vessey observed decision makers sacrificing decision accuracy to reduce effort. She believes that further research investigating information use is needed. Better understanding of how IS presentation affects the cognitive processing may explain how and why IS provide effective support [33]. This can provide insights on how to reduce dysfunctional consequences for some people while promoting more functional system presentations for others [47].

Craik and Lockhart [10] developed a memory-based model that describes human information processing in terms of stimulus recognition and enrichment.

Their framework is based on an individual's depth of cognitive processing. Initial cognitive stimulus processing then involves perceptions of stimulus recognition. Later stages of stimulus processing “are more concerned with matching the input against stored abstractions from past learning: that is, later stages are concerned with pattern recognition and the extraction of meaning”. They feel that the analysis process is a continuum from initial pattern recognition to semantic-associative stimulus enrichment. The person’s ability to perform deeper levels of cognitive processing (i.e., stimulus enrichment) is a function of memory storage capacity, which involves the complexity of the individual’s elaboration scheme within the relevant domain. The dysfunctional effects of information overload associated with more simultaneous information presentations may be inversely related to that individual’s ability to use more complex memory structures while performing stimulus enrichment. People with sufficiently complex memory structures may experience additional cognitive activation from simultaneous information presentation, resulting in an enhanced task performance. Therefore, the appropriateness of information presentations may, in part, be defined by the cognitive processing capabilities of the user in performing stimulus enrichment.

Simon [39] proposed the concept of ‘bounded rationality’ and theorized that these human processing limitations influence the decision-making strategy. Miller [26] presented a strong argument detailing strategies used by individuals to compensate for their human information-processing limitations. Weber [45] investigated the ability of subjects to assemble information cues into semantic categories as a surrogate measure of subject expertise within a specific task domain. Conceptually, ‘cue clustering’ has been posited as one type of strategy used by a subject to enhance memory recall by manipulating cognitive demands [16]. ‘Memory chunks’ have also been found to be closely related to such semantic grouping. Differences in strategies between experts and non-experts have been observed in the task domains of chess and electronics [8]. Egan and Schwartz [12] found experienced electronic technicians tended to ‘chunk’ based on function, while novice technicians tended to ‘chunk’ by spatial proximity. Recognizing differences in memory-chunking strategies between experts and novices suggests that the way an individual interprets an information presentation may systematically vary by level of expertise.

Investigating human problem solving, Newell and Simon suggested that information cue ordering affects the decision maker's cognitive representation of the task. This, in turn, influences the strategy that a subject selects to solve the problem. Chi and Glaser said that “researchers have found that the representation is very important in determining how easy a problem is to solve.”

Information load, as per Biggs et al. [3], is the effect that additional information elements have on a subject's performance. Previous work revealed that information cue order effects (i.e., information load) influence the decision process [44] and the concept of information load has been the focus of recent decision-making experiments. Also, decision-making strategies are influenced by information load. Te'eni found that simultaneous presentation (presumably higher information load) could produce extreme levels of perceived complexity resulting in information overload and sub-optimal performance [42]. A comprehensive theory of information load, as it relates to behavior and performance, is yet to be developed.

## 2.1. Hypotheses

Our work investigates decision behavior as an analog of the underlying information processing capabilities (i.e., memory structure) of the subject. In theory, this underlying cognitive structure establishes limits on information processing capabilities and directly influences the subject's evaluation and execution of decision-making strategies. As stated by Chi and Glaser:

In particular, the investigation of problem solving in domains requiring extensive knowledge has shown how the knowledge organizations acquired by the problem solver, which are stored in long-term memory, influence the perceptual process and strategies of problem solving.

Bonner's research found task-specific knowledge to influence task performance. Brown and Solomon found cases where domain-specific knowledge influenced the manner in which decision makers acquire and process information. Therefore, a user's level of task domain knowledge affects the use of informationprocessing capabilities that determine decision strategy selection and execution. Probably a user's level of task domain knowledge will thus influence IS use. This leads to the first hypothesis:

H1: An individual's level of task domain knowledge affects IS use.

Prior research has found that information presentation influences the decision process. Schroder, Driver, and Streufert [37] identified ‘the degree of organization’ as an element in the complexity of an IS interface. Berlyne [2] and Sabherwal and Grover [36] recognized element heterogeneity as a factor in the complexity of information presentation; low homogeneity increases the difficulty of the search process in information gathering.

Information presentation, represented by cue ordering, may influence the type of information used. Das, et al. [11] described cognitive processing as ‘simultaneous integration’ and ‘successive processing’. Simultaneous integration refers to the synthesis of separate elements into groups. As an example, systems of relationships between elements require simultaneous integration. According to them, this process requires that the cognitive components be simultaneously present for the individual. Successive information processing is serial processing where information cues consecutively activate the mental component of the individual. Information blocked in related sections (more homogeneous) would evoke a different user response from that same information unblocked and diffused (less homogeneous) throughout the presentation. The second hypothesis is:

## H2: Presentation effects resulting from information cue homogeneity influence IS use.

In research related to task design, Gardner proposed that activation theory serves as an explanation between mental stimulation and task performance. He stated that “activation theory is based on the concept of activation level, defined here as the degree of neural activity...”. It predicts that maximum task performance requires an optimal level of cognitive stimulation. When a deviation from maximum task performance occurs, “this decline in performance is most likely caused by impaired information-processing capability". Einhorn and Hogarth [14], Te'eni, and Cooke [9] have cautioned against the dysfunctional effects of information overload. Considering IS presentations as the relationship between activation theory with positive effects and information overload with negative effects may contribute to the design of more effective IS support.

Characteristics of the individual are explicitly addressed by this research model and are believed to be inseparable from the decision process. Task complexity perceived by the user to include IS presentation is expected to interact with the mode of cognitive processing exhibited during task accomplishment. Artifacts of this IS presentation effect are expected to be evident in performance. More homogeneous IS presentations are expected to be associated with less-demanding serial processing. Less homogeneous presentations are expected to be associated with more cognitively intense simultaneous processing. Higher performers should be better able to accommodate and perform simultaneous processing promoted by the less-homogeneous IS presentation. Lower performers will be less able to accommodate and perform the simultaneous processing associated with less homogeneous IS presentations. Therefore, when considering task performance, higher performers should have an inverse relationship with level of presentation homogeneity, and lower performers should have a positive relationship with the level of presentation homogeneity. In general, when considering IS presentation, higher performers are expected to display an activation-oriented response to less homogeneity and lower performers' responses will be more associated with the information overload. This logic leads to the third and final hypothesis:

H3: In considering task performance using an IS, higher performers when compared to lower performers will respond differently to changes in the level of cue homogeneity in the IS presentation.

## 3. Research framework

## 3.1. Experiment

## 3.1.1. Task environment

A computer-administered test served as the task in this experiment. Test material for the task involved multiple sections of a university-level course in IS. The material varied from conceptual abstractions to applied knowledge. The content of the test material was presented to the subjects during class lectures and in assigned readings from various texts. The instructors of the course determined the exact content of the test sessions. Subjects were exposed to the specific test material for the first time during each experimental session.

The interactive testing program used in this study was a sub-component of a comprehensive CAI system. The subjects could only view one question on the screen at a time. Each question in the exam was displayed with a topic heading and a topic sub-heading. In this way, it was assured that each subject was aware of the topic area of the question. Also, feedback information from the system informed them of all questions that had not been answered up to that point in time, along with the topics of those questions. The subjects could go to the top or bottom of the exam with one keystroke, as well as page up and down. The CAI also allowed the user to move directly to any question by number and topic.

The system had been available to the subject approximately 100 h per week over the course of a full semester to allow them to review the assigned material. During these review sessions, the CAI permitted the subjects the opportunity to select the desired material for review by topical reference, receive a variety of feedback on performance, and re-organize the course material by topical reference. The use of the system for review purposes was strictly voluntary. Additionally, the subjects had taken earlier exams during the semester using the CAI system.

Because the subjects had used the CAI system and, in fact, had quite a bit of experience with the system, it is reasonable to assume that they had developed distinct mental strategies and structures in using the system. Although not reminded during exam time, they knew that the questions could be grouped together. That is, questions on the same topic or subtopic could be viewed one after another.

## 3.1.2. Subjects

One hundred and fifty-six business administration students participated in the study. The subjects' performance on the test session was an integral component of the course requirements and accounted for 20 percent of the course grade. This condition promoted subject motivation and contributed to the subjects' commitment to perform at as high a level as possible.

## 3.1.3. Procedure

There were three separate test sessions; students could choose the time that suited them best. Tests were closely monitored. The testing module interface of the CAI system was identical to the review module interface, with the exception of the on-line intra-session feedback capabilities available in review sessions. Performance feedback in a test session was available only at the completion of the testing session. Subjects were reminded of their obligations under the University's student honor code and they were expressly requested not to discuss any aspect of their participation in the experiment.

The subjects were aware of the experimental nature of the CAI system but were not informed of the specific objectives of the study. The CAI system was presented to the subjects as an integral component of the course as both a monitor for specific knowledge assessment and as a tutorial for major exam preparation. Test questions were selected from a database of questions by the course instructors. Questions not extracted for the test database comprised the review session question database. User accounts with passwords were assigned to each subject, and new passwords were issued for each individual testing session. Upon logging onto the system, the subject was given the option of a review session or a testing session. Time constraints were not placed upon the subjects for either review or test sessions.

Upon invoking the testing session, the experimental treatment for that particular session was decided. Treatments consisted of the test material being presented in either a grouped-by-reference order or a randomized order without respect to reference. In essence, the control group received questions in a homogeneous group (semantically blocked); the experimental group received the questions in less homogeneous groups (semantically fragmented). That is, a student with a semantically blocked test will view questions on the same topic one after the other. All the questions on all the tests for both groups were the same, just organized differently by order.

The treatment was assigned to a subject based on the microcomputer's internal clock at the time of invoking the test, thus randomizing the treatments. The number of each question along with its topic area and subtopic area appeared on the screen at all times. The CAI allowed the student subject to move directly to a question on the same topic or subtopic as the one currently on the screen if he or she chose. This, of course, might help jog the memory of the student and help in determining the correct answers.

The subject's ‘IS use’ behavior and ‘task performance’ were expected to depend on the experimental treatment. IS use was measured by the system procedures invoked during a test session. Procedures available in a testing session included selection of the previous or next question in a sequence, selection of any specific question by number, selection of the first question of a desired reference (e.g., first question in chapter 1 or on the ‘database’ topic), and a global re-ordering of the questions by reference (e.g., all questions from chapter 1, grouped together, or all questions on database grouped together to appear on the screen one after another). ## 3.1.4. Data collection

The testing session process was captured using a computerized activity-logging technique. The advantages of this technique include the comprehensiveness of the method, its unobtrusive nature, and its characteristics of providing explicit and quantifiable measures. Computer logging has been suggested as a means of monitoring a subject's cognitive model, analyzing and evaluating interactive systems [20], and as a basis for a theory of information flow and valuation [46]. Todd and Benbasat state that “the use of computer logs to monitor interactive decision-making activity is particularly relevant to DSS research...”. These attributes qualify computerized activity logs as an appropriate data collection technique within a wide variety of research objectives.

## 4. Analysis of results

Gagne identified logical analysis techniques that facilitate theorizing about cognitive processes. These analysis techniques include the subtractive technique, information-processing analysis, and computer simulation [17]. Newell and Simon considered one criterion for ‘data sufficiency’ in process-tracing research as being the capability of directing a computer simulation from the collected data to reproduce the decision outcome observed. They concur with Gagne’s analysis methodologies and use an information-processing analysis technique when examining heuristic-search methods. This research used an information-processing analysis approach.

## 4.1. Results

A subject's final percentage of correct answers on the test session served as the criterion for classifying subjects into performance groups. Table 2 provides the statistics used as the basis for this dimension. Subjects within plus or minus one standard deviation from the mean test score were assigned to the ‘middle performance’ group; subjects above or below this variance were assigned to the higher or lower group, respectively. The analysis of variance (ANOVA) statistical technique was used in data analysis to identify significant differences in IS use between groups.

## 4.2. Information system use by level of performance (H1)

The first hypothesis involves the IS use exhibited by the subject during task accomplishment, as moderated by level of performance. The statistical technique of ANOVA was applied to the data set after defining groups based on level of performance. The analysis indicates that subjects' system use significantly varied between groups on two variables. The difference in the number of questions viewed and the time per evaluation was significant between high and middle performance groups. High performers were found to view significantly more questions than middle performers. High performers also spent less time per evaluation than middle performers. Based on these two statistically significant differences between high and middle performance groups, the first hypothesis is accepted. Therefore, as anticipated, level of task performance was found to serve as a moderator of IS use during task accomplishment. The number of information screens used in the task and the time spent considering and making a judgment significantly vary between high and middle performance groups Table 3.

## 4.3. Information presentation effects on is use (H2)

The second hypothesis addresses the influence of the experimental treatment of information presentation homogeneity on subject task performance. ANOVA applied at this stage of the investigation revealed that the information presentation treatment did not affect IS use for all subjects combined into one general group. However, further analysis indicated that certain performance sub-groups were affected by the experimental treatment. The data analysis suggests that higher performers respond differently than middle performers to the experimental treatment, while the lower performance group did not exhibit any treatment response Table 4.

The ANOVA results indicate that higher performers were affected by the experimental treatment. Considering higher performers, the number of questions viewed and the final percentage correct significantly varied between the control and experimental groups. Middle performers were also influenced by the experimental treatment, but to a lesser degree than the higher performers. The number of questions viewed by middle performers significantly varied between the control and experimental groups. Higher performers were found to be more affected by the IS task presentation treatment. Therefore, the second hypothesis is not accepted, because, in general, the subjects did not exhibit a significant response to the IS presentation treatment. However, when analyzed by performance groups the data suggest that higher performers may be the more sensitive user group with respect to IS presentation effects Table 5.

## 6. Discussion and conclusions

The results of this study are consistent with much of the previous research. These findings support Payne's theory of mental effort being based upon a subject's cost/benefit perspective. Einhorn and Hogarth's finding of judgment and choice being sensitive to minor task changes is also supported. Russo found decision strategies to be the result of deliberate processes [35]. The findings of this study suggest that these strategies are tightly coupled to the subject's level of task domain knowledge.

The findings of this study suggest that the demands of perceived task complexity strongly influence the subprocesses of decision making. In conjunction, the impact of changes in the demands of the task, as influenced by information presentation treatments, vary by decision-maker task domain knowledge.

The experimental treatment in this study influenced the higher performance group by affecting a judgment phase of the decision process (questions answered). The higher performance group receiving more simultaneous data presentation made fewer judgments, suggesting an efficiency effect (Table 4). For higher performers the final percentage correct was significantly affected by the experimental treatment, suggesting an effectiveness consequence. The total impact of the information presentation treatment indicates that, within the higher performance group, simultaneous information presentation promotes more efficient decisional behavior resulting in more effective decisions. In essence, a more efficient and effective support tool for higher performers delivers information in a more simultaneous manner, providing the user with beneficial levels of cognitive stimulation (i.e., increased activation).

In contrast to the higher performers, middle performers exhibited a different treatment effect. The number of questions viewed by middle performers was influenced by the experimental treatment. Those receiving simultaneous information presentation were less efficient than those receiving successive information flows. The effectiveness of middle performers did not exhibit any presentation treatment effects. That is, simultaneous information flows for the middle performers had an effect on decision-making behavior as it relates to the amount of information used.

The data suggest that higher performers have different informational needs. In fact, it supports the premise that the informational needs between performance groups may be in opposition. That is, higher performers benefited both in efficiency and effectiveness when provided with simultaneous information presentation, while middle performers were less efficient. Therefore, providing different information presentation schemes promotes enhancements in task accomplishment for both performance groups. Providing the appropriate IS presentation based on the level of task domain knowledge can result in higher performers benefiting in efficiency and effectiveness, while middle performers become only more efficient.

In this study, based on the activation-theory model, higher performers appear to be more cognitively stimulated when they receive simultaneous sources of information. That is, when the information cues are semantically fragmented (less homogeneous) the cognitive activation level of these users increases. It appears that a subject's information load is a function of the level of task domain knowledge, the amount of information received, and the organization of that information. An important aspect of this finding is that only the higher performers exhibited a significant effect on task effectiveness as a result of the experimental treatment.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates several important principles. The effect of task presentation on a subject's mental representation of the task is evidenced by the higher performers' reaction to the experimental treatment. In addition, different IS use characteristics were exhibited by subjects when grouped by level of task domain knowledge. The instance of perceived task complexity as a function of order treatment and level of task domain knowledge is also relevant to IS design, as it suggests the expanding scope of information requirements determination.

Several limitations of this study should be addressed. While the activation theory was used to explain the subject behavior as well as to derive conclusions, certainly other theoretical models could potentially lead one to alternative conclusions and, therefore, should be explored. Further, the generalizability of the study beyond the laboratory setting is an inherent limitation of the research design. In an effort to address these and other validity concerns the authors attempted to focus on general research principles that mitigated such threats (e.g., theoretical underpinning, random assignment, pilot testing, etc.). Replications and extensions of the research in various settings are necessary to further extend the findings and conclusions of the study.

Based on the findings, this research accomplishes its primary objectives of contributing to the understanding of how, when and why IS provide effective support. Data analysis supports the conclusion that effective IS support is a dynamic state involving research constructs of the user, task, and system. The ability of the perceived task complexity model to represent and capture significant user differences suggests that efficient and effective IS support requires a robust representation of reality that dynamically incorporates influences of these constructs.

The potential impact of these findings on IS design is significant. Based upon empirically measured information preference, classes of IS users based on task domain expertise possess differing IS needs. IS designers should become aware of these differing human information-processing needs. The evidence indicates a lack of subject self awareness of the implications of presentation order effects, suggesting that users may be unable to express their particular information-processing requirements adequately. This places additional responsibilities on the IS design function to provide support capabilities. Based on the findings, IS design and operations can benefit from further research addressing the process of decision-making. Specifically, research emphasizing the construct of perceived task complexity, as moderated by task-domain knowledge, may contribute to significant enhancement of information support systems.
