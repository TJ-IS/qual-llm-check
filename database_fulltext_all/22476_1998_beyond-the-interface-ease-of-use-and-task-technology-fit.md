# Beyond the interface: Ease of use and task/technology fit

Kieran Mathieson $^{a,*}$ , Mark Keil $^{1,b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Decision and Information Sciences, School of Business Administration, Oakland University, Rochester 48309-4493, USA $^{b}$ Department of Computer Information Systems, College of Business Administration, Georgia State University, P.O. Box 4015, Atlanta 30302-4015, USA

Received 20 September 1996; revised 8 May 1998; accepted 20 May 1998

## Abstract

Perceived ease of use (EOU) is an important factor in determining whether an individual will voluntarily use an IS. Many developers focus their attention on the system's interface when designing for EOU. For users, however, perceived EOU can extend beyond the interface. This paper presents the results of a laboratory experiment confirming that perceived EOU is also a function of task/technology fit. When users report that a system is difficult to use, developers should not assume that the interface is the problem. There may be deeper task/technology fit issues that are not corrected by changing the interface. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Experiment; Behavior; Performance; Perception; Ease of use; Usability; Interface; Task; Technology; Fit; Ease of use

## 1. Introduction

Information systems (IS) are designed to help users perform tasks more effectively and efficiently. IS that are not used are no help at all. Developers within an organization can force some workers to use their systems, if only by not providing any other way to access data; but, of course, forcing people to use poor systems breeds resentment and mistrust. Further, developers are seldom able to force executives, middle managers, and other key-knowledge workers to use their systems. They must make their systems sufficiently attractive so that decision-makers use them voluntarily.

Davis, Bagozzi, and Warshaw [10] have shown that voluntary IS use is affected by the system's perceived ease of use (EOU) and perceived usefulness. Their results have been confirmed by others (e.g. [1, 9, 16, 20]). The continuing development of graphical user interface (GUI) technology improves EOU. However, designers' efforts to correct perceived EOU deficiencies are not always successful. Consider the following example from a field study by Keil, Beranek and Konsynski [18]:

“A computer company implemented an expert support system for its sales representatives, but found that usage was low. Feedback indicated that the system was difficult to use, so the company’s developers performed a major rewrite of the user interface. After redeploying the new and improved tool, they surveyed the users (more than 100), and found that there was no significant increase in use. What is more, the users still perceived the software to be cumbersome.”

## What went wrong?

The answer seems to be that the developers assumed that perceived EOU is determined only by the human-computer interface. This is understandable, given that articles in many professional journals tend to equate EOU with GUI design issues, such as the use of colors, fonts, buttons, and 3D effects. While interface-related variables are important, it is possible that other factors also influence perceived EOU. If they are not understood, developers risk wasting their efforts.

In this example, the developers apparently misunderstood the users' needs. The data obtained by Keil et al. did not allow them to test this idea fully. However, based on user comments, they concluded that the EOU deficiencies were a function of the mismatch between the tasks the system supported and those the users wanted to perform.

This paper examines this idea more formally, testing whether perceived EOU is affected by the fit between task and technology. In other words, will an IS that helps a user perform a task more effectively be perceived as easier to use than a system that does not help the user as much, even if they have comparable interfaces?

## 2. EOU and task/technology fit

Perceived EOU has a positive effect on an individuals' attitudes and intentions toward using an information system. This result is consistent with diffusion of innovation (DOI) research, which suggests a relationship between innovation complexity and adoption behavior. Rogers and Shoemaker [25] define the complexity of an innovation as “the degree to which an innovation is perceived as relatively difficult to understand and use.” In their meta-analysis of the DOI literature, Tornatzky and Klein [27] conclude that perceived complexity – which is inversely related to perceived EOU – has a negative effect on technology adoption. Indeed, complexity is one of the few variables that has been consistently linked to adoption behavior [24].

Thus, perceived EOU can have an important effect on the adoption and use of IS. Other things being equal, increasing perceived EOU should increase voluntary system adoption. But how can developers alter an IS to improve perceived EOU?

Prior research has shown that system interfaces affect perceived EOU or related concepts, such as user interface satisfaction. Margono and Shneiderman $[19]$ report that subjects perceived the Macintosh interface to be easier to use than the MSDOS interface, and were more productive using it. Potter et al. $[22]$ and Chimera and Shneiderman $[8]$ also found that interface style affected perceived EOU. In a more recent study, Davis and Bostrom $[11]$ found that a GUI interface resulted in higher productivity than a text interface, but that the difference in EOU perceptions was marginal. They note that, for their study, “other factors may be more important determinants of user perceptions.”

One such factor may be the fit between the technology and the tasks its users are trying to perform. The concept of fit has received attention in a variety of research contexts ([29, 30]). It has been used to refer to, for example, the relationship between a person and his or her environment [5], and the “match or congruence between an information system and its organizational environment” [17]. Fit is used here to refer to the congruence between a technology and a task, that is, the extent to which a particular task can be performed effectively and efficiently with a particular technology. Fit is an interaction; only when both task and system are known can fit be estimated with any confidence.

When people use an IS to complete a task, they perform various types of operations, like opening a file, entering query parameters, etc. Fit is affected by the number and complexity of these operations. Some researchers have examined the effect of fit on performance. Card et al. ([6, 7]) examined keystroke-level models of expert users' task performance, finding that performance using the same system varied with the task, while performance of the same task varied across systems. In our terms, fit affected performance. Wilson and Addo [32] studied computer-displayed graphs. They found that people are more efficient in completing tasks when there is a good fit between the problem and the set of graphs. Spence and Tsai [26] consider the relationship between cognitive style and processes under multiple task environments, concluding that cognitive processes vary according to task.

Vessey [31] considered the effect of fit on problem solving performance, addressing the graph vs. tables controversy. Her model

… views problem solving as an outcome of the relationship between problem representation and the problem-solving task … problem solving with cognitive fit leads to effective and efficient problem-solving performance [31].

When the user's problem representation does not match that supported by the system, the user translates between the representations. This increases the number of operations needed to perform a task. When there is a good match, no translation is necessary and fewer operations are performed. Umanath and Vessey [28] found empirical support for these ideas in the financial domain.

Clearly, there is strong evidence that fit affects performance. However, fit also affects perceptions of IS effectiveness. Miller [21] found a correlation between user satisfaction and the match between perceived IS needs and perceived IS capabilities. Goodhue [12] called this match ‘IS satisfactoriness’, which he defined as the “correspondence between job requirements and I/S functionality.” He also argued that task/technology fit is an important factor in “the system-to-value causal chain.” Goodhue and Thompson [13] found empirical evidence that task/technology fit affected user perceptions of the performance impacts of an IS.

It seems reasonable that fit should also be related to perceived EOU. According to Davis et al., perceived EOU is a belief about whether “using a particular system would be free from effort” (italics added). EOU is an attribute of an individual’s use of a system, rather than of the system per se. Variation in task might change task/technology fit, altering the way a system is used. This might change perceived EOU. This is consistent with Keil et al., who suggested that poor task/technology fit caused low EOU scores, although they did not collect data to test this idea directly.

Thus, given a particular task typically performed with an IS, we hypothesize that:

H1. The task by itself does not determine perceived EOU.

H2. The IS by itself does not determine perceived EOU, controlling for interface.

H3. The interaction of task and IS affects perceived EOU.

## 3. Research methodology

A laboratory experiment was performed to test the effect of task/technology fit on perceived EOU. The system interface was controlled, since it is known to affect perceived EOU. Task performance was measured to check that the manipulations functioned as expected.

## 3.1. Subjects

The subjects were undergraduate business students enrolled in an information technology course at a large university in the western U.S. While using students limits external validity ([14, 15]), this approach is legitimate when the tasks being studied involve basic human information processing and decision-making [2].

The experimental task involved creating database queries to answer questions in a given problem domain. It was important to ensure that the subjects were motivated, understood the task domain, and understood how to use the technology. Several steps were taken to address these issues. First, the exercise accounted for 20% of each student's grade for the class, a weight greater than that commonly assigned to experimental exercises. This encouraged subjects to try hard. Second, the task domain was that of a university's scheduling and student records function. The entities in the domain were students, professors, classes and schedules, all familiar to the subjects. Third, since the questions were to be answered using SQL queries, subjects were given 5 hours of classroom instruction in SQL prior to the experiment. The training was followed by a formal assignment using a task similar to that in the experimental materials. The average score on the assignment was 31.7 (S.D.=6.1) out of a maximum of 36, suggesting that the subjects had become familiar with the technology.

## 3.2. Experimental systems and tasks

There were two systems $(S_{1}$ and $S_{2})$ and two tasks $(T_{1}$ and $T_{2})$ . These were designed to create different levels of fit for task/system combinations. Each system was a set of tables in a relational database. Appendix A shows the tables for each system. The systems were created by vertically fragmenting the PROF., STUDENT and Class tables in different ways. Vertical fragmentation distributes the columns of one table across two or more tables.

It is important to note that all subjects used the same underlying DBMS: XDB. The manipulation only involved changes to the table structures; the user interface itself was held constant. Thus, any effects are the result of manipulating task/technology fit; they cannot be attributed to interface differences.

Are the two systems different? They use the same DBMS with the same interface. Using two GUI systems or two command-driven systems was not enough, since interfaces can vary significantly within each category. Using the same DBMS ensured that the interfaces would be identical. For our purposes, two systems are different if they force users to complete tasks in different ways. Performance data presented later suggests that this was the case here.

Each task consisted of ten questions requiring the development of SQL queries. Both tasks could be completed with either system, although, of course, with different queries. The systems were designed so that $T_{1}$ was easier to solve with $S_{1}$ than with $S_{2}$ , and $T_{2}$ was easier to solve with $S_{2}$ than with $S_{1}$ . In other words, the combinations $[T_{1}|S_{1}]$ and $[T_{2}|S_{2}]$ were designed to achieve a high degree of task/technology fit, whereas the combinations $[T_{1}|S_{2}]$ and $[T_{2}|S_{1}]$ were designed to yield a low degree of task/technology fit.

Fit was manipulated by changing the number of joins in the SQL queries needed to answer the questions. Joins are used when the information required to answer a question is in more than one table. Queries using joins are more difficult to write than queries referencing a single table ([3, 23]), with user performance decreasing as the number of joins increases. Few joins were needed to answer the questions in $T_{1}$ when using $S_{1}$ or to answer the questions in $T_{2}$ when using $S_{2}$ . More joins were required to answer the questions in $T_{1}$ when using $S_{2}$ or $T_{2}$ when using $S_{1}$ .

It seems reasonable prima facie that the fit between task and system should be affected by the number of joins, which should influence the complexity of the task. However, the fit manipulation as implemented in this experiment is reasonably complex, involving several entities, tables, questions and so on. Further, a query also involves field identifiers, relational operators, etc. Differences in the operations required to define these aspects of the queries might have affected the manipulations. We tried to keep them approximately the same for each query, but subtle differences might have occurred.

A manipulation check was necessary to confirm that the task/system combinations yielded the expected levels of fit. Thus, we examined the effect of fit on performance, a relationship already established by other researchers.

internal reliability of the instrument as measured by Cronbach's alpha was 0.90.

Subjects' task performance was measured to test the fit manipulation. Performance has both time and accuracy components [4]. The experimental procedure fixed the time available for the tasks, so performance was defined as query accuracy. Participants earned four points for each question. This gave a maximum of 40 points for each task. Some credit was awarded for answers that were partially correct, such as a query that only gave one part of a join.

## 3.4. Procedure

A total of 271 subjects participated in the experiment. Each subject was seated at a PC and randomly given either $S_{1}$ or $S_{2}$ . After they had been allowed 5 min to study the database definitions, the first task (either $T_{1}$ or $T_{2}$ , chosen randomly) was distributed. The group was given 20 minutes to complete the task. To ensure compliance with this time limit, the subjects' computers were deactivated automatically after 20 minutes. The perceived EOU questionnaire instrument was then presented on each subject's PC.

The second task was then distributed, with subjects using the same system they had used earlier. Subjects who received $T_{1}$ initially received $T_{2}$ for the second task and vice versa. The same administrative procedure was followed.

All subjects received both tasks, with presentation order randomized. This controlled for differences in individual ability and learning between the first and second tasks. Further, the subjects could not complete the tasks in the time available, so they were busy all of the time. This helped control the time aspect of performance.

## 4. Results

Regression was used to analyze the pooled data. The manipulation check was tested first. Fig. 2 and Table 2 show that subjects performed best when we manipulated task/technology fit to be high (i.e. System 1 and Task 1; System 2 and Task 2). When task/technology fit was manipulated to be low, performance was relatively low. The first model in Table 2 shows that the main effect of Task had a statistically significant but very small effect on performance ( $R^{2}=1\%$ ). This effect is inconsequential, especially given the size of other effects. Models 2 and 3 show that the main effect of System did not contribute to the explanation of performance. Model 4 shows that adding the interaction term increased the explained variance to 32%. The interaction term also has a larger absolute $\beta$ than that of Task. In other words, neither task nor system by itself explains performance, but their combination does. These results suggest that the manipulation was successful in creating varying levels of task/technology fit.

## 5. Conclusions

This study provides empirical evidence that task/technology fit affects perceived ease of use (EOU), independent of system interface. Before drawing any conclusions, potential limitations of the experiment should be addressed. First, the subjects were students using a DBMS. It is possible the results would not apply in other situations. However, the mechanisms by which fit influences perceived EOU, as postulated in this study, do not depend on the specific task nor any particular attributes of the user. Second, the subjects were all drawn from the same culture. Members of other cultures may behave differently. However, nothing in the general mechanisms is obviously dependent on culture.

What are the implications of the findings? The results suggest that design choices altering the fit between task and system may also affect perceived EOU. A good interface alone does not make an IS easy to use. When EOU problems are observed, consulting an interface expert might not be effective if task/technology fit is really the issue.

Further, there may be an upper bound for perceived EOU for a system used for multiple tasks. Suppose one individual uses a human resource system for monitoring payroll data while another uses it to help make decisions about health insurance. Because of conflicting task requirements, limits in development resources or the greater political influence of some groups, the system might fit one decision maker's tasks better than the other. This will limit average EOU scores.

In sum, this study has shown that the task/technology fit can influence perceived EOU. Practitioners should consider this effect when addressing EOU problems. Researchers should take the effect into account when studying the EOU construct.
