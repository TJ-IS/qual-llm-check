# Case study: The effect of IS maturity on information systems strategic planning

Narciso Cerpa $^{a,*}$ , June M. Verner $^{1,b}$

$^{a}$ School of Information Systems, University of New South Wales, Sydney 2052, Australia $^{b}$ College of Information Science and Technology, Drexel University, Philadelphia 19104, USA

Received 24 September 1997; accepted 24 April 1998

## Abstract

We present a longitudinal study of the information systems strategic planning process (ISSP) within a large Australian organization and review the changes and developments that have taken place within this organization over a period of four years. We discuss ISSP with regard to its strategic relevance, factors affecting IS strategic planning, key issues in ISSP, the effects of infusion and diffusion levels, together with the effect of IS maturity on ISSP and ISSP on IS maturity. Our study shows that the adoption of ISSP methodologies has had numerous benefits within the organization. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Information systems strategic planning; Information technology planning; Strategic planning; IS maturity

## 1. Introduction

Organizations must have an information systems (IS) strategic plan to facilitate the management and control of information technology (IT) resources $[4, 9, 10, 14]$ . This plan is normally the result of an ISSP exercise. As technology continues to change, grow and become more complex, the ISSP process becomes complex and difficult to handle. Although there are a number of methodologies available $[1, 2, 6, 16, 21, 23, 25]$ , due to the complexity of the planning process there is no one methodology suitable for all situations.

Here, we investigate the planning process within a large Australian commercial organization by interviewing a number of people involved in the ISSP. This allowed us to determine the effectiveness of the organization's ISSP and the difficulties they have encountered. In this case study, we describe the changes and developments that have taken place over a period of four years and consider the strategic relevance of ISSP to the firm, and the growth in IS maturity. We feel that an understanding of the ISSP process within this organization, and seeing how their ISSP process can change over time, can be helpful to both ISSP researchers and the organizations that must undertake ISSP. Greater knowledge of the major factors influencing the implementation of an information systems strategic plan, the benefits that ISS planners believe have been provided by the adoption of ISSP methodologies, and the factors that ISSP personnel consider are important may help other organizations.

## 2. Strategic relevance of IS planning

Research [12, 13] suggests that organizations in which IS strategic planning has been practiced for more than three years, have better user satisfaction with the IS function than organizations, where ISSP has existed for a shorter time. IS planning should take a long-range view. Moreover, if it includes consideration of external factors, it is more likely to contribute to business strategy than if it takes a narrow view.

Focusing IS planning so that it results in a strategic impact, is not an easy task. Lederer and Mendelow [15], found that the difficulty of convincing top management of IS's potential for producing a strategic impact, impedes IS planning. Although IT managers may be able to visualize the strategic impact that IS can have, they then must promote and market their visions to top management.

Even though, the use of IS for competitive advantage has been defined and described, actually using it $[17, 24]$ is still a weakness in IS planning. While many authors state the advantages of using IS for strategic purposes, it is not clear if, and how, organizations can do this $[11]$ . How strategic and relevant IS strategic planning is, will depend on the organization, its environment, culture, and maturity of the IS function $[5, 19]$ . While IS maturity may be relevant to the success of the IS strategy implementation, the setting of an appropriate IS strategy will allow the organization to progress through the stages of IS growth $[7, 8]$ . Methodologies currently used in ISSP are normally highly dependent on the expertise of those doing the planning.

## 3. Linking IS strategic planning with business objectives

There is a general consensus that the effectiveness of ISSP from an IS manager's perspective is dependent on its alignment with business objectives. Independent of the method of alignment used, the organization needs to be able to measure the precision of the alignment to assess the information systems function [20]. Measuring this alignment can be as difficult as the planning process itself.

In a study of IS managers and senior business managers, Nath [21], found that the main factors affecting the alignment of IS management with business management are: (1) educating senior management in IS; (2) a strong set of organizational goals and objectives concerning IS; (3) the ability of IS management to keep up with advances in IT; and (4) educating management in business goals and objectives. This work revealed that both, IS managers and senior business managers agree that ‘educating management in IS’, the most important factor in ensuring success in aligning IS with business strategy and the commitment of senior management to the IS function, depends on senior management’s knowledge of IS. Nath’s research also shows that the alignment of IS planning with business goals and objectives is necessary for the success of IS planning. If there is alignment with business goals and objectives, there is a base for strategic impact and, hence, competitive advantage.

## 4. Factors affecting IS strategic planning

IS managers and chief information officers (CIO) emphasize the importance of their relationship with the Chief Executive Officer (CEO); those closer to the CEO have a better understanding of the CEO's expectations [28]. The reporting level of the IS manager, therefore, affects his/her communication mode with the CEO, and this affects the CEO's perception of the importance of IS planning. Poor communication links between the IS manager and the CEO indicate that the organization does not place a high value on IS, or its planning. Good communication links between the CIO and other business units is important to IS for other reasons, e.g. in obtaining the understanding and support of current and potential clients [26].

ISSP methodologies often result in very satisfactory plans, but a lack of management commitment and the absence of the control mechanisms necessary to ensure the success of the plans can impede its implementation. However, Lederer and Sethi, suggest there are other factors affecting successful IS planning $[16]$ :

\- The lack of a business plan can be a real impediment. However, if business planning is effective, and business goals and objectives well defined, then the IS plan can be more effectively linked to the business goals.

\- Fewer problems and less severe problems occur with a planning horizon of 3–5 years, as this allows more control of the plan.

\- The scope of the IS study can affect the planning process. A broader scope can give IS planners a better vision of the required data architecture.

\- The initiator of the IS planning study can be an important factor affecting the planning process; though IS managers seek the involvement and commitment of senior and line management, they still prefer to maintain control of the planning exercise.

Another important issue is the need for an appropriate understanding of the IT organizational structure [3]. How companies organize their IT activities may have a significant impact on the overall effectiveness of their support.

## 5. Key issues in ISSP

Some key issues in the IS planning process are as follows:

\- The involvement and commitment of senior management is essential to the success of the IS plan. It does not matter how good the plan is, if the involvement and commitment of senior management is absent.

\- Linking IS to business goals is the heart of IS planning, and without this link, the IS function will not have major relevance for the organization.

\- Choosing the right planning methodology depends on the current use and spread of technology within the organization and the importance of the current systems. Resources available (staff, skills, CASE tools, etc.) will also impact this process. It appears that the use of more than one methodology should be recommended.

\- While new technology can be advantageous, it can also pose severe problems if the right skills and expertise are not available to use it properly.

\- On-going evaluation of the IS strategic plan to ensure that the plan is implemented correctly, and the expected results are being obtained.

## 6. IS strategic planning

IS planning methodologies can be classified according to the activities that they imply, as well as the support that they provide. Barlow describes CSF and the strategic planning grid as methodologies that support the discovery of an organization's IS needs. After the business needs have been identified, he suggests the use of strategy set transformation, which allows the linkage of IS with business goals. Then, Barlow suggests the use of portfolio analysis, which will assess the risk of an IS strategy. Finally, the stages-of-growth model can be used for evaluating and reviewing the IS strategy. He compares this four-methodologies approach with the business systems planning (BSP) methodology: an integrated approach that can support the first three activities. An organization with a formal culture may prefer to use an integrated approach, such as BSP, while other less formal organizations may prefer to use a portfolio of methods with a different one for some activities.

Organizations that have experienced minimal infusion and diffusion should find the stages-of-growth approach helpful. If the infusion is high and the diffusion is low, BSP is more likely to be useful. If the diffusion is high and the infusion is low, CSF is more applicable $[27]$ .

## 7. The present study

This Australian commercial organization has approximately 18000 employees distributed over many different countries; it is heavily dependent on complex information technology for its day-to-day operations. The organization includes a main umbrella organization and a number of subsidiaries. The IS function is carried out by an IT subsidiary. Our information about the ISSP process was obtained by using both, a questionnaire and structured interviews with the business planning group, data architecture staff and business employees of the organization. All the IS staff interviewed had over twelve years' IS experience and some years of management experience at a strategic level. In some instances our respondents had different opinions on the topics addressed and activities undertaken in the ISSP process, therefore, we have only included information upon which there was a consensus.

Our discussion and results are presented in two parts: Stage 1 includes information collected in the initial interviews, and Stage 2, conducted four years later, involves information collected after fundamental changes in the IS function. At Stage 1, the organization's structure was centralized with traditional functional divisions, a formal culture, and bureaucratic management style. By Stage 2, the firm had evolved to a more centralized, rather mixed environment, still having a formal culture but with an autocratic management style having some overtones of democracy.

At Stage 1, the subsidiary IT organization had about 600 employees and a budget of \$157 million. By Stage 2, the IT subsidiary had nearly 700 employees and an annual budget of about \$200 million. Structural changes within the organization, and a number of difficulties that affected the company during the last few years make the analysis more complex and challenging. According to the literature, this organization has the optimum structure for its IS function reporting level. The umbrella organization created a position, the CIO.

## 7.1. Corporate strategic planning

The organization has a corporate planning group of about 15 persons, including the CIO; this group reports directly to the CEO. The corporate strategic planning cycle is normally five years, with the planning committee meeting quarterly. The corporate planning group has a budget of about \$5 million. Corporate plans are communicated throughout the organization so that staff learn about the mission statement and the vision and direction of the organization. Such an approach has made the staff feel that they are an important part of the organization.

Originally, the mission statement was badly affected by an economic downturn, and the organization, formerly a market leader, found itself a market follower. The corporate mission had also been affected by the introduction of new competitors. By Stage 2, this situation appears to be resolved, with the organization once again considered a market leader.

The relationship between the IT subsidiary and the CEO should have been very good; however, the relationship at the start was affected by a number of difficulties that were ‘competitive in nature’. This resulted in a lack of complete success of the IT subsidiary and later required changes in the IS management.

## 7.2. Framework for the ISSP process in the organization

## 7.2.1. Stage 1

The IS function at the start was operating with a centralized structure, though most, but not all of the systems were built without the benefit of a proper data administration function. As a result, the organization was re-engineering to include data administration control. All new developments were then under data administration control. We categorized the IS function at Nolan's ‘data administration’ stage [22]. However, because the introduction of new technology such as distributed processing, distributed database systems, etc. were very new to the organization, parts of the IS function were in the ‘initiation’ stage.

## 7.2.2. Stage 2

Later, the situation had changed, with the IS function at Nolan's ‘maturity’ level. According to McFarlan et al.'s strategic grid [18], a typical IS function in this type of organization can be classified as ‘factory’. However, the Australian organization classifies its main systems as of ‘critical dependence’, because they are important to the day-to-day operation of the company. The IS function is a 24 hours-a-day function, with terminals all around the world. Thus, any ‘systems down’ even for a few minutes, can cause very large losses and major delays in the operations. We therefore classify the IS function as ‘strategic’, which agrees with Wilkes [29], who criticized the McFarlan approach and suggested that some organizations classified as ‘support’ or ‘factory’ can still be of strategic importance.

## 7.3. The IS function and the ISSP process

The ISSP process for the organization only began formally in 1990; previously there was merely a budget setting process based on day-to-day resources and technology upgrades. Initially, IS was a part of the main organization, i.e. it had not been made into a subsidiary company and was viewed as a support service.

## 7.3.1. Stage 1

The ISSP was originally performed in the IS planning group reporting directly to the CIO. It consisted of business planning consultants (BPCs), information architecture consultants (IACs) and technology monitoring staff; they met on a monthly basis though the ISSP cycle itself was ad hoc. The BPCs obtained requirements and the IACs decided whether these fitted into the current architecture or changes to the architecture were needed. The approach was based on the IS architecture at that time, and the ISSP was intended solely to satisfy the customer. The proposed and planned requirements were presented to a steering committee before they were presented to the board. This committee made decisions on technology and resources. Major customer input to the process was through the steering committee and the BPCs. The committee was staffed by IS, user, and technical management and included financial advisors.

Although the ISSP did not use any formal ISSP approach or techniques, it did include some ‘analysis of competitive tactics and targets’. IS strategy decisions were prioritized by return on investment (ROI) and informal risk analysis within the area, department, and/or division involved. Some of the people who were interviewed recognized that a formal planning methodology was required, they felt that any methodology would be better than nothing. The people to whom we talked did not see the methods as sufficiently formal or capable of providing the necessary support. The fundamental concepts of ISSP (such as setting up an architecture, linking IS goals with the business objectives and developing a strategy) existed, but the lack of a methodology seemed a main problem. After a planning exercise, the ISSP was frequently revised. These revisions had some of the same characteristics as the ISSP process, i.e. no specific method or approach was used.

Recession in the industry had also badly affected the IT subsidiary, with staff redundancies, budget difficulties, and delays in the introduction of new technology. This also affected the ISSP process itself.

## 7.3.2. Stage 2

Within four years, the ISSP cycle had been formalized into an annual process. The ISS plan was presented for approval (or rejection) to the board of directors. An IS steering committee, formed by executives, management, planning staff, IT specialists, IS users and business representatives was set up. This committee usually decided on new technology and planning issues before the plan was presented to the board. The steering committee (16 persons through the year) cost an estimated \$5 million annually; much of this was spent on salaries and related benefits, but training, conference expenses, business travel, etc. were also included.

The main focus of the IS strategic planning function had changed, and business objectives were most important: information architecture only provided support. The goal was ‘to build all systems in a strategic way’, to gain strategic advantage. Operational systems were recognized as strategic to the organization. Steering committees had been formed for each business area and some across-business-areas committees existed. Several methodologies had been incorporated into the planning process, such as BSP, CSF, strategic planning grid, and information planning resources. While there was still no official planning methodology document, the plan and the programs to support it existed, and were disseminated to key people. It was not, however obvious whether this resulted from a change in management policies or in strategic planning personnel.

The IT subsidiary took a fairly formal approach to ISSP; the level of planning was international, because representatives from branches around the world had to be involved and the organization had to focus on providing a service to international customers.

## 7.4. Output from the ISSP process

The IS strategic planners now have very different perceptions from those at the start. The automation of business functions has increased from 40 to $80\%$ overall. Functions automated are perceived as being critical to the company's operation, reducing operating costs or supplying high quality service to customers. The introduction of new technology was reduced, because a change from a technology-driven to a business-objectives-driven approach had occurred.

Management now perceives the linkage between ISSP and the corporate business plan as very good. The congruence is due to a good understanding of the corporate goals by IS management. This congruence considerably strengthened in the last three years, because of the introduction of planning methodologies, and the linking of ISSP to business goals and objectives.

## 7.4.1. Stage 1

At Stage 1, the linkage between IS goals and corporate objectives was considered very good by respondents. Even though the linkage was considered strong, it only resulted from knowledge of individual members of the organization. It was clear that there was a need for a methodology to identify business needs.

The ISS planning goal of the organization was to set up an architecture that could be considered strategic. At Stage 1, the ISSP included three main proposals: distributed platform, database machine, and full implementation of a corporate database concept. The development of an architecture and its associated technological assessments were considered to be the driving force for the implementation of the strategic plan.

## 7.4.2. Stage 2

At Stage 2, the organization had implemented about 80% of the activities, systems, and tasks, that were planned in the previous four years. About 50% of the systems proposed in the first ISSP were not implemented for several reasons, such as changed priorities, budget, risk, and ROI. The other 50% have been built and are currently in production. All systems now go through the ISSP process to consider their possible development. A small number of systems were developed during the last two years without prior planning; these were either the result of marketing needs (when the organization felt that it could not be left behind), or because of the absorption of another company. These systems were already either under development or had already been completed and were included in our review.

By Stage 2, the main focus was business-driven. Our respondents stated that “replacement of old technology must be gradual and according to business plans.”

## 7.6. Problems with the ISSP process

Every organization's ISSP process is likely to have problems: they sometimes go undetected. It is important that IS strategic planning personnel are fully aware of the problems and know how to tackle them. At each of the two stages, the IS strategic planning personnel gave opinions of the general problems affecting the ISSP; these are shown in Table 5.

They also discussed the major problems affecting their particular ISSP at both stages, as shown in Table 6.

By Stage 2, the planning staff felt that the lack of senior management involvement during the ISSP process was only of ‘some importance’. Our respondents commented that ISSP was a job for IS planning staff and though management commitment is needed, much involvement is not. Their view at Stage 1, however, was different; at the time they felt that senior management involvement was crucial. During the Stage 1 planning exercise, it was necessary to obtain data from senior management because it was not otherwise available. Our respondents did not believe that this was the usual case. At Stage 2, the respondents felt that, in some situations, senior management involvement can be important at times of rapid business change.

## 8. Conclusions

The adoption of ISSP methodologies has had numerous benefits. They provided the means to set up a planning framework, support for the linkage of IS with business goals, aid in assessing the risk of the IS strategy, and they allowed the evaluation and review of the IS strategy.

Considering the organization's culture, size, management style and approach, the IS planning approach that perhaps best fits this organization is the CSF method. This allowed the identification of the business needs at different management levels, and permits the setting up of an IS strategy. However, since this Australian organization has high level of infusion and diffusion, the use of more than one method is valuable.

The ISSP process is still very young in this organization. Based on our case study, we can say that the ISSP process is a learning experience and it will be different in each organization. Perhaps one of the most important issues is how the maturity of the IS function affected the IS planning process. Initially most of the IS function was at the data administration stage, but part of it was at the initiation stage due to the introduction of new technology into the organization. This implies a high level of technology diffusion throughout the organization. As the IS function was growing in maturity (the entire IS function is now considered to be at a mature level) and some ISSP methodologies were adopted by the IS planners, the ISSP process was also achieving maturity. It was starting to play an important role in the whole planning process of the organization.

Even though an ISSP process should take a long-range view, it is a dynamic process that will be affected by external and internal factors. IS strategic planners need to be aware of the factors affecting the process, and to monitor the effects on the ISSP process constantly. This will permit quick identification of problems and appropriate solutions.
