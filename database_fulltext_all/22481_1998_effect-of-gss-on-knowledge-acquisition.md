# Effect of GSS on knowledge acquisition

Ron C.W. Kwok $^{1}$ , Mohamed Khalifa $^{*}$

Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, Tat Chee Avenue, Hong Kong

Received 1 September 1997; accepted 27 June 1998

## Abstract

The study discussed here investigated the contribution of group support system(s) (GSS) to a particular aspect of organizational learning: knowledge acquisition at the group level. We present a model explaining the enabling effects of GSS on important attributes of effective collaborative learning that lead to higher levels of knowledge acquisition and discuss the results of an empirical study designed to test the predictions of the model. A GSS-mediated environment is compared to a non-GSS collaborative environment in terms of the participants' understanding of a problem-solving task. The results indicate that the GSS-mediated environment leads to a significantly higher level of understanding than the non-GSS environment. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Collaborative learning; Organizational learning; Group support systems; Technology-based learning; Knowledge acquisition

## 1. Introduction

There has been much interest, recently, in organizational learning and its effects on organizational performance (e.g. [18, 27, 30]). The creation of organizational knowledge can be viewed as an upward process, starting at the individual level, moving to the collective (group) level, and then to the organizational level [24]. Sometimes it may also reach the inter-organizational level. Group support system(s) (GSS) can play an important role in the first transition. They can enable a collaborative learning environment that facilitates such a transition. Here, we discuss our investigation of the contribution of GSS to knowledge acquisition in a collaborative environment.

During the last decade, numerous researchers have studied the potential of GSS in supporting group meetings. The results of these studies are summarized in a number of articles (e.g. [7, 11, 23]). Key variables, such as characteristics of the group, types of task, features of the GSS and context of environment and organization, have been shown to influence the process and outcome of group meetings. Studies that focused on the group process examined how GSS can alleviate certain process losses and enhance certain process gains. GSSs are expected to reduce group process losses associated with information overload [17], social pressure [28], attention blocking [9] and other difficulties, commonly encountered in group meetings. They are also said to increase process gains by enhancing participation [6] and generating more information. Very few studies, however, investigated an important process gain: knowledge acquisition. Among the few empirical studies that have investigated it are those of Alavi's [1] and Tyran's [33]. Alavi found that GSS-supported collaborative learning led to higher levels of perceived skill development, self-reported learning, learning interest, student performance and evaluation of classroom experience in comparison with non-GSS-supported collaborative learning. GSS increased student participation and active involvement in knowledge construction, supported cooperation and group work among the students and lecturers, and enhanced the quality of acquired knowledge. Similarly, Tyran [33] observed that the use of GSS improved participation and had favorable impacts on perceptions of a classroom climate as well as the discussion experience and structure. These findings indicate that GSS improve collaborative learning environments and enhance self-perceived learning. But, are these perceptions justified? Do GSS really help to achieve higher levels of knowledge acquisition and if so, why? These are the major research questions addressed here.

In this paper, we propose a model explaining the effects of GSS on the acquisition of problem-solving knowledge. We proposed that GSS improve group processes and, in turn, enable important attributes of effective collaborative learning, leading in this way to higher levels of understanding.

## 2. Research model and hypothesis

In the constructivist approach to learning, understanding is the product of knowledge acquisition and is an active process in which meaning is constructed $[3]$ . Although teaching for understanding is one of the ultimate goals of educators $[14]$ , there is no standard way of measuring understanding: it is still open to a variety of interpretations $[5]$ . Summarizing several studies on understanding in the cognitive and educational psychology literature, Entwistle and Entwistle $[10]$ identify two components of understanding: personal and social. Understanding is personal in that it depends on the previous knowledge used by the learner to interpret new information $[20]$ . Previous knowledge is thus necessary and important to the achievement of understanding, but it is the way in which it is organized rather than merely the amount that is important. Understanding has also a social component. Pask [26], in his conversation theory of learning, suggests that understanding can be built-up through conversation with others about the subject. In social conversations, meaning is negotiated and shared. Tan [31] suggests that effective communication in conversation is critical to the development of individual understanding and that effective communication also enhances shared understanding.

Effective learning environments enhance both, the personal and social components of understanding. After reviewing the cognitive learning theory, Alavi identified three important attributes of an effective learning environment:

1. Active engagement in knowledge construction.

2. Cooperation.

3. Problem-based learning (PBL).

Active engagement in knowledge construction contributes to the personal component of knowledge. It is based on the constructivist theory of learning, which views knowledge not as information transmitted from the teacher to the learner but rather as the result of the active engagement of the learner in the comprehension and conception of the information. Understanding is essentially an active process that involves relating new ideas to what is already known, examining the evidence and the logic of the material, and creating an integrated whole. The development of understanding requires an active engagement with the material being learned, involving internal debates and working out a structure to see the logical connections of the new material and their meaning.

Cooperation contributes mainly to the social component of learning. The development of understanding requires discussion with others. The negotiation of a shared meaning comes out as one of the main strategies used to ensure that understanding is thorough. This social component in developing understanding reflects Pask's ideas about the important function of conversations in cognitive activities. Bruner [4] sees this as an essential facet of knowledge to which psychology has given too little emphasis. Cooperation can develop understanding by providing social support and reinforcement.

Problem-based learning (PBL) contributes to both, the personal and social components of understanding. Using a problem as the basis of learning illustrates the usefulness of the learned concepts through a concrete application and motivates the learner. Such motivation can lead to a more active engagement of the learner and a more focused cooperation of the participants in a collaborative learning environment. Also, embedding the knowledge, old and new, in the context of the problem leads to a better integration of the knowledge [36].

In our research model, GSS-supported meetings have the attributes of an effective collaborative learning environment and, hence, facilitate knowledge acquisition in the task domain (see Fig. 1). Gaining a better understanding of the task should, in turn, enable the participant to make a better contribution to the group process and, thus, enhance knowledge acquisition.

The main prediction of our model is that GSS facilitates knowledge acquisition in a group meeting by enabling the main attributes of an effective collaborative learning environment. Since we are interested in higher levels of knowledge, we investigated this in the context of a problem-solving task. Therefore, our empirical study attempted to verify the hypothesis:

Subjects in a GSS supported meeting will acquire a higher level of understanding of a problem-solving task than subjects in a meeting that is not supported by GSS.

Collaborative decision making involves group processes, where people work together to accomplish a decision-making task. The process of decision making can be considered to be a collaborative learning process, where participants exchange knowledge about the task domain. GSS are expected to make collaborative learning environments more effective $[29]$ . The literature $[8, 25]$ suggest that GSS enhance group processes by increasing group-process gains and/or reducing group-process losses. As illustrated in Table 1, their effects on group-process gains/losses contribute to the three attributes of effective learning (i.e. active engagement, cooperation, and problem-based learning) and facilitate knowledge acquisition in the task domain.

More specifically, with GSS support, individuals are encouraged to become more active, autonomous and confident in idea construction. Within an anonymous GSS environment, the participants are not intimidated and are less inhibited $[12, 13]$ . Furthermore, as it is difficult to preclude others from contributing ideas in this environment: domination of the meeting can be reduced. Also, free riding can be diminished, as participants can simultaneously contribute and no longer need to compete for air time. All of these effects contribute positively to the ‘active engagement’ attribute of effective learning.

Furthermore, GSS encourage the participants to provide feedback that is useful for generating and reinforcing understanding of the task through information sharing $[21]$ , individuals' participation and objective evaluation for catching errors, and by reducing individual domination, meeting time fragmentation and fear of reprisal. Finally, a GSS-supported meeting, where the participants discuss problems and develop solutions, qualifies as a PBL environment. GSS help the participants to better understand and analyze task-related information by structuring the task, using techniques, rules, and models. They enable decision makers to perform a deeper analysis of the problem, resulting in a better task understanding. GSSs also help reduce incomplete use of shared information by providing information and computation infrastructure to the group. Decision makers can benefit from electronic access to related information for successful problem solving. In addition, anonymous GSS environments encourage more objective evaluation and more error catching in problem analysis. Such effects contribute positively to the ‘problem-based learning’ attribute of effective learning.

## 3. Measuring understanding

Burns et al. [5] identify two orientations toward understanding: the recognition of order within the subject matter (coherence orientation) and the ability to recall relevant information (knowledge orientation). In this study, we followed the coherence orientation approach. We used the structure of the observed learning outcome (SOLO) taxonomy [2] to measure understanding. This taxonomy is a research-based measure that is extensively used in educational research. Imrie [19] showed that SOLO is a widely applicable framework for judging the structure of essays, answers to technical questions, medical diagnoses, and open-ended problems.

In the SOLO taxonomy, there are five structural levels of observed learning outcome:

1. Pre-structural: The task is started, but the learner is distracted or misled. The use of irrelevant information provides no correct knowledge about the question. There is no meaningful response.

2. Unistructural: The learner focuses on the relevant domain and concentrates on one aspect. The answer contains one correct feature or item of information.

3. Multistructural: The learner picks up more and more relevant or correct information, but does not integrate it. The answer is list-like, containing a number of unconnected items.

4. Relational: The learner integrates the parts with each other so that the whole has a coherent structure and meaning. The answer relates components to make a case or logical whole.

5. Extended Abstract: The learner generalizes the structure to take in new and more abstract features, representing a new and higher mode of operation. The answer not only relates components to make a logical whole, but it also contains a connection to a related area of knowledge beyond the demand of the question.

These categories have an intuitive relation to different levels of understanding. Higher levels in the SOLO taxonomy correspond to higher levels of understanding.

## 4. Empirical study

To test the hypothesis, a lab experiment was conducted. The experiment involved two groups of students engaged in collaborative problem solving. The experimental group was provided with GSS support while the control group did not.

## 4.1. The problem-solving task

The subjects were asked to perform a collaborative problem-solving task: it involved the development of an evaluation scheme for the term project of a client-server course (undergraduate). The students were assigned it as part of a collaborative assessment method $[16]$ applied in teaching the course. The task involved the following three stages:

1. The students were provided with a description of 14 criteria that may be used to evaluate their performance in an IS development project (a required assignment), the course objectives, the available learning resources and the assessment guidelines and policy of the university (the constraints). They were then asked to discuss these criteria with respect to the course objectives while taking into account the resources and constraints. This stage lasted one hour.

2. The students had to select seven criteria to be used in the formulation of the assessment scheme through an iterative process of voting, discussion of the outcome, and re-voting until they reached a consensus, using the Delphi method [32]. The students were also asked to classify the selected criteria according to two project dimensions: outcome and process. This stage was 30 min long.

3. The students had to assign weights reflecting the relative importance of the selected criteria based on an iterative process similar to that used in the second stage. This stage was completed in 30 min or less.

## 4.2. Experimental design and procedure

Eighty undergraduate IS students, enrolled in a client–server course, participated in this study. Although participation was voluntary, all students opted to participate; they were motivated because the outcome of the task was used to evaluate their performance in the term project. The participants were about 50% males, with an average age of 21.5 years. They were randomly assigned to two groups: an experimental one (with GSS support) and a control one (without it). Each group was divided into two sections of 20 students. Each section participated in a separate session, which lasted 2 h and involved the performance of the collaborative problem-solving task.

An Intranet-based GSS developed using HyperText Markup Language (HTML) was provided to the experimental group. To support the three stages of the collaborative learning task, the system provided: (1) brainstorming; (2) voting; and (3) weighting. The system also supported anonymity in order to allow the students to voice their opinion freely without fear of direct confrontation with other students or lecturers.

After completing the problem-solving task, each student was interviewed separately for 20 min and his/her answers were taped. During the interview, each was asked to explain the selected project evaluation scheme. The question was sufficiently open-ended so that students could demonstrate their level of understanding of the problem-solving task relative to the SOLO taxonomy.

The audio-taped responses were analyzed by two different course instructors. The response of each student was assigned a score from 1 to 5, according to the SOLO taxonomy: (1) Prestructural; (2) Unistructural; (3) Multistructural; (4) Relational; and (5) Extended abstract. In order to ensure their familiarity with the scoring scheme, the two instructors performed a pilot test together with 10 sets of audio-data. Afterwards, the two instructors analyzed the data and scored the students' responses separately. For cases where the scores did not match, the instructors were asked to check their scoring without being told of the other's score. If, after the second round, the two scores still did not match, the case was discarded. Only 2.56% of the cases needed to be discarded.

## 5. Results and discussion

As illustrated in Table 2, the results of the statistical analysis of the subjects' SOLO scores support the main hypothesis of this study. The average score of the GSS-supported group is significantly higher than the average score of the control group, as determined by a t-test at the 5% significance level. These results indicate that GSS enhance the individual's understanding of a problem-solving task, as measured by the SOLO taxonomy.

The SOLO score distribution (see Fig. 2) gives more detailed information on the level of understanding achieved by the subjects. Over 62% of the subjects in the non-GSS group received a score lower than or equal to two. They could not explain correctly more than one aspect of the problem-solving task. The number dropped to less than 22% for the GSS group. In fact, over 28% of the subjects in the GSS group could, in addition to explaining multiple aspects of the problem-solving task, relate these aspects in a meaningful way (SOLO score of 4 or 5). However, less than 9% in the non-GSS group were able to do this.

These results can be interpreted as evidence for the significant positive contribution of GSS to knowledge acquisition. They represent a confirmation of the perceived and self-reported positive effects of GSS on skill development presented in previous studies using more objective measures. Our research model explains/predicts these results based on the cognitive learning theory. A GSS-supported meeting qualifies as an effective collaborative learning environment that facilitates the acquisition of knowledge related to the task at hand. Knowing more about the task should, in turn, further enhance the group process.

## 6. Conclusion

Leidnere and Jarvenpaa [22] suggest that learning emerges through a shared understanding of individuals with the assumptions that active participation is critical to the learning process and that individuals have knowledge to share with one another. The active contribution of different understandings from individuals leads to a new and shared knowledge established in a group $[35]$ . Knowledge is acquired by individuals as it is shared. At the same time, the shared group knowledge grows as this loop of individual knowledge acquisition continues. This process of group knowledge acquisition is initiated by the creation of group (collective) memory which refers to the social process of articulating, exchanging and sharing knowledge $[15]$ . Here, we investigated the contribution of GSS to the initial stage of organizational learning: transition from individual knowledge to group knowledge. We proposed and tested the predictions of a model, explaining the potential effects of GSS on individual knowledge acquisition of group members.

Our research model stipulates that a GSS-supported meeting qualifies as an effective collaborative learning environment. The effects of GSS on group process gains/losses enhance knowledge acquisition by contributing to the three attributes of effective learning: active engagement, cooperation and problem-based learning. With GSS support, the participants are stimulated to become more active, autonomous, and confident in knowledge construction. In addition, GSS encourage the participants to share their own knowledge and provide each other with feedback that is useful for generating and reinforcing understanding. Finally, GSS can support the problem-solving approach to learning by enabling the learners to perform a deeper analysis of the problem and, thus, resulting in a better understanding. The results of our experiment confirm the expected positive effects of GSS on individual knowledge acquisition. They provide additional empirical evidence of the positive contribution of GSS to collaborative learning. While previous studies investigated the effects of GSS on perception, self-reported learning and performance, this research examined the GSS effects on understanding the task.

The results of this study have implications for both practice and research. They bring direct attention to variables and processes that bear on GSS supported collaboration applicable to both education and business contexts. In practice, schools can bring GSS supported learning into the curriculum to nurture understanding. Teachers and students can be trained to use GSS to create an effective collaborative learning environment. Likewise, in a business context GSS can be used to facilitate the construction of group knowledge and support the development of a knowledge-sharing culture, contributing in this way to organizational learning.

This study also contributes to the development of a framework explaining the potential effects of GSS on knowledge acquisition. By acquiring a better understanding of the task, GSS users can make a better contribution to the process and that will, in turn, affect the outcome.

This study, however, is not without limitations. Two factors limit its generalizability. The task chosen for the experiment was highly motivating for the subjects, as it affected the way their course performance would be evaluated. Such a highly motivating task promotes active participation, which is one of the main attributes of effective learning. Would the results of the experiment be similar had the task not been as motivating? Also, the subjects of the experiment consisted of students with similar academic background and equal status. How would differences in the participants' expertise and status affect the results? All we can conclude is that, for highly motivating problem-solving tasks and with homogeneous groups, a GSS-mediated environment leads to higher levels of understanding of the task than a non-GSS environment.
