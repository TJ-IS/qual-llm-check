# Impact of information technology on users' work environment: A case of computer aided design (CAD) system implementation

Dr. Kailash Joshi $^{a,*}$ , Dr. Thomas W. Lauer $^{1,b}$

$^{a}$ Associate Professor of MIS, School of Business Administration, University of Missouri, St. Louis, MO 63121, USA $^{b}$ Associate Professor of MIS, School of Business, Oakland University, Rochester Hills, MI 48309, USA

Accepted 24 August 1998

## Abstract

Study of the possible impacts of a new information system or technology on its users is an important area of MIS research. Understanding and identifying favorable and unfavorable impacts of implementation on users should be helpful in developing better design guidelines and choices to take full advantage of the potential of information technology, while minimizing the possible negative consequences for users. This paper presents the details of impacts created by the implementation of a computer aided design (CAD) system for part designers at an automotive parts manufacturer. The paper employs equity-implementation (E-I) model as a framework to help identify and analyze the impacts of CAD technology implementation on part designers' work environment. The paper identifies changes in the designers' inputs and outcomes as a result of CAD implementation. On the whole the impacts were found to be favorable for designers, the manufacturing organization, and its customers. The paper concludes that the E-I model provides a useful framework to help assess the nature of impacts created by the new system. The reactions of designers to the new system were also found to be consistent with the E-I model. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Implementation; Information systems; Organizational impacts; Technology impacts; Work environment; Computer aided design systems; User acceptance; Equity–implementation model; Equity theory

## 1. Introduction

Study of implementation of new information technologies and systems is an important area of MIS research. Increased competitive pressures due to the globalization of economies and rapid enhancements in enabling technologies demand that organizations adopt and implement new technologies and systems rapidly. Those who fail to meet this challenge may lose an opportunity to enhance productivity and their competitive position, which may even threaten their survival.

Although adopting new technologies and learning how to use them to build better systems is a challenge in itself for organizations, even more daunting is the task of introducing these new systems to users. The introduction of these systems can alter job design and restructure users' work environment in different ways [23]. Some impacts of the new technology may have dysfunctional consequences for users, which may subvert the original objective for implementing the new technology or system. Impacts of a new system or technology could include changes in job satisfaction, stress, quality of work life, and other work related outcomes with significant consequences for the productivity and efficiency of operations [14, 15, 20, 22, 26, 27]. These issues have led researchers to look for answers to questions about the effects of information and communication technologies on the cognitive and social experience of individuals who use them, particularly on the nature of jobs and quality of work life [1, 3, 5, 6–10, 12, 21, 28, 30–32]. Researchers have also urged additional research to better understand the complex issues surrounding the implementation of new technologies (e.g. [2, 4, 25]).

In this paper, we present a case study of information technology implementation involving a computer aided design (CAD) system at an automotive parts manufacturer. We use the equity–implementation (E–I) model as a guiding framework to help identify the areas of possible impacts of the new technology on users' work environment $[18]$ . The E–I model can help identify and assess users' concerns and possible reactions to a new implementation through its three level analysis involving changes in inputs and outcomes. Thus, the three level of analysis can be used as a framework to help identify and study the impacts of technology implementation. The three levels in the E–I model provide an opportunity to comprehensively consider issues at the individual level, the work group level, and the organizational level in assessing the impact of IT on users. Additionally, research on the implementation of new systems or technology can serve to refine the E–I model by identifying additional relevant inputs and outcomes not previously examined in the research literature. The E–I model is based upon the theoretical foundation provided by equity theory. Equity theory is a well established and widely researched theory in social sciences. Predictions based on equity theory have been found to be applicable in a wide variety of settings, including organizations $[11, 24]$ . Equity theory has also been employed in MIS research. An instrument has been developed to measure the equity perceptions of information systems users $[16]$ , equity perceptions have been found to be among the most important determinants of the overall user information satisfaction with the MIS function $[17, 19]$ . Procedural justice issues, a component of equity theory, have also been found to be important in user participation and its outcomes $[13]$ .

The case study, presented in this paper, identifies the nature of changes brought about by the implementation of a CAD system in the design department of a large automotive sheet metal and molded parts vendor to major original equipment manufacturers. The reactions of users of CAD system (part designers) to the new system are also documented in the case and analyzed through the E–I model. Section 2 presents a brief overview of the E–I model and discusses the framework employed to study the impacts of technology implementation. Section 3 presents the case study where the details of the changes brought about by the CAD system and their effects on part designers are described. Section 3.1 presents an analysis of the case details on the basis of the E–I model framework. Section 4 includes a summary of the findings and a discussion of their implications.

## 2. Overview of the E-I model

The E–I model provides an understanding of issues useful in determining users' acceptance of or resistance to a new system, technology, work practice, or other change in the work environment [18]. It is based on equity theory, which has a well established, sound theoretical base with support from hundreds of studies in laboratory and field settings (see e.g. [29, 11, 24]).

Walster et al. [29], presented equity theory with a view to integrate various ‘mini-theories’ of social behavior to provide a general theory of social behavior. The theory states that individuals attempt to maximize their net outcomes (rewards-efforts). In any social context, individuals are also very concerned about the fairness or equity of their exchange relationship. In assessing equity, individuals compare their outcomes with their inputs to assess whether they are getting a fair return for the efforts or resources that they bring to the exchange. In addition, individuals compare their relative gain [(outcomes–inputs)/inputs] with that of others in their reference group, and if they find disparity, they view the interaction as inequitable or unfair, and therefore distressing [29]. In an organizational context the employer and other employees or groups may provide a frame of reference for such comparisons. A comparison of the relative gains of two parties A and B can be expressed as follows:

$$
\frac {\text { Outcomes   of   A }}{\text { Inputs   of   A }} \quad \text { vs. }: \quad \frac {\text { Outcomes   of   B }}{\text { Inputs   of   B }}
$$

Researchers in the area of equity and social justice have also examined the response of individuals to perceptions of unfairness or inequity. These responses include altered expectations, comparison standards, beliefs or attitudes, and overt behavior.

The E–I model proposes that there is no fundamental or irrational resistance to a change. Each change is evaluated by an individual affected by the change. It is welcomed if considered favorable, or resisted, if considered unfavorable. On the basis of equity theory $[29]$ , the E–I model identifies a three level framework for evaluating the impacts of a change resulting from the implementation of a new technology or system. Table 1 summarizes the details of analysis for the three levels.

The first level of analysis deals with the changes in inputs and outcomes from the perspective of a user. Individuals are generally concerned about their inputs and outcomes and the resulting fairness of exchange in any social setting. When a new technology or system is introduced in the work environment, it is likely to change the inputs and outcomes for users. Users will assess the changes brought about by a new system in their inputs and outcomes to evaluate the change. If the net change (change in outcomes—change in inputs) is positive, the change will be welcomed. However, if the net change is negative the new system will be resisted. The important issue at this level is the nature of inputs and outcomes that are relevant and how these are affected by an implementation. The E–I model suggests possible inputs and outcomes that may be affected by the implementation of a change. Table 2 summarizes possible changes in users' inputs and outcomes identified in the previous literature [18]. In addition to considerations of changes in a user's own inputs and outcomes, the user may also be influenced by consideration of changes in his/her unit's or department's inputs and outcomes. Thus, work group level issues may also be relevant at the first level of analysis as well as the second level and third level of analysis discussed below.

At the second level, the E–I model examines fairness in relation to the employer in sharing the gains or losses brought about by a change. Therefore, changes in the relative outcomes of the user are compared with that of the employer. Relative outcome for a party is defined as the ratio of change in outcomes to the deservingness of the party based on inputs, merit, or other criteria. In the analysis at the second level, the E–I model permits evaluation of impacts for the employer and examines if the terms of exchange have become more pronounced in favor of the employer. Many technological innovations help improve productivity and profitability for the employer. Employees who participate in implementing such changes also expect the benefits to be shared with them. The employees would judge the change to be negative if the employer benefits come at their expense, for example in terms of increased effort or stress.

Finally, at the third level of analysis, the E–I model identifies the issue of comparison with other users or user groups in the organization. The user is viewed as comparing changes in his/her relative outcomes with that of other users in the organization. Departmental affiliation may also provide a frame of reference to compare the changes in the relative outcomes of one's department with that of other departments. At the third level of analysis, the E–I model provides an opportunity to examine the relative impact of the technology on different departments or users in the organization.

Although the E–I model was originally presented as a model to help understand and explain users' reactions (ranging from system acceptance to system resistance) to the changes brought about by a new technology or system in their work environment, it can also serve as a useful framework of analysis to assess and help identify the impacts of other changes in users' work environment such as changes in communication patterns, organizational structure, reward system, et cetera. In addition to impact on the individual and individual performance in a work setting, implementation of new technologies can also impact the performance of a group or a team of employees. Many new technologies such as group decision support systems (GDSS), computer supported cooperative work (CSCW), and other more general group support systems may significantly impact the performance and other job characteristics of various groups of employees. Membership or participation in a group may provide a frame or reference for individuals who may identify with the outcomes and inputs of the group. In such contexts, individuals may assess changes in outcomes and inputs of their group to assess their equity perceptions and use the group level issues as a reference for comparing relative gains with other groups (level 3) or the organization (level 2). Thus, the E–I model should also permit a consideration of the impact of a new technology that is structured at the level of a group or a team. After presenting the case study of a CAD system implementation in Section 3, we examine the impact of changes for users through the E–I model framework.

## 3. Case: Implementing CAD technology for auto part designers

Silver Industries supplies modular windows to major automotive manufacturers. In order to qualify as a first tier supplier, its customers require a well developed design capability. In the past, most sheet metal body prints, as well as window designs were manually generated by draftspersons. Since window modules are attached to vehicle sheet metal surfaces, accurate information is required in order to assure that they attach at the correct locations flush to the profile of the vehicle body. This assures that the design objectives, such as the prevention of leaks, are met. For the draftsperson using manual techniques, it is a daunting task to define and measure the contours of a body panel and then to match its design to a component such as a window module.

To improve performance for this and other related tasks, one of the major automotive manufacturers developed a software package called product design graphics system (PDGS). Computer mathematicians worked with engineers and designers in order to develop a system that fulfilled all the designer's technical requirements. The system was initially implemented at the auto company's plants. Suppliers were next in line to receive the system that included the software (PDGS), minicomputers, monitors, plotters, and tape and disk drives. To access the system, Silver's designers used a workstation with a 21" screen, a hand held light pen and a keyboard. Data is displayed three dimensionally using raster type graphics. Drawings are typically printed on vellum or mylar material ranging in sizes up to 52"×250', using a drum plotter, which achieves an accuracy rate of 0.008" over 20' of material.

PGDS software is designed for full three-dimensional design programming. Three-dimensional part geometry is created through constructed geometry entities by the operator. Three-dimensional surfaces can be either ruled, parabolic cubic, parabolic linear, chord height blend linear, or planer. Once the data is in this format, it can be used to generate tool cutter paths, and to calculate part weight and center of gravity.

Silver installed the new design system at its corporate headquarters in the Midwest United States as well as at its Engineering, Design, and Sales office in the Detroit area. The objective was to provide a more efficient service and a faster response time in servicing customer needs. In the Detroit area office, a CAD department was established. Several modem connections were also installed to enable Silver to exchange pertinent CAD data in a timely manner, thus improving coordination at the design and checking stage. Modem connections were also installed to connect the

Detroit facility with corporate headquarters in the Midwest as well as with other design and manufacturing facilities in several other states.

The new system enhanced Silver's capability for taking a project from a concept stage, through design, to the development of production drawings of parts and toolings. The basic steps that occur during the development of a new product (program) by an auto manufacturer start, when a product concept is completed at the Design Studio and is presented to the Body Engineering department, which begins to design the structure of the vehicle. Changes during the evolution of a vehicle (product) are negotiated between the designers and engineers who are charged with the detailed design of the various subsystems of the product. During this process, before the vehicle design is complete, component groups such as at Silver enter into the picture in order to assure that the design does not preclude them from being able to manufacture their components.

When the design of a vehicle is completed, Silver's prototype modular windows are manufactured and assembled onto the first prototype vehicles, which are tested and evaluated for future improvements. While Silver engineers' function is to interface with the vehicle manufacturer's engineers, Silver designers are responsible for generating the drawings under its engineers' direction. Silver designers communicate their information needs and provide feedback to Silver engineers, or even to the vehicle manufacturer's engineers and designers when needed. With successive prototype vehicle building cycles, other groups enter the picture and with their input the final production vehicle design develops through an iterative process of negotiated needs and functional requirements.

## 3.1. Changes for part designers

As noted above, the task of part designers is to generate part drawings based on requirements and guidance provided by engineers. Before the new system was implemented part designers used to make drawings on paper and mylar using tools such as drafting board, T square, french curves, pencils, and pens. With PGDS, designers worked at workstations using light pens and keyboards.

Although there may be differences between the nature of work for a person who worked on the drafting board and the one working with the CAD/CAM system today, their final output is similar: to generate printouts of drawings for manufacturing. Certain characteristics of the job remained the same, such as the requirement of a creative temperament, the sedentary nature of the job, and generous overtime pay, particularly during busy periods.

When CAD was first introduced there were some part designers who initially resisted it, but on the whole it was accepted by most designers. One determining factor was age. More older draftspersons resisted the change than younger draftspersons. Their reasons for resisting included: a fear of change, a fear of computers, a feeling that the system could not accommodate all the technical design requirements demanded by the draftspersons, and finally, an uneasiness about job security due to computerization. Even though some training was provided before the implementation, there was a general misconception among most part designers that CAD operation would require just entering the coordinates and the computer would do all the rest of the work. It took a while for them to realize that lines would have to be drawn, surfaces created and features detailed and defined – much like before. However, due to enhanced features such as the ability to instantly blow up a drawing by a factor of 10, and the ability to easily change existing drawings, expectations grew. The number of changes and enhancements requested increased significantly – what used to pass for good enough was not acceptable any more.

Implementation of CAD at Silver had an impact on the organizational structure, skills, and culture in the drafting department. Prior to the implementation, the job hierarchy progressed from the entry level position of detailer, followed by component, assembly and system designer, checker, and at the top, supervisor or manager. This was a rigid structure where the prime consideration for promotion was seniority rather than skill. The pay scale also increased based on time with the company.

With the implementation of CAD technology, a person trained on the PDGS software was able to do preliminary work within months and was able to reach the 90 percentile of the CAD designer pay scale within two years. The drafting skills required by the CAD system could be learned very quickly, especially in comparison to the manual system where the manual dexterity necessary for laying lead onto paper with different darkness and thickness took a long time to master. The skill of drawing a line or defining a feature, that was important in the old system, had been rendered irrelevant by the electronic wand used by the CAD system. The wand makes it easy to draw lines and figures. While drawing lines had become easier, use of the CAD system posed other problems.

The CAD system permitted even novices to create good looking drawings. However, in the view of experienced designers, many of novices had little knowledge of design conventions and standards. They also lacked the experience to judge whether the drawn part could actually be manufactured. Many older designers viewed the new CAD environment as demeaning the value of their skills. They also regarded the new CAD trained designers as lacking commitment to their profession, and changing jobs strictly on the basis of opportunities for higher pay and more overtime.

In contrast, new designers viewed the job as a dead end job with little opportunity for advancement. Thus, they were mainly motivated by their pay which they sought to maximize. Silver management also had concerns about the younger generation of CAD trained designer's lack of commitment and their propensity to move in search of higher pay. The increased transience of their workforce resulted in a loss of knowledge and continuity for Silver.

In the past, draftspersons took pride in their work. They were not only creators of the parts, but there was also pride and artistry in the preparation of the drawing itself. There was a personal attachment to each print and since changes were much more difficult to make, there was significantly more checking and analysis done during all stages of producing and revising a drawing.

However, as CAD systems became widely accepted, both customers and vendors began to ask for more information in the drawings. This was information they had previously generated themselves or had done without. As one manager states, “what used to be a snowball has become an avalanche”. This higher work demand increased the stress level for designers. Because changes were easy to make, the frequency and urgency of requests escalated. The new environment had a negative effect on the morale and disposition of the designer.

As a group, the designers were initially apprehensive about accepting the new system. Some older designers actually resisted when implementation began. However, after the system was installed, the initial resistance was overcome, and designers learned and adopted the new system. The CAD system became a regular part of a designers' job. There was no loss of jobs, which had been a major source of apprehension. In fact after the CAD system was installed, the workload increased and additional design staff were hired.

## 3.2. Changes for other users: Managers/engineers

The effects of the implementation of the CAD system were also felt by those interacting with the part designer. The design manager was required to pay attention to changing needs in the new work environment. The manager had to assess CAD skills when hiring new designers to ensure that they could fit in to the new environment and be productive. Besides their earlier responsibilities such as resource allocation, workload distribution, administration and training, managers now had to develop budgeting and negotiating skills. Since the new CAD systems consumed a higher portion of departmental budgets, managers had to justify those expenditures as well as convince upper management of the need to stay current with rapidly changing technology. Drafting tables, compasses, and sweeps were inexpensive and seldom needed replacing. In recommending the installation of the CAD system, the manager had to become the proponent of IT as a strategic resource to the top management. For the organization, significant initial investments were needed in changing from a well lit drafting room to a darkened, ion controlled, air conditioned CAD station area. Further costs were incurred in training design staff in the use of PDGS as well as in product design. Even the most experienced designers needed to be trained in using the new system. Therefore, there was a significant financial cost and technical change that needed to be managed.

Product engineers were also affected because the CAD system allowed a closer information relationship with the customer while removing them from drawing and design activities. This occurred because the details of preparing a drawing were generally delegated to the designer with the engineer becoming involved only if there was a difficulty or decision to be made. In the past, engineers could easily review drawings at their desk and work directly on a drafting board to make suggestions or changes to a drawing. With the current system, an engineer's review of the design requires much more discipline to ensure regular reviews in view of a lack of easy assess to the drawings and their occupation with other day-to-day problems. Even when the review does take place, a lack of background in CAD systems makes it difficult for them to be as thorough as before. Most often, the review was done on a 27" monitor, which did not portray the full drawing on the screen at one time because most of Silver's drawings are larger than that. The lack of PGDS training and the lack of a dedicated personal CAD station removed the engineer even further from directly manipulating and improving the design.

## 4. Analysis of user impacts on the basis of the E-I model

E–I model identifies a three level framework for examining a users' assessment of the impacts of a change. In this case, we will examine the impact of PDGS CAD system installation on the inputs and outcomes of part designers at Silver who were the main users of the new system.

4.1. Level 1: Changes in outcomes and inputs for designers

## 4.1.1. Increase in outcomes for designers

1. More pleasant work environment: Each designer was provided a private cubicle with a CRT, an improvement over the wide open room with large tables that was shared by all designers in the past. Designers sit rather than stand while working. The CAD area is illuminated at a lower level to avoid eye stress due to glare and reflection as compared to a highly illuminated common area in the past. Each designer is permitted to keep a radio or personal tape player.

2. More opportunities for advancement: As more output demands were put on designers because of the technical capability of CAD, more designers were needed, creating a larger hierarchy with additional positions. Further, some of the design work which engineers previously performed was assigned to designers. This was necessitated by the shortage of engineers and made possible by the new capabilities of the CAD system.

3. Better customer service: Faster response to customer needs for drawings and proposals and changes to them due to CAD capabilities. Faster transmission of changed drawings.

4. Salary increase and higher-level title: Employees who learned to operate the CAD system were given a pay raise and the new title, CAD Designer, instead of the earlier title, Draftsperson. Designers liked the new title better than the previous title.

5. Learning a marketable skill: Learning to use the software and the hardware was viewed by designers as an important step in increased job mobility.

6. Reduced dependence on others: The ability to plot by machine removed the dependence on personal training in the technique of drawing geometric shapes and lines.

7. Usefulness of the system: The system was useful in drawing complex patterns faster, without tedious efforts to maintain accuracy and cleanliness. The drawings can be used for demonstration purposes and transmitted electronically to the customer without using paper.

## 4.1.2. Declines in outcomes of designers

1. Reduced job satisfaction: Less identification with the final product, a higher volume of work, and less understanding of what is being designed lead to increased alienation and therefore less job fulfillment.

2. Reduced bargaining power/Loss of value of marketable skills: Knowing a particular CAD package, such as PDGS loses its advantage if the company where you are applying for a job uses another package (e.g. CATIA). It limits the list of potential employers who view the candidate as an experienced CAD operator. The current employer knows this, which reduces designers' bargaining power. The new system created a level playing field for older and younger designers. This resulted in a loss of value of skills in drafting on paper for the older designers.

3. Reduced importance: A well established, highly experienced draftsman who wields a lot of informal power through personal networks stands to lose the most power. A new draftsperson “could end up with prime design jobs usually reserved under the old caste system for certain more influential, power brokers in the drafting room”.

4. Increased monitoring: Whether it is the physical location in the cubicle or the ability to electronically monitor work activity from a remote location, the supervisor has greater capability to monitor the workforce under the new system.

5. More role conflict: Aspirations of a better educated workforce (most CAD designers have at least two years of college) tend to be upwardly mobile whereas draftsmen had less opportunities to aspire for advancement. There is more role conflict for new, better educated designers, because the CAD work offers them little opportunity to demonstrate or explore other job skills.

6. Potential failure in adapting to the new system: All draftspersons who could not adapt to the CAD system were relegated to updating and maintaining pre-CAD prints or assigned to other less skilled jobs. This led many of them to early retirement.

## 4.1.3. Increase in inputs of designers

1. Additional demand for work: Increased capacity of the CAD system led to an increase in the amount of work, especially changes and enhancements to drawings.

2. Higher level skills and more tension: Increase in the number of skills required (i.e. knowing CAD as well as drafting practices and product design requirements) coupled with less background in substantive matters such as product function generates higher frustration and tension. There is also a need for higher initial skill levels in the new environment.

3. Increased learning effort: Not only do designers have to learn the PGDS software, but to be assigned more interesting work, they need a more in-depth understanding of product design principles. Designers regard the demand for increased learning as negative.

4. More tasks assigned: Previously discussed.

5. Fear of the unknown: The uncertainty accompanying the introduction of PDGS made the draftspersons apprehensive.

## 4.1.4. Decline in inputs of designers

1. Ease of usage: Once the designer learned the software, it was easier to produce a drawing by pointing and clicking than it had been using manual methods.

2. Reduced search for information: The help function that was part of PGDS was easily accessible in contrast to the previous situation where the novice draftsperson was dependent upon experienced fellow workers.

3. Reduced manual effort: Described before.

4. Easier rework of errors: Erasing and making changes are much easier on the screen as compared to erasing and changing a drawing on the paper. This helped reduce inputs of designers.

The net change in the equity status was considered positive by designers after the system was installed. While the items listed above indicate changes in both directions in inputs and outcomes, an overall weighted evaluation would show a much more pronounced advantage of moving to the CAD environment. For example, having one's own work space was valued much more by designers than the negative effect of the potential for increased monitoring.

## 4.2. Level 2: Changes in relative outcomes for the employer

The CAD system was favorable to users, however, there were also some intangible advantages to the employer. This system was installed at the insistence of customers at a very high capital cost, essentially a large increase in employer's inputs. It replaced what had been considered a satisfactory method of generating drawings. The primary benefits to the employer were maintenance of their business relationship and enhanced customer service through the electronic transfer of data. Some other indirect advantages included standardization of prints, increased reliability of print storage, and ease of maintenance and enhancements to drawings. However, these advantages should be weighed against the high cost of the equipment. Thus, system improvements did not result in an immediate tangible advantage to the employer. Thus, the designers regarded this as an improvement in the system rather than an employer gain.

The key advantages to the user are the personal workstation, the ability to be assigned more challenging work soon after learning the system, the elimination of the 'caste system' and better job mobility with wage scale implications. The factors contributing to an initial resistance were: the fear of the unknown; the uneasiness that the system can do everything the customer wanted (which may reduce the value of designers' skills); and the fear of reduced employment due to increased productivity. All of these fears were dispelled during and after the implementation and subsequently there was no resistance to the change after the initial break-in period.

## 4.3. Level 3: Changes in relative outcomes for other users

To assess relative outcomes, the designers compared themselves to the engineers. Engineers still used the drafting table for performing design analyzes, for quickly sketching a concept, for feasibility studies, or for providing direction to designers. One disadvantage of instituting the CAD system was that the engineer did not have the innate ability to go in and work on the design without taking CAD training. Normally, it was not cost effective to give the engineers CAD training or regular system access because of their low level of usage. A pointed out earlier, the new CAD system introduced some complicating factors to the engineers' job. From the designer's viewpoint, the equity comparison with the engineers was favorable.

## 5. Implementation strategy to improve equity impacts

The E–I model predicts that if the assessment of the equity impacts of implementing a new system is unfavorable, the user will attempt to restore equity, usually through resisting the implementation. Another alternative is that when the implementer recognizes the potential for inequity resulting from a new system, steps can be taken to reduce user inputs or to increase user outcomes. Some of the actions taken at Silver and some of the design features of the PGDS can be interpreted in this way. Steps taken to reduce users' inputs included: (a) training programs; (b) the PGDS help function; and (c) the addition of extra staff to assist with regular production demand during the implementation period. Increases in users' outcomes included: (a) regularly scheduled briefing sessions to quell rumors; (b) the introduction of the new title 'CAD designer'; (c) increases in pay; and (d) hiring more designers which allayed the fear of layoffs and reduced prospects for employment. There was also an emphasis on the desirability of their new high tech environment. For obvious reasons, Silver did not emphasize their designers' acquisition of new marketable skills. The users were made aware of the capital outlays required for each workstation which demonstrated the importance Silver placed on the CAD system and the costs that were incurred by Silver. The net effect of these efforts was a high level of acceptance of the PGDS after the initial introductory period.

## 6. Summary and conclusions

Information technology or systems (IT/IS) implementation may impact users' work environment in many different ways. Some of the impacts may be unfavorable for users, leading to negative consequences such as reduced job satisfaction, increased stress, and reduced commitment. Such dysfunctional consequences can limit or jeopardize the expected gains of a new system. Therefore it is important to understand the nature of impacts of a new information system or technology on users' work environment. Such understanding can permit consideration of these issues at the analysis and design stage to develop better design solutions and implementation strategies. Some implementation strategies can adjust user inputs and outcomes, thus increasing the likelihood of a favorable user evaluation and consequently better acceptance of the new technology or system.

The case study presented, in this paper, provides an opportunity to examine the applicability and usefulness of the E–I model in studying the impacts of a computer aided design (CAD) system implementation. The E–I model, through its three level framework of analysis, helps identify possible impacts of the implementation of a CAD system on its users. The E–I model identifies the nature of changes in inputs and outcomes for part designers who are the primary users of the CAD system. The E–I model also provides a basis for analyzing possible changes in the inputs and outcomes of the employer and other users in the organization. Thus, the model makes progress towards meeting the recommendations of researchers to adopt a more comprehensive approach in studying the impact of information technologies on individuals by considering issues at an individual level, a work group level, and an organizational level. The details of the case suggest that the E–I model was useful in identifying many of the impacts of the CAD system implementation.

Several of the impacts considered likely in the E–I model were found to be relevant in this case. The work environment and the task environment were redefined due to the new technology. The value of the skills of experienced designers was undermined due to computerization. Traditional power and seniority of older employees were also challenged. Expectations from the job also changed in terms of faster turnaround time and very frequent changes to achieve perfection. While the technology permitted greater monitoring, it also provided personal work space and freedom to use radio and stereo equipment. The jobs of the Manager and product engineers were also affected by the change in technology. The organization also benefitted significantly from the CAD implementation through improved customer relations, better service, standardization of drawings, easy of storage and access, and ease of maintenance.

The case study also points out the special concerns of older employees in implementing a new technology. Attention should be paid to alleviate older users' concerns in planning the implementation strategy. They may also need separate training programs where additional time may be spent on their learning needs. The case also demonstrates changing user perceptions of the impact of an implementation over its life span. As the changes occur and their impacts emerge users change their initial assessments. Thus, the role of appropriate mechanisms for communication and feedback is emphasized to diagnose and manage user concerns and perceptions.

In this paper, we have employed the E–I model in an ex post manner to explain the acceptance of a system after it had been implemented. The model relied on subjective perceptions of users. These are difficult to predict ex ante or before the system is implemented. Further, when the outcome of an implementation is known to be favorable, it can bias evaluations of observers in favor of the conclusion that the balance of equity should have been positive. Thus, difficulties in capturing ‘real’ perceptions of users should be acknowledged. Though, it may be argued that perceptions may be more important than the reality in the context of users. For ex ante analysis, the E–I model provides a useful list of factors and issues that should be considered by a change manager in planning an implementation strategy. Early consideration of these issues can lead to additional efforts directed at assessing users’ concerns along the three levels of the model. Then a strategy can be formulated to address the issues of concern through appropriate design choices both in the technical subsystem as well as the social subsystem. User participation in this process can be crucial in helping make accurate assessments and appropriate design choices. An ex ante analysis can also help define areas of focus for the training and communication programs to address the identified issues.

There is a need for developing better models to help identify and study the impacts of an IT/IS implementation on users, which has been an important area of focus for prior MIS research. The E–I model provides a useful framework of analysis for this purpose as demonstrated through the case study presented in this paper. Future research should examine the E–I model along with other models that focus on the impact of IT/IS implementation on users. Research efforts should be directed toward identifying strengths, limitations, and possible refinements in these models to help better understand their applicability and relative merit. The study of IT/IS impacts on users' work environment remains important in helping facilitate IT/IS implementation, hence it deserves continued attention from the MIS research community.
