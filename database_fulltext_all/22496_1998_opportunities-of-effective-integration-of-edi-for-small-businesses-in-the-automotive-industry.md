# Opportunities of effective integration of EDI for small businesses in the automotive industry

Virpi Kristiina Tuunainen\*

Helsinki School of Economics, Electronic Commerce Institute, P.O. Box 1210 FIN-00101, Helsinki, Finland

Accepted 24 August 1998

## Abstract

The efficiency gains from electronic data interchange (EDI) for the auto-manufacturers are well documented in current literature, but the subcontractors often being coerced into using EDI have received less attention. Thus, this study tries to better understand the EDI use and its evolution by small partners in a network structure of dyadic relationships where large partners may dominate. The model developed in this study shows that small businesses, often operating as suppliers for larger companies, could benefit in many ways from EDI through more extensive business integration with their own and their partners' value chains or an expanded customer base. Our empirical findings indicate that usually small businesses have used EDI in a way that was originally established by their principal, but they have not used the additional opportunities. We analyzed the reasons for this and developed both, organizational and technological means for small businesses to solve the problems and more fully utilize the possibilities offered by these information technologies. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Electronic data interchange; Small and medium-sized enterprises; Automotive industry; Use; Integration

## 1. Introduction

Modern information technology (IT) offers possibilities for effective coordination of business processes and it is not surprising that organizations are rapidly implementing new links for relating to each other $[7]$ . These links take many forms, such as just-in-time manufacturing, strategic alliances or networked organizations. Technical structures utilize electronic hierarchies and markets, through specific solutions like electronic data interchange (EDI) or the Internet.

EDI-systems are used for standards-based computer-to-computer exchange of inter-company business documents and information $[32]$ . The adoption rate of this technology has not been as high as was predicted a few years ago $[9]$ . Traditionally, EDI has required prior arrangements and dedicated lines or value-added networks (VANs). This is often found costly and complex, especially by small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) (e.g. $[9]$ ), who thus have not been able to realize the benefits of EDI – eliminating paperwork, speeding up communications, and reducing costs while increasing productivity. Nevertheless, EDI can only be of full benefit through its widespread adoption, meaning a substantial EDI network of business partners and elimination of the costs for maintaining parallel, non-EDI systems that are used in transacting with non-EDI-capable partners [15].

EDI presents technical means for efficient transactions processing, inventory movements, process linkage and knowledge leverage $[35]$ . The long-term strategic importance of EDI is believed to lie in the potential comparative advantage that could be gained from its use by learning how to integrate the technology effectively into organizations. In this way, valuable new capabilities can continually be added to the system, while deriving cost savings from increased productivity and decreased overhead made possible by EDI $[32]$ . These kinds of arrangements of low-cost and efficient inter-organizational information sharing relations between firms adjacent on the supply chain, have been prevalent in the automotive industry for many years $[29]$ . The benefits the car manufacturers have gained in efficiency by sophisticated EDI-systems highly integrated with their production systems are well documented (see e.g. $[8]$ ). However, the usage of EDI by the subcontractors, many of them small and medium-sized enterprises without the same kind of resources as their larger counterparts, has received less attention.

Prior empirical investigations (see, e.g. [15]) suggest that a major reason that small companies may become EDI-capable lies in external pressure, especially from trading partners. Mackay [19] conducted a survey on the component sector of the Australian automotive industry which supply parts primarily to the locally based multinational car manufacturers. Two-thirds of respondent suppliers indicated they adopted EDI only because they were told to by their much larger customers (i.e. the car factories). Despite the pessimism on account of involuntary adoption, the component sector received benefits from EDI adoption: improved relations with trading partners, enhanced customer service, improved data accuracy and increased productivity. The greatest benefits were realized by the companies that not only used EDI, but had fully integrated EDI into their internal application systems (sales/order entry, finance or distribution). The research also demonstrated the importance of industry collaboration in achieving individual benefits (direct and indirect) from EDI adoption. Despite being coerced into adopting EDI, even the smaller trading partners can also indirectly achieve some benefits from electronic trading, e.g. larger quantities purchased from them [36]. Nonetheless, although the supplier creates more value, the commonly observed buyer-takes-all outcome often emerges [29].

This study concentrates on IOS (inter-organizational systems) usage, EDI especially, by small partners in network structures where large partners may dominate. The fragmented empirical results presented in earlier studies (e.g. Refs. [19, 32, 33, 36]) are combined into a model that classifies the basic alternatives of IOS use and the potential directions for development in it. The classification is based on two dimensions, the breadth of partner base and depth of business integration. The value of the model lies in its usefulness for the evaluation of the options as well as the potential benefits to be gained by the selected direction. The analysis of possible solutions to the problems of advancing IOS, and EDI in particular, are also analyzed. They include both, organizational alternatives, such as user groups, and technological solutions, such as the Internet.

In the empirical research setting of six selected subcontractor companies and the car manufacturer in the Finnish automotive industry, we explore how the subcontractors in this industry utilize EDI, and how have they developed in the use. Rather than theory testing, the purpose of the empirical investigation is to illustrate the usefulness of the model by showing how it can be applied to generate new and useful insight to the studied problem (for more details, see Ref. [34]). Furthermore, we will demonstrate that, although it is the dominating large partner in the network that gains the most benefits from this electronic hierarchy [20], the subcontractors can, or could, also benefit from EDI-use, potentially also as a basis for wider use of electronic commerce. With the help of our conceptual model, we will describe the realized and potential benefits, analyze the discrepancies and discuss the means and prerequisites needed in order to achieve them. Special attention is paid to the emerging role of the Internet.

## 2. Characteristics of inter-organizational networks in automotive industry

Networks in the automotive industry are typically upstream stable networks linking together independently owned specialized assets, i.e. the suppliers (limited number of carefully selected partners) to a core firm (the manufacturer) [25]. These networks are distinct from pure market or hierarchical arrangements [38] in their heavy reliance on reciprocity, collaboration, complementary interdependence, a reputation and relationships basis for communication, and an informal climate oriented toward mutual gain [18].

Automotive industry uses qualified suppliers or subcontractors to provide key components to assembly lines to guarantee quality, reduce inventory and continually focus supplier research and development on specific components $[17]$ . Since there are considerable transaction costs associated with controlling quality from product design, through process design, to initial and full production the concern for quality requires the car factory to work closely with its suppliers $[27]$ . Nevertheless, seldom are these subcontractors quite as irreplaceable for the factory as it itself might be in many cases for them.

The evident fact in the automotive industries all over the world is that the car manufacturers are decreasing the number of their direct suppliers (see, e.g. Refs. [4, 26, 31]). The total number of participants on the network does not, however, necessarily decrease, because of sub-subcontracting of the system suppliers, and possible strategic alliances and mergers and acquisitions. In other words, seen through the lenses of the car manufacturer, the structure of the network is getting simpler, but when looking at the whole industry, the network is evolving into more complex and ramified structures.

The networks in the automotive industry are electronically coordinated via EDI, typically using industry specific standard, such as ODETTE in Europe. However, the explosive growth and commercialization of the Internet has also opened up new possibilities for small businesses. The Internet can provide inexpensive access to established value-added networks or it can be used to bypass the VANs completely $[30]$ . While most EDI transactions are still sent over VANs, there is a growing number of EDI-based transactions being sent over the Internet $[2]$ . There are numerous Internet–EDI programs being developed or already running, including World Wide Web (WWW)-based forms, suitable EDI translation software and Internet-to-VAN gateways (see, e.g. Ref. $[24]$ ).

The use of EDI is believed to strengthen the existing buyer–seller relationships $[21, 37]$ . The sharing of information can also affect the relative bargaining power of the two parties, and it may also affect the competitive position of the buyer or supplier with respect to their own industry rivals $[5, 29]$ . When firms use coercive power to force trading partners to adopt EDI, less powerful partners may be left more vulnerable. On the other hand, when the event of EDI adoption is viewed as an opportunity to build and reinforce trust between firms, the relationship is able to support organizational changes, e.g. restructuring operational processes or new modes of distribution $[14]$ .

It is possible that the efficiencies generated by an information link, which allows a downstream firm to reduce its inventories (i.e. externalizing certain transactions), are partially offset by higher costs up the value chain. For instance, the cushioning effect of the extra inventory might be lost, so the upstream firms must respond by increasing their inventories $[3]$ . As the car manufacturers have reduced the number of their suppliers by favoring system suppliers, these preferred suppliers might be transferring the extra costs of inventory further upstream to the less powerful players in the network. Looking from the point of view of the whole network, though, the effectiveness of each participant adds directly or indirectly to the effectiveness of the whole industry and total industry welfare and, hence, to performance of each player.

## 3. The use, and evolution in the use of EDI

In a network of business partners, the organizations have various alternatives as to the use of IOSs, as well as the directions into which to develop the use in order to gain more benefits from it. This development is enabled by two dominant properties: improved interconnection; and improved, shared data access $[6]$ . In EDI context, Bergeron and Raymond $[9]$ refer to these dimensions as external integration, denoting the variety of trading partners with which the organization interacts through EDI, and internal integration, meaning the variety of the value chain functions interconnected through EDI within the organization.

## 3.1. EDI use by the subcontractors in automotive industry

In an electronic hierarchy of large principals with smaller partners in automotive industry, we can view the breadth or the complexity of the partner base in three levels: the number of industries operated in; the number of customers; and the number of suppliers. This dimension indicates both, how successful a firm has been and how willing it appears to be in establishing electronic linkages to interact with customers, suppliers, creditors, and other stakeholders in an expedient fashion [22]. Here (see Fig. 2), we make a basic assumption, that the order in which an SME enlarges the EDI partner base is such that it first establishes a link with one customer in a particular industry, usually on request from this customer, then with other possible customers in the same industry (i.e. downstream). Next, the partner base grows in a vertical direction upstream to include the suppliers of this SME. Finally, links will be established with customers in other industries (vertical and horizontal) as well as other business partners.

Fig. 2. IOS use and the evolution paths: EDI use by SMEs automotive industry.

The other dimension, namely depth of business integration, has three levels: we define the EDI use in the first category to mean usage without integration to even the internal databases and applications. In the second category, EDI is integrated to internal processes, applications and databases. In the third category, EDI-partners share processes and databases of the other partners as well, thus extending the integration to include external resources. Massetti and Zmud [22] call this category a coupled work environment, where computer-based applications of one, or both trading partners can directly access data maintained within the computer-based systems of the other trading partner.

The SMEs have three basic directions in developing the use of EDI further. We believe that, of these options, path I (deepening the system integration) is more suitable to an SME with a product of high specificity (e.g. air channels or gearboxes), as manufacturing of a product with, for instance multiple variations can be made more efficient with a closer cooperation (in the form of, e.g. joint R&D or shared CAD) with the principal. Path II (enlarging the partner base) better suits an SME manufacturing a more standardized bulk product (e.g. tires or rubber carpets), since significant economies of scale can be gained even by using the same type of basic messaging with multiple partners. Path III (full integration), being ‘in-between’ paths I and II, would be optimal for a producer of both, specific and standardized products.

Path III leads directly toward complete integration, by which we mean a network of independent organizations sharing databases and processes in an electronic manner. It is not necessarily feasible in practice, or always even a desired solution: while greater permeability does increase an organization's flexibility and adaptability, it can also result in the organization becoming more vulnerable to external intrusions and/or influences [22], as well as collision of interests. However, if not interpreted as meaning “sharing of all databases and processes by all participants,” this kind of virtual network would be the optimal solution for efficient industry network. Paths I and II might be the practical solutions for companies with smaller volumes, that is, usually small and medium-sized enterprises. But, as volume of EDI-traffic increases a company on either of these paths has to make a shift closer to path III, in order to be able to utilize EDI more efficiently. Developing the EDI use only by deepening the relationship with the principal without extending the usage to other partners would lead to under-utilization of existing resources. On the other hand, increasing the number of EDI partners – without any kind of integration of EDI to even internal systems – would lead to ever increasing amounts of inefficient routine work that could be automated by integrating the EDI system with other processes and databases.

As the extent of links with partners increases, the possibilities offered by the Internet also become more important. While operating in one particular industry a proprietary network can be sufficient, but extending from one to multiple industries is significantly smoother in the Internet with common network standards, worldwide connectivity and Internet-to-VAN gateways. Furthermore, to be efficient the virtual network would mean expanding the IOS arrangement beyond EDI, which is merely exchanging of order information, to include sharing operations information, strategic marketing information or even strategic and competitive marketing and sales information $[29]$ .

It is worth mentioning that development can also happen within one category, either when the number of customers or suppliers increases – such as when the volume of business is increased with the addition of new products to the contract along with existing customers, or when the number of distinct document types an organization handles via EDI connections with its trading partners is increased $[22]$ .

## 4. Empirical investigation

## 4.1. Research methodology

For the purpose of illustrating the model, we studied Valmet Automotive (the only Finnish automotive factory) and six of its first-tier subcontractors. These six companies were selected among the Finnish subcontractors, based on their size and different types of products (both, bulk and more specific). Company A supplies the car factories with safety belts, Company B harnesses, Company C tires, Company D windshields, Company E air channels, and Company F thin plates (for more details, see Appendix A and Appendix B).

Valmet Automotive, formerly Saab–Valmet (established in 1968) in Uusikaupunki, Finland, has over 600 subcontractors, of which slightly less than 100 are Finnish. Many of the Finnish subcontractors are small and medium-sized enterprises. This, by international standards, very small factory is specialized into high-class luxury cars, as opposed to mass production practised by the US and German factories. This factory is, nevertheless, significant to the Finnish economy as a lot of especially small and medium-sized subcontractors are dependent on the business conducted with the factory or other parties related to the industry. Only a few of them have Valmet Automotive as their sole customer, while most of them are delivering to other auto-manufacturers or, even more so, other customers in other industries as well.

We conducted semi-structured theme interviews in the six companies, and acquired written documents and other information from them $^{1}$ . The themes covered during the interviews included the implementation, use, evolution and impacts of the EDI application. The interviews were tape-recorded and transcribed, adding up to over 120 pages of interview reports. The answers were validated by the interviewees, who were the persons named to be responsible for the EDI-system in each of the companies. Information on the car manufacturer is based on multiple interviews and discussions around the same themes as with the subcontractors. The main informer has been the IS-manager of the factory. Also, other material, such as annual reports, internal documents and trade journal articles have been collected and used for the analyses.

## 4.2. The use of EDI in the selected companies

Auto-manufacturers have been rather aggressive initiators, having made EDI compliance a requirement for all production suppliers. For instance, Chrysler has stopped doing business with some suppliers because of their inability or unwillingness to comply with the EDI mandate $[28]$ . Today, the use of EDI is not required by Valmet Automotive from the smallest subcontractors, but as the number of their deliveries increases, the requirements change. Of all the subcontractors of Valmet Automotive, almost 40% are using EDI with ODETTE-standard, a common European EDI standard for the automotive industry.

In all six studied subcontractors, the initiative to start EDI-communication had come from a car factory, either the Finnish one (in five companies) or the Swedish factory of the same brand name. All companies have started transmitting EDI-messages between years 1989 and 1992 with a PC-based EDI-solution, but four (companies B, C, D and E) have moved on to mini- or mainframe platforms. All of them are using the X.25 package network to transmit the messages.

## 5. Findings

To evaluate the evolution of the selected companies in their EDI-use, we used the model introduced in Fig. 2. This model proved to be a tool that communicates to practitioners as well as academics. All interviewees were able to place their position on the grid as well as pronounce their future plans concerning EDI use with the terms of the model. The evolution – or the lack of it – by our case companies is illustrated in Fig. 3. The solid lines denote the actual development, while the dotted lines illustrate the plans of the companies. Of these plans only the ones of companies B and C are actual, written-down plans; all the others being plans to be possibly and hopefully fulfilled sometime in the future.

EDI-links are established almost solely on the customer side with car manufacturers. Companies B and C also conduct EDI-mediated trade with one tractor factory, which can be considered to be in the same vehicle industry. Company D is an exception as it is the only one that has also established EDI-links with others besides the car factories, namely with the group's own wholesalers supplying the car parts dealers. Furthermore, Company D is the only company having links on the supplier side, but that is again inside the group, with its own subsidiary (supplier of raw material).

Aside from Company D, none of the case companies had yet established any links with their own suppliers or subcontractors nor to customers in industries other than automotive. In all cases, however, either some of the customers or the suppliers had announced their possible interest in doing so, or were known to be capable of conducting EDI-communication.

When the number of EDI-partners or volume of EDI-traffic increases, staying in the first column (along the lines of the path II, enlarging the partner base) is obviously not efficient: as there is no integration of EDI to even internal applications, the amount of manual work due to transferring or re-entering the data will increase accordingly. This explains why all of our case companies have already moved to the second column or, in two cases, are planning to do so. Furthermore, in the first column, the second class, EDI-link with own supplier(s), has, at least so far, been rarely feasible, as too often these second-tier suppliers tend to be too small to have financial resources or sufficient technical abilities to establish an EDI-link. As the first-tier suppliers seldom are very big themselves, the possibilities to demand or pressure are not very credible.

Moving horizontally, the first row following path I, deepening the system integration, ending in the third column standing for external integration (shared databases and processes) was seen in all cases as posing immense data security hazards. Exceptions here make the links and integration between very closely tied partners, like a company and its subsidiary. However, these data security concerns will have to be addressed in the near future, especially before Internet-based solutions can become more common.

The middle column seems to be perceived as the most efficient and feasible, at least at the moment. Although only company E has developed its use to a state that has reached path III, complete integration, leading to the lower right-hand corner in our model, the virtual network, all of the companies are, nevertheless, striving for this kind of more efficient use. The key phrase used by all the interviewees was, clearly, a need for more partners: they all expressed their distinct disappointment at the surprisingly low number of EDI-capable or EDI-willing partners. At the same time, however, they all announced their assured belief in the future of EDI, be it in the form of Edifact-standard EDI or more likely something resembling, for example the WWW-interfaces on the Internet.

Comparing the actual paths of our case companies with our suggestions for suitable paths for manufacturers of bulk or more specific products, we notice both, similarities as well as some discrepancies. From the bulk product manufacturers, take for instance Company C that is a producer of vehicle tires. Although it has currently established EDI-links only with the car manufacturers, it has clear plans to enlarge the EDI partner base both, to vertical and horizontal directions. Then again, Company A manufactures more specific products, namely safety belts. According to our proposition, Company A could benefit from closer co-operation with its principals, for example in form of shared CAD facilities made possible by the telecommunication link. This would also benefit the car manufacturer, as the subcontractor would then be even more committed to the development of the final products, the cars. However, at the moment Company A is only planning to integrate EDI internally, having no plans to work more closely with its principals.

## 6. Discussion

EDI is clearly not a new innovation any more, but rather a well-established communication technology. In the literature, EDI's diffusion into various industries has been widely reported, including numerous success stories (see, e.g. Refs. [11, 16]). As we started out with our empirical research, we also had firm expectations about the use of EDI among the subcontractors of the automotive industry: we did not have too high hopes concerning the usage by the very smallest subcontractors, but the medium-sized ones were expected to have gained substantial experience and developed their use of EDI further. Our expectations were right about the very smallest companies with low EDI volumes, who most often do not have sufficient IT-knowledge, let alone the financial resources to implement sophisticated systems involving complex telecommunications. However, the state of EDI-use in the medium-sized subcontractors came as a surprise to us. It seems that no matter whether these subcontractors are small or medium-sized, whatever the type of manufactured products or how long the experience measured in years of the SME in the automotive industry, aside from the links with car factories, not much evolution has happened.

All of our case companies were fully aware of the unused potential and benefits that could be gained by both integration and enlarging the EDI-partner base. The central notion by those interviewed was that only by increasing the number of EDI-partners (or, alternatively, the volume of EDI-traffic, meaning more business with current partners) it becomes practical for the subcontractors to integrate the EDI-system with internal applications and, thus, gain efficiency benefits from EDI-use. However, in most cases EDI was perceived mainly as a means of survival as its use is a prerequisite in the automotive industry, and the attitude was usually that of passively waiting for demands from other large partners to start EDI-communication. Even though most of our case companies have, sometimes perhaps only for image reasons, informed their partners about their capabilities, it does not seem to have attracted too much interest. Most puzzling is the case of the system suppliers (customers of Companies A – safety belt producer, and F – thin plate subcontractor) that are using EDI/ODETTE downstream with the car factories, but are not willing to do that upstream with Companies A and F. This cannot be explained by confounding standards which seems to be, at least to some extent, a hindering factor when it comes to conducting business with partners outside the automotive industry.

In addition to problems with different standards, other explanations offered by the interviewees for the slow or even stagnating evolution were the lack of openness in current EDI-practice, technical complexity, high costs of software, hardware and, especially, telecommunications. If there are not enough EDI-partners (i.e. enough business conducted via EDI), making the volume of EDI-traffic sufficient, the companies cannot justify the investment in developing the use, and will, consequently, lose the potential gains.

The evolution of standards is of critical importance, but that is normally beyond the scope of most individual organizations using EDI, SMEs even more so. As to increasing the number of EDI-partners, these first-tier suppliers seldom have but weak bargaining power and non-existing possibilities toward their other customers that are usually also much larger companies, to pressurize them into EDI-adoption. Furthermore, the smaller customers or suppliers of these firsttier suppliers rarely have even as much technical or financial means or expertise as the first-tier suppliers themselves. What, then, can be done about the stagnation in the development of EDI use in SMEs? Could the new opportunities created by the Internet be seen as a way to efficiently implement inter-organizational links, both in an unstructured and EDI-type format with predefined protocols?

## 6.1. Developing the breadth of partner base

By developing the breadth of partner base, that is, by increasing the number of EDI-partners, both inside the industry and to other industries as well, considerable economies of scale can be gained. Lack of EDI awareness and confounding standards seem to be the primary reasons for the slow diffusion of EDI, especially among SMEs, resulting in a small number of partners and low EDI-message volumes for the subcontractors.

As the car manufacturers would eventually benefit from a more efficient supplier and subcontractor network, it would be beneficial for them to promote the use of EDI throughout the industry, and to support their subcontractors in the development. However, this focuses on the automotive industry solely, and consequently, it does not by itself necessarily help the overall evolution of the subcontractors operating in other industries as well. We agree with Damsgaard and Lyytinen [12] that the actions governments can take to facilitate the diffusion of EDI are ever more important; the prominent and necessary government-intervention mechanisms seem to be mobilization of bias, use of varied policies that lead to higher knowledge deployment, improvement of the telecommunications infrastructure, standard setting, and subsidies to create critical mass [12]. We believe that the most efficient solution would be co-operation of the subcontractors, for instance, in the form of a user group, possibly coordinated through an industry association or something similar. This kind of user group could, by combining the EDI-message volumes of each participant, provide joint facilities for the EDI use at lower costs. Instead of dedicated communications links, yet another solution could be the use of services from VAN (value-added network)-providers, as that kind of use would not require substantial initial investment and technical expertise: this is possible even for the smallest participants in the industry. To the extent that small firms can leverage their limited resources with other small firms via cooperative network connections, they may achieve economies of scale and create new sales opportunities [17].

The growing potentials of the Internet should also be borne in mind when considering ways to find new partners, as well as establishing new inter-organizational links. Although adoption of post-sales electronic commerce (EC), typically in the form of EDI, is often a coercive response to large customers' demands, pre-sales EC technologies may be used as a practical means for a small firm to identify new customers and share information that may make them more competitive in an electronic marketplace $[17]$ .

The Internet is an attractive means of transportation for EDI because of cheaper, flat-rate connection prices of the Internet Service Providers (ISPs), instead of the traditional VAN services, which charge by the byte for each message $[13]$ . VANs will, however, continue to play a critical role because many larger EDI users want the closed network security and special processing functions that a VAN provides and the Internet, so far, does not. Recognizing the need for coexistence, VANs are setting up Internet-to-VAN gateways for tying the legacy EDI systems to the Internet [23]. However, as inter-operability of EDI- and Internet-based networks, user communities, and standards becomes more accepted, there will most likely be heterogeneous information technologies that provide complementary, rather than competing, applications [2].

## 6.2. Developing the depth of business integration

Developing the depth of business integration of EDI, first internally and then also externally with partners, could result in substantial efficiency gains as specific assets would be more fully utilized. The main hindering factor here is most often the overly moderate EDI-message volumes, that do not justify the investment in integration. In addition to the costs that are perceived to be too high, many times the technical complexity of, especially, telecommunications is also a reason why EDI remains non-integrated even internally. In addition to these reasons, data security concerns by all participants are also used as an explanation for resistance to external integration.

In addition to being a transport media for EDI, the Internet can be used to interconnect companies without any of the standard structure of EDI. For instance, on-line catalogs can be used to extend the Web-forms approach, giving the buyer more information about the product before ordering; major database software suppliers provide links between data stored in their relational databases and WWW servers $[30]$ . Although the Internet is currently much less secure than a VAN service, numerous major software suppliers have recently introduced security schemes for EDI transactions, including firewalls and encryption software $[13]$ .

The role of government and industry associations can also be important in advancing the level of business integration of EDI in the SMEs, as this kind of integration requires considerable amount of technical knowledge. However, here the car manufacturers have a major role: the consequences of non-integration are directly relevant to the car factories, as, for instance, lack of internal integration at the subcontractors end leads to a higher probability of errors, affecting the inter-organizational communication. Moreover, sharing processes and databases already in the design phase of a new component would greatly speed up the whole process, as well as make it more efficient, when overlapping work done at two ends is eliminated. In addition to being able to promote the integration of the value chain, formation of a user group could assist the subcontractors in functional integration. Joint facilities could be shared by numerous participants, again driving unit costs down. Bargaining power of a user group would be substantially stronger than that of a separate small or medium-sized subcontractor. This increased bargaining power could be exercised, for instance, toward the car manufacturers, for the support discussed earlier, and in developing the industry toward a virtual network. It would also be easier for the car manufacturer to negotiate and form agreements with an organized coalition. When planning the integration, whether internal or external, it should not be forgotten that, rather than just rationalizing the old processes with EDI, these processes should first be looked into critically and redesigned, if needed. Even the new forms of EDI are really nothing more than new technology; the hard part is still changing business processes to do more business electronically $[1]$ and gain efficiency benefits from it.

While most companies that use EDI have traditionally tended to be large, there is clearly a growing interest among small and medium-sized companies (see, e.g. Refs. [1, 10, 23, 30]). Use of the Internet for both, collaborative activities and EDI will reduce the expense of the technology and make it feasible for the SMEs. The Internet can act as a network that small companies can link into and large companies can interface with, helping the SMEs to enlarge their EDI-partner base beyond one particular industry.

## 7. Summary and conclusions

In this paper, we have presented a new model of IOS use, and its more specific version of EDI use by the subcontractors in automotive industry, with three alternative basic evolution paths. With the help of this analytic, as opposed to deterministic, model the subcontractors can evaluate their current situation, as well as view the different options for future evolution. It can help the SMEs in the automotive industry to consider the benefits from additional EDI-links with other partners rather than only with the car factories. We have also identified different types of possible ways to use EDI, including descriptions and recommendations for how to develop this use for different types of subcontractors: a subcontractor manufacturing a specific product is expected to benefit more from deepening the relationship with the principal, while a subcontractor producing bulk products, gains more from enlarging the customer base, especially with Internet–EDI, and with internal integration of the EDI system. In particular, the very smallest enterprises do not always have the sufficient technical or financial means or expertise, thus support from possibly their principals, industry associations and the government are needed in order to facilitate the diffusion of EDI among the SMEs in the first place, as well as helping them to develop the use further. Virtual network is not expected to be a feasible or necessarily a desirable goal, as complete electronic integration of all the organizations would inevitably lead to obvious data security problems. Nevertheless, for an efficiently run industry network, it would be the optimal solution. This classification also demonstrates how benefits can be gained from the utilization of EDI in sufficient volume of traffic.

We believe that the selection of very different types of subcontractors improved the illustrative power of our findings, which proved to be alike across all the cases. We believe that our model, that was also supported by our empirical findings, contributes to the current knowledge of successful usage of information technology by small and medium-sized enterprises. Furthermore, even though the results from the automotive industry cannot be universally applied to other industries, we believe that our findings give clear indications of the state of EDI-use by small and medium-sized enterprises in general. Moreover, the well-being, so to speak, of the subcontractors is also of interest to the car manufacturers, as the efficiency of a single organization adds to the efficiency of the dyad and the mutually beneficial exchange increases the welfare of the whole network.

As the subcontractors are increasingly dissatisfied with current confounding EDI-standards and the general bureaucratic nature of hierarchical EDI, new types of systems are bound to evolve. One possible way to move closer to more open electronic market is believed to be the Internet.

More research on the fast growing usage of inter-organizational information systems by SMEs is clearly needed. There are at least two interesting directions for future research. The first would be to explore the SME usage of EDI and Internet–EDI in more detail, as well as the use of the Internet in general. The second would be to apply our model presented in this paper to analyze the purchase and sales processes of the SMEs, for the purpose of studying the effect of information technology in increasing the efficiency and coordination of these processes.

## Background information on the six studied subcontractors

## A.1 Company A: Safety belt producer

Company A is a small (turnover of $\approx$ US \$3.3 million) producer of safety systems (vehicle safety systems, child restraints, home safety), supplying four car manufacturers with car safety belts. The primary market for the company's main products, the child restraints (about $70\%$ of total production), is Scandinavia, Finland in particular. Company A has no full-time IS-personnel, and although CAD-supported R&D was considered to be of critical importance for the company, PC/LAN-based IT in general was seen more as a useful and necessary set of office tools rather than a potential way to increase efficiency or effectiveness.

## A.2 Company B: Harnesses producer

Company B is also a small (turnover of ≈US \$5.5 million) subcontractor manufacturing harnesses for auto-manufacturers. In addition to the eight car factories, the customers include one tractor factory. As most of the car factories supplied are located in Sweden, that is the main market area of this company producing solely harnesses. The company has no IS-personnel of its own, but the IT support is received from the parent company, where all the central applications as well as the means for the EDI-links, are run on UNIX-based mini-computers. Company B uses these applications through a terminal connection with PCs. Although IT usage in Company B itself is PC-based, because of the support and facilities of the IS-department with three employees in the parent company, the level of IT infrastructure is quite sophisticated.

## A.3 Company C: Vehicle tires producer

Measured by turnover ( $\approx$ US 182 million), Company C is the largest of our case companies. It is a producer of vehicle (car and heavy) and bicycle tires, supplying nine car factories and one tractor factory. The proportion of production sold directly to the car factories is not significant, and the main market area is considered to be all of Europe. Company C has an IS-department of 14 persons, and highly sophisticated information systems from basic office applications to complex production planning and inventory. The IT-infrastructure consisting of mainframes, as well as client/server- and PC/LAN-solutions, is actively maintained, and the central systems are currently under redevelopment process.

## A.4 Company D: Windshield producer

The second biggest (turnover of $\approx$ US \$146 million) company is Company D. The production article of Company D is windshields, and the two car factories supplied directly account for about $45\%$ (including the official replacement parts) of the total sales. Rest of the production is distributed through company's own wholesalers, and the main market area is all of Europe. It resembles Company C in many respects: also this company has an IS-department of nine persons, who are also responsible for a large portion of IS-facilities of the group Company D is part of. IT, both mainframe and PC/LAN-based, is perceived as of very central importance in Company D, and also the group's internal telecommunication links are highly sophisticated.
