# Participation in videoconferenced meetings: user disposition and meeting context

John A. Campbell $^{*}$

School of Information Systems and Management Science, Faculty of Commerce and Management, Griffith University, Nathan 4111, Australia

Received 10 October 1997; accepted 4 August 1998

## Abstract

Although videoconferencing systems are increasingly becoming important in the support of geographically dispersed groups, there are few studies that identify the types of tasks that are most effectively performed within videoconferenced meetings. This study investigates the relationship between (a) technology quality and communication activity during videoconferenced meetings, and (b) the intention of participants to support subsequent meetings. The data consists of a sample $n=295$ of participants in a group videoconferencing system trial conducted by twenty government departments and incorporated bodies. Meeting sessions involved real work activities chosen by each of the participating groups. The results show that perceptions about the quality of technology and the communication activity conducted during the session are significantly related to the intentions of participants to support the use of the technology. Contrary to what might have been presumed, the findings also show that a significant majority of the participating groups chose communication activities least suited to the processing capabilities of the videoconferencing media. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. Keywords: Videoconferenced meetings; Telecommunications; Information richness; Media richness; Technology quality; Communication task; Task and media fit

## 1. Introduction

As markets and organisational activities become more global, group videoconferencing systems are increasingly being recognised as a viable means of enhancing communication flows to any number of geographically dispersed business units and customers. Many organisations are recognising that videoconferencing not only reduces travel costs, but also provides a means to coordinate activities and create a common sense of purpose among groups of people many kilometres apart. Despite the continuing growth in group videoconferencing system installations, very little is known about the factors which influence the disposition of users towards such systems and, in particular, the impact of early experiences with the technology on rates of adoption and usage in subsequent periods.

Campbell [5] assessed how videoconferencing technology influenced the effectiveness and efficiency of meetings. Different contextual factors were found to significantly affect the processes and outcomes of meetings. The functioning of groups using videoconferencing technology was found to be contingent upon the features that are specific to the particular system being used, the task being performed, situational factors, and the attributes of the group and its members (see also $[4, 6]$ ). Despite these findings there is little understanding of the influence contextual factors might have on user proclivity to support the use of videoconferencing as a viable and legitimate organisational communication option. It is important to understand how users form opinions about the utility of videoconference systems, because actual system usage is highly discretionary.

The focus of the study reported here is to determine how trial conditions shape the disposition of potential users towards videoconferencing technology. More specifically, the research objective is to assess the impact that the contextual variables – equipment quality and communication activity – have on the intentions of knowledge workers to participate in videoconferenced meetings. At the centre of the study is the issue of whether the match between task and communication media provides a useful framework for introducing potential users to videoconferencing technology innovations. The research situation examined here involves a trial of group videoconferencing technology designed without reference to task and media fit.

## 2. Theory

Human communication is a communal process of information sharing that takes place in specific social and cultural settings. Communicative interaction enables groups to create meaning and a shared understanding which can lead to acceptance and action. Rogers [28] emphasises that, while mutual understanding is the main aim of communication, it can never be obtained in an absolute sense. Several exchanges of information over time may increase the level of mutual understanding about a topic, but complete understanding is unachievable. Communication is usually adequate when the required amount of mutual understanding is achieved to perform the task at hand. This model of communication views the creation and sharing of information as convergence towards (or divergence from) a common point of mutual understanding. In this way, the model accounts for some of the inadequacies of the linear model of sender–channel–receiver described by Shannon and Weaver [30] and its epistemological assumptions about the nature of information and how it is transmitted and processed by humans. In this sense we wish to understand not only the simple communication act, but also the process of communication.

Communication is a fundamental determinant of organisational effectiveness as it is the means by which organisations allocate, control and coordinate resources internally and respond to the dynamism of the external environment within which they operate $[21, 32]$ . The number of communication options available to organisations has increased rapidly because of the significant advancements in both information and telecommunication technologies. These new means of communication have a significant impact on and between organisations and people.

Videoconferencing systems use video images and sound to duplicate as closely as possible the experience of face-to-face meetings without imposing the burden of travel. Participants can show pictures, graphs and three-dimensional objects, much as they would in a traditional face-to-face meeting. Unlike other organisational communication options, participants can see the reactions of their colleagues in videoconferenced meetings – whether they are smiling or frowning, disinterested or enthusiastic. These nonverbal communication cues can be voluntary or involuntary and are an important aspect in group communication and decision making $[3, 25, 26]$ . The awareness of these signals is an important element in the effective working relationship of team members $[35]$ . Understanding these nonverbal signals enables the recipient to respond with greater sensitivity and comprehension. In addition to the unspoken cues and meanings associated with body language and the synchronisation of body movements during communication, there exists an interactional synchrony and postural congruence between participants that also contain nonverbal cues going far beyond the face value meanings attached to body language $[2]$ .

## 2.1. Technology factors

Videoconferencing and other group support system interfaces designed with a bias towards machine efficiency have been shown to constrain rather than facilitate information exchange and decision making [22, 23]. Ideally, the interface should provide users with sufficient feedback while remaining operationally transparent, to the extent that using the system does not adversely interfere with current or potential uses of existing organisational systems and processes [33].

The speed and appropriateness of verbal and non-verbal information cues are integral components for effective video-mediated meetings. The system interface for videoconferencing applications should not unnecessarily inhibit the free flow of information between participants, yet at the same time provide the ability to easily display a variety of images. This is particularly important as participants have consistently indicated that the cognitive workload in video-conferenced meetings is higher than that for face-to-face meetings $[24]$ . If video-mediated meetings are constrained by the technology, then users will indicate less commitment or willingness to participate in future videoconferenced meetings. Therefore, a priori the higher the perceived quality of the system interface, the more positive the user disposition towards using videoconferencing systems.

## 2.2. Videoconference communication activities

There is a strong relationship between the type of communication activity performed within a videoconferenced meeting and user perception about the quality and outcome of that meeting $[31, 34]$ . How the perceived attributes of alternative communication channels determine preferences for a particular channel over another has attracted much attention in the literature $[9, 29, 38]$ . Much of this research links managerial communication media choice with the information processing requirements of different communication tasks.

Daft and Lengel [8] proposed that equivocality resolution and uncertainty reduction are the two motivating factors influencing information processing within organisations. Equivocality means the existence of multiple and conflicting views of the same problem or issue. The resolution of equivocality requires the resolving of differences, the exchange of subjective interpretations, and the development of a shared understanding. Uncertainty is defined as the gap between the information required to perform some task and the information currently available. Uncertainty reduction involves the acquisition of information to fill this gap.

Different communication media provide varying capabilities for communicating data. Media that have a high capacity for multiple communication cues and rapid feedback are regarded as rich media; these are best suited for conveying information that resolves equivocality; lean media are best suited for conveying information that reduces uncertainty. Media richness should match the information processing requirements of the message for effective and efficient communication to occur.

Prior research has focused on the effectiveness of videoconferencing for different communication activities, comparisons between different communication media, the attitude of users and their acceptance of the technology, and the impact on meeting processes and outcomes (see, e.g. [16, 36]). The findings show that videoconferencing does not facilitate the desired level of interaction during meetings involving negotiation, conflict, or the generation of ideas. However, there is also evidence that the constraint on interaction tends to increase the task focus of the group which can lead to higher quality outcomes. For example, Campbell found that groups undertaking negotiation activities had positive feelings about the results, despite the diminished opportunity for interaction compared to that of face-to-face meetings.

Since the perceived effectiveness of different communication activities can be affected by the choice of media, it is expected that the ‘richness’ of videoconferencing communication media will be better suited to some specific communication tasks more than to others $[7, 20]$ . Therefore, it seems that user intentions towards using videoconferencing systems would be contingent on the demonstrated match between the medium and the information richness requirements of the communication task.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Research design

The adoption of group videoconferencing technology as a legitimate means of conducting organisational meetings requires that the technology provide advantages over existing alternatives. The aim of this work was to assess the impact of system quality and communication task on the intention of participants to support subsequent use of videoconferencing technology. A static-group design was employed to evaluate user intentions towards future system usage. Previous research concluded that positive attitudes towards videoconferencing systems are firmly linked to perceptions of usefulness. The emphasis on user perceptions in this study recognises the sovereignty of user attitudes in ultimately determining system usage and system success $[1, 10, 17]$ .

## 3.2. User intent to participate in future videoconferenced meetings

There is a general consensus within the literature that, as bandwidth narrows (e.g. from face-to-face communication to computer conferencing), the communications channel becomes less suitable for complex social interactions and interpersonal communication $[15, 27]$ . User perceptions of the suitability of videoconferencing technology for organisational meetings should, therefore, depend on the meeting context.

The decision to try videoconferencing is primarily explained by the individual's expectations of improved productivity. The subsequent adoption of videoconferencing technology depends largely on the communication and information requirements of the user group and the ability of the technology to satisfy those requirements. The decision process involved in the adoption of an innovation entails the formation and confirmation of attitudes. Fishbein and Ajzen [12] specified a reasoned action model by identifying relationships between beliefs, attitudes, and behaviours. They proposed that an individual's overt behaviour is determined by his or her intention to perform that behaviour. Therefore, the discriminating variable – user intent to participate in videoconferenced meetings – was operationalised by asking the trial participants whether they would use the system again if it were installed. The scale ranged as follows: (1) yes, (2) maybe, and (3) no.

## 3.3. The quality of videoconferencing technology

The quality of videoconferencing system technology, as perceived by system users, should be an important factor in influencing the adoption of video-conferencing as a valid organisational communication option. Inferior systems inhibit the free flow of conversation, reduce meeting effectiveness, and dissuade individuals from participating. Operational ease-of-use has been identified as a key determinant of individuals' perceptions of the quality and utility of communications media [14]. The major characteristics of videoconferencing system interfaces that reflect operational ease-of-use are the quality of the audio-visual equipment and the simplicity of operation. The quality of the technology was measured by factoring a series of five-point scales that rate the simplicity of equipment operation, quality of sound, and quality of picture.

## 3.4. Communication task

McGrath [19] identified four basic types of group tasks. These are: (1) generate ideas and plans; (2) choose correct or preferred answers; (3) execute performance tasks; and (4) negotiate conflicts of interest and viewpoints. Table 1 combines McGrath's task characteristics with Daft and Lengel's information-richness model to illustrate the proposed relationship between media richness and the information processing requirements necessary for each task type. For example, negotiation activities (high equivocality/low uncertainty) require greater information richness from the communication medium than the choose-and-execute-type activities (medium equivocality/medium uncertainty), while the latter activities require a more information-rich communication medium than for generate-type activities (low equivocality/high uncertainty).

Videoconferencing media provide more information richness than is required for generate-type tasks. Although this excess media richness should not directly impact on the ability to perform generate tasks, inefficiencies may arise because the richness of the medium allows participants to engage in the exchange of information surplus to task requirements. In contrast, the information-richness requirements for negotiate type tasks are higher than those provided by videoconferencing media. Therefore, negotiate tasks are vulnerable to problems of ineffectiveness, as exchanges of very rich information are restricted [20]. As the information richness of videoconferencing is best suited to choose and execute tasks, these two task types are treated as one in this study.

## 3.5. Adjustment for initial group differences due to concomitant variables

## 3.5.1. Group size

A study by Fulk and Dutton [13] found that the availability of videoconferencing facilities tended to facilitate larger group sizes than would normally be the case in face-to-face meetings. They also reported that many participants perceived that the potential for larger group sizes provide for greater access to appropriate information and more direct accountability. The perceived benefits associated with larger group sizes included: more, quicker, and less filtered information. As different communication tasks are likely to have different optimum participant numbers, the effect of group size was treated as a covariate and removed from the main analysis.

## 3.5.2. Location of facilities

The overall quality of different videoconferencing facilities can vary markedly with location and design. Applications employing comparable technology and bandwidth may vary greatly in layout, interface complexity, and ambience. The results of a study by Mantei [18] show that the behaviour of individuals and meeting dynamics are affected by equipment layout and room design. Campbell found significant differences between the perceptions of users at different videoconferencing sites. These findings reaffirm the importance of differences between the various participating locations in determining attitudes to videoconferenced meetings. Therefore, consideration will also be given to the influence of hardware configurations, equipment layout and other features that might differ between videoconferencing sites.

## 3.5.3. Level of participation

Individuals will perceive the same videoconferenced meeting differently from others, depending on their role in the communication transacted $[11]$ . Groups participating in videoconferencing system trials generally consist of two distinct types of individuals – those who actively participate in the meeting and those who are present to observe or provide support without adopting an active or direct role. Individuals in each trial group will be categorised as either direct or indirect participants based on their role within the meeting. The effect of any difference in perceptions between direct and indirect participants will be treated as a covariate and removed from the analysis of the main effect using a control variable.

## 3.6. Survey data

The survey was conducted during a five-day video-conferencing trial between various government personnel located in Brisbane and Townsville (cities more than 1000 km apart in Queensland, Australia). A total of 382 people from 20 government departments and incorporated bodies participated in 21 meetings. The range of activities undertaken across participating groups provided a diversity of meeting and communication tasks. Participants were provided with a 15 min hands-off introduction to the videoconferencing system followed by an additional 15 min hands-on demonstration by the trial organisers.

The operator at each site provided self-administered questionnaires to all participants. Each videoconferenced session involved two distinct groups of participants. The first consisted of subjects who played a direct role in the videoconferenced proceedings (direct participants). The second consisted of subjects who accompanied the direct participants but merely observed the videoconferenced proceedings (indirect participants). There were 186 direct and 196 indirect participants. A total of 295 survey responses were returned from 149 direct and 141 indirect participants, which yielded a combined response rate of just over 77 percent. The number of direct participants in each meeting averaged 8.5 (median=8) and ranged from four through 13.

A total of 200 respondents indicated they would use the systems again if installed; 88 said maybe; two indicated they would not; and five failed to respond. Because of the distribution of results, the discriminating variable was deemed to have only two levels – positive which included all ‘yes’ responses, and negative which included the ‘maybe’ and ‘no’ responses. The relative unwillingness of respondents to register negative responses most likely reflects a bias towards the technology that is generally characteristic of those who are prepared to participate in such trials. Also, it is far easier to declare a commitment than to carry it out.

The communication task variable was operationalised by requesting that the two facilitators each independently identify the single predominant communication activity undertaken during each session. The task variable categorisations made by the two facilitators were in complete agreement, and corroborated by the author viewing archive video tapes of each videoconference session. Both, the accuracy and consistency of each selection was found to be very high as each group activity consistently reflected one type of task only. The most popular communication category was generate (12 groups, 178 responses) followed by negotiate (five groups, 69 responses), and choose and execute (three groups, 45 responses). One group (three responses) was excluded from the analysis because of the absence of an agenda and the entertainment focused role-playing nature of their meeting.

## 3.7. Analysis of data

A one-way multivariate analysis of covariance was used to analyse the data. Prior to the analysis, all variables were assessed for violations of the assumptions of multivariate analysis $[37]$ . There were no multivariate or univariate outliers and the sample distribution measures of skewness and kurtosis were within acceptable limits in all cases. Therefore, no cases were excluded nor any data transformations performed. The total number of cases $(n=295)$ was reduced to 272, after cases with missing values were deleted.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

In today's competitive business environment, organisations face the difficult decision of whether to adopt new and innovative organisational communication systems. If current trends continue, videoconferencing technology will become increasingly important in the exchange of business information. Aversion to using this technology may significantly reduce individual and organisational efficiency and effectiveness because of the time and cost spent in unnecessary face-to-face meetings. The inability of managers to master videoconferencing and other telecommunication technologies may, therefore, entail significant costs for organisations and have detrimental career implications for individuals.

The results of the study provide some insight into the relationship between videoconferencing trial conditions and the intention of trial participants to adopt the technology. Although perceptions about the quality of technology were found to be important, the strongest effect related to the type of communication activity conducted during the trial. In particular, trial participants undertaking communication relating to choose-and-execute activities generally expressed a greater willingness to take up the technology.

It is interesting that, while choose-and-execute-type communication activities were hypothesised to provide tasks best suited to the information processing capabilities of videoconferencing technology, this was the least frequent type of meeting conducted. Despite this, the results clearly demonstrate that participants who undertook choose-and-execute activities viewed the technology more favourably. The groups participating in this study selected their own communication activities as it is believed that this would provide the most substantive and meaningful videoconferencing experience possible. However, in future trials, participants should be encouraged to conduct choose-and-execute tasks or, alternatively, be exposed to all three activity types.

Several effects were revealed by analysing the adjustment provided by the covariates – group size, facility location and level of participation – to the technology quality and communication activity variables. Not surprisingly, perceptions concerning the quality of the technology significantly diminished as the number of direct participants increased, while the level of participation and location had no impact. It is interesting to note that meetings involving negotiate tasks contained a higher proportion of direct participants. Possible reasons for this include a general concern for privacy by participants negotiating sensitive issues or simply a lesser need for support personnel.

While this study has substantiated the existence of a relationship between system trial conditions and user attitude towards videoconferenced meetings, there exist a number of limitations that reduce the external validity of the results. First, self-selection would have created a bias towards a large number of participants favouring videoconferencing in the sample. This is evidenced by the very small number of respondents indicating that they would not use the technology after the trial. A second concern is that the relationship between the attitude intent measure used in this study and actual future usage is unknown. A third limitation is that the trial videoconferencing system was external to the organisations from which the participating groups were derived. Trials using in-house facilities may well yield different responses. Finally, this study has not considered the composition and relationships within each of the participating groups. The functioning and performance of any group will ultimately depend on the structure, composition and interaction within that group.
