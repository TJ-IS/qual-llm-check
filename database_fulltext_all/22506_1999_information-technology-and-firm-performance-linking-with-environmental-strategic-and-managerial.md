# Information technology and firm performance: Linking with environmental, strategic and managerial contexts

Mingfang Li $^{*}$ , L. Richard Ye $^{1}$

College of Business Administration and Economics, California State University, Northridge, 18111 Nordhoff Street, Northridge, CA 91330-8245, USA

Received 10 October 1997; accepted 4 August 1998

## Abstract

The performance impact of information technology (IT) investment is an important research topic that needs to take into consideration the role of key contextual factors. This study discussed and empirically tested the moderating effects of environmental dynamism, firm strategy, and CEO/CIO arrangement on the impact of IT investment on firm performance. The study sample consisted of major US corporations. The empirical results, based on a moderated regression modeling approach, provided preliminary evidence supportive of the hypotheses advanced in this paper. Specifically, IT investment appears to have a stronger positive impact on financial performance when there are greater environmental changes, more proactive company strategy, and closer CEO/CIO ties. According to our findings, companies considering IT investment should assess their environmental contexts, strategic directions, and top management team arrangement to allow CIO's a more strategic role. © 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Information technology; Economic performance; Environmental dynamism; Company strategy; CEO/CIO relationship

## 1. Introduction

We are witnessing both the rapid evolution of information technology (IT), and accelerated investment in it by organizations concerned about their market competitiveness. Increasingly, companies demand and expect future growth and profitability to come from productivity gains achieved through continuous investment in IT and similar innovations (e.g. [9, 14, 57]). While business periodicals often report vivid stories of IT investments leading to either actual or perceived payoffs, rigorous and systematic assessments suggest that the picture is far from clear. Meanwhile, never has there been more pressure on management to make informed decisions so that any perceived positive impact of IT on firm performance will indeed be realized.

During the past decade a great deal of attention has focused on the impact of IT investment. However, studies have frequently generated controversial or inconsistent results. For example, after reviewing previous research, Loveman concludes that corporate

IT investment has had practically no impact on productivity [41]. Meanwhile, others have reported observing varying degrees of positive performance impact due to IT investment (e.g. [4, 7, 11, 13, 30, 43, 54, 58]).

Increasingly, researchers realize that the relationship between IT investment and firm performance is complex and multifaceted. Some contend that measurement problems, methodological deficiencies, and the poor quality of data sets may have contributed to the so-called productivity paradox of information technology $[7, 12]$ . Others suggest that since IT investment is inherently related to company strategy $[8, 36, 42, 49, 53]$ , the relationship between IT and firm performance should be studied within a strategic management framework. While both ideas are sound, the latter points at a very important and promising direction of IT research. It provides a more powerful theoretical framework within which our present investigation was conducted.

Although the extant literature has made significant strides towards explaining IT's performance implications, we believe a deeper understanding can be achieved by linking IT's business value to contextual aspects that are critical to strategic management: environmental; strategic; and managerial factors. Thus, our research question is: how do a firm's IT investment and environmental, strategic, and managerial factors together influence firm performance. Here, we report an empirical study that probed the IT and performance relationship within the context of a firm's external environment, its strategies, and its managerial arrangement for the IT function.

## 2. Theoretical bases

Many believe that the central purpose of investment in IT is to achieve competitive advantages and better firm performance $[26, 49, 52]$ . According to the resource-based view of firms $[5, 20, 59]$ , companies invest in areas that together form a resource configuration that provides competitive advantages. While the transaction cost theory suggests that a firm is the displacement of market mechanics $[18, 60]$ , strategy researchers point out that this, as an organizing format for economic activities, entails more than just displacement $[56]$ . Various activities take place in organizations over time. These help build competitive advantages that can not otherwise be obtained via the market. Application of IT in firms is part of these activities. As such, the investment is an integral part of a firm's attempt to gain competitive advantages and to deal with a firm's external and internal challenges.

Sethi and King [55] have summarized five widely recognized benefits of IT investment; they are operational efficiency, operational functionality, improved positions in competitive environments by fending-off-threats, preemptiveness, or being the first mover and thus having timing advantage, and synergy. Efficiency and functionality are related to the internal facets of an organization. Fending-off-threats and preemptiveness are related to external facets of the organization. Finally, synergy is related to overall integration, leveraging of resources, and competence building. Firms may utilize specific benefits of IT, as their situations dictate. The external environment will present differing challenges to firms. A dynamic environment, for example, may require firms to pursue preemptiveness and fending-off-threats as benefits of IT. The strategies firms pursue require different resource configurations. A firm with an external orientation, for example, may use preemptiveness and fending-off-threats, but a firm with an internal orientation may concentrate on efficiency and functionality. Finally, how IT is integrated into a firm's operation will have an impact on the synergy benefit that can be generated from the IT investment. A critical indication of IT's integration in an organization is the CEO/CIO relationship. Therefore, we expect that the performance impact of IT is likely to be moderated by these three contextual factors. Fig. 1 shows our model.

With multiple, and often conflicting demands from various stakeholders, a firm's performance objectives are multidimensional. There is no one single objective that can effectively capture overall performance. The studies of IT's performance implications have used measures such as profitability, growth, values to customers, and reactions of stock market. For business firms, two groups of measures may serve as a basis for performance assessment. They are growth measures such as sales growth, and profit measures such as return on assets (ROA) and return on sales (ROS). The former is indicative of how effectively a firm can open up new markets or expand in existing markets. The latter shows the efficiency of its operation. Firms may also emphasize one measure over another as their environments and strategies require.

The environment is the totality of outside factors considered by top managers in their decision-making. Two frames of reference are used to describe the environment. The first divides the environment into different segments, such as customers, competitors, and governmental agencies. This helps identify relevant factors in the environment. The second describes the environment along a variety of critical characteristics. Environmental dynamism appears to be a critical dimension of a firm's external environment [17, 22, 23, 38, 39]. It involves the degree and instability of change in the firm's environment. In an environment characterized by greater dynamism, top managers will experience much more uncertainty, or lack of information related to the current state of the environment, potential impact of those developments on their firms, and furthermore, strategic options available to them [47]. In addition, environmental munificence appears to be an important dimension that needs to be considered [23, 38]. It refers to the availability of resources and support in the environment. These two dimensions together present external challenges to the firm [38]. Investment in IT may be an effective way to provide timely and relevant information to top managers and thus to help reduce uncertainty (e.g. [1]). In addition, in a dynamic environment, trends and developments transpire rapidly, while opportunities come and go. In fact, the recent literature in strategic management has begun to focus on time-based competition. Investment in IT, therefore, will be effective in a dynamic environment due to its preemptiveness benefits. These considerations lead us to propose that:

## H1: The greater the degree of environmental dynamism, the stronger the impact of IT on performance.

Environmental munificence may have a direct impact on the firm's performance, hence it is important to incorporate it as a control variable. Environmental dynamism, we believe, moderates the IT and performance relationship. Therefore, we offer no hypothesis linking munificence with the IT and performance relationship.

In general, strategy attempts to achieve an alignment between a firm's external environment and its resource configuration [21, 46]. Two important aspects of a company's strategy are its product-market scope [48] and its competitive approach [50]. Firms adopting different strategies will tap into different benefits of IT investment. For example, a firm pursuing a differentiation strategy may explore the operational functionality benefit of IT, and a firm pursuing a cost leadership strategy may rely upon the operational efficiency benefit. Furthermore, a firm pursuing a product-market expansion strategy may tap into the preemptiveness benefit and realize its IT impact on growth. Capturing a central dimension of strategy presented a challenge for us, as strategy is multidimensional in nature. Recent developments in strategic management practice seems to suggest that companies are combining both cost leadership and differentiation advantages. In addition, the strategy typology proposed by Miles and Snow [46] seems to present three viable strategies, prospector, analyzer, and defender, along a key dimension of external orientation. This external orientation taps into both product-market scope expansion, and competitive approach. We consider this a reasonably parsimonious dimension of strategy. Firms with greater external orientation may need to depend on the preemptiveness, fending-off-threats, functionality, and synergy as benefits of IT investments, while firms with greater internal orientation may only need to depend on efficiency and fending-off-threats. It stands to reason that firms with greater external orientation may require more IT investments. Therefore, we propose that:

## H2: The greater the firm's external orientation (as reflected in a firm's market expansion and product development), the stronger the impact of IT on performance.

The extent to which a specific area is a strategic part of an organization is reflected in its top management team. It is a crucial group that is charged with navigating the future of a company. To fulfill its strategic task, top managers need to consider both external challenges and internal integration. Those areas that are critical to a firm will be represented by top managers in more powerful positions [27, 28, 29, 32]. A dimension of group structure important to this study is the distance between the CEO and the Chief Information Officer (CIO) [45]. To the extent IT is an integral and strategic part of an organization [34], the CIO would probably assume an important position in the organization [25], closer to the CEO. Indeed, there are significant relationships between the rank of a CIO and a firm's IT competitive orientation, and the importance given to IT planning [35, 51]. There is evidence that a firm's extent of IT deployment in business strategies and value-chain activities is often influenced by the CIO's participation in top management teams [3]. Moreover, a resource-based analysis seems to suggest that, compared to other IT resources, the relationship between IT management and the rest of the firm is the most important contributor to any sustainable competitive advantage [44]. We therefore expect that the strategy and managerial arrangements will affect the type and extent of IT impact on firm performance. This leads to the hypothesis:

H3: The better IT is integrated into a firm's strategic management, the stronger the impact of IT on performance.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. Study sample

Three sources were used to assemble the data set for this study. For firm strategy, performance, and industry classification, we used the COMPUSTAT database. For firm investment in information technology and CEO/CIO relationship we used the annual survey provided by InformationWeek. Finally, the assessment of environmental dynamism and munificence was made at the industry level, hence we used the industry level data made available in U.S. Industrial Outlook (1994) published by the US Department of Commerce.

Although the data for IT investment were primarily from US firms, we felt that, as a first step, we might benefit from concentrating on the experience of firms in a single country with reasonable data availability.

The InformationWeek magazine has been conducting a survey of major US corporations for its annual report on IT investment for several years. During its span of IT survey, there have been substantial changes in its survey methodologies. Our evaluation has shown that the basic survey methodology has been relatively consistent since 1992. Because our study also needed to obtain data from COMPUSTAT, and U.S. Industrial Outlook, we compiled a database of US firms that have IT data, firm strategy data, environmental data, and performance data available between 1992 and 1994.

The resultant study sample included 216 firms across the span of three years. This included firms in all major industries in the US economy. Unfortunately, the InformationWeek data set on IT-related matters provided information about only a portion of the 500 firms it features each year. However, our data analytical approach demanded a larger sample size [2]. Meanwhile, we needed to make sure that firm strategy and performance measures were available from the COMPUSTAT database. Given the pooled cross-sectional and time-series nature of the database for our study, we had 513 observations (Of the 216 firms in our database, some firms have only two years of information.).

## 3.2. Measures

## 3.2.1. IT investment

The data from the InformationWeek annual survey reveal the IT budget for each given year for the major firms in the US economy. For a measure of IT investment, we divided IT budget by total assets to provide an indicator of a firm's relative investment in the IT area.

## 3.2.2. Environmental dynamism

One of the three contextual variables, environmental dynamism, was operationalized as the standardized variation in industry-level sales revenue over the last five years. We regressed annual industry sales data over five years for each industry at a four-digit SIC level (a level management scholars believe to be appropriate) against time, and divided the standard error of the beta coefficient by the average annual sales revenue for each industry to obtain the index of environmental dynamism for each industry.

## 3.2.3. Strategy orientation

Often the realized strategic orientation of a firm has been measured by one of two forms of resource allocation. One is efficiency, while the other is market expansion $[24, 61]$ . In this study we used an index of market expansion vs. efficiency to assess a firm's strategic orientation. Specifically, we divided the sum of marketing and R&D expenses by capital expenditure. Marketing and R&D expenditures reflect an attempt to expand a firm's product-market. Capital expenditure reflects an emphasis on efficiency. The ratio between the two constitutes a parsimonious measure of a firm's strategic orientation, as reflected in the resource allocation already taken $[40]$ .

## 3.2.4. IT-strategy integration

We measured, in this study, the integration of IT with company strategy by the reported relationship between the CIO and CEO [45]. Specifically, if the CIO is also the CEO, the CEO/CIO distance is 1. If the CIO reports directly to the CEO, the distance is 2. If the CIO reports to someone who then reports to the CEO directly, the distance is 3, etc.

## 3.2.5. Firm performance

Measuring firm performance has been a major challenge. Being a multidimensional construct, performance may not be effectively assessed by using any single indicator [15]. We therefore used two variables, ROA and ROS, in our study. ROA focuses on the overall performance of the firm. It is measured as net income divided by total assets. ROS avoids the effects of differential asset valuation methods across firms, and the impact of new investment and depreciation. It is measured as net income divided by total sales revenue. As such, it represents a firm's ability to generate income from sales revenue.

## 3.2.6. Control measures

This study also included three control variables. The first is the debt-to-equity ratio. Because this ratio reflects a firm's risk-taking tendency and the relative control of financial community on the firm [6], we believe its potential impact on firm performance must be controlled. The second is environmental munificence. We regressed industry level revenue against time, and divided the beta coefficient by the average industry sales revenue over five years to obtain a standardized index of this variable [23]. The third is firm size. We used the reported number of employees as its proxy measure. Greater size may entail not only size and scope economies [16] but inflexibility and administrative costs as well [33]. Although it is not a central variable here, it is apparent that its role should be controlled for in studies related to firm performance.

## 3.3. Analysis

Various scholars propose interactive regression modeling as an effective approach for studying moderating phenomena $[2, 10, 19, 31]$ . We therefore selected this method to study the moderating impacts of three contextual variables on the relationship between IT investment and firm performance. This modeling approach essentially involves creating product terms between independent variables and moderating variables and entering both product terms and their respective main effects into the regression equations. Specifically, we used environmental dynamism, firm strategy, and the CEO/CIO arrangement as moderating variables, and IT investment as the key independent variable. We therefore created multiple product terms between moderating variables and IT investment. A statistically significant product term indicates a moderating impact. Specific directions of moderation will be determined by the signs of regression coefficients related to these variables.

Table 1 presents both the descriptive statistics and correlation matrix for the sample investigated in this study.

The empirical tests of the hypotheses were performed by means of the two interaction regression equations shown in Table 2. ROA and ROS were used as dependent variables. Two methodological considerations are critical when analyzing pooled cross-sectional and time-series data sets [37]: the data set may be autocorrelated over time (within the same unit of observation) and heteroskadastic across units of observation. However, our analyses using a variety of estimating methods led us to use the ordinary least square regression (OLS) procedure. First, as there is only a three-year time span, the threat of autocorrelation is minimal. We tested the models using the mean values for each unit of observation over time and derived the same conclusion as the OLS procedure.

## 4. Results and discussion

The most important findings of this study have been highlighted in bold font in Table 2. For the regression model with ROA as the dependent variable, the three-way interaction term among strategy, IT, and environmental dynamism is statistically significant, and exhibits the predicted direction. Specifically, we conclude that for firms operating in a more dynamic environment and with an externally oriented strategy, greater IT investment will lead to better profitability. For the regression model with ROS as the dependent variable, the two-way interaction term between IT and dynamism is statistically significant in the predicted direction. Specifically we conclude that for firms in a more dynamic environment, greater investment in IT will lead to better performance. In addition, the interaction term between strategy and CEO/CIO distance is statistically significant, and in the predicted direction. Specifically we conclude that with a greater external orientation, a greater distance between CEO and CIO will lead to poorer performance.

These findings provide some evidence that IT's performance impact is dependent upon several crucial contextual factors. The research results suggest that firms need to make greater investment in IT if they are in more dynamic environments and are also pursuing more externally oriented strategies. Furthermore, making investment in IT itself is not sufficient. Firms must integrate IT into their strategic management process.

Several observations can be made for the impact of control variables. For both dependent variables, the debt-to-equity ratio and environmental munificence seem to be positively related to performance, while firm size appears to be negatively related to performance.

We are mindful of potential delimitation of this study. First of all, constrained by the data availability and out of consideration for comparability with other studies of IT-performance relationship, we only applied our conceptual model to a small sample of firms in the US economy, and these firms are predominately large. While we were successful in providing evidence on IT's positive performance impact in relation to several key contexts, our findings might not be generalizable to medium- and small-size firms, or to firms with headquarters in other countries. Secondly, we only used two performance-related variables; growth-related variables might also be important for a better understanding of the relationship between IT and performance.

## 5. Conclusions

Our study attempted to determine the links between three key contextual factors and IT's performance impact. We proposed that a firm's external environment (depicted primarily by the degree of environmental change or dynamism), its strategic orientation, and its integration of IT into the overall strategic picture of the firm would influence the business value of IT investment. Our analyses provided evidence supportive of these hypotheses.

These findings have important theoretical and practical implications. Our understanding of the IT-performance relationship can be significantly expanded if we take into consideration the impact of key contextual factors. This may help remedy the problems that have resulted from the relatively simplistic conception of a direct relationship between IT and performance. Furthermore, the IT community, as well as company management teams, will benefit from this improved understanding.

For future research, several directions may be considered. Replications of this study with different settings should be useful. These may include firms with different sizes (i.e. small and medium), and firms in other countries. Additional dimensions might be explored. For example, cooperative strategies such as strategic alliances and joint ventures are becoming increasingly important. How firms involved in such strategies might utilize IT investment should be of great interests to the IT community. Finally, the performance impact of IT is likely to be more evident as companies gain experience through their continuous efforts over time $[12, 53]$ . Longitudinal studies will be necessary to provide any insight in such cumulative effects of IT investment.
