# Knowledge-based systems and knowledge management: Friends or foes?

Paul H.J. Hendriks $^{*}$ , Dirk J. Vriens

Nijmegen Business School, University of Nijmegen, P.O. Box 9108, NL-6500 HK Nijmegen, The Netherlands

Received 23 December 1997; accepted 5 September 1998

## Abstract

Knowledge-based systems (KBS) provide a way of formalizing and automating knowledge. Their worth for managing the knowledge assets has not gone unnoticed: they have been promoted as safeguards to retain expert knowledge, to avoid knowledge erosion, etc. KBS are the outcome of a knowledge engineering process that may be seen as providing some of the building blocks of knowledge management. Although ‘knowledge’ is the first word in knowledge-based systems, they are hardly ever considered from a knowledge perspective. As a result, a biased view of the organizational value of KBS exists in the literature, putting an undue emphasis on technology. The key issue addressed in this article is: how does knowledge engineering relate to a broader perspective of knowledge management? A way to identify the issues to be addressed when valuing KBS as potential measures for knowledge management is presented. To illustrate its value, the outcomes of a recent empirical investigation of how KBS function within organizations are presented. © 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Knowledge-based systems; Knowledge management; Knowledge processing strategy; Strategic application identification; Organizational impact of knowledge-based systems; Organizational conditions for knowledge-based systems

## 1. Introduction

Will knowledge-based systems (KBS) make an organization more knowledgeable? They are distinct from other information systems by their claim to store and handle knowledge rather than information. In the literature, these systems are usually valued according to their technical merits. What happens if, instead, they are judged from the perspective of how knowledge is used and produced within the organization?

Will this result in identifying KBS as friends or foes? Discussions of the organizational aspects of KBS usually stress the friendly character of these systems. They are associated with a variety of benefits: they may be used to retain knowledge even after an expert has left, they can be used for training-on-the-job programs, they are said to improve the consistency of decisions, they may increase the availability of expertise, they can provide the building blocks for what is called the ‘corporate memory’, they may promote ‘knowledge sharing’, etc. [8, 20, 21, 23, 24, 29, 43, 44, 55]. If the potential relationships between KBS and knowledge are well documented, the potential rivalry between the two generally escapes attention. Yet, the risks are not imaginary. They are not the effect of failure of KBS implementation projects $[18, 39, 60]$ , but the undesirable side effects of successful KBS implementations. These mainly derive from the fact that KBS depend on determining and storing knowledge at a given point in time. Critical empirical assessments of the harm that KBS may cause are, however, hard to find. KBS may make the organization more rigid and decrease the incentive for thought. KBS may, as a result, have a negative impact on job satisfaction and labour quality $[26, 33, 67, 79]$ . Establishing the value of knowledge-based systems for the organization will usually not result in a True/False answer: it will rather be placed on a continuum. The friendship and rivalry between KBS and the knowledge of organizations are the two poles of this continuum. The main objective of this paper is to discuss how the organizational value of KBS can be assessed and how the grey area between friendliness and rivalry can be explored. A systematic response to these questions will need to address three issues. Firstly, an assessment is needed of the role and value of knowledge in the organization and of the measures of their activation. Considering and implementing measures that deal with organizational knowledge is usually called ‘knowledge management’. A suitable definition of this concept is, therefore, needed. Secondly, the definition of KBS has to be appropriate in the context of investigating the organizational aspects of knowledge. Also, the relation between knowledge management and knowledge-based systems has to be established. An appropriate assessment of the value of KBS needs a careful description of how KBS relate to other ways that the organization can dispose of its knowledge. Thirdly, the main question is: what must be done so that KBS can earn their place as tools for knowledge management? To assess the role of KBS in managing knowledge in organizations, a framework is presented. This assists in defining relevant features of KBS that help in satisfying knowledge management goals.

## 2. The management of knowledge

Without knowledge, no organization will survive. It is needed to perform the day-to-day routines and, perhaps even more important, it is needed to reflect upon these routines and, when necessary, to change them. Knowledge application and knowledge creation are at the core of any organization's existence. The relevance of various knowledge differs between organizations and changes over time. A professional organization, that is, an organization of so-called ‘knowledge workers’ [16, 17], leans more heavily on its intellectual assets than the average production firm. The more crucial knowledge is to an organization, the more important its capacity to renew its knowledge. The growing importance of knowledge in organizations also has its implication for the management of professional organizations [53, 63, 64, 65]. It is risky to assume that knowledge is ‘naturally’ at the right place, let alone that it is the ‘right’ knowledge. Specific efforts are needed to ensure its presence and to guide its (re)creation. Thus, it is necessary to ensure that the right knowledge gets to the right place and so increases the innovation power of the organization and its knowledge workers. Both require knowledge management.

The following quotation may help pinpoint some crucial elements of knowledge management: “At its core, knowledge must be seen as tied to the personal or human element. Knowledge, as we generally understand it, resides in peoples’ heads”... “The representation of knowledge can be mechanical, digital, visual, and so forth. For knowledge to provide a company with sustainable competitive advantage, such knowledge must be independent from any given individual.” [52] Two important fields of tension are: between knowledge at the level of the individual and knowledge at the level of the organization and between knowledge that people possess as opposed to something that can be represented in procedures, books, etc. This latter discrepancy is commonly considered the distinction between explicit and tacit knowledge, as discussed by Polanyi [62]. Explicit knowledge is also called public knowledge, since it covers those aspects of knowledge that can be articulated in formal language and transmitted among individuals. Tacit knowledge is more of a personal nature, involving such intangible factors as personal belief, perspective, and values, and embedded in individual experience. Polanyi’s distinction is mentioned frequently (e.g., [28, 58]), and it is one of the key premises underlying assertions about the close connection between knowledge and action [75]. The basic problem is that tacit knowledge, largely, defies recording and explicating, while it is, at the same time, indispensable for making knowledge productive.

Although these two fields of tension shed light on the core problems of knowledge management, they do not show where the focus for this function is or can be. Knowledge management raises two main problems (see Fig. 1):

A: managing the available pool of knowledge;

B: managing the creation of new knowledge.

The former is that of improving the use of knowledge: “make sure to get the right knowledge at the right place at the right time.” It is associated with the static nature of knowledge assets. The latter is that of improving conditions for knowledge creation. This suggests that the value of knowledge goes beyond its presence at a given time. The problem involves the dynamic nature of knowledge assets. This need for knowledge creation leads to the concept of the ‘learning organization’ [3, 68, 71, 85]. The specific importance of tacit knowledge for knowledge creation and innovation has already been noted. Underlying both problems a third one should be noted, concerning the storage of knowledge: “make sure that the current knowledge is available.” Efforts to reach this objective are usually discussed under the heading of organizational or corporate memory [1, 73, 74, 83]. An organizational memory is not only important for the application of current knowledge: it is vital for the creation of new knowledge (e.g., [22]). One driving effort to assure an ‘organizational memory’ is avoiding the risk of knowledge erosion. This refers to the situation where “there is no growth of knowledge, or, what is far more problematic, when an organization loses knowledge, for instance when a working-person retires or resigns and his knowledge and experience are not passed on to his successor” [45].

Fig. 2 provides a more practical perspective on how knowledge management may be implemented. It translates the three objectives or problems into goals and measures for knowledge management. A variety of knowledge management measures can be found. These may be grouped into three classes: organizational measures (e.g., team-based work, fuzzy structures or virtual organizations, job rotation, multi-skilling), those in the realm of human resources management (e.g., competence management, double career ladders, employability measures, training programs, financial participation programs, dedicated performance evaluation routines), and those relating to the information system (e.g., intelligent document imaging and retrieval systems, several forms of groupware, and intranets). Within the third class KBS may be effective. Their exploration and implementation have been suggested as a feasible measure for KM (e.g., [45, 87, 89, 29, 50, 66]). They have, for instance, been promoted as safeguards against knowledge erosion, as tools for maintaining the organizational knowledge base (is it up to date, efficiently stored, etc.; cf. [49]), and for offering support in problem solving situations [38].

## 3. What are knowledge-based systems?

The most common definition of KBS is human-centred. This highlights the fact that KBS have their roots in the field of artificial intelligence (AI) and that they are attempts to understand and imitate human knowledge in computer systems (e.g., [88]). However, KBS dramatically fall short of human intellect. They lack creative powers of reproduction and their learning capabilities are relatively primitive, etc. It is generally acknowledged that the intelligence of artificially intelligent systems is quite different from human intelligence (e.g., [15, 90]).

![](/api/attachments/R8CWQKAQ/fulltext/images/901dbd27bc0d8b2687c3cc40960441efe647914da49f9ec2ac2274082f6f6120.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Outline of a model for knowledge management.

A second set of definitions looks for characteristics in the architecture of a KBS (e.g., [46]). Four main components are usually distinguished: a knowledge base, an inference engine, a knowledge engineering tool, and a specific user interface (often natural language based). The knowledge base and the inference engine define the core of the KBS. The former is an active database with some form of ‘formal knowledge’ about how the data may be used in practice. The inference engine defines the ways in which the knowledge base may be put to use. Typical supplements for these two components are a knowledge engineering tool, offering instruments for filling the knowledge base, and a dedicated user interface. Characteristically, a KBS user interface should allow ‘why-’ and ‘how’- questions, having the system explain its behaviour when dealing with a given problem [14]. Definitions of KBS centring on architectural peculiarities are hardly more satisfactory than the human-centred ones. One only has to point to systems based on neural nets to see the limitations of these definitions: separating the aspects of a knowledge base and inference engine is hard. Such definitions mistake the historical form chosen in several KBS for the hallmarks of these systems.

A third set of definitions uses the term KBS to indicate all those organizational IT applications that may prove helpful for managing the knowledge assets of the organization (e.g., [40]). Expert systems and groupware, data warehouses, or even intranets would be included in this class. These definitions, however, are rejected here since they lead to erosion of the meaning of the term. KBS, in our view, are not just any IT systems used for dealing with knowledge.

The definition of a KBS that, in our view, provides the most satisfactory organization-centred viewpoint comes when focusing on ‘knowledge modelling’. Then, the key activity in building a KBS is in finding a formal model that allows a description of knowledge at a conceptual $[7]$ , or knowledge level $[56]$ , which aims at uncovering the basic schema linking the central concepts in a given domain. The best-known examples of formalisms for knowledge representation are production rules, predicate logic, frames and objects, and neural nets (see $[78]$ ). The definition of a KBS as the outcome of a knowledge modelling process is particularly useful, because it does not direct discussions regarding the organizational value of KBS to characteristics of the technology. The goal of ‘knowledge modelling’ can only be achieved by examining which elements of the knowledge can validly be described in any of the formalisms for knowledge representation. Embarking on the process of knowledge modelling not only assists in gaining insight into the characteristics of the knowledge, it can also guide discussions concerning the pros and cons of the use of knowledge. It may lead, for instance, to the identification why or in which respect formalization is not possible or advisable (e.g., because it reduces flexibility or does not do justice to specific intricacies). Knowledge modelling thus appears as a vehicle to guide thought about organizational knowledge and its functionality: the central theme is exploring which part of the organizational knowledge can be captured in formal schemata, and describing the benefits and pitfalls implied.

## 4. A framework for including KBS in KM

So, how should KBS be evaluated as KM measures? It should be noted that simply equating managing the development and use of KBS with KM is to be avoided. This tendency is present in the emerging standards for KBS; e.g., IMKA, the ‘Initiative for Managing Knowledge Assets’ [34], and ERIKA, the ‘European Initiative for the Management of Knowledge assets’. These efforts focus on a standard model for capturing expert knowledge and on standardization of knowledge retrieval (notably SQL-extensions, [11]). These initiatives equate knowledge management with establishing conditions for coordinated efforts to control KBS-implementations. As a starting point for fitting KBS into KM this approach is too narrow. A broader perspective is called for.

– direct the KM discussion of KBS to identifying the functionality of KBS as KM-objectives and goals;

– formalization and automation should not be the single goal of the knowledge modelling process, exploration of KBS functionality may prove fruitful even if it does not result in the actual use of these systems;

\- KBS should be considered as potential decision aids.

The idea of a knowledge system provides a framework that places the role of knowledge at two levels (see Fig. 3). First, the organization as a whole (level A), and secondly, the information system (level B). At level A goals and subgoals can be discerned, as well as various means for reaching these goals. It represents the specific view that the knowledge manager takes when seeing the organization as a ‘knowledge system’.

Fig. 3. Positioning KBS within KM.

There are three steps that may be taken in moving from the organizational to the IS level (see Fig. 3):

Step one: The diagnosis question is: How can an organization assess the ‘functionality’ of KBS as KM measures: where is the gain for the organization? This implies a way of establishing the opportunities.

Step two: The development question: How should KBS be designed and implemented given the context of intended usage in the organization? This implies issues such as the implementation strategy, the consideration of strategic KBS applications, matters of maintenance and updating, and knowledge acquisition.

Step three: Assessing and evaluating changes: What are the consequences to the organization if it decides to deploy the KBS? This involves an evaluation of KBS implementations in relation to other KM measures (organizational, HRM, etc.).

## 5. The diagnosis step: assessing the functionality of KBS for knowledge management

The first step is to diagnose the potential value of KBS for the organization. The approach proposed here focuses on the value of knowledge modelling, and more specifically on how it relates to the knowledge of an organization. It starts from a suitable description of the organization as a knowledge system. This may also be described as a dedicated SWOT analysis (SWOT=Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats). KBS may offer opportunities or threats; the organization as a knowledge system with its strengths and weaknesses provides the context for assessing whether these will be opportunities or threats.

This is not the same as specifying conditions under which developing a KBS is feasible (e.g., see $[41]$ ). A description of these conditions can only draw part of the picture. Diagnosing KBS potential is also different from identifying where KBS have proven their worth. Several authors build upon the well-known list of successful KBS application areas drawn up by Hayes-Roth et al. $[27]$ ; see also the standard list of functions in Ref. $[91]$ . Typical functions for which KBS are good include interpretation, prediction, diagnosis, and design.

What is called for is an inspection of the knowledge system of the organization to determine bottlenecks and opportunities. To do so systematically we need a classification of knowledge issues. Eight ‘knowledge value assessment realms’ can be identified (see Fig. 4); together they make up the ‘knowledge value chain’ (a term coined in Ref. [84] for a smaller set of functions). The ‘knowledge value assessment realms’ point to the activity classes of knowledge that are essential when drawing up a strategic knowledge plan for an organization. The eight are:

Fig. 4. The eight ‘knowledge value assessment realms’ contributing to the strategic knowledge plan.

A: assessment of current knowledge, that is, identification of the various elements in the knowledge portfolio and determining their status;

B: establishment of the strategic value of the elements of the portfolio;

C: comparison of the portfolio with that of the competition;

D: determination of knowledge required;

E: creation of new knowledge;

F: distribution and sharing of knowledge;

G: application of knowledge;

H: evaluation of knowledge (including disposal of outdated knowledge).

If KBS fit in somewhere in the value chain, it may be worth considering embarking on the process of knowledge modelling. Positioning KBS in the knowledge value chain refers to three interrelated elements: the functionality of KBS, the circumstances within the organization, and the nature of the knowledge domains (cf. [36] and Fig. 5). The first element requires an assessment of whether KBS may be valuable in one or more links of the knowledge value chain. The second element concerns the question: do the strengths and weaknesses of the organization make the use of KBS feasible. Suppose, for instance, that the improvement of the accessibility of knowledge is set as a goal. Computer literacy and the well-integrated use of computer networks within the organization may then serve to support the use of KBS. The third element involves the characteristics of the knowledge domains; for instance, the stability of the given body of knowledge. If changes can be attributed to well-defined circumstances (e.g., changes in administrative regulations), this will make a successful use of KBS more likely.

## 6. Survey results

In Spring 1997, the operation of the KBS in seventeen organizations in The Netherlands was investigated. These included government organizations, banks, insurance companies, and production firms. Interviewees were the managers (mostly at the tactical level) responsible for the implementation and operation of the systems. The interviewees were asked, in retrospect, to pinpoint which problems and opportunities had acted as incentives for the implementation projects. The eight ‘knowledge value assessment realms’ were used in the assessment. Table 1 summarizes the outcome of this exercise. It shows that most incentives were reported in three realms. Firstly, problems and challenges in the assessment of current knowledge frequently led to KBS projects. In seven organizations, for instance, there was a wish to establish an ‘organizational memory’, an intelligent database augmented with associative search or case based reasoning facilities. Secondly, a relatively large number of incentives were mentioned concerning the application of knowledge. For six, for instance, a reason for considering KBS was the need to enhance the consistency of decision-making; in most cases the underlying desire was to use the available knowledge completely and consistently and thus to add to the reliability of knowledge application. Thirdly, the distribution of knowledge was reported to be a main reason for implementing KBS projects. ‘Knowledge sharing’, for instance, was mentioned by three organizations as the main goal for implementing KBS. They wished to improve the accessibility of organizational knowledge and thus spread it.

## 7. The development step: KBS-implementation and knowledge acquisition

Technical feasibility should always be addressed in conjunction with organizational desirability. Five stages in managing the development of KBS have been discerned [20]:

1. knowledge processing strategy: an overall plan should be chosen for directing the development of KBS (e.g., a guided, centralized approach versus a specialist approach, a dispersed points approach versus a dispersed clusters approach);

2. strategic application identification: KBS should be valued as components of the functional strategies of the organization to see how they may support its competitive strategy (most KBS are apparently used for operational purposes; a recent review of 380 research articles on KBS reports only some seven percent of strategic applications);

3. application feasibility: the resources, style, history, culture of the organization should allow the introduction of KBS, the selected knowledge domains should not impede their formalization;

4. application creation: designing and building the system. This is the actual ‘knowledge modelling’ consisting, for instance, of the construction of the strategy, task, inference and domain layers in KADS [31, 57, 69, 86];

5. deployment and maintenance: the assignment of responsibilities for data input and correction, for the update of the knowledge base, dealing with day-to-day use of the systems (e.g., [19, 77, 80]).

Some would add the assessment of costs and benefits of the planned implementations as an additional element to the list (e.g., see $[13, 35]$ ). Others, however, argue that no valid cost/benefit assessment of dealing with knowledge is conceivable. We feel that the best path to take lies somewhere in the middle.

A scan of the literature shows that discussions regarding the technical aspects are dominant (see, for instance, [42]). Most attention in the KBS literature centres on the last three stages, particularly the fourth step, which is concerned with knowledge acquisition. Several well-documented methods for knowledge acquisition are available (see, for instance, [9, 25, 37, 72]); the de facto standard in this realm is the KADS methodology. The contribution presented by Dutta is an essential extension to the existing literature. It fills two significant gaps for arriving at a balanced managerial perspective on KBS. Firstly, it emphasizes the links between KBS implementation and the management of an organization's knowledge assets. Secondly, it provides an approach to incorporating KBS in the information systems strategies, starting from ‘managerial concepts’ of the ‘knowledge level’ and ‘implementation responsibilities’.

Yet, we feel that the presented list of stages has three shortcomings. The first is that, by focusing on individual knowledge assets, the approach introduces the risk of sketching a partial, fragmented picture of the knowledge of an organization. Focusing, instead, on the ‘knowledge system’ will provide a more integrative view. A second, and related, issue is that insufficient justice is done to the diagnosis step, while the change evaluation is completely missing. The third point is that insufficient attention is paid to the ongoing nature of the design efforts. The term ‘development’ should be preferred to ‘design’. A static design effort would introduce the risk of reducing the leverage potential of knowledge.

## 8. The change evaluation step: assessing the implications of using KBS

The third step in the framework for linking KBS to KM involves the ex-ante evaluation of the consequences the deployment of KBS will have on the organization. A key element is in learning from experience. Two lines of action may be suggested: performing a mental experiment: simulating the work processes when using the KBS, and conducting a scan of the suggested KBS uses that resulted from the diagnosis and development steps, comparing them with known examples (see [30]; for a description of simulating the effects of KBS in education: see [12]). Following the second method, one should:

1. gather experiences on the (positive or negative) changes that resulted from the KBS and

2. see if the lessons from these experiences apply to the given situation. The effects reported by others should be evaluated in connection with the exploration of strengths and weaknesses of the own organization and the nature of the knowledge domains.

Publicly accessible material for the first step is scarce. Most of the studies addressing the organizational impact of KBS are of a contemplative nature, exploring at the theoretical level what might happen and presenting models to organize or to corroborate these claims $[2, 5, 6, 32, 51, 54, 59, 70, 81, 82, 92]$ . Only few studies present empirical material allowing readers to assess whether predicted impacts are mere conjectures or actually occur $[4, 47, 76]$ . It should also be noted that these empirical assessments only measure the positive effects and ignore the negative ones $[61]$ . A factor negatively influencing the availability of KBS evaluations is the secrecy surrounding the organizational impact of these systems $[10]$ . Even more striking than the lack of empirical evaluations in case studies is the fact that the impact question is almost completely ignored in studies addressing the managerial issues regarding KBS (e.g., $[48]$ ). Yet, if the impact assessment issue is not explicitly put on the managers agenda, it seems hardly conceivable that a satisfactory overall picture can be drawn of the organizational value of the KBS. The implementation of a technically feasible, cost-effective, functionally useful system should, at least, be reconsidered if it is thought that using the system is likely to result in serious damage to the knowledge of the organization.

A common trait of the empirical assessments of the impact of a KBS is that they focus on isolated issues. For instance, some study the effects on job content and job satisfaction (a decrease in job satisfaction is predicted by some). The issue of user satisfaction, which may be seen as subsidiary to job satisfaction, is investigated in Ref. [93]. Others focus on the increased dependence on the technology, or on productivity (increases in productivity are reported by some, while threats to productivity are documented by others). Other positive effects are, for instance, the increased information processing capacity of the organization. Such a perspective calls for these individual assessments to be imbedded in an approach for uncovering the impact of the KBS.

The impact of the KBS was measured by presenting the interviewees with a set of statements describing individual effects. These statements were ordered by the eight knowledge realms. For example, in the realm ‘creation of new knowledge’ the statement was included that “the use of the KBS has led to a decrease in the need of active reflection.” In the realm ‘dispersal of knowledge,’ one of the statements was: “the use of the KBS has led to attenuation at the social level: for instance, because less need for mutual consultations arises.” The interviewees were asked to indicate whether the effect had occurred or if the opposite effect could be noted. They were also asked to support their assertion. Table 2 presents the results. The two statements were the most frequent in the categories ‘slightly negative’ and ‘negative’. The statements that, on average, received the most positive ranking related to the reduction of dependence on individual experts, the increased focus on the more interesting tasks for experts, and higher degrees of efficiency. Given the answers to the individual statements, the interviewees were asked to give an overall assessment of each individual realm (for instance, "Did the implementation of KBS have a positive or negative impact on the application of knowledge?"). Table 3 presents an aggregate picture of the answers. The table shows that much more positive than negative effects were reported. Both tables also show that most benefits were reported for those areas where problems or challenges had initiated the KBS projects. However, empirical material can never be conclusive for the ex-ante evaluation of the impact of KBS. Assessing KBS potentials and threats should be conceived as an ongoing self-diagnosis of the organization that should be integrated with the actual use of these systems and with intended change.

## 9. Conclusion

This article has drawn the outlines of a framework for linking KBS and KM built around the notion of a knowledge system. Three main classes of relations between the organization as the overall knowledge system and KBS then emerge: the relation from the overall knowledge system to KBS (diagnosis), the position of KBS within the overall knowledge system (development), and the relation from KBS to their encompassing knowledge system (evaluation). In the literature most attention has been directed at the second, the development issue. The framework presented in this article does not expect to resolve all the issues of the relation between knowledge-based systems and knowledge management. It does not provide the answers to all problems of assessing the potential value of KBS within knowledge management efforts, nor does it intend to do so. It attempts to provoke a shift of focus, away from a technological, engineering perspective and from a generic technology assessment perspective on KBS, towards a focus on the specific challenges and problems within a knowledge-based organization. Only with this perspective on the role of knowledge within the organization can an assessment be made of knowledge-based systems and knowledge management and whether they are friends or foes.
