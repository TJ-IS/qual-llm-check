# Compensating effects of GSS on group performance

Wei Huang $^{a}$ , Kwok-Kee Wei $^{b}$ , Bernard C.Y. Tan $^{b,*}$

$^{a}$ School of Information Systems, University of New South Wales, Sydney NSW 2052, Australia $^{b}$ Department of Information Systems, National University of Singapore, 10 Kent Ridge Crescent, Singapore 119260, Singapore

Received 1 May 1997; revised 9 May 1998; accepted 26 July 1998

## Abstract

A GSS often fails to aid in providing group consensus and user satisfaction. To understand the reasons behind this lack of impact, we investigated the issue from an influence-process perspective. The independent variables manipulated were support (GSS vs. face-to-face) and task type (intellective vs. preference). The dependent variables measured were three process variables (informational influence, normative influence, and influence distribution) and three outcome variables (group consensus, decision satisfaction, and decision process satisfaction). The findings suggest that the lack of impact of GSS on decision-making groups may be due to their compensating effects. With the preference task, the use of GSS led to a more even influence distribution; this should improve group decision outcomes. But the use of GSS was also found to attenuate normative influence; this should negatively affect group decision outcomes. For an intellective task, the use of GSS resulted in an uneven distribution of influence; this should negatively affect group decision outcomes. But the use of GSS also amplified informational influence; this should enhance group decision outcomes. Collectively, such compensating effects of a GSS may explain why a lack of GSS impact on group decision outcomes has been observed in many studies. © 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Group support system; Task type; Group decision process; Group decision outcomes

## 1. Introduction

A review of empirical research on group support systems (GSS) conducted over the past decade (e.g. see Refs. [1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10]) yields several observations. First, GSS tend to raise decision quality, quantity and quality of ideas generated, and equality of participation, but not group consensus, decision satisfaction, and decision process satisfaction [3, 5]. Reasons behind the inability of GSS to raise group consensus and satisfaction are not fully known. Second, GSS research normally neglects the ‘process’ part of the input-process-output framework for conducting group research [25, 30]. In most GSS studies, group decision process is treated as a black box. Third, group work involves solving a variety of tasks in a range of settings, but investigation of task variables has been inadequate [20, 26].

Given that group decision outcomes depend, to a great extent, on the group decision process [21], this study explores the lack of impact of GSS on group decision outcomes from the perspective of the group decision process. Moreover, since the effects of GSS are likely to differ for different task types, we assessed the effects of GSS on group decision process and the outcomes, using two different task types.

## 2. Relevant literature

## 2.1. Group influence process

Here, the group decision process is examined from an influence perspective, because this has been a basis for many studies of group research in social psychology $[17]$ and there has been a growing interest in this perspective among GSS researchers $[22]$ . Social psychology research on influence processes has focused on two main modes: informational and normative influences $[8, 15]$ . Informational influence is based on the acceptance of information from others as evidence about reality $[4, 15]$ . This mode of influence involves exchanging messages, such as facts, rationale, and arguments. Normative influence is based on the desire to conform to the expectations of others. This mode of influence involves exchanging messages pertaining to preferences and values $[4, 15]$ .

## 2.2. Task type

Task is an important determinant of the information exchange pattern in a group, often accounting for as much as 50% of the variance in group performance [23]. Hence, task type has been included as an independent variable in assessing how GSS affects the group decision process and outcomes. Given that informational and normative influences can be better elicited when groups are completing intellective and preference tasks, respectively, both these types of task have been chosen for this study.

## 2.3. GSS effects

Correct answers exist for intellective tasks. With such tasks, group decision outcomes depend, to a great extent, on factual information exchange. A typical group activity path will include project inception, issue discussion, and project execution $[18, 19]$ . A GSS can be used to support this activity path. First, the electronic meeting agenda can regulate group interaction and encourage factual information exchange. Second, the anonymity feature can allow group members to exchange factual information with less fear of social disapproval and reprisals. Third, the simultaneity feature can facilitate exchange of factual information by providing group members with parallel and open access to the communication channels. Prior research has found that GSS groups tend to generate more task-focused messages when working on intellective tasks $[16]$ . Therefore, GSS are likely to increase the amount of factual information exchange when groups are working on intellective tasks.

Solutions for preference tasks are based on subjective values. With such tasks, group decision outcomes depend to a great extent on the exchange of personal preferences. A typical group activity path will include project inception, conflict resolution, and project execution $[18, 19]$ . To complete such tasks, group members need to reconcile their preferences and conflicts are inevitable. A GSS may hinder this group activity path in several ways. First, personal preferences are best expressed and exchanged using multiple social cues (e.g. tone of voice and facial expression). But GSS tend to dampen these social cues $[14]$ and hinder exchange of personal preferences. Second, the anonymity and simultaneity features encourage group members to focus on merits of ideas instead of initiators of ideas. The result of disassociating people from ideas is that personal preferences cannot be effectively conveyed. Hence, GSS are likely to reduce exchange of personal preferences when groups are working on preference tasks.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

## 3.1. Research model

In our research, we used two independent variables: support (GSS vs. face-to-face) and task type (intellective vs. preference); the dependent variables involve group process (informational influence, normative influence, and influence distribution) and outcomes (group consensus, decision satisfaction, and decision-process satisfaction). The former includes the evenness of participation among group members, which is an important factor during group decision making $[7]$ .

Group consensus is commonly used as a measure of group performance [28]. Decision satisfaction and decision process satisfaction measure the contentment of group members with their decision and decision-making process, respectively [9].

## 3.2. Research hypotheses

GSS are likely to increase the amount of factual information exchange (informational influence) when groups are working on intellective tasks, and reduce exchange of personal preferences (normative influence) when groups are working on preference tasks.

H1a: When solving a preference task, normative influence will be less in GSS groups than in face-to-face groups.

H1b: When solving an intellective task, informational influence will be greater in GSS groups than in face-to-face groups.

Some group members tend to be better at oral communication than others and are likely to be better at conveying their personal preferences. This is likely to result in uneven distribution of influence in face-to-face groups. In GSS groups, the anonymity feature can encourage reticent group members to contribute their preferences and stop any group member from dominating the discussion $[12, 24]$ . Consequently:

H2a: When solving a preference task, influence distribution will be more even in GSS groups than in face-to-face groups.

When solving an intellective task, some group members may be more knowledgeable than others and are likely to contribute more facts and arguments to the solution. This is likely to result in some even distribution of influence when an intellective task is being solved by face-to-face groups. In GSS groups, the amount of factual information exchange is likely to be amplified. The simultaneity feature provides an open and parallel communication channel that should encourage more knowledgeable group members to contribute even more. Therefore, GSS groups are likely to experience even more unequal distribution of influence.

H2b: When solving an intellective task, influence distribution will be more uneven in GSS groups than in face-to-face groups.

Solving a preference task involves exchange of personal preferences. By restricting normative influence, GSS may hinder the ability of group members to reconcile their different preferences to reach a consensus $[11]$ . However, by promoting even distribution of influence and allowing all group members to contribute, GSS may encourage a more complete representation of preferences in the group decision $[13]$ . Group members are likely to agree with the group decision when their preferences have been incorporated, leading to greater consensus. In summary, such compensating effects of GSS suggest that GSS can alter group consensus either way. Hence, we posit a result from prior research:

H3a: When solving a preference task, group consensus will be lower in GSS groups than in face-to-face groups.

By increasing the amount of informational influence, GSS allow group members to raise relevant facts and arguments while working on an intellective task. This should facilitate group consensus. However, GSS may also promote uneven distribution of influence. This may lower group consensus if group members, whose views are not represented, disagree with the group decision. Thus, from prior research we posit:

H3b: When solving an intellective task, group consensus will be lower in GSS groups than in face-to-face groups.

By reducing normative influence and hindering group members from the needed exchange of personal preferences when working on a preference task, GSS are likely to reduce the satisfaction of group members. Moreover, since the solution of a preference task is anchored on subjective values, the lack of exchange of personal preferences means that group members may be less satisfied with the group decision. Conversely, by promoting a more even distribution of influence, GSS allow all group members to participate in the discussion, thereby making them more satisfied with their decision-making process. By giving all group members the opportunity to contribute to the group decision, GSS may also increase the satisfaction of group members for their group decision. Thus, we again posit from prior GSS research:

H4a: When solving a preference task, decision process satisfaction will be lower in GSS groups than in face-to-face groups.

H5a: When solving a preference task, decision satisfaction will be lower in GSS groups than in face-to-face groups.

By facilitating informational influence and allowing group members to exchange relevant facts and arguments when solving an intellective task, GSS are likely to enhance the satisfaction of group members. In addition, since exchange of useful facts and arguments can lead to a better quality decision, GSS are likely to raise the satisfaction of group members for their group decision. Conversely, by reducing the evenness of the distribution of influence, GSS may cause some group members to participate less during the discussion and be less satisfied. Since the opinions of such group members are less likely to be incorporated into the group decision, they may also be less satisfied with the final decision. Again, we rely on prior GSS research to posit:

H4b: When solving an intellective task, decision process satisfaction will be lower in GSS groups than in face-to-face groups.

H5b: When solving an intellective task, decision satisfaction will be lower in GSS groups than in face-to-face groups.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Experimental settings and subjects

This experiment had a $2 \times 2$ factorial design, manipulating support and task type. Face-to-face groups engaged in traditional group discussion. GSS groups were equipped with the software aided meeting management (SAMM) system developed at the University of Minnesota (USA). Every group member was provided with a private terminal to enter inputs. Collective inputs of the group were captured and displayed on a public screen visible to all. All meeting sessions were video-recorded for group interaction analysis.

A total of 160 undergraduates from the National University of Singapore participated in the experiment and were given course credit. They were randomly assigned to five-member groups, which were randomly assigned to the various treatments to fulfil the requirement of independent samples.

## 4.2. Measurement of dependent variables

As the Kaplan and Miller [15] coding scheme is designed to capture informational and normative influence during group interaction, it was used in this study. The categories of this coding scheme are: task facts, inferences from task facts, values or norms, personal preferences, etc. The first two categories measure informational influence while the third and fourth categories measure normative influence. By viewing videotapes and examining computer logs, every time that a group member exerted influence, the fact could be recorded and coded. Total informational and total normative influence in a group were obtained by summing the coded scores of all group members. Given that the meeting duration of GSS and face-to-face groups might differ, the coded scores were divided by meeting time to correct for unequal meeting duration. Total influence of each group member was obtained by summing all five categories. Influence distribution in a group was then measured as the variance of total influence among all group members. A sample of the coding scheme is provided in the Appendix A.

Group consensus was computed using a fuzzy set method $[27]$ which took, as inputs, the decisions of all group members before, and after, their meeting. Decision satisfaction was measured using five questions taken from Green and Taber $[9]$ :

1. To what extent do you feel personally responsible for the correctness of the group solution?

2. To what extent does the group solution reflect your inputs?

3. To what extent are you confident that the group solution is correct?

4. To what extent do you feel committed to the group solution?

5. How satisfied are you with the quality of the group solution?

Decision process satisfaction was measured using another five questions taken from them. These questions assessed the group decision process on five dimensions as follows:

1. inefficient-to-efficient;

2. uncoordinated-to-coordinated;

3. unfair-to-fair;

4. confusing-to-understandable; and

5. dissatisfying-to-satisfying.

All questions measuring decision satisfaction and decision process satisfaction were anchored on five-point Likert scales. Group scores for both the variables were obtained by summing scores of all group members.

## 4.3. Task type

The intellective task was the International Studies Program task adopted from Zigurs et al. [30]. It required group members to score a list of competing applicants. The causal relationships between applicant attributes and success in the program were assured, but the exact nature of these relationships must be discovered or learned by group members in a training session. The preference task was the Personal Trust Foundation task adopted from Watson et al. [29]. It asked group members to allocate funds to a list of competing projects, based on the members' personal preferences and values.

## 4.4. Experimental procedure

The experimental steps for both the tasks were as follows:

1. group members were given a warm-up task;

2. members of intellective task groups were allowed to learn the task criteria in a training session;

3. group members were asked to perform the tasks individually prior to the meeting;

4. members of GSS groups were given training on the agenda and the features of SAMM;

5. group members were asked to perform the tasks together;

6. group members were asked to perform the tasks individually after the meeting; and

7. group members were asked to respond to questions measuring decision satisfaction and decision process satisfaction.

No time limit was imposed on the meetings. All meetings ended within two hours and thirty minutes.

## 4.5. Manipulation check

Since the support provided to GSS and face-to-face groups was physically different, no manipulation check was considered necessary. A manipulation check was carried out for task type. Informational influence was expected to dominate when groups were solving an intellective task but normative influence was expected to dominate when groups were solving a preference task. T-tests showed that informational influence was indeed higher in intellective than preference task groups $t=22.72, p<0.01$ and normative influence was indeed higher in preference than intellective task groups $t=12.89, p<0.01$ . Hence, the manipulation on task type was considered successful.

## 5. Analyses and results

All data analyses were carried out at a 5% level of significance. Table 1 summarizes the descriptive statistics of the dependent variables. Where appropriate, transformations were applied to dependent variables to allow them to fulfil homogeneity and normality requirements of t-tests. Table 2 shows the results of the t-tests. H1a, H1b, H2a, H2b, and H5a were all supported. No other hypothesis was supported.

## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1. GSS impact on group decision process

With the intellective task, a GSS has been found to increase the exchange of factual information but reduce the evenness of influence distribution. With the preference task, a GSS has been found to reduce the exchange of personal preferences but increase the evenness of influence distribution. Given that GSS impact on group process variables differed for the intellective and the preference task, these findings indicate that task type is an important factor in moderating the impact of GSS on group decision process. Hence, task type appears to be an important factor for consideration when designing and using GSS.

## 6.2. GSS impact on group decision outcomes

We explored why a GSS fails to enhance group consensus and satisfaction from an influence process perspective. Collectively, support for hypotheses relating GSS to group process variables and lack of support for hypotheses (except one) relating GSS to group outcome variables suggest that the inability of GSS to enhance group consensus and satisfaction might be due to the compensating effects of GSS. With the preference task, positive effects of GSS arising from greater evenness in distribution of influence might have been negated by effects of GSS due to suppressed normative influence. With the intellective task, positive effects of GSS due to more informational influence might have been negated by effects of GSS resulting from an uneven distribution of influence.

These results suggest that groups can take appropriate actions to alter their interaction process. Consider the example of a group working on an intellective task. The greater amount of informational influence exchanged can enhance group consensus while the uneven distribution of influence can reduce group consensus. To maintain evenness in distribution of influence at a satisfactory level and to prevent a GSS from reducing evenness in distribution of influence, a professional GSS facilitator can be employed to encourage more participation from reticent group members. In this way, a GSS can increase exchange of informational influence without causing excessively uneven distribution of influence.

## 7. Conclusion

While GSS research has examined the effects of GSS on group decision outcomes, the group decision process has not been thoroughly studied. Our work adopted a group influence perspective to explore an important question: Why should a GSS fail to increase group consensus and satisfaction? The results suggest that this failure of GSS to improve group decision outcomes may be due to the compensating effects of GSS on the group decision process.

## Appendix A

The five categories of the Kaplan and Miller [15] coding scheme are:

1. Task facts: statements citing facts provided in the task description.

2. Inferences from task facts: statements of facts not given in the task description but were inferred from those given.

3. Values or norms: statements of personal values or social norms on right or wrong in actions and outcomes.

4. Personal preferences: statements alluding to personal preferences, including both simple declarations and normative pressure to reach a specific consensus.

5. Others: statements excluded from the above categories and irrelevant to the task.
