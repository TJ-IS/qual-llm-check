# Information technology for collaborative advantage in healthcare revisited

Athanasia Pouloudi\*

Department of Information Systems and Computing, Brunel University, Uxbridge UB8 3PH, United Kingdom

Received 3 August 1998; accepted 1 November 1998

## Abstract

Interorganizational systems are expected to provide opportunities for various stakeholders, often competitors, to collaborate. At the moment, there is a great need for research evidence on the factors facilitating or impeding the achievement of collaborative advantage between interorganizational systems stakeholders. A recent contribution has been the use of a health-information network case to derive lessons for policy makers in this respect. The development and use of the NHSnet in the United Kingdom provides an opportunity to review these lessons in a different context of health-information exchange. Combined with the use of stakeholder analysis, these lessons can enhance our understanding of the context, the stakeholders involved and affected by the network, their interests and their perceptions of collaborative advantage. It, therefore, provides useful insights in the lessons that healthcare policy makers can apply in the development and use of a collaborative network. Further theoretical and practical research is, however, needed to improve our understanding of success and failure factors for the achievement of collaborative advantage in different environments. © 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Interorganizational systems; Stakeholders; Collaborative advantage; Health information network; NHSnet

## 1. Introduction

Interorganizational information systems create new opportunities for collaboration between cooperating or even competing organizations (e.g. see Refs. [11, 12, 23]). Recently, Ferratt et al. [6] explored the role of information technology for collaborative advantage through a case study of a community health-information network (CHIN) and, in particular, the successful development of the Greater Dayton Area Community Patient Health Information Network. This case study has been a useful contribution to the understanding of success factors for developing such networks for collaborative advantage. The authors have put their findings together in nine lessons for information systems and other managers; these lessons concern competition, customers, suppliers, purchasing, incentives, leadership, priority, research and development as well as history.

Ferratt et al. note the usefulness of their work in the light of the contribution that interorganizational systems can have in improving cost-effectiveness in healthcare. Because of the ever-rising cost of healthcare, this is a critical issue for policy makers, healthcare providers, health insurance companies as well as the patient population. It is a worldwide concern and it has been a driver of recent healthcare reforms in the

United Kingdom, where cost containment in health expenditure is of high priority $[1, 14]$ . Information systems play an important role in the UK in the efforts to contain expenditure. One example is the collection and electronic processing of dispensed prescriptions by the Prescription Pricing Authority (PPA), a special health authority within the British National Health Service (NHS). The PPA is primarily responsible for reimbursing dispensation of drugs. However, the vast amount of data collected provided an opportunity to return comparative prescription data (in printed or electronic form) to individual primary care doctors and the local health authorities to which the doctors report so that both parties can audit their dispensing expenses $[22]$ .

On account of the increasing role of information systems in the provision and management of healthcare, the NHS Executive, the body responsible for the execution of healthcare policy in Britain, has set up the Information Management Group (IMG), aiming “to improve the ability of the NHS to benefit from the management of information and the use of information technology” [18]. Recently, the NHS-wide networking project was launched on the initiative of this group. The NHSnet, as the resulting network is known, has been described as “an integrated approach to interorganizational communications within the NHS” [15]. Similar to the Greater Dayton Area Community Patient Health Information Network described by Ferratt et al. [6], it was created to improve the exchange of information between healthcare stakeholders. However, unlike the American case, the implementation of the NHSnet has been problematic.

Section 2 of this paper describes the research approach employed to understand the conflicting perspectives of the NHSnet stakeholders. These are used, in Section 3, to provide an overview of the complexity and the factors that have so far hindered the wide use of NHSnet. Section 4 reviews these problems against the lessons suggested by Ferratt et al. The differences between the two cases are analyzed and used to complement the suggestions of Ferratt et al. with other factors affecting the extent to which information technology can be used for collaborative advantage. Finally, the paper concludes with the main findings of the two research approaches and puts forward suggestions for further research.

## 2. Research approach

The methodological approach adopted for exploring the issues related to the implementation of the NHSnet is a stakeholder analysis as described by Pouloudi and Whitley $[20]$ . Stakeholder analysis has been originally developed to facilitate the work of managers engaged in strategy planning $[7, 13]$ . However, it can also be applied to study an interorganizational system. This is because such systems inherently involve many people, groups and organizations that can have an impact or be affected by the interorganizational system in question. These stakeholders are more than just the current or prospective users of the interorganizational system. They also include parties such as the sponsors and providers of the system, as well as other less obvious stakeholders. Previous research has made the case for an interpretive and iterative approach that takes into account the interactions of each stakeholder with other parties $[21]$ . This allows the identification of a broad range of stakeholders.

The data collection technique applied comprises in-depth interviews, lasting one-to-two hours, with representatives from key stakeholder groups. These interviews have been used for identifying further relevant stakeholders, but also for understanding how the different stakeholders felt about the progress of the NHSnet. Thus, the interviews focused on the perceptions of the stakeholders regarding the exchange of healthcare information as this has developed to the present. They also looked at what the stakeholders expected to happen (or would like to happen) in the future. Additional material, such as the official publications of the NHS Executive regarding the NHS-wide networking project, annual reports of the interviewees' organizations, other advertising material as well as other research work in the area has been collected and used for understanding the current situation of the NHSnet use.

## 3. NHSnet: a brief case description

The Information Management Group launched the NHS-wide networking project in 1993 to enhance communication and information exchange between various healthcare providers and administrators. Since

1996, wide area networking services for data and voice have been available and can be purchased by those interested. Despite the technological success of the project, and, in particular, its completion within schedule, its implementation has suffered from the lack of acceptance by the medical profession. Thus, although the network is used for administrative and purchasing purposes, its use falls behind the ambitious NHS Executive plans. These plans perceived the exchange of patient information as an important implementation objective. This paper concentrates on the lack of success in this particular aim of the project; this is an area where the objectives of the NHSnet meet those of the Dayton Health Information Network.

Why has the NHSnet not succeeded in establishing electronic exchange of patient data? The doctors, through the voice of their professional association, the British Medical Association (BMA) argued that they were not prepared to exchange confidential patient information on this network unless their security concerns were answered. Doctors feared that the information available through the network would be extremely vulnerable for two main reasons. On the one hand, a large database with information about millions of patients would create a valuable resource (and could be sought after by insurance companies, pharmaceutical companies, prospective employers). On the other hand, the fact that all NHS members could be granted some access to the network would increase the likelihood of serious security infringements. In short, the attitude of the medical profession is based on the belief that “confidentiality is the cornerstone of trust between doctors and patients” (Dr. Macara, Chairman of the BMA council).

The NHS Executive have responded to the doctors' concerns first by assuring them from the early stages of the network development that adequate security mechanisms were in place, to safeguard information against unauthorized access:

[T]he safeguards built into the NHS-wide networking infrastructure and the computer systems it supports will ensure that confidential information cannot be accessed by unauthorized people but that networking services are accessible to authorized users when needed [15].

As the doctors were not convinced of the effectiveness of these mechanisms, the NHS Executive undertook a study for encrypting data [17]. The proposals made in this report raised more questions, related to the reliability of encryption as a security mechanism and to the identification of those who will be able to read the encoded messages.

The use of stakeholder analysis to explore these questions revealed that the situation is more complex than the first image of two conflicting parties, both claiming to be concerned with the confidentiality of patient data but failing to agree on the appropriate way to safeguard this data. The stakeholders involved and affected by the network are multiple. They interact and influence one another, each trying to promote their own interests. As a result, there are several issues besides the concern about confidentiality that may implicitly or explicitly affect the current conflict situation. In practice, this means that the outcome of the debate and the future use of the network remain uncertain. The following paragraphs illustrate in further detail the complexity of information technology use in this situation, in terms of the NHSnet stakeholders and in terms of the variety of issues that come into the debate about its form and use.

## 3.1. Great number and diversity of stakeholders

Besides the two conflicting parties, the NHS Executive and the doctors (the sponsors and the adopters of the network, cf. [3]), there are other groups or organizations that are connected through this network and will thus be affected by the outcome of their debate. These stakeholders include hospitals (the ‘providers’ of health services), health agencies (responsible for purchasing healthcare from providers on behalf of their local patient population), local communications management groups (looking after the technical issues for users and promoting national standards and policies at the local level) and the Prescription Pricing Authority (responsible for collecting information on dispensed prescriptions, reimbursing pharmacies and dispensing doctors, and informing doctors and their health authorities about prescribing habits and expenditure locally and nationally). Each of these stakeholder groups may access the network at different levels or exchange different pieces of information. However, they expect that the doctors’ decision to participate in or boycott the network will affect the applications that will be used across the network and, ultimately, its efficiency.

There are also a significant number of stakeholders that will not be connected through the NHSnet but will, nevertheless, be affected by the way in which it is used. A most obvious example of those ‘unconnected’ stakeholders are the patients. The attitudes and expectations of those stakeholders are also important. Some can be seriously affected by the implementation of the NHSnet, whereas others may be in a position to define how the network will be used. The patients are an example of the former. Despite the threat to their privacy, they have not explicitly voiced their concerns as a stakeholder group. Instead, they rely on the medical profession to do so on their behalf. The Data Protection Registrar, the independent officer responsible for the promotion and application of data protection principles in the country, is another actor who is interested in and often called to protect patient confidentiality. Insurance and pharmaceutical companies are also stakeholders of the NHSnet as others fear they may attempt gaining illegitimate access to confidential patient data. Moreover, there is a concern that they may convince the legislators or other key stakeholders of their right to access certain confidential data. Suppliers of computer systems and telecommunication services, as well as consultants on computing and security issues are also stakeholders who may lose or gain from the extent to which systems are used for electronic exchange of information through the NHSnet. Standards, the level of sophistication for the systems and their interfaces are some obvious concerns for these parties.

Finally, within each of these stakeholder groups there may be different interests and attitudes; they cannot be viewed as a homogeneous group. To take doctors as an example, some worry about the confidentiality of electronic medical records, whereas there are others who are unable or unwilling to use computers. This could be a result of their geographical location or because of a highly mobile patient population which does not make it worthwhile to record patient information. Also, doctors may be working in a non-highly computerized practice, i.e. one that does not encourage computer use. Although the level of primary care computerization is very high in the United Kingdom, a successful use of the NHSnet may critically rely on its accessibility and use by all primary care doctors.

## 3.2. Confidentiality is not the only issue ‘at stake’

At the moment, the lack of trust of the medical profession in the NHSnet is primarily attributed to the lack of adequate measures to safeguard patient confidentiality. Yet, there are other important issues intertwined in the conflict about the future use of the network; even national security has come into the debate when alternative encoding measures were discussed. Clearly, it is the broader set of issues that need to be taken into account. As mentioned previously, not all doctors use computers to the same extent. The level of computerization is quite dispersed across the country $[8]$ and there are significant differences in the ways and the extent to which family doctors and hospital doctors use information technology. This diversity becomes more visible if we also include health agencies or other providers of healthcare such as pharmacies in the picture. Their diverse technical capabilities can be an impediment to the effective use of the network across the country.

Apart from the technological issues, political and organizational issues also become important in the case of the NHSnet. For example, the unwillingness of the doctors to use the network has offered an opportunity for another manifestation of the lasting conflict between doctors and managers. For example, some healthcare provision managers argue that not all doctors have the patients' interests at heart. Rather, their reaction to the network results from the interest of family doctors in maintaining their central role in the health system, as professionals who would know the whole medical profile of a patient. Doctors react strongly to this criticism. Their concern, they contest, is that unless their patients know that their doctor is the only person that holds their medical information on computer, they are not likely to confide in the doctor, with potential detrimental implications for deciding on appropriate therapy.

Other problems of political nature concern participation in the supply of services to the network or of systems that can interface with the network. This may create tension and problems for some suppliers, whilst others may take advantage of the network to promote more sophisticated products that their competitors will be unable to match. Participation in the use of the network has also political implications. Will pharmacies, for example, be allowed some access? The access rights of pharmacists, the types of information they will be able to receive and send out may change their role, their relationship with doctors or their relationship with patients. For example, prescriptions could be sent electronically from doctors to a pharmacy, in which case the patient would not have free choice in selecting a pharmacy and pharmacists would become dependent, to an extent, to doctors' favoritism. Furthermore, the superior technological capabilities of some pharmacies in relation to others may pose problems of unfair competition in community pharmacy. Each change in the role or interorganizational relationship of the pharmacists is likely to be met with different reactions from different stakeholders. As a result, such political and organizational implications of NHSnet will also play a significant role in its form and future role.

## 3.3. Uncertainty and opportunities for the future

The debate between the NHS and the BMA has currently subdued, after the NHS took seriously the suggestion of the BMA to consider encryption of the data that is exchanged. Many of the stakeholders who are not connected to the network feel that the debate will eventually be resolved. Provided encryption mechanisms are introduced and given the need for efficient exchange of information across healthcare organizations, doctors are expected to agree to the use of the network. Yet, encryption algorithms may not be adequate to safeguard the confidentiality of patient data if most security breeches come from within the NHS as several researchers and security specialists believe (e.g. see Refs. [2, 5, 27]). Thus, according to some stakeholders, it will be members of the NHS who have legitimate access to the network that are more likely to accidentally or deliberately misuse it. The danger is not so much in the disclosure of isolated items of information, but of the possibility to aggregate and link pieces of data together [4]. As long as doctors (or other stakeholders) perceive these to be important issues, encryption and control of access to the network will not be regarded as adequate measures.

At the same time, there are important concerns about the selection of encryption mechanism, too. The Pretty Good Privacy (PGP) algorithm, which has been used reliably on the Internet, has been rejected in favor of the ‘Red Pike’ encryption algorithm, a less reliable algorithm, but one that would allow the Government Communications Headquarters (GCHQ) to have access to the data transmitted over the NHSnet. This choice is desirable to the NHS Executive for cost reasons and necessary according to the GCHQ for reasons of national security. Understandably, the ‘Red Pike’ raised new concerns within the BMA as it makes personal patient data accessible to further stakeholder groups [19].

It is worth noting that the problems in the exchange of patient information do not prevent the network from being useful in the exchange of other administrative information. Also, there is continuing research and suggestions about alternative uses of the network. For example, there have been suggestions for taking advantage of the existing infrastructure for professional education $[25]$ or as an education tool for hospitalized children $[24]$ . Although these proposals can be seen as sources of collaborative advantage, they do not have the far-reaching effect that the exchange of patient information between health care providers would have. Section 4 examines the problems in the use of information technology for collaborative advantage in this respect, by comparing the NHSnet with the Greater Dayton CHIN in the light of the lessons suggested by Ferratt et al. $[6]$ .

## 4. NHSnet and CHINs: Reviewing the lessons

The purpose of the NHSnet in the UK is similar to that of the Greater Dayton Area Community Patient Health Information Network in the US. Both these networks are expected to create collaborative advantage for healthcare providers, who can all benefit from the electronic exchange of information, improving at the same time the level of service provided to the final customer, namely the patient. The results of their implementation are nonetheless different. Whilst the particular community health information network has been viewed as a success and there are yet higher expectations about accrued benefits in the future, the NHSnet has not so far been considered particularly successful, despite the absence of significant technical problems. The aim of Section 4 is to use the nine lessons for successful development of interorganizational networks for collaborative advantage that

Ferratt et al. drew from the American case as a canvas for studying the lack of success in the case of NHSnet. This will, on one hand, enhance our understanding of the NHSnet problems and, on the other, allow for a critical evaluation of the relevance of these lessons for other applications where information technology has been used for collaborative advantage.

## 4.1. Reviewing the competition lesson

Competitors can find collaborative advantage in acquiring the IT to share information, provided doing so gives no competitive advantage to any subset of collaborators acquiring the IT.

First, it is important to note that the notion of competition is more straightforward in the American case. Similar organizations (hospitals) are competing for similar customers (patients). According to Ferratt et al., the competitors are prepared to make their medical information accessible because they do not perceive this as a source of competitive disadvantage. In the NHSnet case, who is competing with whom – and to whose benefit – is less clear. This is mainly due to the gatekeeper role of the family doctor (GP). Recent healthcare reforms aimed at the creation of an ‘internal market’ with ‘purchasers’ and ‘providers’ of healthcare. These reforms were meant to increase competition between NHS members and thus increase efficiency in the provision of services. More specifically, primary care (and the health agencies that allocate budgets to the GPs that do not have own budgets (the ‘non-fundholders’) has assumed a purchasing role of services supplied by secondary healthcare. However, the purchaser is not always in a position to freely choose a cost-effective supplier. Social considerations, such as keeping hospitals running in the local community for the convenience of the local population but also to ensure employment and not to abandon significant investments of public expenditure, mean that competition between NHS hospitals is qualitatively different from the competition experienced between American hospitals.

Another important, subtle dimension of competition within the NHS is that GPs may lose part of their professional power if the information they hold on patients is to be shared and if access to this information will not be subject to the permission or even the knowledge of the GP. As we explained earlier, loss of professional power may not be – at least overtly – the main concern of the doctors. It is, however, a significant element of competition between dissimilar (in roles and power) participants in the NHSnet.

In theory, general practitioners may find themselves competing for patients in their constituency and those doctors providing a better service should normally attract more patients. However, the effect that participation in the NHSnet will have in the competitiveness of a GP remains unclear, because of the concerns about the sharing of patient information as explained in Section 3. Will GPs that allow the medical records of their patients to be exchanged electronically attract more patients (because they will be able to speed up process and avoid mistakes) or will they dissuade patient enrolment (because patients will be concerned with their privacy)? There is no clear answer at the moment, not least because many patients are unaware of the potential role and implications of information technology use in the delivery of healthcare.

## 4.2. Reviewing the customer lesson

Competitors can have a collaborative advantage in acquiring IT for sharing customer data if service to customers is improved through sharing the data.

A key problem for the interpretation of this lesson within the context of the NHSnet is that it is not clear what an improved service to customers means. This is partly because of the complexity of relations between healthcare stakeholders in the NHS: it is not clear whether the customers are the patients, the GPs or the health agencies. Furthermore, the expectations of these various customers for service are also different; the dilemma that efficiency in information exchange poses, as opposed to safeguarding confidentiality of patient data, is the clear manifestation of the divergence of opinion.

Some healthcare providers believe that confidentiality is safeguarded as long as the ‘need to know’ principle applies: providers should only have access to the part of patient information that they need for making sound scientific decisions. This, however, is a problem because it is not always easy to identify the relevant pieces of information in advance. Moreover, such a contingent access to different pieces of information cannot easily be represented electronically. Thus, there is no guarantee that the data stored will not be sought even if unnecessary. Granting different levels of access to different people according to their professional background and experience is not adequate for addressing this problem. On the other hand, if the level and conditions of access are to be reviewed for each case, the benefit of efficiency that the NHSnet is seeking to achieve will be lost; the delay may also prove critical if emergency treatments are delayed for such administrative reasons.

To make matters more complicated, the current state of fragmentation in the NHS, whereby not all NHS members are equally prepared to use the network, raises questions about the efficiency of making the medical history of patients electronically available. Some of the legitimate but inadequately equipped users of the information will be unable to access it; therefore, the improvement of service to the customers will be doubtful whereas the fears for compromising their privacy will persist.

## 4.3. Reviewing the supplier lesson

Competitors can have a collaborative advantage in acquiring IT for sharing data if collaboration provides suppliers (e.g. physicians) with data that will help the suppliers better perform their jobs.

The inefficiency of the previous system has been one of the main reasons that the IMG of the NHS Executive put forward the NHS-wide networking project. In other words, allowing physicians (but also administrators) to do their work more efficiently, that is with less delays and mistakes and be able to have more information available as and when needed, has been of primary importance. There are some differences, however, with the American case. In the Greater Dayton case, the hospitals perceived an advantage in using their network in its potential to attract physicians. Such an advantage does not apply to the United Kingdom where doctors are employed by hospitals. At the same time the patient is, in principle, out of the hands of the family doctor for the period of his hospitalization. Thus, family doctors are more concerned with the effectiveness of therapy and cost of the hospital than with the extent to which it uses advanced technology. The level of computerization is of doubtful value for them in making referral decisions: in some cases, it may simplify and speed up the referral process. However, such improvements do not alleviate the concerns about the efficiency and cost in healthcare delivery.

## 4.4. Reviewing the purchasing lesson

Competitors can find collaborative advantage in the acquisition of the IT for sharing information through reduced purchasing costs.

This lesson implies that collaborators can take advantage of the marketing agendas of computer-systems and network-connections providers to negotiate better prices or even to pose as potential pilot sites for the use of innovative products. In view of the central role of the NHS Executive's Information Management Group in launching NHS-wide networking, this lesson has no immediate application for the end-users of the network. However, the decision of the NHS to negotiate on a national basis with telecommunications providers has resulted in significant savings in costs in setting up the network. Still, these savings will not be meaningful unless the NHSnet is used extensively or unless a critical mass of users are able to upgrade their equipment to make it compatible with the network.

So far, participation in the network is voluntary, and the NHS hope that the benefits will act as an incentive for expanding the current user base. However, the reluctance of doctors to participate has made potential users of the network hesitant in connecting to it. GPs find the connection fee to be unjustifiably expensive. Also, the management in some hospitals seems particularly wary of the costs that participation to the NHSnet will incur in the short run (need to upgrade systems, connection fees, user training) but also in the long run (connection fees, doubtful benefits if others do not participate, changing standards). This attitude has resulted in suppliers offering a free month's connection hoping to boost orders and tempt customers by the benefits of the connection.

## 4.5. Reviewing the incentives lesson

Where the incentives for collaboration to share information do not exceed the incentives for competition, project initiation is more likely if those desiring collaboration provide incentives for it.

As we have already discussed, the notion of competition within the NHS is not as simple as in commercial organizations. Reasons for ‘competition’ between healthcare stakeholders may be political rather than economic and, therefore, more difficult to perceive and disentangle. In this case, it may be hard to come up with appropriate incentives. These would need to be based on an in-depth understanding of the perspectives and interests of those stakeholders, which means that this lesson may be hard to apply in the NHSnet case as well as in other interorganizational systems, where stakeholders are multiple with diverse and conflicting views. Local health authorities often assume the role of encouraging collaboration between primary and secondary (hospital) healthcare providers. However, such initiatives are unlikely to be successful unless they are supported by individuals that other stakeholders respect and consider legitimate.

## 4.6. Reviewing the leadership lesson

With no central authority in a collaborative IT project, project success is more likely if each competitor provides a project leader with a common understanding of the project and a willingness to allocate resources to it.

It is evident that the role of the IMG of NHS Executive has been critical for initiating the NHS-wide networking project. First, they have the resources (especially the time, which other stakeholders lack) to plan strategically and to negotiate at a large scale with suppliers of technology and telecommunications. Their initial involvement has been extended with the establishment of national and local user communication groups $[16]$ . These look after all aspects of communications at the national and local levels; their role is to plan, procure systems, communicate the strategy to the users across the country, as also to represent the users and attend to their needs.

Despite this impressive infrastructure and staff involvement, the IMG leadership has not been adequate for committing NHS members to the use of NHSnet. Part of the reason is that not all the stakeholders and their distinctiveness have been taken into account. It is interesting that, although there was a pilot of the system in which doctors participated, the problems and concerns about confidentiality of patient data did not rise at that early stage, at least not to the extent that would raise the concern of the IMG about the final success of the project. One explanation for the issue of confidentiality not being raised then is that the aggregation of data and its possible misuses become more evident once the project has been considered at the national level. The involvement of information technology and security experts played also a role in raising awareness within the medical profession of the potential dangers.

## 4.7. Reviewing the priority lesson

If a collaborative project has a relatively low priority for a competitor, project leaders in that firm can benefit from outsiders' help to keep their team members committed.

Again, the idiosyncratic nature of the healthcare ‘market’ makes it difficult to envisage this lesson in the NHS case. According to Gillies [8], the motivation for computerization over the years, at least at the general practice level, has been “a combination of carrot and stick.” Government legislation has played an important role in providing incentives for further computerization or placing such targets for the healthcare providers that the adoption of advanced information technology solutions became indispensable. Still, these measures do not apply to the whole of the health-providing population and discrepancies in progress, as far as information technology use is concerned, are great.

It is not surprising that this lesson is closely related to the lesson of leadership. Cost pressures do not encourage managers to make information technology investments. Wherever the priority for information technology use is low, this will certainly impact the future of the NHSnet. It is again important to consider the priorities and pressures of the different stakeholders. More importantly, priorities need to be considered at an appropriate level. For example, the initiatives of health authorities at a local level have often been catalytic for, at least, a partial adoption of the NHSnet and its applications.

## 4.8. Reviewing the R&D lesson

Competitors can find it easier to initiate a collaborative project seen as a research and development system than one seen as a production system.

This lesson does not seem directly applicable in the NHSnet case. It would perhaps be more relevant to commercial and more competitive environments, but this is an issue that requires further research in other sectors before any conclusions can be drawn. Although awards for collaboration and innovative usage of information technology are available, e.g. the ‘Healthcare IT Effectiveness Awards’ [9], these incentives would normally motivate those IT users who are already at the forefront of technological developments; perhaps those that would not need encouragement for taking up complex and ambitious projects. There is a danger that these may work against networking projects where implementation success is also contingent on the extent of participation. ‘Weaker’ partners are unlikely to become motivated through R&D concerns.

At the same time, there is a question of prioritizing when it comes to research in potential collaborative structures or the use of information technology to support these structures. Thus, research and development related to the use of technology is unlikely to attract the attention and commitment of hospital staff who are typically underresourced or have prior unsatisfactory experience of information technology. Witness to the latter factor is the extremely small number of successfully integrated hospital information support systems (HISS) in the United Kingdom [10].

## 4.9. Reviewing the history lesson

Collaborative advantage can be easier to obtain after competing participants first learn to collaborate.

According to the NHS Executive, the NHSnet has not challenged the way information was exchanged in the past. Only, by computerizing it, the NHSnet is expected to render the process quicker and more efficient. If that is the case, if this network is ‘automating’ rather than ‘informating’ [28] data exchange between the various NHS members, then the history of collaboration between them should not be a primary issue to consider. So, the NHSnet could be expected to support existing collaboration, improving but not seriously affecting the way things are done. However, many stakeholders see that the NHSnet can have a radical impact on their professional roles. More specifically, if use of the network will mean that the GP will no longer act as the ‘guardian’ of confidential patient information, then the whole context of information sharing in healthcare is about to change. Some interpret this as being the core concern behind the debate between the BMA and the NHS and view it as a hidden agenda of the medical profession. The political nature of the statement makes it difficult to assert the extent to which the history of collaboration between the prospective users of the network will affect its future use. At the same time, it is evident that if the way in which information was exchanged between healthcare professionals in the past has changed radically, those who benefit from the present situation will react to any profound change.

## 5. Discussion

The application of the nine lessons for success of information technology used for collaborative advantage by Ferratt et al. presents some interesting and important points for discussion. First, the Greater Dayton Area CHIN study that led to these nine lessons is extremely valuable. It sheds new light on issues that worked out well and those that did not in a case of information technology implementation. At the same time, the NHSnet that was investigated in this paper illustrated that these nine lessons can be interpreted differently in different situations and when different research approaches are adopted. For example, the less homogeneous and competitive nature of health provision in the United Kingdom means that a different set of stakeholders were involved, each with different considerations and priorities. Thus, the relevance of the nine lessons can become qualitatively different.

All nine lessons need to be considered in making decisions about the use of information technology for collaborative advantage. Although different subsets of those may be relevant in different occasions (e.g. the ‘the research and development lesson’ does not seem relevant for NHSnet), each lesson alone is inadequate for judging the success or failure of an interorganizational system application. Moreover, these lessons cannot be viewed in isolation; they are interrelated.

For example, the effect of NHSnet on the role of the GP in the context of healthcare is something that can be discussed from the perspective of at least three lessons, those of supplier, customer and history.

It is clear that these lessons can be used to offer insights about success and failure factors of an information technology project. Thus, the competition lesson illustrates that the network may undermine the professional role of family doctors and as a result be resented. The customer lesson shows that confidentiality of patient data needs to be safeguarded for the final customer, namely the patient, to be satisfied (and for those serving the patient to feel that they have not neglected their duties). It also means that efficiency in electronic exchange of data may require some standardization or compatibility between the systems used before it can be realized. The purchasing lesson shows that the NHS Executive has first saved costs by handling this project at a national level (however, these cost savings will only be justified if the system is widely used). The leadership lesson illustrates that the establishment of the communication groups has proved a helpful infrastructure that can be used by both users and the NHS to evaluate the progress of the system. Finally, the history lesson illustrates that changes cannot be overlooked, as they are likely to cause reactions; still, if the changes are subtle and difficult to describe, not dealing with them may reduce (perceived) conflict between stakeholders. These lessons are therefore useful to policy makers, such as the Information Management Group, who need a framework to understand different aspects to evaluate information technology projects and to inform future decisions.

The nine lessons, as presented in the original study, may be inadequate to guide future information technology implementation. This has become evident in the study of the NHSnet case for a number of reasons. First, the main problem of NHSnet, that of confidentiality, is not directly included in these lessons. Although Ferratt et al. refer to confidentiality when discussing the customer lesson, they do not perceive it as an issue, because data is shared without prior consent from the patient only in cases of emergency. This does not seem to be satisfactory in the UK context, as the circumstances of access to confidential data have not been agreed. Thus, while the issue of confidentiality may arise when discussing improved service to customers, because there are different ideas what this improved service entails and what compromises need to be made, the customer lesson is unlikely to facilitate an in-depth discussion of issues that need to be explicitly addressed. Issues such as that of confidentiality or the privacy of personal information also depend on the broader cultural and national context. Indeed, in the recent Fourth International Conference on Ethical Issues of Information Technology (25–27 March 1998, Erasmus University, The Netherlands), panel discussions revealed very different attitudes to privacy between American and European participants. In particular, European participants appeared more anxious to safeguard their privacy, whereas American participants took the disclosure of some personal information for granted.

Another key problem in the analysis of the NHSnet case using the nine lessons is the sheer complexity of this case in comparison to the American network. One illustration for this is the number of stakeholders that are involved; they have multiple roles and they are not easy to compare with the types of stakeholders that exist in other competitive and more commercial environments. The division of primary and secondary care is an eloquent example. Furthermore, in the American case, the users of the network are homogeneous organizations: hospitals. In the NHSnet case, in contrast, the users are a mixture of family doctors, hospital doctors, other hospital staff and administrators. Evidently, their roles and interrelations are more complex to understand and analyze in information terms.

Finally, an important difference between the two networks which limits the application of the lessons for the NHSnet case is the scope of the information that is exchanged as well as the scope of the network. The American network is established between hospitals at a local level. The NHSnet, however, is a national network connecting NHS members and potentially other parties that need to exchange information with them (and these obviously include a broader range of stakeholders than hospitals). This means that integration and aggregation of data becomes more complex, and hence more difficult. Also, there is a more substantial danger for security breaches. Moreover, the ‘collaborative advantage’ of the network may depend on the technological capability of some participants. The differences in the level of sophistication of information technology use in the health service across the

UK are an important inhibiting factor: “Although we already have a single health service in the UK, we are still struggling with integration issues at trust [i.e. hospital] and health-authority level. Any higher level integration is likely to falter until the lower level issues are more advanced” [26].

The foregoing discussion shows that the general context and culture in which an interorganizational system is introduced is of primary importance. This is an important lesson to add to those suggested in Ferratt et al. This ‘tenth lesson’, namely the context lesson, will affect the way in which the other lessons are interpreted. It also signifies that the number of lessons that are relevant in each situation will depend on the particular implementation. Likewise, it is worth noting that, especially in healthcare, collaborative advantage should not necessarily be viewed in economic terms; technological, social and political considerations also come into consideration and may affect the evolution and the success of an interorganizational healthcare network.

The context lesson is particularly important for decision makers in different national contexts. This paper has made explicit some of the differences in healthcare provision between the United States and the United Kingdom. Further differences across a broader international level are worth investigating, not the least for their implications in interorganizational-systems development and implementation. For example, the division of primary and secondary care is not observed in all European countries, presenting problems for policy makers and systems developers that operate across different countries. More generally, each national context is characterized by different stakeholders, with different mindsets and interests, that will need to be considered and evaluated within that particular context. Such differences, therefore, have implications at a practical level but also make cross-national research more challenging.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper has been based on previous work regarding the use of information technology for collaborative advantage in healthcare. In reviewing the same work with different case evidence, the paper has contributed to the effort of learning about successful development of interorganizational systems. The focus of this paper has been the implementation and use of the NHSnet. After applying stakeholder analysis as a means of understanding the context of the case, the lessons that Ferratt et al. drew during their research served as a canvas for evaluating the new case evidence. The applicability of most lessons to the new case material endorses the merit of the previous research. The findings also add to previous research by suggesting that the study of the perspectives of a particular network's stakeholders can help to look at these nine lessons from different perspectives and, thus, enhance their value.

An important implication of this paper has been that each interorganizational-system case is different. There are always some different elements in the context of development and implementation: the systems are probably developed in different domains, at different times, in different cultures or by different people. However, as information-systems researchers and practitioners are still in the process of learning about successful development of systems within – as well as amongst – organizations, the study of cases of success and failure is very important. The use of interorganizational systems in healthcare still lacks a substantial body of research and it is an area worth studying separately from other domains because of the wider social implications and ethical dilemmas that the use of information technology may raise in this context. Also, the diversity of stakeholders that are involved and the qualitatively different roles they assume if compared with other domains of human activity make it a domain rich in specific research lessons.

Further research is necessary in both the applications of information technology in healthcare and in the use of information technology for collaborative advantage. Information systems researchers should, therefore, look into other cases of interorganizational-systems use in healthcare including both, successes and failures stories from different environments. At the same time, it is also important to record the different information-system research and implementation approaches that are followed and to study their impact in reaching research and practical conclusions. This will improve our understanding of interorganizational-systems development. In this respect, this paper joins the Ferratt et al. call for further research into how organizations can generate ideas for projects using information technology for collaborative advantage. The lessons derived from two cases of information networks in the healthcare environment will hopefully serve as a starting point.
