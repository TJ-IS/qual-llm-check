# Patterns of senior executives' personal use of computers

Monica Seeley $^{a,*}$ , David Targett $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Mesmo Consultancy, 128 The Vale, London NW11 8SL, UK $^{b}$ Imperial College Management School, London SW7 2PG, UK

Received 25 September 1997; accepted 3 July 1998

## Abstract

This paper reports on a study of senior executives and how their use of the computer as a personal productivity tool has changed over time. Using process methodologies, four distinct patterns of use were identified. The episodes and encounters associated with these patterns were explicated and are reported. The importance of enabling executives to maintain their PC expertise, regardless of its level is highlighted. This paper demonstrates the application of process methodologies to produce a deeper understanding of executives as end-users. © 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: End-user computing; Senior management; Executive information systems

## 1. Introduction

The role model demonstrated by senior executives is one of the most important and influential factors in determining the level of success that an IT project achieves $[25, 46]$ . Nonetheless, the extent to which they themselves accept and use computers, and hence the impact on how they operate, is debatable $[4, 28, 36, 48]$ . There is an extensive body of literature on the use of computers by middle and junior managers and professionals; this is commonly termed ‘end-user computing’, but few studies focus exclusively on senior executives whose modus operandi, and rationale for using PCs, is different from junior managers $[49]$ . The majority of studies of senior executives focus on their use of ‘Executive Information Systems’ (EIS), which are primarily designed to support them in performing decisional roles (for a review of this literature see $[39, 42, 43]$ ). The decisional roles are only one of the sets of roles that executives perform $[34]$ .

Regardless of seniority, learning to use a PC often involves a considerable investment of user's time and external resources [12]. Evidence suggests that many executives do not realise the full value of their investment in learning to use a computer: Ein-Dor and Segev [10] and Martin [30] note patterns of discontinuous use amongst managers. Martin studied the use of decision support systems (DSS) and found considerable differences in the intensity of systems usage, even though individual managers had received the same initial level of training, support and resources. Some who discontinued use did become users again, though it is unclear how their use changed with time. For example, did managers stop using all the available applications or only some of them? Did their frequency and depth of use change? If so, how?

Dreyfus and Dreyfus [9] note that many end-users often acquire an initial level of expertise and never develop past the beginner's phase. In other cases, they may develop their expertise to a level akin to an expert, but then for some reason do not make full use of that expertise and regress to being a beginner. There are several well-documented frameworks which show the stages of growth one might expect for organisational end-user computing (e.g. [14, 16]) and temporal changes [23] but as Amoroso [2] and Cotterman and Kumar [7] observe, far less is known about individuals' patterns of use over time. Is it reasonable to expect that individuals also move through some clearly defined growth path? What factors enable some end-users to develop their competence but inhibit others so that they do not progress past the beginner's stage?

Time is probably a senior executive's most precious resource. Given this, and the influence they have on the organisational culture, one might argue that once executives decide to become competent with computers it is important that they maintain their skills and capitalise on the investment in both their own and corporate resources. This paper reports on a study of 85 executives, and how and why the use they make of computers as personal productivity tools has changed over time.

## 2. Previous research

## 2.1. Variables effecting computer usage

There have been a substantial number of studies of the factors that are felt to affect both a user's disposition to use PCs and their intensity of use. These can broadly be divided into those factors external to the user (e.g. [6, 22, 27]) and those factors internal to the user (e.g. [8, 20, 21, 32, 51, 53]). Whilst both sets of variables can influence use, the internal ones, namely perceived ease of use and usefulness are generally regarded as dominant.

## 2.2. Levels of end-user expertise

The term ‘use’ is often stated ambiguously. It can imply a range of applications, frequency, depth of expertise, tasks for which the computer is used, etc. Recently, Seeley and Targett [47] showed that ‘use’ comprises at least three dimensions: frequency, depth of expertise with a software package and the breadth of software with which the executive was competent. They found that executives could be split into one of the four following broad end-user types:

Novice – use only e-mail and conventional mainframe MISs and EISs.

Intermediate – use a narrow range of software (primarily spreadsheets, word processing, and e-mail) for all tasks, regardless of suitability.

Advanced – make more regular and extensive use of computers and a wider range of software and graphics packages that intermediate users rarely use. Expert – make extensive use of computers for a very wide range of tasks and a wide range of software (average six different types), being able to choose the software most appropriate for the task.

## 2.3. Research methodologies

The majority of previous studies have been variable based, and have identified the factors influencing the acceptance and use of PCs. This may be why the results of such studies are often equivocal. Whilst these studies can isolate the factors that influence use, they do not explain how and why these variables have an effect. Moreover, most previous research has been based on a single episode of use, namely current use, and there is often an implicit assumption that use is consistent although other research has shown that it can be dynamic. Some authors suggest that one of the major issues within MIS research has been capturing changes in behaviour towards computers over time $[11, 19, 44]$ . One reason is that use is generally measured as a single data point, especially where the emphasis is on factor-driven quantitative approaches. It is suggested that more attention should be placed on change over time and that this could be achieved by taking a process rather than variable approach $[18, 41]$ .

Mohr [35] divides explanatory theory into two types, namely variance and process. Variance theory, the more common one, is concerned with explaining the change in some dependent variable. Process theory, on the other hand, focuses on the series of events that occur over time to explain how the phenomenon occurs. The difference between these two theories is summarised in Table 1. He further argues that process models can complement variance models, but the results from one type of model are not easily either interchanged or integrated with those from another and that it is not helpful to do so. Recently, Sabherwal and Robey [45] showed that variance and process strategies can be used jointly, and allow the researcher to gain additional insights.

Newman and Robey [37] argue that another significant difference between the two types of models is the way they treat the antecedents. Variance models treat them as conditions that may affect the outcome, whereas process models focus on the sequence of events within the conditions described by the antecedents. They explored the events that affected the user–systems analyst relationship, how the relationship changed, and how these changes influenced the way the systems developed. They classified events as either episodes or encounters. “An episode refers to a set of events that stands apart from others, thus signifying the end of one sequence of activities and the beginning of another. . . encounters mark the beginnings and ends of episodes. . . encounters are often shorter.”

One problem with this type of process-based research is the requirement to observe and capture data over a period of time. Access and continuity can be a problem in general $[5]$ and, especially, to senior executives $[26, 29]$ . Franz and Robey $[11]$ find that some researchers also object to the use of process type techniques in a single data collection situation, feeling that such changes in behaviour can only really be captured during a longitudinal study. They suggest that single period observation can be acceptable for discovery of factors, but that multiple periods of observation are needed to generate true process theories.

## 3. Research design

The purpose of this study was to elicit the encounters and episodes that caused any change and to determine what form the change took with respect to their personal use of the computer: hence, to generate a model of the process executives go through in developing their PC expertise. The overall design for this study was based on process research methodologies and the grounded theory $[15, 50]$ .

## 3.1. Data collection

One hundred and three senior executives (board level or just below) from twenty very large organisations were interviewed. The organisations were all either ranked within the top 150 UK organisations, according to the Financial Times Index, or of an equivalent size, for example a government agency or a multinational listed on an overseas stock market. Each interview was recorded and lasted, on average, one-and-a-half hours. Executives were asked to draw how their use of computers, and especially PCs, had changed over time since their first interaction. It was anticipated that some of the interviewees' description would be sufficiently 'thick' [13] to allow the episodes and encounters to be explicated, despite making single rather than multiple period data collection. During this research project, papers were published, for instance [38]; these used a similar technique of asking interviewees to discuss historical events and provide a chronology of key events. Executives were also asked to complete a Myers–Briggs type personality inventory (MBTI), as others have also noted a link between use and personality type (e.g. [31, 54]).

## 4.1.2. Steady-state intermediate users

There are those who have reached a medium level of expertise but show no signs of changing their breadth of use, see Fig. 2. Like the novice, they just do more of the same using their basic knowledge of PCs. As an example, Executive J3 said: “I got involved with Lotus very early. . . . Since then Lotus has been upgraded a number of times. . . . I tend to try and master the basics of the thing first and if I’ve got a particular application, I think I wonder if the machine does this. . . . So I tend to add various skills.”

These users may go through learning cycles (episodes (ep2B)). The encounters (en2B) which trigger them will be either a task that PCs can help or the arrival of a new version of software. Whilst the depth and frequency of use may vary slightly, overall PC expertise remains constant.

## 4.1.3. Steady-state advanced and expert users

There are those who develop their competence to a very high level: advanced or expert. The expert users never really stop developing their level of expertise in terms of the depth and breadth of applications. They are continually adding new layers of use.

Typically, the initial encounter (en1A) is a personal interest in PCs. The encounters (en2A) which push them up the learning curve are typically, the emergence of new technology (hardware or software), a recognition of the potential of PCs and a desire to find more ways to use it. There may be several mini-encounters during the episode (ep2A) on the way up the learning curve, as shown in Fig. 2. Twelve (71%) of these had been users for more than 10 years. Consequently, most could only recall major encounters, such as the advent of Windows. It was, therefore, sometimes hard to achieve ‘thick’ descriptions.

Executive (E4) had only been a user for the last three years and could recall the encounters more clearly. E4 first used computers (on leaving college) to find information on the company in which he was being interviewed: “That was the first I realised that there was information tucked away somewhere that was available. … they were impressed with my knowledge.” Prior use of a computer thus represents a very positive experience. E4 had not used a PC as a management tool until he started to use his wife’s Apple Macintosh at home (en1A). “… that’s when I started to realise what it could actually do for me in terms of presentations, in terms of communicating with people, so it started from there (ep1A) … Once I got my own machine (en2A) that’s when use sky rocketed (ep2A), … it became every second day.”

He changed jobs and location within the company (en3A) and: “... my use has increased dramatically even since I came back. ... I’m using it a lot more productively and efficiently now ... I don’t know if I’ve learnt more about what it does, because I still don’t know a lot about the programmes, but I’ve a lot more on my machine now.”

Although E4 has reached some sort of steady state, ep3A, there have been several mini-encounters (en4A) which prompted further use of PCs, such as the need to manage his business remotely and the discovery of a commercially available CD-ROM database of organisations with whom he deals. However, they are no major encounters which led to revolutionary changes in his level of competence. Orlikowski [40] has suggested that change could be viewed as a continual process more akin to metamorphosis, and this might have been the mechanism during episodes such as ep3A and ep4A. For these steady-state expert-users, their use of PCs has become interwoven into their modus operandi. They are continually expanding their base of knowledge and skills.

## 4.2. Declining users

Twenty one executives' competence has declined, as shown by the set of patterns in Fig. 3. The decline can vary from quite a shallow path, primarily in depth of use, path (A), to those for whom it is quite a sharp decline, paths (B) and (C), where there is a decrease in the frequency of use and the depth and the breadth of expertise; some have even become non-users. The two most common reasons for the initial interaction with a PC (en1) were peer pressure or the fact that use of a PC was necessary for the job. Initially their pattern of use is similar to steady-state users; however, after reaching a steady state (ep3), they now experience an encounter (en4) which causes them to decrease their depth and breadth of use.

C7 is typical of an executive who, although still a daily user, feels his overall frequency and breadth and depth of use has decreased path (A): “I use it less and less, but the decision support that I do (use PCs for) is actually more critical. . . . The sort of modelling I do now, it’s simpler and easier because by definition it’s higher level.”

He is responsible for MIS and has made it an organisational standard that everyone does his or her own word processing work unless it is something that is either difficult or needs to be very well presented: “. . . my chance of not using a PC in the office is zero.” In this case there is only a shallow decline, primarily in terms of the depth of use, (ep4A), and he has dropped from advanced to somewhere between intermediate and advanced. A culture whereby everyone produces his or her own short documents has acted as an encounter (en5A) which arrested his decline in competence.

There can be a very steep decline, path (C), as illustrated by executive M9. At one time a high-level user, producing his own spreadsheets, word processed documents, graphics, trend analysis and personally handling his e-mail, he is now at the bottom of a long episode (ep4C) of declining use resulting from a series of promotions and has become (en5C) a novice-user. The organisation has implemented some MIS systems that he needs to use and this may have acted as the encounter (en5C) to stop him becoming a non-user: “I found that as I move up the organisation (en4) I use my PC less and less (ep4C). Now I have what’s called a finance and IT account manager who is meeting my information needs. . . . It’s (personal use) still essential for some of my information needs and some of the systems which are computerised (en5C) (e.g. requisition authorisation), but its not a big part of the working day, and there will be days when I don’t use it all. . . . I have regrets. In a way I would have liked to remain computer literate, and I feel I’ve fallen behind.”

Overall, the way the declining users use has changed in terms of the tasks and software used is shown in Table 7. The main changes are related to the use of spreadsheets and presentation software. Whereas in the past these declining users would prepare all their own work, now they delegate to others.

The most common cause of a decline is a change in MIS culture, either from one where it is the norm for executives to use PCs to one where this is not the case, or a decrease in the level of available technology, as exemplified by M5: “I don’t use the e-mail system at M, having used e-mail in my previous company constantly. And the reason is that it is a lousy mail system, old technology … Technically a very robust system … but if you have an Excel spreadsheet you can’t incorporate that …”

For the majority of the 21 executives, the decline appeared permanent, there were no signs that they intended either to increase their expertise or use PCs in the future unless they had no choice. In a few cases, behavioural intentions suggest that executives would re-adopt PCs as personal productivity tools. M9 has had an extra telephone line installed at home: “... with a view to setting up an office at home. ... There are times I work over the weekend, and I want to find the time to get in to Powerpoint and understand it a bit better to pull my own presentation together.”

The theories of reasoned action $[1]$ suggest there is more likelihood that those who have such plans are more likely to restart using a PC as opposed to those who have no such stated plans.

## 4.3. Born-again users

In six cases, executives had either already started to re-use PCs or were in the processes of re-developing their PC skills. Clearly, some either will or have moved back to their previous level of competence as denoted by ep6A on path (A) in Fig. 4, whilst others will find a steady state position which is lower than before, as denoted by ep6B on path (B). One critical factor determining the level to which they re-build their expertise would seem to be the duration of the decline episode (ep5). Those for whom the episode was short are more likely to become users again.

D8 had worked with word processing, spreadsheets and e-mail since the 1980s. When she moved to organisation D and found she did not have these tools let alone a PC, “it was a bit of a shock” but she “quickly persuaded an IT vendor to give her one.” Whilst her decline in use was steep (ep4), the length of the ‘minimal use’ episode (ep5) was short and lasted

## 4.4. Growing users

These were executives who had only been using PCs as a personal productivity tool for eighteen months or less. They were still developing their competence and had not yet found their steady state. These growing users (Fig. 5) are still finding new ways of using PCs: they have not yet reached their steady state. The majority (73.3%) are over 40 and they are all in senior positions. They could probably continue to be non-users but have been motivated to learn to use PCs. In each case, the encounter that led to use of a computer was seeing what their peers and direct reports could achieve, and feeling in some way left out, as exemplified by D10, who hardly used a computer. Two years ago he recalled: “I’ve really got to crack this. . . . other people around me at managerial level were using them, so that was quite a powerful driver. It was competitive pressure (en1). I was in an environment where managers were quite adept at, you know, doing the things I do now, younger than me, people who worked for me. . . . And there was the whole element about not wanting to be seen, well afraid to show my ignorance.”

An episode (ep1) of low use and competence follows. “I didn’t have a PC but it (the course he attended) began to fill in gaps, and then I took the plunge and went out and bought a PC for home (en2) and just started using it, ... about an hour a day. And that was all about not using it for work at that stage (ep2).” ... “then when I came here I could actually see the practical application for work (en3).” In ep2, he started by producing letters, and spreadsheets for home finance, and a database of his wine cellar. Now, in his third learning phase (ep3), he produces most of his own documents, presentations and spreadsheets and accesses external databases via CD-ROMs. Essentially this episode is a learning curve rather than an equilibrium phase, with several minor episodes en route that prompted D10 to continue to develop his PC competence. At the other end of the scale, in terms of depth and breadth of use, is executive Q5. His main interaction with computers is e-mail and an in-house mainframe sales and marketing management information system that he queries but cannot update. Q5 is over 50 years old and has had a computer on his boat for 10 years for navigating; he used computers as an undergraduate, but never used them for work. “I always wanted to use it, but for a long time the kind of information I needed was not on the machines. ... When it was available and reliable enough, I took the step to have it on the computer (en1).”

## 5. Discussion

The question remains: Why some executives continue to use PCs whilst others (nearly a quarter of this sample) allow their competence to decline? A comparison of the declining and steady-state users' antecedents and specifically their Myers Briggs Type Inventory (MBTI) profiles, age and gender and previous computer experience suggested that certain MBTI types are more likely to become declining users, and males are more likely than females to become declining users. $\chi^{2}$ values were calculated and there were no statistically significant relationships between continuity of use and these antecedents.

Rather, the extent and continuity of use that executives make of computers is related to a more complex set of issues which includes their perception of their role(s), the organisational culture, and the associated demands and constraints within which they operate. Three qualitative differences emerged between the declining users, especially those whose use is now quite low, and other users, especially advanced and expert ones, as summarised in Table 9.

First, for most declining users the initial encounter was not proactive but reactive. Second, once they had started to use computers, it remained an adjunct, never becoming part of their modus operandi. Steady-state high-level users had apparently gone through ‘double loop’ learning $[3]$ . They had re-examined and modified their managerial behaviour as a result of their use of PCs. Moreover, this was a continuous process of evaluation and re-evaluation as their roles changed and new technology emerged. Conversely, the declining users gave the impression that their use of PCs was primarily an adjunct to their managerial style. Third, there was no sense that they had any fun when using PCs, while one expert steady-state user (Executive P1) said: “Technology brings such fantastic opportunities either to be more efficient or just to have fun.”

## 6. Conclusions

This study has shown that changes in executives' PC usage can be described by one of four distinct patterns, with episodes and encounters that shape them. With the pace of technology changing so rapidly, those who experience a decline in use are faced with severe skills loss. Consequently, they find themselves caught in the ‘competency trap’ [28].

The majority of the growing users were over forty; for several, this represented their first interaction with a computer. Their reasons for their first encounter with PCs are very similar to those of other users, although there would seem to be more emphasis on the need to demonstrate leadership plus peer pressure. Given their level of seniority, and hence authority, most could have continued to make minimal-to-no use of PCs, and delegate it to others. To some extent this is, therefore, contrary to the findings of previous authors, such as Grindley [17], who suggest that many of the current problems regarding the strategic use of MIS are due to older executives being less inclined to become involved with new technology.

Our study indicates that the use that senior executives make of PCs is related to a dynamic and complex interaction between both, internal factors, such as the executive's perception of his or her managerial roles, modus operandi and personality, and certain external factors, such as the MIS infrastructures, nature of work and organisational culture.

The main limitations of this study lie with the use of the interview method and qualitative interpretation of the data in terms of both, the researchers' personal ontological and epistemological bias [19] and the accuracy of the events and episodes being recalled by the interviewee [34]. It was not possible from this study to isolate the exact sequence of events which lead to changes in executives' use of PCs. As a consequence of rapid change and technological developments within the field, some outcomes may be transient. For example, at least fifteen executives spoke about the likely impact of voice-activated computers and felt that when this was available they would use PCs more.

By synthesising the epistemologies of management and MIS, we have provided a deeper understanding of the way in which executives use computers as management-support tools. The existing knowledge base has been extended to help provide an understanding of the executive as an end-user.

This research has several applications for practitioners. It provides guidelines on how to motivate executives to become more competent users. The results illustrate the importance of providing on-going end-user education and development, especially as new versions of software are implemented within organisations. The outcomes could be used to anticipate when a decline in use may occur and how it might be either pre-empted or minimised. Also highlighted are the cost and consequences to both, individuals and organisations of allowing executives' PC skills to deteriorate.
