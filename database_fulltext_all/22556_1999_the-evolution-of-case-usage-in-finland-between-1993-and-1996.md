# The evolution of CASE usage in Finland between 1993 and 1996

Jari Maansaari $^{*}$ , Juhani Iivari $^{1}$

Department of Information Processing Science, University of Oulu, P.O. Box 333, Linnanmaa, FIN-90571 Oulu, Finland

Accepted 23 December 1998

## Abstract

The purpose of this paper is to report the evolution of CASE adoption in Finland based on two comparable surveys, the first conducted in spring 1993 and the second in winter 1996/1997. The study uses a process model for innovation adoption, consisting of three stages: conditions for adopting and using CASE tools; adoption and using CASE tools; and consequences of adopting and using CASE tools. At each stage, the paper points out key aspects related to CASE adoption and studies their changes from 1993 to 1996. The analysis of key aspects reveals that not so much has changed in CASE adoption during the time between the two surveys, although the expectations concerning improvements to be brought about by the CASE technology have increased significantly. There is also increased emphasis on tool interfaces and the coverage of the development process both, in the criteria for selecting CASE tools and in performance evaluations. CASE usage has progressed very slowly, if at all. CASE tools are increasingly perceived to lead to intensified project and product standardization and higher end-user participation, but the two surveys did not find any significant change in the perceived impact of CASE technology on the productivity of development or in the quality of software products, and still less in other dimensions of work unit effectiveness. © 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. ## 1. Introduction

CASE (Computer Aided Systems/Software Engineering) tools have a history of almost 30 years. The first wave of CASE technology consisted of academic projects, such as CADIS [6], CASCADE [2], DIFO [13] and ISDOS [31]. It took almost 20 years before CASE technology really started to enter into practice, when the second generation CASE tools emerged in the late 1980s. Even though this second wave had more realistic goals in the sense of technical implementability and closer industrial focus, it was introduced with totally unrealistic expectations (see, e.g. [17]). This has led to a clear disappointment with CASE technology during the 1990s [26]. Yet, even though CASE has generated a great deal of hype, one of the tenets of the present paper is that CASE technology still forms a significant technology in IS development, because it is difficult to imagine extensive systems development without any computer tools to support documentation, configuration/version management, etc.

CASE adoption, in practice, has been the focus of a number of empirical studies in different countries since the late 1980s [1, 4, 7, 9, 11, 15, 19, 20, 21, 22, 24, 25, 28, 29, 32, 35]. Most of the above are snapshot surveys of the state of CASE adoption, however, and there is a serious paucity of longitudinal studies [8], to show the evolution of CASE adoption and to indicate possible future trends.

The purpose of this paper is to report the evolution of CASE adoption in Finland, based on two comparable surveys, the first of which was conducted in spring 1993 and the second in winter 1996/1997. The paper uses the framework of Orlikowski [21] and divides CASE adoption into three stages: conditions for adopting and using; adopting and using CASE tools; and consequences of adopting and using CASE tools. The paper points out key aspects related to CASE adoption at each stage and studies their change from 1993 to 1996. It also reports on the most notable associations between organisational factors and the variables studied.

The composition of the paper is the following. Section 2 explains the theoretical background and Section 3 introduces the two surveys. Section 4 reports on the results, and Section 5 discusses the major findings. Finally, Section 6 contains some concluding comments.

## 2. The CASE adoption process: a conceptual model

There are several process models for an innovation adoption ([14, 23, 27]), of which that of Orlikowski [21] is the most convenient for the present purpose. It is based on the assumption that the adoption and use of CASE tools should be conceptualised as a form of organisational change. Orlikowski argues that such a perspective allows us to anticipate, explain and evaluate different experiences and consequences following the introduction of CASE tools in organisations. She distinguishes four components in her model: the institutional context for adopting CASE and three stages of the adoption process: conditions for adopting and using CASE tools; adopting and using CASE tools; and the consequences of adopting and using CASE tools. The institutional context comprises the environmental, organisational and the IS contexts. This paper picks out some key aspects related to each component and attempts to reveal the changes that have occurred in these from 1993 to 1996. In the case of institutional context, it focuses on the organisational and IS contexts. The main portions of the framework proposed by Orlikowski [21] and the key aspects studied in the present paper are depicted in Fig. 1.

The organisational profiles in Fig. 1 comprise factors such as industry, size of the organisation, size of its IS function, size of typical projects and CASE experience. In accordance with Orlikowski [21], the first stage in the adoption process in Fig. 1 is labelled ‘Conditions for adopting and using CASE tools’. In this stage, IS managers’ actions – articulating a problem and formulating a solution – lead them to acquire a particular set of CASE tools and put them into use in systems development. Key aspects studied are problems affecting the development process prior to CASE adoption, expectations regarding improvements to be brought about by CASE and the importance of the various criteria used to evaluate and select CASE tools. The problems affecting the development process and expectations with regard to CASE technology are taken directly from $[21]$ and can be seen as the ‘driving forces’ of the adoption process, the4reby helping management to justify its acquisition of CASE tools.

An organisation ‘enters’ the second stage, ’Adopting and using CASE tools’, as soon as it actually acquires these tools. “Having acquired CASE tools, IS managers may take action such as changing IS policies, practices, operations and relations with clients” [21]. The key aspects at this stage are: the performance of selected CASE tools, introduction strategies, changes in work practices, introduction problems and the actual CASE usage.

“Actions regarding adoption and use, in turn, result in various perceptions, experiences and outcomes as the key players – IS managers, system developers and clients – act on and react to the changes” [21]. This stage is labelled ‘Consequences of adopting and using CASE tools’. The key aspects studied are productivity/quality impacts and impacts on aspects of the software development process.

## 3. Research design

This section describes the research method, namely the questionnaires, sampling, and response rates and the statistical methods used in this paper.

## 3.1. The questionnaire and statistical methods

A structured questionnaire with 47 sets of questions was used. This was divided into three sections covering CASE tool adoption, CASE tool usage and the impacts of a CASE tool. There were also some questions intended to profile organisations and respondents. The total number of questions was about 250. The questionnaire of CASE'96 was a slightly extended version of the one used in 1993. Minor changes included grouping of the questions and a few additional questions. The additions comprised about 50 questions.

The idea of the questionnaire was to employ existing validated measures as far as these were available. In addition to implementation innovation research $[18]$ and research into organisational assessment $[33]$ , the questions were heavily derived from earlier CASE studies, especially surveys by Wijers and van Dort $[34]$ and Aaen et al. $[1]$ . A five-point Likert scale was used in the questions concerning respondents' subjective opinions or judgements. Otherwise, ordinal scales with various ranges were applied. Each questionnaire issued was supplemented with the name of the organisation and the brand(s) of the CASE tool(s) used.

The focus of the present paper lies at the level of organisation rather than that of the individual respondent. Respondents' answers from each organisation are aggregated to the organisational level using the SPSS aggregate-function. The means presented in this paper are calculated from the organisational level answers. Differences between the samples are tested using either the $\chi^{2}$ -test or the Mann–Whitney U-test, depending on the type of variable. The tables, have p-values <0.1 in italics. The Kruskal–Wallis (KW) one-way analysis of variance is used to compare the distributions of variables in two or more groups determined by an organisational factor. Before using the KW-test, two samples are merged and some of the original groups of the organisational factors to be tested are combined in order to increase the numbers of cases in the groups to be tested. The combined classes are described in Table 2.

## 3.2. The samples

The CASE'93 survey was carried out in spring 1993. Based on customer information supplied by CASE tool vendors operating in Finland and on previous Finnish studies of CASE adoption, a list of organisations was identified. After telephone contacts, 52 organisations were found to be co-operative. The questionnaires were mailed to the contact persons, who distributed them within their organisations. The contact persons were mainly IS managers. Of the 322 questionnaires mailed, 109 were returned from 35 organisations. The questionnaires were returned directly. Four of the returned questionnaires were rejected. As a result, the final response rate was 33% at the level of questionnaires and 67% at the level of organisations. The CASE'96 survey was carried out in winter 1996/1997, much in the same way as that of 1993. Based on the customer information from CASE tool vendors operating in Finland and previous studies of CASE adoption, an initial list of 139 organisations was drawn up. The survey was addressed to 87 organisations after 52 had been excluded because of failure to obtain their contact information, or the discovery that they did not use CASE tools or were not interested in participating. The questionnaires were mailed to contact persons, who distributed them within the organisations. The respondents returned the completed questionnaires directly. Of the 420 questionnaires mailed, 63 were returned from 44 organisations. As a result, the final response rate was $15\%$ at the level of questionnaires and $50\%$ at the level of organisations. The profiles of the 63 respondents are depicted in Table 1.

The response rate in the CASE'96 survey was much lower than that of the CASE'93 survey, although the only clear difference between them was the slightly extended questionnaire. One can imagine two additional reasons to for this: the timing of the second survey in (November–December), and the decrease in general interest in CASE technology [26]. By comparison with earlier CASE surveys, for example [1], with a response rate of 35% at the level of organisations, and [34], with a response rate of 29% at the organisational level, the present response rates can be regarded as acceptable.

As seen in Table 1, $70\%$ of the respondents in the CASE'96 survey classified themselves as project managers or analysts. Practically speaking, all the respondents had at least a college education. The respondents' CASE experience had increased when measured in years $(\chi^2 \rightarrow p = 0.0006; \text{mean}_{93} = 2.39, \text{mean}_{96} = 2.64)$ , but had remained the same in terms of projects completed $(\chi^2 \rightarrow p = 0.4)$ , even though the majority of the respondents $(54\%)$ used the tool at least weekly.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Institutional context for adopting and using CASE tools

The profiles of the participating organisations in both these surveys are presented in Table 2, which suggests that the profiles of the CASE'93 and CASE'96 organisations can be regarded as homogeneous. The respondent organisations were relatively large by Finnish standards, but the average size of software development project team was relatively small, typically comprising 3–10 persons. The most common tools in both the surveys were Oracle, IEW/ADW/Key and Prosa. IEW's disappearance from the 1996 sample was partly compensated for by the popularity of Key. Select OMT is the only new tool, which has gained popularity. All the others were used by less than four organisations in both these surveys. As Table 2 indicates, organisations in the of 1993 survey had an average of 1.7 CASE tools, and in 1996 an average of 1.5 CASE tools. The idea of this paper is to focus on perceptions concerning CASE technology rather than individual CASE tools. An organisation may employ several CASE tools which together provide a certain functionality and the necessary interfaces and methodology support (see the performance criteria in Section 4.3).

## 4.2. Conditions for adopting and using CASE tools

## 4.2.1. Problems

Conditions in the organisation/unit and its development process before CASE adoption were measured using 13 variables (Table 3). The means in both surveys and the statistical significance of the difference between the samples are presented on each dimension. Only one significant difference was found: the productivity of application developers, which was perceived to be a more serious problem in 1996 by a margin of 0.33 units.

Perceptions of the quality of software products and their documentation were significantly affected by the industry (p = 0.08 and 0.05, respectively) in that respondents at software houses and in insurance/finance felt the quality of software products and their documentation to be better than those in public administration and to some extent also in manufacturing. The industry concerned was also associated with the extent to which the heterogeneity of software development environments was perceived as a problem (p = 0.03), insurance/finance reporting the lowest heterogeneity and the public sector the highest. Industry also affected perceptions concerning the achievement of goals (p = 0.02), innovativeness (p = 0.08) and reputation for quality (p = 0.002). These are all dimensions of a more general concept, namely organisational effectiveness [33]. Respondents in public administration, and to some extent also in manufacturing, perceived the effectiveness to be lower than those in software houses or in insurance/finance companies. Organisational size did not affect any of the problem dimensions in Table 3, but IS size was associated with perceptions of the quality of software products (p = 0.05) and their documentation (p = 0.04) respondents in the smallest IS units perceiving the lowest quality. The size of the software projects affected problems associated with maintenance (p = 0.04), with small projects understandably entailing the least maintenance problems.

## 4.3.3. Changes in working practices

The CASE adoption process brought with it changes of varying magnitude in working practices (Table 9). Only one organisation in each survey reported that no changes had occurred. The most notable changes concerned analysis and design methods which had changed significantly (options 4 and 5) in $80\%$ of the organisations in the CASE'96 survey. At the same time, over half of the organisations reported that there were no changes at all or only minor changes (options 1 and 2) in working practices related to management and organisation. Table 9 also shows that the changes in working practices reported in 1996 were generally perceived as more significant than those in 1993. Statistically significant differences between the samples were found in analysis/design methods (mean difference $0.44$ , $p \leq 0.05$ ) and in implementation techniques ( $0.91$ , $p \leq 0.001$ ).

There were only two significant associations between organisational factors (industry, organisational size, IS size, average project size) and changes in working practices. Both, industry and organisational size were associated with changes in analysis and design methods (p = 0.04 and p = 0.03, respectively). Respondents in the public sector reported the most significant changes and respondents in software houses the least significant ones. The changes were perceived as most significant in medium-sized organisations (101–500 employees) and least significant in large organisations (>500 employees).

## 4.3.4. Introduction problems

No significant difficulties in the CASE introduction process were reported in either surveys (Table 10) but the respondents in general reported slightly more severe problems in 1996 than in 1993. Three statistically significant differences were found between the two surveys. Installation problems had increased by 0.70 ( $p \leq 0.01$ ), problems in the functioning of the software by 0.47 ( $p \leq 0.05$ ) and problems related to learning the tools by 0.33 ( $p \leq 0.10$ ). The learning problems may reflect the fact that the functionality of the tools has increased since 1993 (see Table 6 above), may have increased their overall complexity.

Perceived problems in the functioning of the software and the adoption of new methods differed significantly between industries (p = 0.05 and p = 0.04, respectively), with software houses experiencing the least problems in both the cases. Consistent with changes in analysis and design methods, respondents from the public sector reported the most serious problems in adopting new methods. Organisational size was not significantly associated with any of the dimensions of introduction problems, but IS size and average project size were associated with perceived problems in responsibility delegation (p = 0.09 and p = 0.07, respectively) and problems of user resistance (p = 0.06 and p = 0.05, respectively). Small IS units (1–25 employees) reported the least severe problems in both the cases, and medium-sized units (26–100 employees) the most severe ones. The perceived severity of problems increased with the average size of projects in both the cases, and average project size also affected perceived problems in motivation towards the work $p = 0.09$ . Here again, the severity of the motivation problem was perceived to increase as the projects grew in size.

## 4.4. Consequences of adopting and using CASE tools

4.4.1. Changes in aspects of the system-development process caused by CASE

The adoption of CASE tools caused changes in certain aspects of the system development process. As indicated in Table 12, their use of has significantly increased the standardisation of both projects and products. Somewhat contradictory to Table 13, the respondents were of the opinion that CASE usage had reduced the duration of projects. Three significant changes were found between the two years, the respondents in 1996 sample estimating that CASE implied more project standardisation, more product standardisation and more end-user participation than those in 1993.

The only significant association between organisational factors and changes in the systems development process was between average project size and project standardisation $p = 0.08$ , the respondents representing from organisations with the smallest projects reporting by far the least changes in this respect.

## 4.4.2. Productivity and quality effects

According to Table 13, the strongest impact of CASE at the organisational level concerns the documentation of software products and the use of standards. On the other hand, there is no support for the claim that CASE improves the organisations or units competitive position. The argument that CASE clearly speeds up the development of new applications is also rejected, even though CASE is seen to slightly shorten the duration of project (Table 12). Tables 12 and 13 suggest that CASE tools are less perceived as speeding up development, the difference in Table 13 being almost significant at the level of $p \leq 0.10$ .

Table 13 also indicates that the quality effects of CASE (documentation, product quality, process quality) clearly surpass both, the productivity effects (productivity of developers, speed of the development) and the work unit effects (attainment of goals, improved morale, improved reputation). Large variances (not reported here) in the impact perceptions in both surveys denote that there was no clear consensus on the impacts of CASE. This also explains why none of the quite large differences in mean values emerged as statistically significant.

There were only two significant differences in perceived impacts between the respondents in the public sector, reporting reduced cost of software development considerably more than respondents those from other industries, and those in finance or insurance considerably less, the significance between branches of industry being p = 0.06. Respondents at software houses, and somewhat surprisingly those in the public sector as well, perceived markedly greater improvement in their competitive position than did those in manufacturing, the significance of differences between the industries being p = 0.02. Organisational size was associated only with the impact on competitive position (p = 0.06), respondents in small organisations reporting the greatest improvement and those in largest organisations the least. The size of the IS unit had statistically significant effects on five dimensions, functionality of software (p = 0.05), cost of software development (p = 0.005), quality of software (0.06), maintenance costs (0.08) and reputation for excellence of work (p = 0.07). Rather surprisingly, respondents in medium-sized IS units (26–100 employees) perceived all these effects by far the lowest. Even though the respondents in the smallest organisations gave the highest rankings on all five dimensions, the differences between the small and large IS units were in most cases fairly marginal.

## 5. Discussion

One of the surprising findings in the present comparison is that so little had changed in CASE adoption during the periods between the CASE'93 and CASE'96 surveys and the joint Finnish/Danish survey [1]. Not so surprisingly, the profiles of the participating organisations in the two Finnish surveys were very much identical and also comparable with those of the survey by Aaen et al. [1]. Most CASE user organisations were found to be fairly large in terms of their total number of employees and their number of IS employees, whereas the average size of projects was quite small, typically from three to five employees. Organisations' CASE experience measured in years has increased since the survey by Aaen et al. [1] and the majority of the enterprises in both of the later surveys has been using CASE for more than two years. However, CASE experience was more uniformly distributed in the 1996 survey than in that of 1993.

## 5.1. Conditions for adopting and using CASE tools

The problems prior to CASE adoption remained much the same from 1993 to 1996 and can be best characterised as classical problems of poor documentation of software products and low productivity of software development and maintenance. Otherwise, the organisations were perceived as being relatively healthy. Their effectiveness in terms of morale, achievement of goals, reputation for excellence and innovation [33] was considered to be fairly good.

In both the surveys, CASE tools were expected to bring improvements primarily in working procedures and in use of standards, thereby enhancing the quality of processes and products. The present results also support the findings of Aaen et al. [1] that CASE tools are expected to contribute significantly to the qualitative aspects of the development process and the deliverables produced, but less significantly to increased productivity. In view of the general disappointment with CASE technology [26], it is somewhat surprising that overall expectations had significantly increased from 1993 to 1996. The reasons behind this lie beyond the scope of the present survey, however.

The main selection criteria identified by Aaen et al. [1] were also valued among the highest in the two surveys reported in this paper. These criteria were: reliability of the tool; its support for various phases of the software life cycle; user friendliness; quality of documentation; quality of the diagrams produced; the vendor support and reliability; and the central data dictionary. Two of the four significant changes between the surveys were associated with tool interfaces (DBMS and code-generators), indicating that tools are expected to be more open in the future. This is also supported by the fact that traditional development environments are also extending towards the design phase, thereby making more open CASE tools inevitable. Microsoft's decision to include a repository, that allows developers to store design information in its development products [5] is a good example of this trend. On the other hand, the significance of price and hardware requirements has decreased considerably since the survey by Aaen et al. [1]. Quite surprisingly, compatibility with earlier analysis and design methods and implementation techniques was not considered important, although some studies have found that method compatibility supports the adoption process. Sumner [30] found resistance from system developers and a lack of fit with current methodology to be major barriers to the introduction of CASE, while according to Alavi [3], organisations where CASE methodology is compatible with existing development practices are more likely to favour the introduction of CASE technology. The insignificance of project management support as a selection criterion suggests that CASE tools are acquired purely as a production technology in the sense of Henderson and Cooprider [10], omitting their role as a collaboration and organisational technology. An open question is whether the insignificance of the support for project management is an outcome of the poor support of this kind attributed to current CASE tools in the present study or whether the low general level of interest in project management support explains the poor support in existing CASE tools.

## 5.2. Adopting and using CASE tools

The respondents gave very positive evaluations of the performance of their CASE tools with respect to check capabilities, quality of diagrams, development phases supported and the central data dictionary in both the surveys, and also in that by Aaen et al. [1]. Code-generator interfaces were assessed to have been considerably enhanced since the work of Aaen et al. [1] and the multi-user capabilities of the tools were also enhanced and the coverage of life cycle phases increased. This indicates that CASE tools had greater functionality by 1996 than at the beginning of the decade and explains the finding that the respondents perceived the learning of CASE tools to take more time in 1996 than in 1993. The significant changes in the performance of CASE tools seemed to correspond closely to those in the importance of the various selection criteria. This suggests that these have developed in close contact: either CASE suppliers have managed to respond to changes in user preferences or the changes in preferences reflect the actual evolution of CASE tool functionality.

The most typical introduction strategy was to use pilot projects in which either all or some of the tool's functions were assessed. An introduction strategy, in which some of the CASE tool's functions were adopted in all projects, had also gained some popularity (19 percent) since 1993. Neither organisational factors nor usage-related factors explained the increased popularity of this introduction strategy, however. The introduction phase for a tool lasted 0–24 months, the average being four months.

The CASE adoption process was associated with changes of varying magnitude in working practices, generally more so in 1996 than in 1993. Analysis and design methods were changed most of all and working practices related to the organisation and its management the least. Iivari and Maansaari [12] found that the adoption of CASE tools as reported in the CASE'96 survey was associated with a considerable redistribution of the methods used. This suggests that the adoption of a new development method and the acquisition of a compatible CASE tool often took place together and is supported by the insignificance of compatibility with earlier analysis/design methods and implementation techniques as selection criteria. The most significant change between the two surveys concerned implementation techniques, which is consistent with the finding that interfaces with code-generators were perceived to be significantly enhanced in 1996 relative to the situation in 1993. The modest changes in working practices related to management and organisation remind us that the adoption of CASE tools was expected to affect primarily the technical side of the development process. It is often emphasised in the literature that the adoption of a CASE tool also has pronounced social and organisational repercussions, so that changes in these areas are often considered important for the successful introduction of CASE ([16, 21]).

No significant problems associated with tool introduction were reported. Problems in adopting new methods were ranked highest in 1993 and the installation problems in 1996, while problems related to tool installation and its learning increased significantly between the surveys. This can be considered a consequence of the fact that tools were perceived in 1996 to include more functionality than those in use in 1993 and, therefore, to be more difficult to master.

According to previous research, a long time elapses in most organisations from buying a CASE tool to its extensive use. In the Dutch CASE-user survey conducted in spring 1992 [15], the majority of the organisations (53 percent) had had CASE more than two years, but in 62 percent of them less than half of the analysts actually used the tool. Aaen et al. [1] report that, in the majority of enterprises (56–80 percent) up to 25 percent of the analysts used the tool on a regular basis, but only in a small minority (8 percent) did more than 75 percent of the analysts did so. The diffusion of CASE technology was also found to be slow in the surveys studied here, so that 69 percent of the organisations in 1993 and 57 percent in 1996 had <25 percent of their developers using CASE tools. The tools were used primarily in the analysis and design phases but the proportion of organisations using them for maintenance increased from 46 to 61 percent. The problems related to installation and learning discussed here may explain the slow progress in the actual use of CASE tools.

## 5.3. Consequences of adopting and using CASE tools

Increased project and product standardisation were perceived as the most conspicuous changes in the systems development process, a finding which is consistent with earlier ones ([1, 15, 34]). The respondents in the CASE'96 survey were of the opinion that both the projects and the products were more standardised as a consequence of CASE introduction than those in the CASE'93 survey. The result is in agreement with the fact that the CASE was increasingly perceived as providing a common development environment. The respondents in 1996 also saw the introduction of CASE as implying more end-user participation than did those in 1993.

CASE tools are claimed to be the means for increasing the productivity of systems development and the quality of the developed systems (products). Because of the complex relationships between CASE tools, development productivity and product quality, there is no definite evidence for the foregoing claims. In fact, the results of previous surveys on productivity and quality effects can be considered somewhat contradictory ([1, 15, 19]). One explanation may be that CASE usage has not been taken into account explicitly when studying CASE impacts, so that it is assumed that either CASE tools are used when acquired (an assumption which is repeatedly found not to be generally valid) or CASE tools lead to increased productivity, even though they are not used. Iivari [11], when taking actual CASE usage into account, found it to have a strong positive impact on the perceived quality of software products and a less significant positive impact on productivity, Guinan et al. [9] point out that there are a number of ways of viewing the impact of CASE tools on IS development performance. Based on data from a large survey in the United States, they suggest that a CASE tool may be abandoned when its positive impacts occur in areas that are less visible to developers and managers (who make the primary decisions as to whether a tool is successful or not). The data collected in the two surveys reported on here also indicate that there is no clear consensus on the impacts of CASE, so that perceived productivity and quality gains differed significantly between organisations. The large variations in perceptions concerning productivity and quality impacts may be explained by the variation in CASE usage, and, in turn, serve to explain why no statistically significant differences in productivity and quality effects were found between the surveys.

## 6. Conclusion

Rapidly expanding attention was paid to CASE technology in Finland during the late 1980s, as CASE tools of the second wave began to appear on the market. According to data collected in spring 1993, 94 percent of organisations had acquired CASE tools between the years 1988 and 1993. For this reason, CASE technology can still be considered a relatively new technological innovation, despite its history of almost thirty years. It is, therefore, justified to assume that perceptions of the technology itself, its impacts, etc. will change as the area matures. There is, nevertheless, a paucity of research into the evolution of CASE adoption [8]. The purpose of the present paper was to analyse some aspects of CASE adoption and reveal possible changes from 1993 to 1996.

Surprisingly, the results indicate only a few changes between the two surveys, although expectations concerning the improvements to be brought about by CASE technology had increased significantly in the interval, and an increased emphasis was being placed on extended tool interfaces and the coverage of development process, both in selection criteria and in performance evaluations. The introduction of CASE tools was also found to be associated with significantly more changes in analysis and design methods and implementation techniques in the CASE'96 survey than in the CASE'93 survey. The introduction problems were increasingly problems in installation and learning. CASE usage progressed very slowly, if at all, however. CASE tools are increasingly perceived as leading to increased project and product standardisation and end-user participation, but the two surveys did not find any significant evolution in the perceived productivity of development or in the quality of software products, and still less in other dimensions of work unit effectiveness.
