# An exploratory field study of differences in information technology use between more- and less-innovative middle managers

Tor J. Larsen $^{a,*}$ , James C. Wetherbe $^{1,b}$

$^{a}$ Norwegian School of Management, P.O. Box 580, 1301, Sandvika, Norway

$^{b}$ Center for Cycle Time Research, Fogelman College of Business, University of Memphis, Memphis, TN, USA

Accepted 22 January 1999

## Abstract

Based on data collected in 1988, this research explored the differences between more- and less-innovative middle managers in their use of information and information technology (IT). It involved a field study of ninety-nine middle managers in a division of a major defense contracting manufacturing organization in the St. Paul/Minneapolis area of the USA. Results suggest that the more-innovative middle managers are inclined to use data drawn from personal experience and insights rather than historical data and found IT generally important. Apparently, innovative middle managers use less complex programs, and when in-depth analyses are needed, their execution was generally delegated. The prime interpretation of the results is that innovative middle managers use IT as a vehicle for networking. They distribute more of their IT output to others. Even though they do not use IT more, they frequently contact local and expert support functions. These findings indicate that future research efforts should address the issues of how managerial business and IT innovativeness are related to IT use and networking behaviors. © 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Middle managers; Innovativeness; Information-technology innovation; Information-technology change; Information-technology use; Information use; End-user computing

## 1. Introduction

Managers' use of information and information technology (IT) is a recurring theme in the literature [8, 23, 33]. Although many attempts have been made, no study has directly addressed the question: Do more-innovative middle managers use information and IT differently than their less-innovative peers?

The increased competition that business organizations face today puts emphasis on their ability to innovate, and middle managers in a line organization play a vital role in this process $[2, 3, 9, 16]$ . Insight into the differences in information and IT use between more- and less-innovative middle managers may assist business organizations in developing more effective policies and practices that would be of value to their various user segments; knowledge about the differences between more- and less-innovative middle managers might also highlight particularly effective usage patterns.

## 2. Research issues and theory

Innovative managers have the ability to direct their attention to activities and phenomena that have organizational value $[16, 31]$ . Consequently, this study treated the degree of innovativeness (the terms ‘more-and less-innovative’ and ‘degree of innovativeness’ are used interchangeably) among managers as the independent variable, the degree of perceived information and IT importance as intermediate variables, and aspects of information and IT use as the dependent variables. Individual, job, and organizational factors that have been commonly found to explain variations in information and IT use were included as controls. The detailed research model is shown in Fig. 1.

It was developed as a result of the following discussion and hypotheses.

## 2.1. Identification of more- and less-innovative middle managers

The literature discusses concepts that may identify and describe the characteristics of innovative individuals $[13]$ : it is assumed that innovative people present good new ideas that lead to implemented changes $[1, 13, 16, 31]$ . If the more-innovative persons are those who present new ideas that lead to change, some researchers have argued that peer identification would be the least biased variable $[26, 27]$ . A positive attitude toward change has also been found to be related to innovativeness $[6, 10, 11, 21]$ .

Based on these considerations, the middle managers' degree of innovativeness was measured in three ways. First, attitude toward change was measured through a ten-item scale developed by Ettlie [10] and O'Keefe and Ettlie [11]. Second, the number of implemented innovations was gaged through a self-report on the number of implemented changes the respondent had forwarded over the last three years that required the consent of the respondent's supervisor (the objective of the study was to investigate changes that had impact beyond improvements on the individual level.) Business and IT innovations were measured separately. To increase reliability, respondents were asked to give a short verbal description of each idea for innovation they had forwarded and indicate whether it was implemented or not. Third, organizational recognition was tapped through the number of times peers nominated a person who they perceived had forwarded new ideas that were implemented. A factor analysis indicated that attitude toward change, self report of number of implemented changes, and peer rating of middle managers responsible for implemented change were closely related. Variables representing networking behavior [5] among middle managers loaded on two separate factors. [These analyses are documented in Larsen, 1993 [18].] Since attitude toward change, number of implemented innovations, and organizational recognition loaded on a common factor, each variable was normalized to a scale ranging from 0 to 1 and the final score calculated as the sum of the scores for each of the three variables. The strength of this approach is that it combines commonly recognized approaches to conceptualization and measurement of the degree of innovativeness and, hence, counteracts the theoretical and operationalizational weaknesses in using only one of these measures.

## 2.2. The perceived importance of IT

IT has become a crucial element to ensure organizational adaption to a rapidly changing business environment. Innovative managers are the most likely group of employees to understand this link.

H1.1. In general, more-innovative middle managers will perceive IT as more important than will less-innovative middle managers.

Considering IT support of individual managerial tasks, it was assumed that the innovative middle managers will choose those aspects of IT that benefit them most. The three main areas of IT activity in this study were hands-on use of IT, delegation of IT tasks, and use of support for help and IT-related discussion.

## H1.2: More-innovative middle managers will attach greater importance to the IT resources they use than less-innovative middle managers will.

(A) More-innovative middle managers will perceive the importance of the applications they use ‘hands-on’ as greater than the less-innovative middle managers.

(B) More-innovative middle managers will perceive the importance of the indirect computing done for them as greater than the less-innovative middle managers.

2.3. Innovative middle managers' use of information and IT

Information and IT use span a wide field. Since this investigation was exploratory, the objective was to analyze many modes of use. Previous taxonomies of end-users $[25]$ were regarded as inappropriate, since usage differences between more- and less-innovative middle managers may occur in many areas. The researchers developed a hierarchy of dimensions of use; it is not intended as a theoretical foundation for categorization of use but a practical tool for differentiating among aspects of use and their relationships.

## 2.3.1. Information use

Only two dimensions of information use were considered here: its time dimension and the degree of formality. The first might be particularly important to innovative middle managers. The introduction and implementation of change implies a concern for future events: innovative middle managers presumably plan for the future more than other middle managers $[7]$ .

H2.1.A. More-innovative middle managers will use more data about future events.

H2.1.B. Less-innovative middle managers will use more historic data.

In view of their orientation toward data about future events, innovative middle managers might draw their data from personal experience and insights rather than from documents and files. For the need for formalization, the hypothesis was forwarded.

H2.2. The innovative middle managers will use more data drawn from personal experience and insights than the less-innovative ones.

2.3.2. IT support of phases of information processing Using time as the delineator, information processing may be divided into four phases.

## 2.3.3. Information capture

Gochenouer [14] found that successful managers were not extensive users of IT, and even when IT was employed, they used subjective information. The not-so-innovative middle managers may find that the data they need are already stored in databases. If so, they may copy most of it. If a computer system does not provide easy access (because of programming delays, etc.) or does not contain the necessary data, the less-innovative middle manager may have to rely on reports and manual files.

H3.1. More- and less-innovative middle managers will employ different methods of data capture.

(A) More-innovative middle managers will key in their own data to a larger extent than will less-innovative middle managers.

(B) Less-innovative middle managers will use formal data copied from databases more often than will more-innovative middle managers.

(C) Less-innovative middle managers will copy data from reports and files more often than will more-innovative middle managers.

## 2.3.4. Information analysis

In areas where ready made IT-procedures are not available, users may create their own programs or algorithms to detect or confirm basic trends in the data. Todd and Benbasat [29] found that “… subjects behaved as if effort minimization was an important consideration” when studying manual versus DSS use. Consequently, innovative middle managers may use IT for analytical purposes with smaller and less complex self made programs than their colleagues.

H3.2.A. Innovative middle managers create smaller programs.

H3.2.B. Innovative middle managers create less complex programs.

## 2.3.5. Information enhancement

In view of the fact that communication is an important aspect of social interaction, it would seem reasonable to assume that innovative managers are more concerned with the quality of their IT output during networking activities.

H3.3. More-innovative middle managers will perceive high-quality IT output as more important than will less- innovative middle managers.

## 2.3.6. Information communication

The IT output from innovative middle managers will often be presented and read by others rather than for personal means $[35]$ .

H3.4. More-innovative middle managers will perceive their peers, supervisor, subordinates, and people in other organizational units as receivers of IT output more often than will less-innovative middle managers.

## 2.3.7. Mode of IT use

Usually, managers can decide whether to execute IT tasks themselves or delegate them to secretaries or specialists $[19]$ . Often, managers perceive the delegation of tasks more important than their own hands-on use. Additionally, managers generally delegate IT tasks more frequently. This might be particularly relevant for analytical tasks that require more elaborate IT use.

H4.1.A. More-innovative middle managers will execute their own in-depth analyses less often than will less-innovative middle managers.

H4.1.B. More-innovative middle managers will delegate their in-depth analyses to subordinates or staff specialists more often than less-innovative middle managers.

It has been argued that innovators are concerned with the enhancement of output. Small tasks may need quite simple improvement of the output if the objective is to allow informal discussion of an idea or its consequences. In contrast, formal presentations may require quite an elaborate and extensive preparation and enhancement to develop presentations and reports. Computer graphics may be an important support tool. Perhaps innovative middle managers use graphics packages ‘hands-on’ and delegate such tasks more.

H4.2.A. More-innovative middle managers will execute business graphics ‘hands-on’ more often than will less-innovative middle managers.

H4.2.B. More-innovative middle managers will delegate business graphics tasks more often than will less-innovative middle managers.

Since innovative middle managers may distribute more of their output to others, they may utilize e-mail more.

H4.3.A. More-innovative middle managers will be more frequent hands-on users of e-mail than less-innovative middle managers.

H4.3.B. More-innovative middle managers will delegate e-mail tasks more often than will less-innovative middle managers.

## 2.3.8. IT arrangements

As the use of IT depends on availability, our research focused on the distribution of work-stations (PCs, terminals, or other similar type of equipment) in the middle-management population. In organizations where there is <100 per cent distribution, managers of the organizations may be able to decide on the distribution of the equipment, although individual employees may have some influence over work-station placement.

We propose that managers have some influence over hardware installations and, if IT use is important, they will make it directly available. Conversely, if innovative managers do not perceive IT use to be important, they will have less local work-stations. Innovative managers are pressed for time. Equipment that is shared is often in use by others. Time constraints in a shared work-station environment may create access and usage problems. Therefore, if more-innovative managers find IT use important they may have the equipment on their desks to a greater extent than is the case with their less-innovative colleagues.

H5.A. More-innovative middle managers who perceive IT as important will have work-stations installed more often than less-innovative middle managers.

H5.B. More-innovative middle managers who do not perceive IT as important will have work-stations installed less often than less-innovative middle managers.

## 2.3.9. Use of support

When line managers need help or want to discuss IT related issues, they are inclined to contact local non-experts (e.g. secretaries and peers) rather more than distant experts. The suggestion that innovative managers do not spend much time on elaborate IT tasks may mean that they use IT experts less.

H6. More-innovative middle managers will use support sources differently than less-innovative middle managers.

(A) More-innovative middle managers will use support functions more frequently than will less-innovative middle managers.

(B) More-innovative middle managers will use local support (peers, secretaries or other non-computer experts) more often than will less-innovative middle managers.

(C) More-innovative middle managers will use expert support functions less often than will less-innovative middle managers.

## 2.4. Other factors that may explain use

## 2.4.1. Individual factors

Research shows that age has a negative, education a positive, and time with the company (tenure) no relation to the use of IT [5].

## 2.4.2. Job factors

Job characteristics include the nature of the job and its hierarchical position. Whether the job influences the incumbent or the incumbent the job $[24]$ , researchers have identified task difficulty (analyzability and predictability) and task variability (the number of exceptions encountered in the work) as being among the most influential factors in determining the nature of the job $[17, 32]$ . Possibly, a more predictable job that contains many analytical tasks may have a higher potential for IT support, although use of mainframes may be more appropriate for such tasks. In a situation where exceptions are common, managers may use a greater variety of IT tools $[12]$ . However, they may not have time to develop IT support or find relevant available data $[22]$ .

The degree of task difficulty and job variability may not be the same for all managers: it probably varies with organizational level—the higher the level, the less predictable, and the task is more likely to be concerned with strategic issues. Additionally, higher level managers have traditionally been less frequent users of IT [4, 20], though the increased use of executive information systems among middle managers may modify this behavior [28, 34].

## 2.4.3. Organizational factors

Radical change creates a different organizational situation than steady evolution. In this research, a steady rate of innovation was the norm, controlled through the careful selection of collection sites.

## 3. Research method

The investigation used a field study design. The objective was to understand innovativeness and IT use in a line management context. Managers below the level of divisional executive officer and above the level of foreman were labeled middle managers. A supervisory role, as is often the case with foremen, was judged insufficient for inclusion. The middle managers worked with traditional planning and control activities but they also took active part in changing product and production processes, quality assurance, contract bidding, and administrative procedures. There was concern that non-innovative middle managers or managers who do not use IT might not respond to a request for data.

A division within a major defense contracting company in the Minneapolis/St. Paul, MN, USA, was asked to participate in the study. A prerequisite for the selection was that the division had not experienced any radical or drastic IT change over the last three years. The division's main tasks were marketing, contract bidding, production planning, production, and distribution of finished goods. Sales in 1987 were $\approx$ 200 million and had tripled since 1984. The division had experienced steady and continuous growth. The number of employees had increased approximately at the same rate, and there were employees of every age.

The division began using PCs in 1982, and the number of work-stations had increased steadily until approximately half of the management population had work-stations on their desks at the time of the data collection (1988). Managers used many different IT applications and many support functions were in place. IT use and support had grown rapidly. Development in the main business area, as well as in computing, could be characterized as rapid but steady.

In all, 130 middle managers were identified as candidates for participation. Of these, 20 managers took part in instrument development and the remaining 110 in data collection. The average age of the managers was 46.3 years. They had, on average, 24.9 years work experience, had worked 18.2 years for the company, and 10.8 years in the division.

In view of the relatively large number of respondents, a questionnaire was developed as the main vehicle for data collection. The instrument was tested in several steps. After a preliminary pre-test and discussion among colleagues of the researchers, four managers in staff positions in the participating division were asked to respond to the questionnaire while researchers were present. Finally, 20 managers who were candidates for the real data collection, were selected randomly to participate in a pilot study. The pilot participants were asked to give written comments, and the results were used for control so that answers to questions used at least 50 percent of the scales. Pilot participants were omitted from the final data collection, and pilot questionnaire data were not included in the final analysis. Participants were told that the information they provided would be treated confidentially and that only aggregated data would be reported. Ninety-nine middle managers returned the completed questionnaire, for a response rate of 90 percent.

Keeping in mind the continued evolution of the use of IT in organizations, data on managerial use of IT collected in 1988 may seem inappropriate. However, the research database is still regarded as appropriate for two reasons. First, the objective of the analyses was to compare some characteristics of more- and less-innovative middle managers in broader issues. Second, documentation of differences in 1988 may serve as a baseline for future analyses over time. Appendix A contains information regarding operationalization of independent, intermediating, dependent, and control variables.

With the exception of hypothesis-group H5, variance analyses were employed. Whenever possible, a standard least squares regression equation was developed, one for each hypothesis. Multiple regression was considered inappropriate.

## 4. Results of the research

The number of hands-on users and of middle managers delegating IT tasks varied among applications (see Table 1).

On account of the variation in numbers, different statistical variance analysis approaches had to be employed. The basis for deciding which method to use was the number of observations available. In cases where the number of available observations was <50, correlation was used. When $\approx$ 50 observations were available, a short variant regression analysis was employed. It was determined that 50 observations would allow the inclusion of two control variables, in addition to the dependent variable and the independent variable of degree of innovativeness. Job task difficulty and variation was selected to ensure a relative degree of consistency between the two selected control variables in the short-variant regression analysis. Finally, in cases where >80 observations could be used, a long variant regression analysis was employed, using all six control variables in the model. (Table 2).

## 5. Discussion

In the sample, the number of hours per week innovative middle managers spent using a PC was comparable to that of the other middle managers. The tendency among innovative middle managers to develop less complex programs and delegate more may imply that they are more casual IT users. However, because innovative middle managers were inclined to delegate more, they may, in total, utilize IT more.

It is well established that innovative persons emphasize networking and can concentrate on critical aspects of getting the job done. The correlations among the variables describing the number of implemented IT innovations, hands-on use of IT, IT delegation frequency, and use of support functions, may illustrate some of these aspects (see Table 7).

There was no correlation between hands-on use of IT and the use of support functions or between delegation of IT tasks and the use of support functions.

The tentative inclination to delegate e-mail tasks may seem to be in disagreement with the findings of Zmud et al. [35], who concluded that managers looked upon e-mail as a powerful communication tool. Since only 50 per cent in the surveyed company had access to e-mail, the reason for the result might be that the number of e-mail users had not reached the critical level, where the people that the innovative middle managers would like to reach used e-mail. However, the innovative middle managers seem to delegate e-mail tasks more often than other categories of managers.

## 6. Conclusions

Because of IT developments over the nine years since the data collection and the one organization data collection design, the results of this study may not be generalizable. The strengths of the present approach are the careful sample selection, a high response rate, and the focus on general aspects of IT and information use rather than specific vendors or procedures.

The results show that significant differences between more- and less-innovative middle managers were found between many of the predicted independent-to-dependent variable relationships, but not between controls and dependent variables. Although the analysis results may be regarded as tentative, they indicate that IT and information use varies with the degree of innovativeness. It was found that the more-innovative middle managers were inclined to use data drawn from personal experience and insights rather than historical data and found IT generally more important. Apparently, innovative middle managers made less complex programs, and when in-depth analyses were needed, their execution was more often delegated. Innovative middle managers used IT as a vehicle for networking. They distributed more of their IT output to others. Even though they did not use IT more, they used local and expert support functions more.

Since data was collected in 1988, the obvious first future research effort is to test whether the findings in this study hold in the present managerial IT use environment. More experienced users, the increased focus on information as a critical element in business, e-mail as a common and widespread communication tool, and the relatively recent introduction of Internet and truly integrated software packages may lead to different results. The analyses presented here are based on relatively crude measures of IT use, delegation patterns, and use of support functions. Although many recent publications have investigated managers' use of IT, these contributions do not address the key question raised here; differences within and among these issues as a function of the degree of innovativeness. We also need to increase our understanding of how the innovative managers take advantage of support functions and IT experts in idea development and implementation efforts.

Most practitioners and academicians claim that the need for IT-based business renewal and change will continue to escalate. If this is true, we must position our research efforts accordingly.

## A.3 Dependent variables

Use of data describing future events: A scale (ranging from always to never) measuring whether the respondent used data describing future events (such as forecast data). Measuring a construct with only one item is a weak method. Because of the wide scope of the research many detailed questions were asked. Therefore, the length of the questionnaire instrument was a major concern and did not allow for ideal exploration of every construct. Additionally, analysis results may indicate areas where future research undertakings should include development of more robust instruments.

Use of historic data: A scale (ranging from always to never) measuring whether the respondent used data describing past events (such as data from an order entry or accounting system).

Use of data drawn from personal experience and insight: A scale (ranging from always to never) measuring whether the respondent developed her/his own data (which is often based on judgment or simulation).

Use of the keyboard: An item (ranging from always to never) measuring how often the respondent keyed in her/his own data.

Use of data copied from databases: An item (ranging from always to never) measuring how often the respondent copied data from databases (central or departmental computer, floppies or hard disk).

Use of data from reports and files: An item (ranging from always to never) measuring how often the respondent used data directly from reports, files, or archives.

Computer program size: An item (ranging from always to never) measuring whether the respondent typically made small and simple programs. The researchers had also included questions about the typical number of cells in spreadsheet applications and typical number of statements in programs. However, while 23 middle managers responded to the scale item, only six responded to the questions about number of cells and statements. Therefore, only the one item was used.

Computer program complexity: A scale of three items (ranging from always to never) measuring how often the respondent used:

1. simple commands to produce reports;

2. executed simple arithmetic calculations; and

3. employed programming instructions such as IF and DO.

Only responses from those managers that made computer programs were included. The scale for item 1 (use of simple commands) was rotated. That is, the higher the numerical value the more frequent the use of more complex programming features.

The rationale for this operationalization was the assumption that the middle managers would have difficulties in recalling relative complex phenomena such as data structure complexity or module structure. Therefore, the researchers chose three aspects of programming that would reflect increasing complexity; at the lowest level using database macrocommands or assistants to produce reports, at the next level introducing some calculations in a linear manner, and at the most complex level introduce branching (IF) or repeating groups (DO). The final measure was calculated as the average of the three items.

High quality IT output: A scale of three items (ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree) measuring whether the respondent agreed that:

1. IT output does not have to look nice (in the calculations the item values were reversed);

2. that they spend extra time on making the output look nice when the respondent executed her/his own computing; and

3. that the manager instructed people who performed IT tasks for them to spend extra time on making the output look nice.

The final measure was calculated as the average of the three items.

Distribution of IT output to others: A scale of four items (ranging from always to never) measuring how often the respondent distributed IT output to:

1. his/her subordinates;

2. his/her supervisor;

3. persons in other organizational units; and

4. persons outside the company.

The final measure was calculated as the average of the four items.

Hands-on use of IT: A perceptual measure of the typical number of hours per week over the last six months the respondent had used a work station. Number of hours per week was also noted per application (word processing, spreadsheet, graphics, database, e-mail.)

Delegation of IT tasks: The average frequency (ranging from always to never) with which the respondent delegated IT tasks to secretaries and other staff relative to their own hands-on use of IT. The data were recorded per application.

IT arrangements: Whether the respondent had a work station (PC, or other computer) installed on their desk.

Use of support functions: The typical number of times over the last six months the respondent had contacted others for help or to discuss computer-related matters. The number of times such contacts were made was recorded per source, for example, colleagues, user group, information center, and vendors. Colleagues and user group were classified as local support. Information center and vendors were classified as expert support.
