# Project selection criteria by strategic orientation

James J. Jiang $^{1,a}$ , Gary Klein $^{b,*}$

$^{a}$ Department of Computer Information Systems, School of Administration and Business, Louisiana Tech University, Ruston, LA 71272, USA $^{b}$ College of Business and Administration, University of Colorado at Colorado Springs, 1420 Austin Bluffs Parkway, Colorado Springs, CO 80933-7150, USA

Received 29 July 1997; revised 20 June 1998; accepted 21 January 1999

## Abstract

One information planning decision involves project selection from among a portfolio of options. This involves multiple steps, including selection and weighting of alternatives. Choice and weighting on criteria become crucial in the selection of the projects to pursue. A survey conducted by the authors found that organizations with an expectation of future IS importance rely heavily on organizational goals, management support and environmental factors. Organizations with low strategic expectations of IS rely more heavily on management support, political considerations, and risk. The results allow managers to position selection criteria according to their strategic use of information technology. © 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Project management; Strategic grid; Information system selection

## 1. Introduction

For years, the planning of improved information systems (IS) has been one of the challenges facing IS professionals $[23]$ . Effective IS planning can help organizations understand the impact of IS on the overall organizational strategy, and use them to reach business goals, before deciding to develop new IS. IS planning puts a firm in a better position to evaluate potential IS projects and facilitates effective resource allocation.

By definition, IS planning is “the process of identifying a portfolio of computer-based applications that will assist an organization in executing its business plans and realizing its business goals” [26]. This portfolio might include a wide variety of applications, including accounting, human resources, marketing, manufacturing, research and development systems, electronic mail systems, expert systems, decision support systems, CAD/CAM, and so forth. To allocate scarce resources among diverse IS projects, IS professionals often need to conduct a multiple-round, in-depth evaluation of potential IS projects. The evaluation criteria routinely include technological, economic, and behavioral aspects [22].

One group of project selection literature describes various methods. A comprehensive taxonomy of these is provided in Ref. [14]. In general, they are divided into four subcategories; comparative approaches [33], scoring models [21], benefit contribution or economic models [9], and optimization methods [6]. However, none of these has achieved even limited use in industry [4]. Even though complex computer-based models were shown to have more of the desired features, their use is also not well accepted [17].

Use of a practical IS project selection approach is said to be essential for an effective IS planning $[30]$ . As a result, IS professionals often adopt the analytic hierarchy process $[37]$ , which includes three major steps:

1. identify and select criteria;

2. weight the criteria and build consensus about their relative importance; and

3. evaluate the project proposals using the weighted criteria.

To implement such a technique requires a firm grasp of the importance of each individual criterion.

Criteria can represent organizational needs that address the impact of the IS on business objectives $[24]$ . Other criteria focus on project risk, including complexity, budget, development time, and newness of technology. The political dimension in system prioritization is also emphasized and the importance of considering the competitive environment is stressed $[25]$ . Once criteria are chosen, the decision makers must place importance weights on each chosen element, but little help is given in the literature. The weighting of criteria is a critical step, since an improper selection of weights can radically alter the selected portfolio [20].

We also suspect that the IS professionals' perceived importance of evaluation criteria can be changed by the strategic relevance of IS to an organization (e.g. Refs. [28, 34]). One of the most useful IS architecture planning methodologies is the IS strategic grid [29]. This is a contingency approach that can be used to determine the strategic relevance of IS to the organization. The strategic grid is a useful structure for explaining differences in IS postures for organizations and provides a framework for categorizing organizations for the purpose of studying differences. The scheme defines a continuum over a two-dimensional plane, along which a firm moves as it assimilates IS technology, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Four major zones have been identified:

Strategic organizations, where IS activities are critical to existing operations, and planned IS applications are critical for future success.

Turnaround organizations, which are not totally dependent on current IS to function but new IS methods, vital for reaching organizational objectives, have been developed

Factory organizations, which are dependent on IS for their day-to-day operations but will not gain significant advantage from further development. Support organizations, which are neither highly dependent on IS nor will be improved by them in the future.

Each of these classes has very different needs for IS and technology. IS professionals must be able to make more informed decisions on the IS mission and resource allocation for competing IS projects.

## 2. Methodology

This study examines how IS professionals' project selection decisions are affected by their IS environment. Along with other researchers [41], we contend that the strategic relevance of IS to the organization affect IS professionals' perceived importance on project selection criteria. To provide evidence to support this belief, we designed an instrument that allows examination of project selection criteria relative to the organizations position in the strategic grid. The survey was conducted using IS professionals as subjects.

## 2.1. Instrument

The questionnaire (see Appendix A) consisted of multiple parts: two assessing the strategic relevance of IS in the organization; another measuring the importance of an IS project selection criteria. A final part collected demographic data. The scales used to determine the strategic relevance of IS to the organization was adopted from $[31]$ . The questionnaire of IS project selection criteria was designed to be consistent by including items found in a review of the literature $[11, 13, 36, 39]$ . The six subcategories of IS evaluation criteria include: financial, organizational, competing environment, technical, risk, and management (see Table 1).

To ensure understandability, the researchers first had two colleagues critically review the questions. Their suggestions were incorporated into the questionnaire. Second, a pilot study used the questionnaire on five MBA students who were already full-time IS professionals. All ambiguous items were removed or modified. A final of 32 items remained. Subjects were asked to rate the importance of the items as if facing a new project portfolio. All questions were recorded on a five-point Likert-type scale (1, not important; 2, slightly important; 3, somewhat important; 4, important; and 5, very important).

## 2.2. Subjects

The questionnaire was then distributed to 300 members of the Data Processing Management Association (DPMA) randomly selected from the membership roles in the region that includes Michigan, Illinois, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Indiana, and Wisconsin. The sample was chosen because these members of DPMA come from a wide variety of organizational settings. Self-addressed return envelopes for each questionnaire were enclosed. Ninety-three questionnaires (for a response rate of 30.1%) were returned to the researchers. This rate appears consistent with other mail surveys. Five questionnaires were eliminated due to missing data, leaving a final sample of 88 subjects. The participants in this study held a variety of positions within the computer field, reflecting considerable heterogeneity with respect to IS professions. Table 2 presents a summary of their demographic characteristics. The profiles match those of published studies involving DPMA members [15, 16].

Each of the demographic variables was run in a separate MANOVA to determine any introduction of bias by the study population. The independent variables were the mean scores of the metric categories.

## 2.3. Metrics

Determination or verification of criteria categories is the first step to permit examination of the differences by strategic posture. A principal components analysis was conducted. Seven components had eigenvalues greater than one and were retained for further analysis. These factors were used in a Varimax rotation with loading results shown in Table 3. Item loadings greater than 0.50 were assigned to the seven global metrics as shown in Table 4.

Examination of the items indicates the seven global items generally follow the a priori metrics, with one exception: Factor #3, which is composed of internal political decisions, regulations, and political acceptance. These three items are used as a seventh metric termed ‘political reality’. This factor has been proposed as a valid concern during system selection [3, 27].

The results from the principal components analysis drive the computation of seven global metrics. Each of these is computed as an average of the items loaded onto the associated factor. The Kaiser's measure of sampling adequacy [19] was conducted and the results (MSA = 0.08027) indicated the adequacy of the sample size. The homogeneity of the items within each factor was established further by computing their internal consistency reliability coefficient (coefficient $\alpha$ ) applying the formula recommended by [8]. The seven metrics wound up with $\alpha$ s ranging from 0.64 to 0.84, which suggests they are good results for social science research. In order to assure the instrument is consistent and agrees with other established measures of the same attributes, we correlated the mean responses of each factor to the one question directly asking about the overall importance of that factor [2]. The results are shown in Table 5.

We examined the following alternate hypotheses:

H1: The criteria of meeting organizational needs and objectives will be perceived more important by IS professionals in a strategic IS firm than those in other firms.

An organization in the strategic category depends heavily on information technology for identifying opportunities and avoiding risks. IS professionals in these firms must understand how the business strategy influences business processes and organization and then prompt an alignment with the IS architecture and organization [5]. We, therefore, predict that the criteria of meeting organizational needs and objectives will be perceived more important by IS professionals in a strategic IS firm.

H2: The criteria of the external environment will be perceived more important by IS professionals in a strategic IS firm than those in other firms.

Firms in the strategic category are likely to be dependent now, and in the future, on information technology for survival in the marketplace. They are most likely to rely heavily on information technology to provide a competitive advantage. They often adopt a leading edge strategy and have a strong commitment to resources, technology innovation, and strong user commitment to new technology $[10]$ .

H3: The criteria of financial consideration will be perceived more important by IS managers in a turnaround IS firm than those in other firms.

Firms that fall into the turnaround classification are not heavily dependent on the IS function at present.

They may, however, look to new applications in the future to bolster their competitive position. These firms may be unable to muster or commit the resources necessary to pursue a leading edge approach and, therefore, concentrate on high return-on-investment projects $[7]$ .

H4: The criteria of management support will be perceived more important by IS managers in a support IS firm than those in other firms.

Management support of an IS is critical to its success. However, firms that fall into this category are neither dependent on IS, nor will new IS applications be likely to be critical in the future. If a new technology has not been viewed as logical and necessary, then major resistance can be expected $[38]$ . On the other hand, firms that have a strategic emphasis are more likely to have fostered the willingness to be creative and reception to change. Thus, we expect that support firms will place more emphasis on this criteria in a relative sense, though we expect it to be important for all.

H5: The criteria of project risk will be perceived as being more important by IS managers in a support firm than those in other firms.

Unlike organizations in the remaining quarters, an organization that falls in the support classification is less likely to have experience in developing a leading edge technology. The application of such technology contains risk elements shunned by organizations with few strategic plans for IS [35]. We, therefore, argue that IS professionals in a support organization will consider the project risk factor more importantly than in other firms.

H6: The technical criteria will be perceived more important by IS professionals in a strategic firm than those in other firms.

Organizations that fall into the strategic quadrant often adopt a more aggressive approach to new technology $[12]$ , which may demand a different set of technical requirements, including IS personnel skills, the latest hardware, and integration with existing systems. Therefore, we predict that IS professionals in strategic organizations will place more weights on the technical criteria for project selection.

## 3. Results

First, apparently no respondents fall in the Factory quadrant of the strategic grid. There may be several reasons for this. This is consistent with other studies where the factory quadrant is sparsely populated $[31]$ . This is, in effect, good news in that organizations with a strategic emphasis on current systems are not considering lowering the importance of systems in the future. The rarity of factory organizations in studies could also be due to a professional bias on the part of the respondents. IS professionals may not admit or recognize that information technology has no strategic, future impact in their organization. Or organizations with little emphasis on future IS may not have a sufficient population of IS professionals to catch in a random sample.

In order to determine whether there is a relationship between the strategic importance of IS in an organization and project selection criteria, a MANOVA test was conducted. The dependent variables are the seven metrics and the independent variable the classification on the strategic grid. The result is shown in Table 6. The MANOVA result (F = 2.30 (Wilks' $\lambda$ and p-value = 0.001) suggests that there is a significant relationship between the strategic importance of IS in an organization and the relative importance of IS project selection criteria. The independent ANOVA results indicate that the differences exist on the technical considerations, external environment, and organizational goals. Thus, the general hypothesis is rejected to support evidence of differences.

Perhaps the wisdom of gathering managerial support has taken root. This lack of a difference is also unique, in that this dimension ranks the highest in both, the support and turnaround quadrants, and it is still second in the strategic quadrant. Such a consideration of ranks is of importance, since many project selection techniques use dominance or lexicographic ordering. Lack of difference in risk concern and the consistently low importance indicate a universal acceptance of the risk, at least in information investments.

The political reality metric has similar values in the mean across quadrants. However, this metric is a high priority scale in the support quadrant, but is a much lower priority in the remaining quadrants. On account of the unanticipated emergence of this criteria, no formal hypothesis has been presented, but the results match previous literature in that political factors have continued significance in system success $[18]$ . Further, organizations in the support grid tend to be reactive to technology and may not have established decision structures to deal with technology issues $[32]$ . Thus, systems required by regulations and determined by internal political decision will be emphasized, thereby pushing the rank higher than for other criteria.

## 4. Discussion

A survey of IS professionals was conducted to determine the importance of various criteria in the selection of IS projects. The responses were categorized according to a strategic grid model in order to verify differences in criteria weighting across differences in strategic outlook. The results indicate that selection criteria does indeed vary according to strategic posture, although no criteria were rated as unimportant by any class.

Organizations with a ‘strategic’ orientation rate organizational goals most highly. This follows with management support, responding to the external environment, technical considerations, financial attainment, political reality, and risk. In general, a firm well established in strategic IS use understands the need to match the selection of IS projects carefully to the goals of the organization.

Organizations with a ‘turnaround’ orientation rank criteria similar to those with a pure strategic posture. The major difference is the drop of technical considerations to the bottom of the stack. Perhaps organizations in this class are not preparing fully to grasp the technology that must accompany strategic advances in information technology usage.

Lastly, some organizations do not express current use of critical systems, nor do they expect any in the future. These ‘support’ organizations still rank managerial support highly, but follow that with political goals and risk considerations. It is clear that these organizations are merely reacting to technology needs and advances.

The results show clearly that differences exist in strategic posture of an organization. The differences allow organizations to compare their approach to those of other organizations in the same category for benchmarking or to identify difficulties in maintaining the desired level of competitiveness. Organizations wishing to switch from turnaround status should not continue to choose projects on political and risk factors.

If one views the grid of Fig. 1 as a map for transitions, the organization has a set of asserted standards. An organization wishing to shift from a support to a turnaround status, they should retain the importance of management involvement, but elevate the consideration of meeting future financial projections, satisfying organizational goals with the system, and consider how the new systems will enable better positioning in the external environment. To further move toward the strategic quadrant requires that an organization retain the importance of existing measures, but also elevate the consideration of technical advances and further elevate the match of the system to the competitive environment and organizational goals.

It should be noted that transitions through the quadrants does not ‘swap’ importance of any criteria in an absolute sense. Criteria retain importance once they enter the picture and others join them in terms of consideration. This is a testimony to the complexity levels in strategic systems. In addition, where more criteria must be met, more resources must likely be committed. Thus, it may be difficult to muster resources to go straight from the support posture to the strategic posture without passing through the turnaround quadrant.

Reversal of the move to the strategic posture is also a possibility. An organization could intentionally, or through lack of attention to IS, adequately maintain existing systems of strategic importance, but fail to plan and deploy systems of future importance. This would drop the organization into the factory quadrant. The data do not exist in our study to show how the criteria would be modified to achieve this particular shift.

The results of this study must, however, be viewed with caution. Even though the sample represented a good population of IS professionals, it excluded one of the theorized classes. Even though the model has been repeatedly validated, perhaps the model or instrument should be modified to account for the missing category, which has been observed in only small numbers in other studies. Likewise, the sample size limits generalizability of the absolute values attained for criteria importance, putting more emphasis on the rankings and changes in the level of importance of the criteria.
