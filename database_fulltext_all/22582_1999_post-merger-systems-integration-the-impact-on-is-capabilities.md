# Post-merger systems integration: the impact on IS capabilities $^{1}$

Stephanie S. Robbins, Antonis C. Stylianou $^{*}$

Department of Information and Operations Management, The Belk College of Business Administration, University of North Carolina at Charlotte, Charlotte, NC 28223, USA

Received 1 May 1998; revised 1 March 1999; accepted 1 April 1999

## Abstract

Mergers and acquisitions may disrupt the operations of the organizations involved. Major issues include the need to integrate personnel, business processes, information systems, and diverse information technologies across the merging organizations. However, if carefully planned and properly managed, the merger/acquisition and the resulting integration process can become an opportunity to strengthen the capabilities of the combined organization and place it in a better competitive position. This paper uses the results of a field survey to examine the effects of post-merger systems integration on information systems area capabilities. Factors influencing the success of the systems integration are identified. © 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Systems Integration; Mergers; Acquisitions; Category

## 1. Introduction

During the 1980s, organizations desiring rapid growth in size and strength, increased market share, and acquisition of new products, patents, technologies, talent, and/or geographical territories along with economies and efficiencies of scale, used mergers and acquisitions as a method to achieve these outcomes $[13, 34]$ . In other words, corporations sought to create synergistic effects by integrating two separate business entities in order to increase competitive advantages $[24]$ . This activity resulted in a historic wave $[1, 19, 26, 30]$ of financially motivated mergers and acquisitions not all of which were successful [10, 28, 35]. In fact, it has been estimated that up to two-thirds of all mergers do not work as anticipated [9, 11]. Some of the failure can be attributed to pre-merger discussions which tend to focus on the financial components of the deal while ignoring the problems associated with integrating the technical architecture and organizational infrastructure of the two separate entities [17, 20].

The integration of corporate entities can produce dynamic and synergistic opportunities, but the benefits do not flow automatically and the process can be extremely disruptive [33], particularly when trying to integrate information systems (IS). Corporations depend on their IS to provide accurate, reliable, and up-to-date information. To minimize the disruptive nature of integrating them, the acquirer and target's technical architecture and organizational infrastructure should be assessed prior to the acquisition [5] and IS professionals should be fully involved in the entire process, including pre-merger discussions, so that integration problems can be identified early [31]. However, merger discussions tend to be secret, high level, financially oriented [15] and, in general, do not include IS professionals [7]. This is unfortunate, because the process of quickly integrating systems is extremely complex and the lack of pre-merger IS integration-related planning delays the process. Furthermore, mergers and acquisitions can create attitudinal and productivity problems [6, 21, 29] as well as turnover of key personnel [14, 32]. The immediate benefits expected from the merger are often not achieved because of unrealistic expectations relative to IS integration.

Successful IS integration can generate a wide range of positive outcomes that support the underlying motives for the merger. For example, IS integration can enhance the organization's competitive position [27] and can help shape or enable critical business strategies [16]. It can contribute to the overall organizational financial performance as measured by return on investment and return on assets. Successful integration can provide good quality, accurate, useful, and timely information as well as create systems that operate efficiently by ensuring system availability, reliability, and responsiveness [5]. The integration of related technologies across organizational units, as well as the development of systems that are efficient and effective, can be achieved. Successful integration can lead to the identification and assimilation of new technologies and can also help recruit and maintain a technically and managerially competent IS staff [12]. Furthermore, the process of integrating acquirer and target IS can be used as an opportunity to achieve a number of other benefits, such as the integration of IS planning with organizational planning and the ability to provide corporate-wide information accessibility.

The purpose of this research was to examine IS professionals' ability to generate positive outcomes as a result of the integration of acquirer and target information systems. Positive outcomes include a wide range of results, such as: enhancing the organizations' competitive position; contributing to the overall organizational financial performance; and providing corporate-wide information accessibility. ## 2. Research design

Much of the prior research on mergers and acquisitions has focused on financial issues $[4]$ , is case-specific, or anecdotal in nature $[2, 3, 8, 18, 25]$ . However, Stylianou et al. $[31]$ have developed a research model that was used to empirically evaluate the successful integration of IS in merged organizations. In this model, IS integration success was presented as a multi-dimensional construct that can be expressed through the following measures:

\- The ability to exploit opportunities arising from the merger

\- The ability to avoid problems stemming from the merger

\- The end user satisfaction with the integration process and integrated systems

\- IS assessment of the success of the integration process and integrated systems

While this model does include some important measures, it does not appear to provide a complete picture. For example, the measures relating to the ability to exploit opportunities or avoid problems arising from the merger are important, but are unidimensional and, therefore, do not include measures specifically relating to improved IS capabilities that help support the underlying motives for the merger. Furthermore, while useful to know, IS assessment of success may be biased, because it deals with the IS perspective and, as a result, should not be considered a primary measure of success. In addition, the efficiency and effectiveness of IS resource utilization during the integration process has not been given consideration. In light of this, the model has been revised by removing the fourth measure and adding (see Fig. 1):

\- Improved IS capabilities that help support the underlying motives for the merger

Organizational and IS factors influence IS integration success. Organizational factors include in part: company size, based on assets and revenues; the overall number of employees; industry type; organizational structure; distribution of decision-making; the original relationship between the acquiring and acquired organizations before the merger; prior merger experience; the degree of IS participation in merger planning; and the quality of merger planning. IS factors include: the number of IS employees, personnel skill levels; structure (i.e. the distribution of activities); distribution of hardware; data sharing; degree of compatibility between acquiring and acquired organizations; the desired degree of integration; the current status of the integration process; integration planning activities including audits prior to integration and the development of integration priorities; and IS personnel issues. The 84 factors included in the study were examined to determine the degree to which each correlates to IS ability to generate positive outcomes by improving IS capabilities as a result of integrating IS in merged organizations.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Survey design

The survey instrument was developed in a three-step process. First, a preliminary questionnaire designed to include a wide range of constructs was developed. Second, the questionnaire was administered during interviews with local IS managers who had experienced the integration of systems resulting from merger activity. This step provided a number of minor revisions that helped clarify questions and improve the instrument's readability. A pilot study was used as the third step. A number of IS managers from selected corporations across the United States were contacted and asked to participate in the pilot study. In addition to completing the questionnaire, these respondents were asked to identify any problems. Comments received led to some additional minor adjustments.

## 3.2. Data collection

All companies that were identified in Mergers & Acquisitions—The Dealmaker's Journal (published by IDD Enterprises, New York), as having undergone a corporate merger during the time period from 1989 to 1991, were included in the research sample. The research instrument was mailed to the CIOs of 1000 organizations (selected from the sample using a structured technique) and follow-up postcards were sent two weeks later. While Mergers & Acquisitions appeared to be a useful source in identifying organizations that had experienced a merger during the time frame under investigation, some problems became apparent. Several respondents indicated that they were not aware that a corporate merger had taken place. Furthermore, Mergers & Acquisitions did not identify companies that had undertaken a system integration project as a result of the merger/acquisition process. It became apparent that many of the mergers during this period were characterized by transactions designed to obtain quick results and, thus, did not involve the integration of IS components. Therefore, the 44 valid responses received represented an 18 percent response rate from the estimated eligible organizations (sample size adjusted for surveys returned as undeliverable, and responses indicating that the questionnaire was either not applicable or that company policy prohibited responding).

## 4. Data analysis

The Cronbach's $\alpha$ for the thirteen IS capability outcome variables was 0.88, suggesting relatively strong internal reliability [22, 23]. Although this paper does not address the other measures of IS integration success, our correlation analysis indicates that the IS capability variables are significantly correlated with most of the other measures of integration success.

A Pearson product/moment correlation analysis of the relationships between improved IS capability outcomes and the organizational factors, and the relationships between improved IS capability outcomes and information systems factors was undertaken. The results of the analysis can be found in Table 2, which only contains those factors that were correlated at a significant level.

## 4.1. Organizational factors

There are three important organizational factors that are controllable within the context of the integration process. The first relates to the level of executive management (non-IS) support of IS integration activities. This behavior signals to the organization the importance of IS integration and, in turn, helps the IS function to create a positive integration environment. The second controllable variable is the quality of merger planning. While merger planning is tedious, time consuming, and often does not include IS professionals, the reward for such activity is the development of new systems that help support the underlying motives for the merger. The third variable relates to the quality of communication between the IS and user areas regarding the progress of merger activities. Good communication helps generate understanding and support from the end-user constituency.

There are also several significant organizational variables that are non-controllable at the time of integration. Those organizations that have gained experience as a result of previous merger activity were more likely to understand the difficulties and problems associated with integrating systems in merged organizations. A second non-controllable variable relates to the primary organizational structure of the target business. It would appear that the more complex the organizational structure (i.e. Matrix), the more difficult it is to generate positive outcomes. Finally, a broad geographic distribution of the organization's systems operation permitted the IS function to tap into a wider range of resources than would otherwise be available.

## 4.2. Information systems factors

The variables that are controllable within the context of the integration process include both, the quality of IS integration planning and the quality of communication between the IS and user areas regarding the progress of IS integration. These IS variables correspond to similar organizational factors. Communication is also enhanced with end-user involvement with IS integration activities resulting from the merger. If end-users understand the process and problems associated with IS integration resulting from a merger and are able to provide input, an atmosphere of cooperation is created that allows the IS department to more readily achieve its objectives. The use of user committees that are responsible for strategic IS decision making during the integration process also has positive benefits. A user committee would be sensitive to end-user needs and, thus, could help direct the IS function to make decisions that would have a favorable impact. Another controllable end-user variable relates to the quality of technical support available and provisions for end-user re-training due to IS integration. As end-users understand the new system, they should recognize the value of including IS in the overall organizational planning process. All of these factors clearly point to the importance of involving end-users in the entire IS integration process.

A final controllable variable relates to the potential decline in IS employee morale as a result of the merger. In the highly competitive IS market, poor morale makes it difficult to recruit and maintain a technically competent staff. Furthermore, potential employees will shy away from environments that are in disarray. Therefore, this is an important component that needs close monitoring.

Variables that are uncontrollable at the time of the integration include both, the degree of standardization and the level of data sharing found in the acquirer and the merged organizations. Standardization and data sharing across applications indicates the existence of an infrastructure and an architecture that is conducive and/or encourages high levels of integration. Furthermore, this kind of environment can more easily produce synergistic results.

Other uncontrollable variables include the size and complexity of the existing systems, the geographical distribution of integration-related IS activities and the distribution of the acquirer's hardware. Clearly, it is more difficult and expensive to undertake an integration project if the size and complexity of the existing systems are large, the development of applications are globally dispersed and there is a decentralization of hardware. Of course, these factors also suggest that there may be a more sophisticated and wider pool of IS talent available to address the problems relating to these issues.

The final uncontrollable factors, that must be considered, include the planning approach used by the IS department, the quality of IS planning relating to that approach and the degree of outsourcing in place at the time of the system integration. An IS planning approach that is enterprise or industry based (CSF) as opposed to a narrower view is more likely to allow the IS function to shape or enable critical business strategies. Furthermore, a broader understanding of organizational needs allows the IS function to be responsive at a strategic level which, in turn, should help support positive outcomes from merger induced system integration. In addition, the outsourcing of a significant proportion of application development by the acquirer frees up IS time that can be used to address the difficult problems encountered with the integration of systems resulting from a merger.

## 5. Conclusion

It is widely recognized nowadays that information technologies represent a critical resource for most business organizations, whether in a role of supporting business operations and managerial decision making or (increasingly) as a means of gaining a strategic competitive advantage. It is to the best interest of any organization that relies on IT to have high quality systems that are well-integrated, and operate in a synergistic way. Mergers and acquisitions, and the resulting task of integrating diverse systems in the entities involved, have the potential to disrupt and throw out of alignment the smooth operation of even the best managed systems. On the other hand, an IS organization could use the opportunities offered by a merger or acquisition to achieve a net positive impact on its capability to perform and contribute to the organizational objectives.

As the results of this study indicate, beside past integration experience, the critical factors in achieving a net positive outcome in the IS area following a merger/acquisition are managerial in nature and are largely controllable. A successful IS integration requires high quality merger as well as IS integration planning, positive support by executive management, high-quality communication to the end-users, and a high level of end-user involvement in strategic IS decision-making during the process. In addition, as expected, an emphasis on IS standardization is a positive factor.

As indicated in our model of integration success, improvements in IS capability represent only one of the ways in which integration success can be measured. Future research could focus on the other measures, such as user satisfaction with the integration process and the integrated systems and the efficiency and effectiveness of resource utilization during the integration. Furthermore, given the exploratory nature of the literature in this area, additional studies need to be designed that overcome some of the weaknesses of the data collection process while replicating the findings.

In an era of renewed merger and acquisition activity, and with IT becoming increasingly important for most business organizations, it is important to learn how systems can be best integrated with the least disruption to business operations and how the IS area can take advantage of the integration opportunity to propel itself to higher levels of achievement.

His current research interests include the application of total quality management techniques in the IS function, in impact of information technologies (IT) on organizational efficiency, the potential of IT for providing a competitive advantage in an international market, electronic commerce, and issues related to the use of IT in the banking and health-care industries.

Dr. Stephanie S. Robbins is an Associate Professor of MIS in the information & Operations Department at the University of North Carolina at Charlotte. She received her Ph.D. in Management from Louisiana State University. Her research focus is in the area of strategic and global information systems and the issues associated with system integration. Dr. Robbins has published in journals such as: Information & Management; The Journal of Computer Information Systems: Journal of Systems Management; Behavioral Science; Journal of Academy of Marketing Science; as well as numerous international, national, and regional conference proceedings.
