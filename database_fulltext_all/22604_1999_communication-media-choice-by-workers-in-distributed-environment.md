# Communication media choice by workers in distributed environment

Janaka Wijayanayake $^{a,*}$ , Kunihiko Higa $^{a}$

$^{a}$ Department of Industrial Engineering and Management, Graduate School of Decision Science and Technology, Tokyo Institute of Technology, 2-12-1 O-okayama, Meguro-ku, Tokyo 152-8552, Japan

Received 14 August 1998; accepted 21 June 1999

## Abstract

Information technology (IT) is the facilitator for successful implementation of distributed-work. However, research on the implication of different types of IT in distributed-work in an organization is limited. The aim of our research was to study the distributed-worker's media use empirically, and determine the influential factors that affect the media selection and its effect on distributed-work. To achieve the objectives of the research, a hypothetical model that shows the relationships between media use and contextual factors, individual characteristics, media richness, and social influence was developed and validated. Distributed-workers' day-to-day, job related, communication information was collected through log-sheets. Data analysis showed that simple communication media, such as telephone and e-mail, is effective enough for carrying out job related activities from remote sites. Contextual, individual, and social level factors were found to influence the media selection of distributed-workers. Though, e-mail is thought to be a lean medium in media richness theory, this study shows that e-mail oriented workers use it effectively even for complex communication tasks, such as discussion. © 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Distributed-work; Media choice; Communication media; Media richness; Social influence

## 1. Introduction

Advances in telecommunication and computer technologies have made computer networking, electronic mail, facsimile, and teleconferencing cheaper, faster, and easier to use. The technological advances have already had a profound impact worldwide on business and industry. This has allowed dramatic change in the way workers are communicating and performing job related activities. There is now a new style of work environment: distributed-work (Telework), where people work outside the conventional office.

The concept of distributed-work has received a great deal of attention around the world in recent years. This arrangement has proven to be an effective way for organizations to meet the challenges of the modern world. It is believed that distributed-work has numerous benefits, such as improved worker productivity $[6]$ and cost reduction of expensive office-space $[20]$ . Distributed-workers claim there is less stress $[19]$ due to reduction of commuting time and also improved job satisfaction $[4]$ due to flexibility and autonomy of the work environment. It also allows organizations to improve competitive recruitment and retention of their best workers $[11]$ ; at the same time, workers can maintain the balance between the job and family life $[18]$ . This arrangement can also be used to broaden employment opportunities for handicapped people, support regional economic development by bringing the employment to workers in underdeveloped regions $[14]$ , as well as make energy conservation possible, and reduce air pollution $[9]$ .

Despite these advantages and benefits, the adoption and diffusion rate of distributed-work is very slow $[15]$ . One of the main reasons is identified as management fear and resistance $[13]$ : they believe that they cannot manage someone they cannot see. Past experience has shown that unless management is assured that the coordination, supervision, and control of workers can be maintained in a distributed work environment $[5]$ , rapid growth of distributed-work is unlikely. Using information technology (IT), it is possible to solve many of the problems that arise from distributed work $[1]$ ; e.g., if we can show that IT can be used effectively to coordinate, control, and supervise distributed-workers, there should be greater proliferation of distributed-work. However, research on the impact of various ITs on distributed-work is rare. Furthermore, the results of research on the need for highly advanced communication technologies are contradictory. Some claim such technology as desktop video-conferencing could provide means for managers to keep in touch with satellite office employees $[12]$ while others report that employees freely use familiar technologies and are reluctant to use highly advanced technologies such as teleconferencing and tele-eye $[8]$ . Hence, we suggest that usage of hi-tech media alone will not make a distributed-work program a success; this will depend on other factors, such as type of work, individual attitude, and social aspects.

The aim of this research is to study the distributed-worker's media use, factors that affect media selection, and implications on the effectiveness of distributed-work. To achieve our objectives, a hypothetical model that shows the relationship between media use and contextual factors, individual characteristics, and social influence is developed and tested.

## 2. Theories related to media selection

## 2.1. Social context

According to Sproull and Kiesler [17], social context influences information exchange through perception, cognitive interpretation, and communication behavior. Social context barriers, such as status differences, will not be an influential factor if sender and receiver are unaware of the difference. The effect of geographic location can even be avoided by perception.

Static cues emanate from people's appearance and local artifacts, such as a private office or a big desk. Dynamic cues emanate from people's non-verbal behavior that changes during the course of an interaction. When people perceive social context cues, they create cognitive interpretation and emotional states. People adjust the tone and content of their communication in response to their interpretation of the situation. The telephone reduces dynamic and static cues by eliminating the visual information in communications. E-mail reduces static cues and eliminate dynamic cues altogether. But, in contrast, teleconferencing has static and dynamic cues very much similar to face-to-face communication.

## 2.2. Social influence

Social influence theory explains media selection behavior of workers in a group environment. Fulk et al. [3] propose a social influence model in which the media choices of individuals are believed to be influenced by those of their superiors and co-workers. The theory says that the use and consequence of IT for communication emerge from complex social interactions. They suggest that the effect of any medium varies from one individual to another and from one social context to another. This suggests that even a lean medium with low information richness can be used effectively for communication by changing the group norms of workers and management support.

## 2.3. Media richness

Information richness is the ability of information to change understanding $[2]$ : uncertainty and equivocality influence the information processing required by organizations. In general, oral media are believed to be richer than written media. Furthermore, synchronous media are believed to be richer than asynchronous ones. In order of decreasing richness, the media classifications are assumed to be face-to-face, teleconferencing, telephone, e-mail, facsimile, and personal documents such as letters or memos. Face-to-face is the richest media because it provides immediate feedback so that the interpretation can be checked. It provides multiple channels, such as body language and different tones of voice. Documents are the leanest medium and numeric documentation is the lowest.

Communication tasks high in ambiguity, such as the settlement of a dispute, require information riche media. Tasks low in equivocality, such as providing a sales plan, do not require riche media. Lean media lacks adequate support for high equivocality tasks and rich media have more than enough capacity to perform low equivocality tasks. Therefore, appropriate media choice leading to the most effective outcome is very important.

## 3. Hypotheses proposed

In this study we assume that media selection is a result of three forces: Contextual, Individual, and Social structure.

## 3.1. Contextual structure

The cost of communication via telephone increases with geographical distance. This becomes high if the communication is between two parties who work in two different countries. On the other hand, e-mail is cheaper than telephone. Therefore, it is hypothesized that

H1: Distributed-workers use e-mail more than the telephone as the geographical distance to the communicating party increases.

Some jobs may need immediate feedback while others may not. Therefore, time pressure may influence the selection of media for a particular task. Synchronous media, such as the telephone, is considered to be richer than asynchronous media, such as e-mail. Therefore, it is hypothesized that

H2: Distributed-workers adopt telephone more than e-mail as the urgency of communication increases.

According to Nakamura et al. [10] there are different complexity levels of communication tasks. In ascending order of complexity they are: report, inquiry, work coordination, discussion, negotiation, and persuasion. To measure the complexity of the communication task, a survey was conducted and distributed-workers were asked to describe the order of complexity of their communication tasks. Then the complexity was computed based on the two-sample t-test between neighboring categories. This test indicates that distributed-workers did not differentiate the complexity of technical/non technical inquiry and work coordination $t = -0.91, p = 0.94$ .

Communication tasks high in complexity need higher information richness than those low in complexity. Further, communication media vary in their ability to carry rich information. Therefore, it is hypothesized that

H3: Distributed-workers adopt telephone more than e-mail as the complexity of the communication task increases.

## 3.2. Individual structure

According to Sirkka et al. [16] network organizations rely on their knowledge workers and their continued ability to learn and grow. Knowledge of new innovations is one of the key factors that influence the adoption of the innovation. Therefore, we hypothesized that

H4: E-mail experience of a distributed-worker is positively associated with its adoption.

E-mail is an asynchronous medium in which a user can communicate with one or many people. It is a lean medium and is not particularly appropriate for tasks that are especially important in the work of senior managers whose jobs have greater ambiguity $[7]$ . Therefore, it is hypothesized that

H5: Status of a distributed-worker is negatively related to the use of e-mail for communication.

## 3.3. Social influence

According to social influence theory, workers perception of a medium results from a variety of social definition processes, such as peer pressure, group communication culture, and organizational influences. Markus argued that the nature of the social relationship influences the media selection: e-mail was used as the primary medium for internal work-related communication and telephone was used as the primary medium for maintaining social relationships at work. Therefore, it is hypothesized that

H6: Management support of e-mail increases its usage by distributed-worker.

H7: Level of acquaintance with the receiving party is positively associated with the selection of media for distributed-work.

H8: Selection of e-mail is positively associated with co-workers media selection.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Organization surveyed

To accomplish our research, media usage behavior of two distributed-work groups at Fujitsu Kyushu Communication Systems Limited (QCS) was investigated. The organization is a subsidiary of the Fujitsu Corporation. The main objectives of the QCS local offices are to create a comfortable working environment, enhance productivity, and improve local community relations of employees. Thus, the main goals of local offices are different from traditional branch offices, which are normally established to satisfy strategic factors, such as proximity to raw material, low labor cost, or faster service to customers.

QCS is mainly engaged in software development and their customers are in both the USA and Japan. As jobs are distributed among offices, it is very important to provide local workers with support tools and communication equipment to carry out their work smoothly and satisfactorily. In the case of local offices, communication media is very important, as distributed-workers do not have face-to-face communication opportunities. Since all the business operations are distributed among local offices, distributed-workers have to rely on communication media in discussion, supervision, inquiry, and reporting of job related activities.

There are two distributed-work groups, Group A and Group B, involved in our research. They are similar in their size, type of job, composition, and technology. There were 24 members in Group A and 22 in Group B. All but one member from each group participated in the study. These group members were distributed throughout several local offices all over Japan; some are separated by more than 1000 km. Both the groups were involved in large-scale software development projects. Group A was developing private exchange software, and Group B was developing central office exchange software.

The composition of each group was: manager, group leader, group sub-leader, programmers, and support personnel. Manager and leader were executives and the sub-leader was on the borderline between management and programmers; they handled matters related to project management, as well as programming. Support personnel were outside the hierarchical structure of the development staff. Their main job was to maintain the technical environment of the distributed-work group. Some of their responsibilities included maintenance of local area networks and installation of computers and software tools. The composition of each group is shown in Table 1.

Identical communication technology was available to both group A and B. One telephone was provided for every three or four distributed-workers. Every distributed-worker had a personal computer with an e-mail connection. Group A received e-mail facilities 2.5 years before the survey and Group B received e-mail facilities 2 years before. In addition to these facilities other communication media, such as facsimile, audio conferencing, and video conferencing were also provided for both the groups.

There were few differences between the two groups (See Table 2). The main difference was in management support for use of e-mail. In general, all the members from both the groups had a fair knowledge of e-mail and its functionality. They had more than 2

## 4.2. Data collection

As there are advantages and disadvantages of various data collection methods, multiple data collection methods were administered to overcome some limitations. The survey data about communication media usage and their influence were gathered using log-sheets and questionnaires. Only questionnaires could have been used to measure the differences in media choice behavior of distributed-workers. However, the accuracy of the questionnaire based survey may be suspected, as there could be differences between real usage and users' perception as answered in the questionnaires. Actual media usage data collected through log-sheets would have been more appropriate, though there are limitations in this method as well. Filling in a log-sheet with detailed information whenever communication occurs is time consuming and cumbersome. Therefore, both the methods were used.

In the First survey, each individual was asked to fill out a log-sheet whenever he or she communicated on a project. Filling in the log-sheet was required in both sending and receiving modes. This survey was conducted in 5 consecutive days: from Monday through Friday. Before the start of the experiment, a meeting of the workers was held to explain the objectives of the survey and to ask for cooperation from all members in completing the survey successfully. During the meeting, all members were told how and what should be written on the log-sheet. After the survey, another meeting was held to exchange opinions on the survey results.

To gather additional information, a further two perception based surveys, a supplemental survey and a follow-up survey were conducted. The supplemental survey was conducted to gather information such as: individual status of the log-sheet survey; follow up behavior of media; and level of acquaintance with the receiving party. The follow up survey was conducted to collect data related to factors that influence media choice, such as perceived media richness, experience with the media, opponent behavior, etc.

## 4.3. Data analysis

The analysis of data collected through log-sheets shows that both groups used e-mail and telephone to make about 96% of their job related communications. Facsimile, audio conferencing, and video conferencing were used only for 4% of the communications. This strongly indicates that general purpose simple communication media is effective enough to carry out job related activities from remote sites. During the 1 week period, a total of 395 outgoing messages were recorded. 56.2% of them were made through e-mail, 40% through telephone, and 3.3% through fax. The number of incoming and outgoing messages and their averages are shown in Table 3. Both the groups have somewhat similar patterns of outgoing messages. Average number of outgoing messages were 7.65 and 10.43, respectively for groups A and B. Though there is a difference in averages, two sample t-test showed that there is no significant difference $p = 0.419$ between outgoing messages among the two groups. But, the test shows that there is a significant difference $p = 0.0089$ between incoming messages of group A and B. The average number of incoming messages is 12.13 for Group A and 29.0 for Group B.

Data analysis shows that the two groups have different patterns of media choice. Group A mainly relies on telephone and Group B relies more on e-mail. This is clearly indicated as 68% of communications were made through telephone by Group A members while 80% of communications were made through e-mail in Group B. ## 4.4. Hypothesis testing

A single log-sheet shows one worker's communication with another party and does not show the distributed-worker's general media choice. Selecting the log-sheet as the unit of analysis could produce incorrect results, as it does not represent the worker's overall choice. To minimize this drawback, aggregated data were used for an individual worker. Aggregated data represent the overall individual media selection behavior. This was calculated by averaging the log-sheet observations for each individual distributed-worker.

Hypotheses were tested separately for group A and B using aggregated data of log-sheets, supplemental survey data, and follow-up survey data. Multiple regression analysis and the two sample t-test were used for testing the hypotheses, which were tested separately to measure the influence of related variables on media choice of distributed-workers. A two-sample t-test was used to measure the differences between variables and multiple regression analysis was used to measure the relationship between independent and dependent variables. Where there are multiple independent variables, factor analysis was used to extract the most significant composite variables for testing.

## 4.4.1. Contextual structure

Hypothesis 1 is not supported for Group A (t = 0.817, p = 0.433) but, supported for Group B (t = 2.24, p = 0.049). The results showed that the distributed-workers of Group A used telephone and e-mail for their communication irrespective of the distance to the communication party while the use of e-mail among Group B members rose when the distance to the receiving party increased. As Group A members prefer communication by telephone, they used it to cover most communication within Japan. Even though the overall test indicates an insignificant association, the cost of communication seems to be a decisive factor of media choice, as all the communications outside Japan of Group A were made through e-mail.

Hypothesis 2 is supported for both groups A (t = -5.45, p = 0.0004) and B (t = -5.24, p = 0.0002). The statistical test shows a significant association between media choice and urgency of communication. In both Groups usage of e-mail drops as the urgency of communication increases (see Fig. 6). When it reduces to the ‘tomorrow’ category, e-mail usage is twice as high as the telephone in Group A and 28 times in Group B.

On the other hand, use of the telephone increases in both Groups as the urgency of the communication task increases. When the communication is within the '10 minutes' category, telephone usage is six times higher than e-mail in Group A but twice in Group B. Comparison of the media usage indicated that Group A, which prefers telephone, used e-mail more than telephone only when the urgency category falls to 'tomorrow' or later. In contrast Group B, which prefers e-mail used the telephone more than e-mail only when the urgency was within '10 minutes'. It is also noted that 52% of Group A communications are within the 1 h urgency category, which it is less than 35% in Group B (see Fig. 7).

Neither group utilized e-mail or telephone for persuasion or negotiation. More than $65\%$ of Group A's communication is in reporting, inquiry, and coordina tion. Less than 35% of communication depends on the type of discussion. In Group B, more than 55% of the communication is in reporting, inquiry, and coordination and, the balance is for discussion. It is noted that the higher number of messages within Group B may be due to a higher ratio of decision related tasks; these require a larger number of transactions.

Hypothesis 3 is supported for Group A (t = -2.36, p = 0.042) but not for Group B (t = -1.58, p = 0.139). This indicates that Group B members do not discriminate media according to the complexity of the communication tasks, whereas Group A members do. Data analysis shows a continuous drop in e-mail use and continuous rise in telephone use when the complexity of the communication task increases in Group A, though there is no such pattern in Group B (see Fig. 8). The two different patterns of media use by the two Groups suggest that if distributed-workers are encouraged to use a particular media, it can be used effectively to communicate complex tasks such as discussion.

## 4.4.2. Individual structure

Hypothesis 4 is supported for Group A ( $F = 16.6$ , $p = 0.001$ ) but not for Group B ( $F = 0.318$ , $p = 0.58$ ). These results indicate that only those experienced in using e-mail in Group A use e-mail while others are reluctant to use it. On the other hand, in Group B, e-mail is used widely irrespective of the members' e-mail experience. However, the average e-mail experience of Group B members is higher than in Group A. Average experience of e-mail is 1.15 years and 1.82 years, respectively for Group A and B. The greater experience of e-mail can be a factor that influence the greater use of e-mail in Group B. Statistical analysis shows that there is no association between e-mail experience and pattern of usage of e-mail, possibly this may result from the support and encouragement of management for e-mail in Group B.

Results of the statistical analysis indicate there is no significant relationship between the status of distributed-workers and use of e-mail. The significance of the statistical test for Group A (F = 0.38, p = 0.55) and for Group B (F = 1.46, p = 0.25) reject Hypothesis 5. In Group A, one sub-leader sent 26 mails more than 55% of the total; those who are in lower hierarchical positions sent fewer e-mail messages. Even in Group B, the two leaders sent 26 e-mail messages (second highest in Group A) and 14 e-mail messages out of 29 and 23, respectively. Those two leaders made more than 75% of their communications through e-mail.

## 4.4.3. Social influence

The different pattern of media use between the two distributed-work groups indicates a fundamental difference of the choice of media in the communication network. Results of the statistical analysis show a significant difference $t = -3.39, p = 0.004$ between use of e-mail among group A and B members, therefore, supporting Hypothesis 6. Encouragement and support of management for the use of e-mail in Group B leads to greater adoption of e-mail. The support from management also contributed to a change of attitude about the effectiveness of e-mail, and lead to it being widely used. Managers in Group B actively use e-mail in their job related communication. In Group A, the manager used e-mail only in 2 out of 14 transactions (14% of the total). Yet in contrast, the two leaders of Group B used e-mail to cover 76% of their communications.

Hypothesis 7 is strongly supported for Group A (F = 28.5, p = 0.013) and Group B (F = 257, p = 0.001). Data analysis shows that both the Group members use telephone and e-mail to communicate with their close friends. Group A members use telephone more than e-mail while Group B members use e-mail more than telephone to communicate with close friends. However, two sample t-test shows that there is no significant difference $t = -0.349, p = 0.737$ between the two Groups in their use of telephone and e-mail in communication with close friends.

Co-worker media selection seems not to influence the selection of e-mail for communication. Hypothesis 8 is rejected for both the Groups A (F = 4.40, p = 0.057) and B (F = 0.614, p = 0.445). However, those in Group A who used e-mail twice or more than twice during the experiment strongly agreed with the statement “I used e-mail because my co-workers used it.” In Group B, apart from three members, there was no agreement with this statement. E-mail oriented members in Group B apparently use e-mail whether the co-worker use it or not. In contrast, telephone oriented workers in Group A use it only if the recipient uses e-mail.

## 5. Conclusions

The lack of face to face communication opportunities is considered to be one of the main problems of distributed-work. Therefore, the use of communication media and its selection hold a very important place in the distributed-work environment: it has direct influence on the effectiveness and productivity of the arrangement. This research investigated the media selection behavior of distributed-workers. Media richness theory, social influence theory, and the theory of contextual structure guided the selection of the independent variables and building up of the hypothetical model for our study. The hypothetical model of the association between media selection and individual characteristics, contextual factors, and social influence was tested with the use of data collected at Fujitsu Kyushu Communication System Limited.

This study indicated that simple communication media, such as telephone and e-mail was effective enough to carry out distributed-work-related jobs satisfactorily: distributed-workers generally used telephone and e-mail in complex communication tasks, such as discussions. Apparently, distributed-work does not need to be costly and arrangements can be made with current available common telecommunication facilities without additional sophisticated new technologies. The contextual factors, such as distance, urgency of communication, and complexity of the task have some influence on the media choice of distributed workers. The only individual factor affecting the selection of media is the experience of workers in use of e-mail. Social factors, such as management encouragement and friendship with the recipient of the call also influence media choice.

It is likely that distributed-workers who are engaged in software development projects are more familiar with new technologies compared to other distributed-workers. Software development projects may not have decision making stages that require lengthy and complex discussion. Therefore, investigation of media choice of distributed-workers who are in different work environments is needed to generalize the findings. Further, our sample size was small and thus, a detailed analysis of influence of the independent factors on media choice could not be made.
