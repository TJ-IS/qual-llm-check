# The paradox of information systems: strategic value and low status

D.E. Avison<sup>a,\*</sup>, C.H. Cuthbertson<sup>b</sup>, P. Powell<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>De´partement SID, ESSEC, Cergy-Pontoise 95021, France

<sup>b</sup>Department of Informatics and Simulation, Cranfield University, RMCS Shrivenham, Swindon SN6 8LA, UK <sup>c</sup>School of Management, University of Bath, Claverton Down, Bath BA2 7AY, UK

Received 1 September 1997; received in revised form 1 December 1999; accepted 27 February 2000

## Abstract

Information systems (IS) have provided dramatic returns for some organisations. For many others, IS underpin most of their activities. Spending on IS can be very high. Few organisations can function without adequate IS. Yet, there is an apparent paradox in the reliance on information systems and supporting information technologies (IT) on the one hand and the status of the information systems function on the other. While information systems can be critical to an organisation’s ability to conduct and develop business, the information systems function is often considered as a secondary activity. To explore the paradox, this paper uses a cultural web model to assess the status of IS and IT activities as evidenced in the literature and through the study of four organisations. These serve to demonstrate the contradiction between the extent to which large organisations appear to rely on information systems and the low status of the information systems function. The implications of this are explored, and it is suggested that IS can be exploited fully only if there is a change in attitude and thereby an increase in status of the information systems function. q 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. ## 1. Introduction

Information systems (IS) can transform business and revolutionise the way business is conducted. Yet, senior managers and others often perceive the information systems function as having a secondary status within organisations. Information systems are judged in terms of speed of delivery, quality and cost—in the same way as contract cleaning and building maintenance might be judged. These comparisons and measures are not appropriate for a function that can transform business, yet they exist, perhaps, because of the low and inappropriate status attached to the IS function.

Jarvenpaa and Ives (1991) argue that general managers need to be involved in information systems due to the magnitude of expenditure involved, the ability of IS to enable business initiatives such as business process re-engineering, total quality management, globalisation and downsizing, and the weight of evidence which demonstrates that when general managers are not involved in IS, investment is wasted on automating inefficient processes. General managers are more likely to be willing to get involved with functions they perceive as having high status.

In order to investigate this paradox of high impact but low status, it is necessary to be able to describe the status of a function and those within it. Investigating status raises problems of formal definition, and its operationalisation in any study. Status concerns position, rank or importance. Status is an element of power, and while there has been little previous research on IS status, there is work on power—the ability or the authority to do something—which can inform the analysis.

Power is the ability to influence others’ behaviour. Lucas (1984) posits that the IS function “will be powerful” as it displays the characteristics that Hickson et al. (1971) identify as being sources of power. These sources are:

1. High coping with uncertainty—units which help to absorb and control uncertainty should have high power.

2. Low substitutability—easily substituted departments have low power.

3. High workforce pervasiveness and immediacy—these capture the extent to which a department is connected to others and how quickly the rest of the organisation would be affected if the department ceased operating.

4. High interdependence.

Yet Lucas’ empirical work does not support the contention that IS departments are powerful. He suggests that his results may be due to a lack of knowledge of the role of the IS function by others in the organisation. Subsequent work by Saunders and Scammell (1986) concurs that IS has low power. Again, the lack of awareness by others is suggested as a cause, along with ISs purportedly low political and negotiation skills. Saunders and Scammell also suggest that the type of IS used will affect the IS function’s power. In those organisations where IS may be described as residing in McFarlan and McKenney’s (1983) support or factory quadrant, it is likely that the IS function will not have high power, while the converse is probable if IS is strategic, or at least potentially so. In a related vein, Saunders and Scammell (1982) suggest that IS power will vary from industry to industry. Markus and Bjørn-Andersen (1987) differentiate between having and using power, and exercising power but that power not being perceived. This dichotomy of users and IS staff being either aware or unaware of the other’s power leads, the authors conclude, to either mutual negotiation, professional manipulation, user resistance or unintended influence.

Since this work was carried out, the role of the IS function and hence its power (and status) have been affected by a number of countervailing forces. The moves to IS outsourcing may have the effect of reducing IS power. On the other hand, IS becoming increasingly strategic is likely to have increased its power. Similarly, the growth of interorganisational systems and end-user (distributed) systems may well have had a positive impact in power terms.

This paper investigates one element of power, that is, status. There are a number of aspects to status that can be operationalised. The status of a function may be revealed by its position within the hierarchy and the value of resources given to it. It may be that function status is reflected by that of the function head. For example, the high status of the finance director might be reflected in a place on the board and a large budget. Yet, many indicators of status are more subtle and context-specific. The finance director’s allocated space for a large company car, the size of the office and the colour of the chair might all contribute to the aura of a high-status employee. Status provides the power to influence others and control working practices in the organisation. It is unlikely that a group with a low status could transform a business. The effectiveness of the IS function depends, in part, on its power vis-a\`-vis other departments. As Lucas (1984) argues: “to a large extent, the success of information processing in a firm is dependent on the information services department and its relationship to other departments, managers and users”. There is also an associated issue of credibility. A function that lacks credibility with users and other managers is unlikely to be perceived as one that can effect major change in the organisation.

There is no universal set of status indicators. Status is largely dependent on internal context. For example, a desk by the window in Japan might indicate low status whereas in the West it would generally be considered desirable and thus suggests higher status. Indicators need to be found that participants agree, point to relative status within their particular environment. In this research, these indicators emerge through the study of four organisations, which were investigated using in-depth surveys; they do not necessarily translate between organisations. In exploratory, qualitative research, broad categorisations of status only are possible but this should not be seen as inhibiting the investigation. However, the evidence suggests that information systems (IS) and the supporting information technologies (IT) have crucial importance to the success of each organisation; although this is not reflected in the status of the IS function within these organisations.

This paper also uses evidence from the literature which further demonstrates this paradox. Johnson and Scholes’ (1993) cultural web model is used as a framework for assessing the literature and is also applied to the material obtained through investigating the four organisations. The paper concludes by considering ways of addressing the situation.

## 2. The strategic importance of information systems in organisations

We will first review the literature which comments on the strategic importance of information systems in organisations and the status of the IS function. There is a widespread view that IS can play a major strategic role in organisations. Lee and Kim (1996) argue that “the role of IS has changed dramatically over the last three decades, from passive automation or augmentation tool to a strategic, competitive device for transforming organisational structures”. In some business sectors, particularly financial services, information systems have revolutionised the way the business is conducted. First Direct, the first UK organisation to offer “armchair banking” could not do business without IS/IT; a UK retailer Tesco gained competitive advantage through its introduction of a loyalty card; others, such as Xerox, seek to re-engineer through the use of IT.

These add to the database of key strategic projects which started with American Airlines’ SABRE, United Airlines’ Apollo, Baxters’ (American Hospital Supply’s) ASAP, Federal Express’ COSMOS, McKesson’s Economost, Citicorp’s GTN, Merril Lynch’ CMA and the Philadelphia National Bank’s MAC which have all been incorporated into the literature since the 1960s (Avison et al., 1996).

IT/IS has been cited as an enabler of business process re-engineering, strategic alliances and joint ventures, and competitive advantage. McKeen and Smith (1996) claim that “with re-engineering, IS has the opportunity to demonstrate conclusively its value to the organisation and to participate in business decisions at the highest organisational levels”. Hewitt (1995), reporting on re-engineering at Xerox, states that “with today’s technology it is possible to support infrastructures which allow corporations to be innovative and flexible in ways previously impossible”. Other literature contradicts this view (see, for example, Galliers and Swan, 1999).

Parker (1996) describes a strategic alliance as the “coming together of equals to jointly produce some product or service” and is a response to the “global market place”. It may occur between an organisation and its customers, suppliers or competitors. In the UK, a recent alliance between Tesco and the National Westminster Bank provides banking facilities for supermarket customers. Porter (1985) argues that “the growing sophistication of information systems is…a powerful force in opening up possibilities for interrelationships”.

The SABRE ticket reservation system and the ASAP customer ordering system are among the most quoted examples of organisations using IT for competitive advantage. Porter (1985) states that “competitive advantage grows fundamentally out of a value a firm is able to create for its buyers that exceeds the firm’s cost of creating it”. Information systems provide a way by which competitive advantage may be gained. Large organisations continue to investigate emerging technologies. Examples in the UK include smart cards for BP petrol stations, self-scanning at Safeway supermarkets, data warehousing at Woolworth and virtual reality for warehouse planning at Boots (a chain store) in an effort to gain competitive advantage.

These examples demonstrate the ability of IS to transform or support the transformation of businesses. There is an issue of who is, or who should lead such transformations. Often it is senior management, but Bashein et al. (1993) argue for IS professionals leading BPR efforts. IS would only be able to lead such activities if it has sufficient power, credibility and status in the eyes of the rest of the organisation.

However, this pivotal role for IS has not led to increased status for the information systems function. Outsourcing (Willcocks et al., 1996) might be viewed as a trend that demotes the IS/IT function, reinforcing the requirement that it cuts costs and makes efficiency gains. Ainger et al. (1995) argue that “the step beyond the current enthusiasm for business process re-engineering will be increased outsourcing of IT facilities, potentially making a nonsense of intelligent IT strategy and corporate strategy in general”. Earl (1992) claims that IT budgets are being capped and pruned. He argues that the IT function is under attack with IS departments being rationalised or sold off. IT operations are being outsourced and IT directors replaced.

## 3. Model selection

The assessment of status is largely a matter of judgement and there is no prescriptive method for assessing that of IS/IT. Though the subjective nature of status assessment is problematic, it is the purpose of this paper to question rather than resolve the contradiction between the capability of information systems to transform organisations and the status of the IS/IT function. The study, based mainly on four organisations, is largely qualitative because there are no quantitative indicators of status. Status indicators “emerged” in conversation or through observation, essentially through an analysis of organisational culture.

Morgan (1986) defines culture as “the process of reality construction that allows people to see and understand particular events, actions, objects, utterances, or situations in distinctive ways”. Culture as a metaphor for organisations has been discussed increasingly since the late 1970s. Many authors suggest ways of modelling organisational culture including Kotter (1978), Pettigrew (1979), Peters and Waterman (1982), Deal and Kennedy (1988), and Johnson and Scholes (1993). All cultural models include elements to describe formal and informal organisational structures, power and control structures, routines and storytelling, and, as stated by Morgan, should not be used mechanistically. Here, Johnson and Scholes’ cultural web is employed because of its breadth, but other approaches, in particular, Kotter’s organisational dynamics are drawn upon.

Ward and Griffiths (1996) adapt the cultural web to look at information systems in an organisation, with the view of developing an appropriate IS strategy. They use a questionnaire approach as their fact-finding method, whereas Kotter uses available documentation to begin modelling, followed by discussions to enrich the model. In this research, semi-structured interviews, documents and observation are used to describe the organisations studied. Ward and Peppard (1996) use the cultural web to good effect when exploring the supposed “cultural gap” between the IS function and the rest of the business.

The cultural web is described by Johnson and Scholes (1993) as “a useful tool for understanding the way in which beliefs and assumptions, linked to political, symbolic and structural aspects of the organisation, guide and constrain the development of strategy”. With its inclusion of both hard and soft aspects of organisational modelling, and the acceptance of the effect that these elements have on decision making, the model provides a good basis for determining the status of IS/IT. Ward and Peppard (1996), in their exploration of the cultural gap between the IS function and the rest of the business, claim that the cultural web provides “a powerful tool to enable the business and the IT organisation to articulate and understand the cultural gap that exists between them”. In this research, the model is used as an interpreter of secondary research as well as the basis for primary research.

The use of Johnson and Scholes’ cultural web extends the study into less well-trodden areas for information systems, particularly stories and myths, rites and rituals, and symbols. Though this caused some difficulties, both in linking to the existing literature and seeking data from primary sources, the overall result is an enriched discussion.

The literature is reviewed through each strand of the cultural web.

## 4.1. Organisational structures

Organisational structures are most clearly and traditionally evidenced in an organisation chart showing hierarchy, reporting structures and job grading. Management style, location of employees and patterns of informal contact can provide further evidence. Formal and informal organisational structures suggest other important relationships and support of power structures. The status of any particular function is likely to be reflected in the organisational structure. For example, if the head of a business unit is non-executive, the implied status of that unit will be lower than one where the head is on the Board.

Porter (1985), in his value chain analysis, describes an organisation’s activities in terms of two categories. Activities may be “involved in the physical creation of the product or service and its sale and transfer to the buyer as well as after sales assistance”. Such activities are considered by Porter to be primary. The remaining activities “support the primary activities and each other”, and are termed secondary or “support activities”. Other authors, for example, Earl (1989), use this classification, or similar groupings, to differentiate the activities that form the organisation’s core business and those activities that are necessary to ensure resource allocation and co-ordination of the main activities. In Porter’s generic value chain, the primary activities are those of inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service. Support activities are firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development and procurement.

IS/IT, then, seems decidedly a support activity. However, Porter claims that “information systems technology is particularly pervasive in the value chain, since every value activity creates and uses information.” Here perhaps, one of a number of paradoxes is revealed: information systems might be omnipresent while the information systems function is impotent; members of the function might find themselves at once vital and powerless. Information is seen as valuable, but the information systems function is not seen as equally valuable.

McKeen and Smith (1996), in their study of ten leading organisations, claim that “it is not uncommon for IS to be reorganised every two or three years as its management tries to keep up with evolving organisational needs, technical trends, and the latest IS fashions”. They go on to state that “poor structure should not be used as an excuse for poor performance” and that an effective structure derives from a close alignment of objectives to business needs. Peppard and Ward (1998) support this when they state that “as IT assumes an increasingly central role in the strategies of companies, how they organise for IT becomes crucial. The task is not just the provision of IT and IT services to the business, but to ensure that the benefit and value of any investment is leveraged”.

However, IS executives infrequently have direct contact with the Board and are even more rarely members of the Board. Kelly (1996) reports that only 37% of organisations had IS representation at Board level and in some organisations the role was being removed. Indeed, the post of chief information officer (CIO) in the US has such high turnover rates that CIO was deemed to stand for “career is over”. Peppard and Ward (1998) observe that in many organisations, the IT director is not part of the “inner sanctum” and very often reports to the finance department. Earl (1989) claims that “IT directors frequently complain of inadequate corporate support” and, though the literature contains much advice to CEOs and IS professionals about the potential role of information systems people as senior executives, there is little indication that this advice is followed. A low status for information systems is clearly indicated.

## 4.2. Control systems

High status individuals and groups have control over resources and working practices, and the autonomy is reflected in the organisation’s control systems. These include methods of appraisal and reward, promotion opportunities and grading, employee development, and methods of funding and evaluation. They support the organisational paradigm to “emphasise what is important to the organisation, and focus attention and activity” (Johnson and Scholes, 1993). The way in which IS/IT is funded, responsibility accounting relating to information systems and the way in which IS projects are justified, might be indicative of the status of the IS function.

In contrast to treating information systems as an investment, Verner et al. (1996) claim that long term goals may be neglected when charge-out systems are introduced in organisations that use financially driven evaluation procedures for department and managerial performance reviews. Thus, a business unit may not be able to afford a project with longterm benefits. The tension here is between funding for information systems and responsibility accounting. A poorly implemented, or poorly understood, accounting system is able to reduce the impact that IS/IT can make by taking the initiative away from the information systems professionals.

If budget size alone were an indication of status, then IS/IT would have high status. However, Earl (1989) identifies the question of “how much should we spend on IT” as “the dominant concern” when attempting to resolve “the funding issue”. He goes on to outline the major methods employed as aligning IT funding to typical spending ratios in other organisations, or viewing IT as capital expenditure and directing funding towards IT projects with a zero or positive net present value. The popularity of spending ratios is confirmed by the Price Waterhouse IT Review (1996) when the average IT budget as a percentage of turnover was 1.8%, with variations from 4.5% in computer services, 2.5% in financial services to 0.8% in manufacturing. Earl (1989) refers to “the felt need [by IT directors] to continually justify IT expenditure levels or the feeling that recent financial support is about to disappear”.

Willcocks (1994) studied 50 organisations of which “only 8%…could tell us whether the IT investment subsequently returned a higher or lower return than other non-IT investments”. If the IS function finds difficulty in calculating and communicating its success, it seems unlikely that others will perceive the function as successful, regardless of the way in which IS/IT is funded or accounted.

## 4.3. Power structures

As discussed, power and status are inseparable. A high status group is able to influence others, taking an active part in decision-making and policy formulation. Kotter (1978) defines the dominant coalition as “that minimum group of co-operating employees who oversee the organisation as a whole and control its basic policy making” and a study of power structures has the potential to differentiate between the status of business units. Power structures are not necessarily made explicit by the organisation chart, which may only imply powerful groupings, devolution of authority and influential personalities. Decision-making may be centralised, devolved throughout the organisation or exist in pockets of authority.

In relation to information systems, a powerful champion for the business unit may increase its status. Devolution of responsibility and authority throughout the organisation may provide unit heads with the ability to reflect their attitude towards IS/IT in their buying decisions. Power structures might be identified by exploring such areas as the type of decisions made by information systems professionals, how the IS/IT strategy is formulated, and the means by which IS strategy is linked to business needs.

McKeen and Smith (1996) suggest that taking business decisions does not rest lightly on the information systems professionals’ shoulders. They found that “IS managers who are charged with the responsibility of maximising the benefit of information technology within their organisations often find themselves forced to make critical decisions regarding IT with little guidance”. Yet Martin et al. (1995) claim that “IT risks are becoming increasingly entangled with business risks so that the CEO can no longer afford to delegate these decisions to IS managers alone”. The implication of this might be that power transfers to the CEO in the absence of a CIO.

Some decisions appear to be taken in the IS user units, though McKeen and Smith warn that “effective decision-making can only be achieved if user managers are familiar with systems development work”. In IS project management, Newman and Sabherwal (1996) have isolated three important decisions taken by senior managers: making initial commitment, withdrawal of commitment and making commitment to a new approach. Customer commitment is considered essential for the success of a system.

It is therefore interesting to consider what factors affect commitment. Abdul-Gader and Kozar (1995) attempt to “investigate what motivates a manager to purchase a computer for his or her organisational unit’s use or request an information technology application”. Although this study focuses on the purchase of “computers”, in looking at the decision to purchase (as against post-purchase satisfaction), the authors raise interesting questions relevant here. Based on a sample of 97 decision-makers in the US and Saudi Arabia, the authors found that the decision to purchase information technology products and applications was affected by personal beliefs and attitudes relating to IT. The authors conclude that “computer alienated decision-makers are less likely to have an intention to buy (IT products and applications)”. Over 40% of decision-makers, regardless of nationality, were judged computer alienated.

## 4.4. Stories

An organisation’s culture may be apparent through its stories and myths. The stories that are retold may reinforce the status of a group. Such stories include those about employees who tried to “beat the system”, runaway projects, spectacular successes or failures, or the project leader who never seems to go home. The stories, through telling of the unusual, may reinforce the culture of the organisation or alienate one business unit from that culture.

According to Boje (1991) “storytelling is the preferred sense-making currency of human relationships among internal and external stakeholders”. The stories are incrementally refined and re-interpreted over time. Boje stresses the need to understand the linguistic context of the stories and that there are implicit rules in story-telling—who can tell them, to whom and when. Peters and Austin (1985) show that story-telling is more prevalent in turbulent times as a mechanism to help understand the unfolding dynamics. Martin et al. (1983) argue that stories share common elements and express common concerns, despite claims of unique cultural manifestations. They identify seven types of stories that are common. These are seen to generate as well as reflect changes in organisations and include rich detail and carry multiple interpretations. The commonalities between stories stem from dualities—expressing tensions arising from a conflict between organisational exigencies and the values of individual employees, from the stories as serving rationalisations of the past, and from identification and dichotomy—where either the organisation and individual is seen to have control that others do not have, or, in negative stories, where employees distance themselves from the organisation.

Unfortunately there is little IS literature (though lots of anecdotal evidence) on these important issues, though McKeen and Smith (1996) observe that “management’s acceptance of new roles for IS is frequently coloured by how effectively IS has worked in the past”. Stories of failure may lead to a reluctance of senior management to share decisionmaking with IS management. McKeen and Smith go on to point to the “techie” image surrounding IS professionals, poor user–IS relationships, delivery of systems that have not benefited the business and a lack of internal marketing. The implication is that these factors contribute negatively to the status of the IS function.

## 4.5. Rituals and routines

Pentland and Rueter (1994) suggest that routines as used to explain the inertial quality of organisational structure and see them as the cornerstone of theories of organisational learning and adaptation. “Routines…are essentially complex patterns of social interaction”. The literature on routines encompasses the cognitive processes of individuals and structural and institutional constraints. Giddens (1984) cautions that routines should not be thought of as mindless or automatic but that they are an effortful accomplishment. Routines and rituals share common roots as any moves are shaped by the physical and ritual dimensions of organisational structure as well as the need to draw on cognitive resources distributed among individuals (Pentland, 1992). Pentland and Rueter (1994) argue that the enabling and continuing structures that typify organisational work situations—such as hierarchy, division of labour task-specific and situation-specific issues— naturally give rise to regular patterns of action, or routines.

Procedures and how they are followed, the organisation’s rituals and routines may give an indication of what is rewarded and valued by the organisation. Martin et al. (1995) acknowledge the importance of the rituals and routines to the information systems function when suggesting that “the new systems that work best are those that are aligned not only with the business but also with the way people think and work”. Rituals and routines might include the coffee break and the office party, but in an IS context, might also include the use or abuse of IS development methods and the conventions surrounding the interaction between IS professionals and users.

It is revealing to consider that it is the altering or dismantling of rituals and routines of other business activities within the organisation that forms a major part of the work of the IS/IT function, particularly when IS is used to drive re-engineering. When Ainger et al. (1995) claim that “people resent not being involved in the introduction of new technologies, altered values and revised working practices” they describe one possible source of friction between IS/IT and user business units.

## 4.6. Symbols

Finally, the status of an individual or group may be evidenced in such symbols as the size and location of their office, make and model of their company car and the like. Little attention in the IS literature seems to be given to this.

However, Feldman and March (1981) investigate information as a signal and symbol. They contrast the “theoretical” use of information with its “real” use. Information as a symbol involves, “the gathering of information (which) provides a ritualistic assurance that appropriate attitudes about decision making exist”. They also see information as a representation of competence and a re-affirmation of social virtue. Command of information enhances perceived competencies and inspires confidence. Thirdly, their work evidences that “ritual acknowledgement of important values celebrates a shared interpretation of reality”. Finally, Feldman and March argue that requesting and assembling information are ways of making social life meaningful and acceptable. In conclusion, what starts out as a symbol becomes a party in its own right and influences the organisation. In some situations and types of organisations (policy making, public, and at senior levels) over-collection activities may exist because information is seen as a signal and symbol. These observations again suggest that information is seen as important but information systems are under-valued.

## 4.7. Organisational paradigm

The organisational paradigm, evident in the centre of Fig. 1, describes the overall picture of an organisation as supported by the cultural web. Organisational values and beliefs are seldom formalised. Where information to describe an organisational paradigm is available—in mission statements, historical reference, location and vocabulary—this evidence is rarely discussed in the IS literature. However, it is here that the paradox might further reveal itself in a mismatch between the organisation’s reliance on information systems and the status of IS/IT. The importance of information systems to organisations has been demonstrated. Yet Ward and Peppard (1996) describe a “classic IT department” with an organisational paradigm reflected in the attitude that IS/IT is “here to serve the business but we do what we think they want in a way we want to do it”. Several authors, including Earl (1992) and Grindley (1995), support the perception that the IS department has a remit to serve, and a tendency to create and maintain information systems with insufficient consultation, often unrelated to business goals. Use of the word “serve” is interesting, perhaps describing the IS function as a secondary activity, often seen in information systems department objectives (Earl, 1989; Ward and Griffiths, 1996) given pre-eminence alongside terminology, suggesting an enabling or catalytic role.

Johnson and Scholes’ (1993) cultural web has provided us with a lens through which to view the IS literature from a cultural perspective. The review uncovers a number of contradictions: information systems should be considered an investment but are often seen as a cost, information systems can change the direction of the business yet are considered a service, and information systems may be strategic but important decisions are taken without consultation.

Many authors have commented on the apparent disparity between “what is” and “what should be”, and offer both criticism and advice. Information systems professionals are called upon to become more business-aware and better communicators. McKeen and Smith (1996) suggest that “it is no longer enough for IS professionals to see themselves as service providers” and entreat IS professionals to develop “improved consulting and communications abilities and to become visionaries with an appreciation for enterprisewide business operations and the transformational nature of IT”. Senior executives are not blameless according to Ward and Griffiths (1996) who conclude that “over the long term, any organisation will get the information systems it deserves, according to the approach adopted to the use and management of IS/IT”. Jarvenpaa and Ives (1990) investigate CEOs’ views of IT as reported in firms’ annual reports. They find that IT is referred to more in information intensive firms’ reports, and that while the CEOs’ views of IT did not change much between 1982 and 1987, there was an improvement compared to reports from the 1970s. The contradiction apparent in much of the literature may lend weight to the view that the IS function may sometimes have difficulty finding its place in organisational life. It might at once be considered both an integral, vital component of the organisation and a minor support to the business activities. Yet, the tag of “strategic”

onto a number of other functions (strategic accounting, strategic marketing) shows a similar bid for a role within organisations.

Insofar as a status can be examined in the literature, it appears that though researchers might wish for a high status for the information systems function, as with power, a low status in generally conferred.

An indication of whether the perceived low status of the information systems function within organisations has changed over the last 10 years or so is offered by the Price Waterhouse IT Review (1996). Since the early 1990s, cost control, speed of delivery and integrating IT with corporate objectives were reported as being the dominant concerns of IT directors. These measures of speed, cost and quality may indicate a low status group, appropriate to catering and cleaning contracts. In 1995/96 they were joined by the major issues of transforming business through the use of IT, the provision of an organisationwide IT infrastructure and organisational and business uncertainty. This might suggest that the concerns of the IS function are increasingly business-oriented. The evident business orientation appears to have resulted in many organisations in the development of a “service culture”, inconsistent with a high status group. Peppard and Ward (1998), whilst not considering the servicing role as an indication of low status, claim “the traditional role of the IT organisation as the developer and maintainer of IT systems has been usurped by a variety of factors and its function now includes a significant service component”.

## 5. Research method

Having explored the elements of the cultural web in an IS context, we wished to assess the status of the information systems function as represented in organisational life using empirical evidence. We first designed a questionnaire and this was sent to 120 organisations. This attempted to reveal the status of the IS function as evidenced by elements such as size, structure, job titles and responsibilities. However, this provided a shallow analysis, ranking the status of IS/IT in terms of easily observable and measurable phenomena. It would have revealed very little within the cultural web model. Perceived status is complex and reflected in observations difficult to capture by simple surveying methods. Myers (1996) provides examples of alternative qualitative research methods in an IS context. Examples of more complex phenomena are the vocabulary in common use and a rich vein of gossip. The questionnaire therefore proved to be an unsuccessful strategy on its own. Happily, however, it revealed eight organisations willing to participate in an in-depth survey. These were large, UK organisations, which ranged from public sector, through government-regulated organisations to the private sector in financial services, manufacturing and retail.

Remenyi et al. (1999) suggest that an in-depth survey “attempts to obtain in-depth evidence from a relatively small number of informants through a series of interviews. The informant is allowed to speak freely on the subject of interest to the researcher”. Robson (1993) argues that such an approach allows the researcher “to seek new insights” so that the phenomena can be explored whilst gathering explanatory and descriptive material.

Of the eight organisations, four allowed sufficient access to satisfy the requirements of the study. Although this is disappointing to some extent, it is difficult to see what could have been gained from a wider study for the purposes of this paper. More industries could have been covered or different sizes of organisation. However, it seems unlikely that more significant conclusions could have been drawn. The inclusion of four cases, rather than one in-depth case study, however, does add a valuable dimension to the study. The four organisations are an aviation company, a government agency, an oil company and a health trust.

A largely qualitative research paradigm was adopted which allows exploration of the apparent contradiction between the all-pervasive and powerful presence of information systems with the perceived low status of the IS function. A picture of each organisation was built and status indicators gradually emerged within the cultural context. The work was undertaken over a six-month period starting in 1996. Conventionally, semi-structured interviews are used in an in-depth survey, but we supplemented this with observation and documentary analysis to build a fuller description of each case. The information gathered was largely directed originally by a questionnaire used by Ward and Griffiths (1996) to aid the development of an appropriate IS strategy and Kotter’s (1978) approach to documentary analysis. The information obtained was used to populate the cultural web.

Interviews were conducted with a cross-section of information systems professionals and users in each organisation. The use of IS and non-IS personnel was valuable in capturing a variety of “world views”. A multi-faceted description of each organisation and its information systems provision was essential to explore the status of a function that lies both inside and outside the function itself. Questions were intentionally open-ended and the interviews semi-structured. This made the data difficult to record, cross-reference and compare, and the interview format difficult to prepare for, control and predict. However, a rich picture emerged consequently for each organisation.

Significant information was available from published company reports and internal documentation. Documents were useful, not only for quantitative data such as turnover, profit and organisational structure, but also for qualitative data, such as the Health Trust newsletter with its emphasis on “care” and “patient needs” or the language used in Aviation Co’s to describe the information systems migration objectives.

Observation provided an additional source of data on topics such as location, accommodation, activity and body language. The wealth of observable data was not fully appreciated early in the study and a formal method of recording such data would have been valuable. However, much of the observed data was included in the results, such as the separation of the business migration team from their information systems colleagues at Aviation Co.

In his “rules” of qualitative research, Silverman (1993) warns against “treating the actor’s point of view as an explanation” and states that interview data are “never simply raw but are both situated and textual”. Yin (1994) also suggests that some of the criticism of the case study as a research method is derived from researchers taking a single perspective. By using interviews, observation and document analysis from a variety of sources, and including a wide range of personnel and a minimum of two people at each level at each site (senior IS executives, IS professionals and IS users) it is hoped that there is convincing evidence to provide a rich description of each organisation within the context of information systems provision. It is hoped that this rich picture leads to insights not easily gained by the actors within the situation, and therefore outside an individual’s explanation.

The data gathered from the co-operating organisations does not lead to the identification of a unified experience for the IS function in all organisations. The explanation is multifaceted, and leads to recommendations requiring further research. Silverman (1993) agrees that it is important to avoid “single-element” explanations, and that fruitful research might focus “upon the processes through which the relations between elements are articulated”. For example, an IS manager reporting to a finance director might be seen as an indication of low status in one organisation but a sign of elevated status in another depending, perhaps, on the personalities involved, the historical context or the status of the finance director.

Silverman (1993) argues that researchers commonly seek a context-free “truth”, but, whilst accepting that the study is mediated through the researchers’ reasoning, it is hoped that the use of Johnson and Scholes’ (1993) cultural web, and the involvement of both IS personnel and their customers, may make the organisational values and beliefs more apparent and bring them openly to the discourse. No attempt is made to produce a single explanation.

## 6. Strategic value and low status?

A convenient and purposeful sample was chosen, providing good contact with a variety of organisations in the following four different industries.

(a) The aviation company (Aviation Co): Aviation Co is part of a portfolio management organisation. It manufacturers aircraft and supports and upgrades existing fleets. It has 2500 employees at their manufacturing site. The parent organisation has an annual turnover in the region of £3,500 M of which Aviation Co forms £250–300 M.

The transforming capability of IS is fully appreciated at Aviation Co, where up to 2% of the turnover is spent on information systems. The information systems group comprises 100 individuals. The organisation began improvement of all business processes in 1994 and is due to complete process improvement by 2001.

(b) The government agency (National Supplier): National Supplier is a public corporation involved in overseas development. National Supplier acts as an international supplier of goods and services. The organisation has 650 employees based in London, with fieldworkers deployed around the world. National Supplier has a turnover of £65 M and has been in receipt of government funding until its recent change to a private foundation.

Information systems were considered to have an important role to play at National Supplier both in terms of the way business is conducted and as a product for sale. National Supplier trades on its international reputation for providing, among its many products, “sustainable institutional and infrastructural strengthening, capacity building and the transfer of appropriate technology”.

(c) The oil company (Oil PLC): Oil PLC employs 10,500 people world-wide, in the chemicals and petroleum industries and has a turnover of £4,750 M. The organisation has recently appointed a director of IT to co-ordinate information technology development.

Oil PLC is the only one of the case organisations to have appointed an IT director. This recent appointment reflects the stated view at Oil PLC that there has been too little investment in IS. It appears that where business units have gained from exploiting information systems, they have rarely transferred benefits to the organisation as a whole. Information systems are seen as having great potential to transform the organisation.

## 6.1. Organisational structures

In organisations where the IS function achieves a high status, it might be expected that organisational structures separate support activities from the strategic activities, and give representation on the board of directors. By reviewing Figs. 2–5, some comparisons are possible. Aviation Co and National Supplier use a separation of technical systems, applications systems and client services. In Health Trust, information services and IT support, whilst declaring a “close relationship”, have independent line management. A significant amount of end-user computing is apparent in Health Trust. Within National Supplier one group has its own information systems team of 12 employees, again with independent line management. For Oil PLC, the separation of strategic IS from operational IS is so complete that there is no line management from the director of IT to the operational units.

Only one organisation, Oil PLC, has a Director of IT, a non-executive reporting to the Director of Finance. The appointment has been made within the last 14 months. Previously, the responsibility for IT fell to the Director of Finance. The remaining three organisations have a varied route to the board. In Aviation Co, the Technical Services Director has responsibility for IS together with quality, pre-production stores, avionics stores, flight shed and site facilities. Perhaps unsurprisingly, his post is referred to as the “odds and sods” directorship. For Health Trust, Information Services is represented by the Business Support Executive, who has no line management to the Department of Information Services. Information Services became separate from Business Support two years ago and a more direct route to the Trust Board has not been established. The Information Services Manager for National Supplier reports directly to the Managing Director.

All four organisations studied have undergone significant changes in structure over the last two years, and anticipate further changes over the next five years. The IT Director for Oil PLC makes the observation that the advantage of structural change is the “energy it generates”, commenting particularly on the move from a centralised IS function to decentralised and back to centralised as a balance is sought between infrastructure and provision.

The four organisations studied have varying levels of distance from the board and this may reflect the perceived importance of IS. The Oil PLC appointment of an IT director was in response to a perceived pressure to exploit IT. In Health Trust, the great distance from the Trust Board makes it difficult for Information Services to adopt a strategic role. Distance from the board emerges as a status indicator, as does separation of the support or secondary activities from those activities with strategic implications. In this regard, all the organisations seem to display low status for the IS/IT function.

## 6.2. Control systems

The status of a business unit might be reflected in systems to monitor and control activities. High status units would be expected to have a great deal of autonomy and have generous rewards. In all the organisations studied, IS projects are approved and prioritised based on a business case. Further, small projects, or those considered operational such as test bed monitoring at Oil PLC or Lotus Notes advice from Information Services at Health Trust, are treated as an expense, with charge-out in all but Aviation Co. Charge-out, when applied, is a paper transaction at cost price.

In Aviation Co, there is no charge-out for internal customers, though some external work is undertaken at market price. All projects deemed to be of strategic importance and/ or highly visible may be viewed as an investment and have to be signed by a sponsoring director. Evaluation may involve the user community as it did with the evaluation of Windows95 and Windows NT. There are user groups so as to enable communication. For Oil PLC, an “enthusiastic senior sponsor with an ability to share a vision” may be the only justification needed for multi-million pound IS investments.

In Health Trust, the Chief Executive chairs an Information Strategy Group. The group comprises a Clinical Information sub-group, made up of doctors, nurses, paramedics, a general practitioner and the head of Information Services, and a Capital IT Project Management Board responsible for prioritising and monitoring IT projects. Strategic projects for Health Trust are presented to the Capital IT Project Management Board and capital funding is sought from the Capital Control Group, a sub-group of the Capital Review Group. A consultant must support each business case.

The four example organisations admit to little post-implementation assessment. Methods for the development of IS and project management are used in Health Trust and National Supplier where the UK Government’s Central Computer and Telecommunications Agency (CCTA) remains influential in choice and use of methods. Aviation Co has used structured methods in the past but considers them “bureaucratic”. A movement towards packaged solutions is seen as part of the reason for this shift away from formalised methods. Oil PLC has no imposed standard on operational units.

There appears from the organisations studied to be a move towards separation of service from strategic IS development, with operational systems treated as an expense, but strategic systems requiring less cost/benefit justification. Business drivers are sought in all cases, placing IS projects in competition for prioritisation in line with other projects.

The status indicators to emerge from a study of the systems used to control IS activities include methods of justifying, funding, accounting and evaluating information systems projects, and control of working practices. A high status group might expect information systems to be initiated by the IS function, selected with regard to business criteria, considered an investment and measured by long-term returns. Such a high status scenario was not evidenced in any of the four organisations studied.

## 6.3. Power structures

The size of the budget under the control of the information systems function varied from £0.1 to £35 M, and this appears to depend in part on industry, maturity and degree of centralisation of the information systems provision. In all the organisations, the board is responsible for deciding the size of the budget, with IS personnel having a great deal of influence in all but Health Trust. The criteria used for deciding the size of the budget are the percentage of turnover or the previous year’s expenditure. Control of the IS budget resides with information systems management.

At Aviation Co a tension was revealed between the corporate desire for standardisation and the great desire for aircraft groupings to be independent. The IS professionals reject both standardisation and independent development where such initiatives lack business benefits. For example, an enforced change to MS Office cost 980 working days for limited business gain. The number of PCs has risen from 1500 to 1900 without justification.

Pressure is also perceived as coming from a more technically literate workforce lacking understanding of information management issues.

No evidence of a dominant coalition containing any IS professional was detected. As power and status are inseparable, the conclusion is that the IS groups have low status. The widely held view that there is a separation of business decisions and information systems decisions is consistent with the organisations studied.

## 6.4. Stories and myths

Both IS members and customers tell stories and myths that underpin the culture of a business unit. Stories that are commonly retold might give clues relating to the status of the business unit as perceived by members and others. Personnel in the contributing organisations were encouraged to tell stories that are often retold and any newcomer to the organisation might be expected to learn about within a week or two. Though asking for stories seemed to surprise interviewees, they often quickly recounted tales and could tie stories directly into organisational values and beliefs.

A good example was provided by Aviation Co with the stories surrounding the development of a “home grown” materials requirement planning system (MRPS). The failure of MRPS cost the business three months’ trading and provides much material for storytelling both inside and outside the IS function. Stories abound about the 150 IS staff and users involved in the MRPS project working hard for long hours. A post-mortem by external consultants blamed failure on the lack of a senior sponsor or champion, lack of business focus and unrealistic goals. However, as the net result was a poor quality system, IS personnel were viewed as unprofessional, felt undervalued and suffered from stress. Redundancies followed and the users involved consider MRPS as a career hiatus. IS professionals, users and senior management at Aviation Co thought that the stories serve as a warning for future major developments. The development of the current business migration project, it is felt, is “flavoured” by the MRPS disaster.

Other stories often told at Aviation Co promote an organisation-wide culture of enterprise within the confines of an institution. This is one of Martin et al.’s (1983) archetypes. One story was about a fitter who refitted a dinghy in the workshops and was discovered by Security trying to get it back over the high security fencing. The quick-thinking fitter gave the impression that he was trying to get the dinghy into the workshop and was told to take it back home. The story even suggests that the security guard helped the fitter complete his journey to his car. It is remarkable how often this story is retold. As Boje (1991) suggests, there are many possible interpretations for a single story. It is interesting to note that Aviation Co was the only organisation where those interviewed inside and outside the IS function told the same stories in the same way.

Health Trust is mostly staffed by health care professionals with pockets of IT end-user expertise. Many stories promote a view of an enthusiastic amateur, or information systems misuse or avoidance. One story relates to an anaesthetist who developed a system for prioritising waiting lists. On the basis of this system, the anaesthetist appeared on radio and in the press, and the hospital still gets visitors from around the world who have shown an interest in the system. However, Health Trust has never used the system as it was never accepted by the consultants. Another story, told perhaps to emphasise the differences between a caring environment and a business, surrounds the introduction of an automated ward rota system. Before the system was introduced, busy nurses reluctant to place administration above patient care, would create the rotas at home. After introduction, the nurses, whilst declaring themselves satisfied with the system, prepared the rotas at home and entered them on the system when they returned to the ward. The system had simply added to their workload.

Many of the stories at National Supplier are termed “laptop stories” and revolve around a technically incompetent fieldworker misusing or misunderstanding information technology.

Oil PLC provided perhaps the most surprising accounts of stories relating to IS. Unusually, in both the operational units and at the corporate centre, it appears that stories are seldom shared. Even this lack of storytelling has cultural implications and may reflect the high degree of autonomy between units. All IS development has been entirely the responsibility of the operational units, with a mean of 3.5 IS personnel in each unit. Though this autonomy has led to “reinventing the wheel”, there have also been creative solutions evolving through lack of restrictive corporate standards or policies. For example, the unit in Singapore used its expertise and technology to develop plans for a site for a car dealership with 300 service bays. This innovative partnership placed Oil PLC in an ideal position to exploit a premier site for the marketing of its products. However, few operational units are aware of this story and the innovative method of “trying-in” the customer has not been repeated elsewhere.

The recording and interpretation of stories and myths is a difficult undertaking. However, during discussions with actors within the situation, interpretations are sometimes forthcoming. Aviation Co employees feel a part of the organisation as a whole, and though career moves out of IS are rare, most employees have social contact with other business units. Aviation Co stories reflect an organisation-wide culture. Members of Oil PLC have few shared stories, indicative of their entrepreneurial culture. Health Trust IS stories are about the users, but not as common as might be expected. National Supplier stories are about fieldworkers and foreign travel to reflect the organisation’s “colonial” past.

Status indicators emerging from a consideration of the stories retold are peculiar to the organisation and can only be interpreted with the help of the informants. For example, the Aviation Co information systems group told Aviation Co stories, unconnected with their everyday work. While this might be interpreted as indicating a submerging of an IS culture within a dominant organisational culture, it was viewed favourably as a reflection of the low staff turnover, and a largely local and loyal workforce. However, stories of IS failures, misuse and avoidance might generally be considered to indicate a low status for the information systems group. In general, the stories, indicate a low status for the information systems function in the organisations studied.

## 6.5. Rituals and routines

Aviation Co have had some movement between the IS staff maintaining legacy systems and the team guiding the business migration project, but few moving to other management roles. One difficulty for IS staff changing career paths is their “techie” image.

All the organisations have some organisation-wide events including staff seminars, buffets with the board, social groupings and Christmas parties. For example, Oil PLC divisions attend staff seminars and have two presentations from the Board each year. IS groups also have rites and rituals of their own. For example, the IS Department at Aviation Co holds charity coffee mornings and meetings where guest speakers and videos related to department developments are shown. Informants perceived no great difference between business units.

There is no acknowledged Oil PLC way of doing things. However, there appears a clear identity in the operational units. The remaining three organisations admitted to an amount of bureaucracy in IS, but again this was not different from experiences throughout the organisation.

Members of high status groups might be expected to have mobility within the organisation and take part in high profile, organisation-wide activities. However, there is little to suggest in the study of rites and rituals that IS functions have this high status within their organisations.

## 6.6. Symbols

Symbols might suggest some of the most obvious indicators to status with perquisites, such as named parking bays or extra office space, offered to members of a high status business unit. A further potent symbol is the name of the function—information systems, information technology or information services. Aviation Co, with its “forces” appearance and large workforce, abounds with symbols of rank and role. For example, parking within the perimeter of the security fencing was once reserved for senior staff, though recent changes have increased the number of spaces “inside the wire”. However, this study is concerned with symbols of status that differentiate the IS professionals from the rest of the organisation, and such symbols are difficult to find except with prolonged involvement with the organisation.

For this purpose, Aviation Co provided the most useful material. Many changes have taken place at Aviation Co that reflect an acknowledgement of the importance of symbols to organisational life. One of the most rewarding changes for IS staff was the removal of a security door. Business users, previously excluded, are now encouraged to visit. A recent move to an open-plan office is intended to break down communication barriers, encourage customers and allow for informal communication. A more business-like dress code makes IS staff indistinguishable from the business users. A number of the IS professionals have become a part of the team guiding the business migration project of process improvement. The migration team is distinguishable from those IS professionals concerned with legacy systems in terms of location, methods of work, social groupings and vocabulary.

The information systems professionals in all the organisations wore business dress and were mostly indistinguishable from staff in other business units. Perquisites were said to be the same throughout the organisation, though dependent upon seniority. All the organisations had made some recent symbolic changes to encourage communication with business users, including changes in accommodation and dress code.

## 6.7. Organisational paradigm

The organisational paradigm is viewed through the cultural web to reveal a picture of the organisation as a whole, in this case focussed on the IS function in each. Organisational values and beliefs are often difficult to articulate but may be apparent in such phenomenon as mission statements, historical reference, location and vocabulary. In line with the evident importance of IS to each of the organisations studied, mission statements and objectives might be expected to reflect a pro-active approach to IS provision. The vocabulary used might reflect the enabling and catalytic capabilities of IS, rather than simply the service aspects. This section investigates each information systems function organisa tional paradigm.

Health Trust, with its mission statement emphasising “caring” and an intent to be “sensitive to people’s needs”, might be considered to reveal a rather different organisational paradigm to that suggested by Aviation Co’s desire to “be the most professional and responsive Aviation Company in the world”. However, there are many similarities between mission statements, formalised vision statements and statements of intent. There is often a focus on “customers” and “responsiveness”. Some extent of penetration of the market might be evident, and a requirement for high quality is often present.

Within Aviation Co, the IS function has developed what is termed by the staff a “service” culture in recent years. The “Help Desk” has been replaced with a “Service Desk” with customer feedback sought, analysed and published. Business users are known as customers and IS professionals are “internal consultants”. However, the department’s objectives include those that suggest a more pro-active role, such as to “communicate an integrated vision” and to “explore opportunities to exploit Aviation Co’s world-wide presence”. The organisation is undergoing process improvement that began in 1994 with the launch of the business migration project to establish common processes, common systems and shared data. The current process improvements are scheduled for completion by the year 2000.

National Supplier employees describe a “public sector mentality” evident in dress, working hours and attitude to change. There is a moderate to high turnover of staff, and shortages in some areas. Some employees see the organisation as bureaucratic. IT/IS is contained largely in the IS departments. The service culture suggested in the title is considered by IS staff and users to be emerging, though history has helped create a significant IT provision within the development group, which is outside the control of the information services departments.

Authority at Oil PLC is devolved to the operational units that appear to function separate from each other and the head office. IS are developed and maintained locally, and only within the last year, are more strategic systems being developed for the organisation as a whole using corporate information systems provision.

As a provider of health services, Health Trust is staffed largely by clinicians and administrators. Values and beliefs are reflected in “a caring environment”, where issues of “quality, commitment and hard work” are promoted. The IS department is within clinical support, and the information systems provision is given an emphasis on information and value of information to the clinician. This is emphasised by the information services department’s vision that seeks “to promote the quality of patient care by empowering health care professionals to continually evaluate and improve their clinical practice through the use of information systems and information technology”.

The organisational paradigm, for example the “caring” values associated with Health Trust, clearly affect the way “things are done” by all employees. However, it is more difficult in some cases to distinguish an IS paradigm, indicating perhaps that the information systems professional is more immersed in organisational life than might be suggested by the literature.

## 7. Discussion and conclusions

A variety of experiences might be expected from each of the four cases. Some differences were startling, for example, representation in the executive and the extent of storytelling. Other differences were less apparent, for example, in controlling the IS budget, though the IS group in one organisation did not influence the setting of the budget. This leaves little room for general conclusions and this was not the intention given the exploratory nature of the research. However, the investigation of four organisations, rather than a single in-depth case study, has provided a breadth that raises interesting issues.

Although there is no intention to generalise, it is noteworthy that there is no direct and obvious correlation between, for example, turnover and number of IS staff employed, or size of budget and patterns of decision-making. Value from the study may be obtained in the qualitative data but snapshots of the dynamics of the organisations can both reveal and obscure. There is no obvious structure or pattern of growth for IS provision (which would concur with Galliers and Sutherland, 1991). It appears impossible to predict the size, structure and responsibilities of the information systems function based on the size and type of organisation. The size and pattern of growth of the IS function must be dependent on factors individual to the organisation, and should be viewed in the light of history and the personalities involved.

The gathering of stories is particularly exciting and unusual in an information systems context. Many stories appear to underline the difference between the “technocrats” and the business users or serve to keep IS disasters in mind. The former does not really fit with any of Martin et al’s (1983) common stories. However, where the stories are told in the same way across the whole organisation, it may indicate that the information systems professionals have an equal part of organisational life.

In questioning the status of the information systems function in organisations, this research extends the work on power and the IS function. It may be misleading to view a function responsible for constructing, enhancing and maintaining information systems as “having a profound impact” (Porter, 1985) or “directly influencing an organisation’s competitive position” (Ward and Griffiths, 1996). The IS function is more likely to be considered a secondary activity. On the other hand, the literature provides many examples of information systems and the supporting technologies providing the means by which organisations can gain and sustain competitive advantage, diversify and offer innovative products, form and maintain alliances and joint ventures, and re-engineer business processes. The four organisations provide evidence of the expectations that organisations have of the potential impact of information on business growth and development. For example, the multi-million dollar Oil PLC information systems project was agreed without financial justification and Aviation Co has major interest in a very expensive data warehousing project. It would appear that IS have the potential to direct as well as support organisational activities.

However, the literature and the cases provide evidence that the status of the information systems function is generally low. The number of IS/IT executive directors is low and the route to the board for the IS group may be through several layers ending in an executive with many functions. The IS function’s presence in business decision-making is likely to be restricted to business projects with a pre-determined technical content rather than including information systems providers earlier in the decision process. Information systems department objectives are likely to stress the support and service role, even when there are expectations that the IS providers should take a more pro-active role.

The contradictions evident in the literature and the difficulty experienced by practitioners in turning theory into practice comes from in-built values and beliefs which place the IS function as a supporting activity. These often unchallenged attitudes, incorporated into the organisational paradigm and written into mission statements and objectives, lead practitioners and commentators to bemoan their lack of status while “tugging their forelock”. This is not to suggest that the service desk be replaced. However, of the themes to emerge both in the literature and in practice, the concept of the separation of the support activities from the information management activities best supports our argument.

The resolution of the evident contradiction is the responsibility of both the IS function and the organisations within which they reside. There appears a general desire to exploit the emerging technologies for business benefits. Most primary and secondary research seems to suggest that this may be achieved by a combination of high level commitment, a separation of strategic and support activities, and the linking of IS objectives to business objectives. This would suggest high level commitment for highly visible or strategically important information systems development with such projects being treated as an investment, with long-term returns.

In terms of development, the four organisations might be roughly plotted, not in terms of number of employees or the size of the IS budget (these factors were found to be poor indicators of status) but in terms of how they compare to the literature, as summarised above. Examples were found in two of the organisations, where information systems are seen as an investment with long term benefits. At Oil PLC, a £25 M IT project was declared to have “little financial justification. It is the cost of not doing it that is important”. The organisations aspire to a separation of strategic and operational IS, with Oil PLC and Aviation Co envisaging a move in that direction.

All the organisations studied had a requirement to derive IS that meet business objectives, and justify new developments by business case in consultation with business users. An illustration of the importance of the concepts of linking IS objectives to business objectives is given by Aviation Co which conducted a change management programme for information systems specialists to help make the move towards internal consultancy. Information systems personnel in three of the four organisations studied reported difficulties communicating with the executive, either through difficulty of access, vocabulary or vision. Observation and documents provided many examples of confused purpose and misunderstandings. An obstacle to progress is the attitude, articulated by executives, business users and IS personnel, that the information systems function is a merely a service function.

Attitudinal changes are difficult to make. It may be considered easy to alter the IS department structure or change job descriptions. However, the service culture of IS/IT is more deeply embedded. A radical rethink of the approach to adapting the role and nature of the information systems function is required. The findings here parallel some of those in the IS function and power research. This is not unexpected. However, that literature speculates that IS type and industry sector may influence the function’s power. This is not demonstrated for status. The organisations cover a number of the categories Saunders and Scammell (1982) identify, yet the over-riding finding here is of low status even when the organisation is private and the IS strategic.

The information systems function might also profit from an attitudinal change. Perhaps Earl (1992) comes closest to considering the importance of an attitudinal change among IS professionals. However, even Earl talks of the need to “straighten out managerial thinking” and claims that “IT is different”. These claims seem to emphasise a gap between IS and the business. Earl goes on to use the vocabulary of a low status function whilst, confusingly, demanding that information systems are given increased status. The IT director of Oil PLC articulated this dilemma when he claimed that IT directors are “constantly bleating about their lot” and saw as counterproductive the IS profession “holding conferences to say how different they are”. Organisations need to be able to balance the strategic value of IS with issues of status.
