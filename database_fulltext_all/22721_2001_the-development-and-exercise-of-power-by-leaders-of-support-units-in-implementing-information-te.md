# The development and exercise of power by leaders of support units in implementing information technology-based services

B.V. Cendon $^{a,*}$ , S.L. Jarvenpaa $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Escola de Ciência da Informação, Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais, Av. Antônio Carlos 6627 C.P.1606, 30161-970 Belo Horizonte, MG, Brazil

$^{b}$ Graduate School of Business, University of Texas at Austin, MSIS, CBA 5.202, Austin, TX 78712-1175, USA

Received December 1999; Accepted 19 February 2001

## Abstract

This paper reports on three case studies of support units: medical libraries whose directors transformed the role of the libraries by implementing new information technology services. This study explains how the leaders developed and exercised power and transcended the limits of their organizational position. Most research on power has taken either a behavioral or a structural perspective. This study maintains that these perspectives are complementary rather than alternative approaches to the study of power. The analysis suggests that behavior and structure interplay in the exercise of power to achieve desired objectives. Behavioral power tactics at the individual level gained macro structural sources of power, such as grants and connections with other units. Structural position can hinder as well as facilitate the exercise of power. Finally, this paper shows that the leaders of the support units used a non-possessive conception of power and non-threatening power tactics to achieve their vision of a transformed library. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information systems; Support units; Power

## 1. Introduction

Power, defined broadly, is the ability to achieve desired outcomes (Giddens, 1984; Salancik and Pfeffer, 1977). The use of power plays an important role in the adoption of technological innovations (Ibarra, 1993; Dean, 1987; Kanter, 1983; Burgelman, 1983). Technology champions use power to ‘persuade others of the desirability of the change and to mobilize support, information and material resources’ (Ibarra, 1993) and to achieve their visions (Beatty and Gordon, 1991; Howell and Higgins, 1990).

When the technology champion is the leader of a support unit, $^{1}$ the use of power faces some challenges. Studies that compare that power of organizational players traditionally gauge power through the players' involvement in organizational decision-making (Pfeffer, 1981; Provan, 1980). Support units, and by extension their top managers, have by definition little involvement in key decision making close to the mission of the parent organization. Their power is limited by their position in the organization (Lucas, 1984; Saunders and Scamell, 1986). Nevertheless, support units often need to implement technology-based projects affecting core units that are involved in key organizational decisions.

This paper includes three case studies exploring how leaders in support units developed and exercised power to implement technology-based projects. These cases were carried out in three medical libraries considered exemplars for their success in implementing advanced information technology. Libraries, as parts of larger organizations (universities, medical schools, business organizations, communities, etc.) are examples of support units that are not traditionally associated with power. The literature widely portrays both the librarian profession and the institution itself as having low status and image (Scherdin and Beaubien, 1995; Eisenberg, 1989; Shaw and Culkin, 1987; McCrady, 1986). Yet libraries face a pressing need to change their existing service and introduce new services exploiting information technologies (Crawford and Gorman, 1995; Gorman, 1994; Smith and Johnson, 1993; Park, 1992; Malinconico, 1992; Harris, 1992; Penniman, 1991; Govan, 1991; Cartee, 1990).

Although the study was conducted in libraries, the results can be useful to other support units that need to implement information technology yet are in an unfavorable organizational position. The data shows the simultaneous use by library leaders of four types of behavioral power tactics: (1) discursive tactics to create an organizational climate to ensure the acceptance of the proposed ideas, (2) interaction tactics to compile information about the organization, (3) non-threatening negotiation tactics to negotiate the proposed projects, and (4) structural tactics to enhance structural sources of power that enabled the proposed projects to come to fruition.

The structural position of the leaders in supports units both hindered and facilitated their exercise of power. In all three cases, the leaders exercised power that was cooperative rather than possessive and used non-threatening tactics in the implementation of their technology-based projects. Ultimately, implementing the projects, themselves a structural element, facilitated the exercise of power for the introduction of other projects. By contrast, much of the literature on information technology implementation portrays the exercise of possessive power and negative, manipulative tactics (e.g. Markus and Bjørn-Anderson, 1987).

## 2. Literature review

This section reviews literature on power and provides the conceptual grounding for the study. First, we examine the structural theories of power, which adopt a macro perspective on power, and study structural sources of power. Next we present the behavioral theories of power, which adopt a micro perspective and focus on individual actions. The relationship between information technology and power is then examined. Finally, we integrate the perspectives.

## 2.1. The sources of power: the structural perspective

Much of the literature on power and information systems is based on structural theories of power (Hart and Saunders, 1997). Structural theories of power include the strategic contingencies theory (Hickson et al., 1971) and the resources dependence theory (Salancik and Pfeffer, 1977). In the structural perspective, power is derived not from the actions of people but from organizational structures. Resources, interconnections among actors in the organizational structures and organizational position are considered structural sources of power. They determine the relative power of various actors (Thompson, 1967; Perrow, 1970; Hickson et al., 1971; Hinings et al., 1974; Pfeffer and Salancik, 1974; Pfeffer, 1981; Astley and Sachdeva, 1984). Empirical studies have established the relationship between structural sources of power and the relative power of the players in an organization (for example see Hinings et al., 1974; Pfeffer and Salancik, 1974). $^{2}$

One of the major structural sources of power are resources that are highly valued by the organization (Salancik and Pfeffer, 1977). Organizational actors who can obtain resources critical to the organization, such as grants and contracts, will acquire power. Another structural source of power is the degree to which the activities of an organizational actor are connected with the workflow of others. A high degree of connections in an organization can generate more dependencies from other organizational actors. For example, collaborations with other units in the organization should make an actor more powerful. Organizational position is also considered a source of power. The power of organizational actors will be influenced by their hierarchical level, their span of control over other actors, and their position either as core or support units of the organization. The organizational position of a leader in the support unit places a constraint on that leader's power (Pfeffer, 1981, p. 137; Brass, 1984, p. 518).

The implication of the structural perspective on power is that leaders of support units, inherently in a disadvantaged position, will be less able to achieve their goals. However, models of power emanating from the structural theories have been criticized for being limited in their explanation of power. For example, Lawler (1992, p. 19), in his study of bargaining, points out that a structuralist approach generates anomalous findings. Additionally, studies focusing on information systems departments found that these units were not perceived to be as powerful as other organizational units (Lucas, 1984; Saunders and Scamell, 1986), in spite of their high involvement in the workflow of the organization and the dependence of other units upon their services.

A number of reasons have been given to explain the shortcomings of structural models in their prediction of power. First, such models provide a static picture of power, a phenomenon also developed through processes (behavior) (Pfeffer, 1981). Second, they take an inflexible view of power by assuming that power, which has a fluid nature, is a thing that is owned.

Next, we discuss other views of power, namely, the non-possessive nature of power and the behavioral perspective.

## 2.2. The nature of power: possessive and non-possessive power

The underlying premise of the structural theories is that those who exercise power possess it and ‘that power is apportioned within a zero-sum game’ (Bloomfield and Coombs, 1992, p. 463). This approach assumes, by definition, ‘a fixed sum of power in a relationship’ and that an increase in one party’s power automatically decreases the other party’s power (Lawler, 1992, p. 21). Power is seen, therefore, as a critical and scarce resource that organizational players struggle and compete to acquire, fostering a highly competitive image of power manipulation and suggesting that there has to be a winner and a loser (Bacharach and Lawler, 1981, p. 67–68). The possessive view presupposes that players of the power game pursue independent and conflicting interests and the game is necessarily negative as it ‘denies, forestalls, represses, prevents’ (Clegg, 1989, p. 156).

While the possessive view of power has prevailed in the study of organizational power, it can be restrictive in the explanation of complex situations involving power. Another view of power can be drawn from the works of Foucault (1977). In Foucault's post-modern conception, power is not owned. Each player has a certain measure of autonomy and power. As power is not viewed as a one-way process, it can be ‘shared jointly or interdependently with others’ (Knights and Willmott, 1985). The emphasis on interdependence rather than on dominance and resistance means also that the use of power can have outcomes other than the achievement of personal or group interests. Power does not preclude empowerment of others nor a benefit to a broader collectivity (Bloomfield and Coombs, 1992; Knights and Willmott, 1985, p. 25).

The concepts underlying this view of power better explain the kind of power that organizational players in support units might exercise. In collaborative situations, all parties' ability to exercise power might increase irrespective of the power asymmetries associated with their organizational position. In a non-zero-sum conception of power, the amount of power in a relationship is not fixed but variable and, in some contexts, both parties can increase their power (Bacharach and Lawler, 1981, p. 67–68; Bacharach and Lawler, 1980, p. 145; Lawler, 1992, p. 21).

This means that power can be exercised in a non-threatening way. For example, one study (Rosener, 1990) found that successful female executives take this non-possessive view of power. They feel that sharing rather than guarding power has helped them ascend in the organization. In addition, this view of power contributes to the analysis of the processes by which power is developed.

## 2.3. The behavioral perspective: the tactics of power

While structural theories focus on sources of power, the behavioral perspective studies the actions of individuals to obtain their goals (Brass, 1992). In other words, in the structural view the focus is on the sources of power that are the factors that enable one party to acquire power and possess it. On the other hand, the behavioral perspective focuses on the exercise of power and views power in terms of power tactics. Emphasizing the fluid, informal and dynamic quality of power relationships, the behavioral perspective recognizes that individuals who, by force of structural constraints, have low levels of power, can compensate for their disadvantage and obtain the outcomes they need by using skillful behavior (Mowday, 1978). They benefit from tactics that create alliances with or ensure support from other more powerful players (Pfeffer, 1981; Pfeffer, 1992; Brass and Burkhardt, 1993). A tactical approach to power emphasizes ‘the active, manipulative quality of power relationships. Power is not simply a structural or contextual condition…’ (Bacharach and Lawler, 1981, p. 46). Therefore the possession of power is only loosely connected to the exercise of power and in unequal power relationships, the exercise of power tactics may not reflect relative power of parties (Lawler, 1992, p. 19, 30). For example, actors who possess structural power may not recognize that fact or choose not to exercise their power. Actors who are in less favorable power positions may succeed in their exercise of power tactics.

Tactics are moves or set of moves that aim at influencing other party's behavior (Lawler, 1992, p. 23). Although several writers discuss alternative tactics for exercising power, there is no agreement on a typology of tactics (Mowday, 1978). Political tactics have been classified into legitimate and illegitimate (Farrell and Petersen, 1982), negative and positive (McClelland, 1970; McClelland, 1985; House, 1988) or as conciliatory and hostile (Lawler, 1992, p. 24). Behaviors viewed as negative include coercion, manipulation, intimidation, resistance, competitiveness or aggressiveness and are directed towards self-interest. Positive behaviors are open, communicate a willingness to collaborate, are rationally intended and focus on the interest of the broad community. Power tactics may be used by superiors to influence subordinates to improve their productivity or morale (downward influence), or by people in general to influence their colleagues and superiors (upward influence) (Kipnis et al., 1980, p. 440). Empirical work has attempted to uncover different behavioral tactics in the exercise of power (e.g. Allen et al., 1979; Kipnis et al., 1980), to study the effectiveness of different tactics (e.g. Mowday, 1978; Schilit and Locke, 1982) and to understand which power situations lead to the choice of positive versus negative tactics (e.g. Lawler, 1992).

The use of language is central to the behavioral tactics of power (Foucault, 1977; Clegg, 1989, p. 191). Discourse can be used to obtain ‘information, resources and support, which are the power tools’ (Kanter, 1988). Individuals communicate to acquire knowledge of the organization’s social system and establish connections with powerful players (Pfeffer, 1981). By the skillful use of language, individuals may influence organizational decision-making, convince others in the organization of their necessity and value, and alter existing beliefs (Pfeffer, 1981). Discursive practices through ‘talk, text, writing, cognition, argumentation, and representation’ (Clegg, 1989, p. 151) can be used to create new meanings and understandings.

In the exercise of power to implement information systems, manipulating existing beliefs is important because beliefs condition how individuals in an organization interpret reality, orientate themselves and shape their behavior towards an information system (Smircich 1983; Coombs et al., 1992). In information technology research, studies of champions of technological innovations address the use of behavioral tactics of power by individuals at lower organizational levels (Howell and Higgins, 1990; Beatty and Gordon, 1991). Champions are usually informal leaders that do not hold formal power positions. Yet, to achieve their visions, champions have to promote an idea in an organization (Beath, 1991). Because of their use of language and discourse, champions have been compared to evangelists that cross ‘departmental boundaries to build alliances, to preach the gospel of (advanced technologies) and to win enough converts so that the new technology is espoused by a critical mass of believers’ (Beatty and Gordon, 1991, p. 93). Howell and Higgins (1990, p. 323) discuss a variety of influence tactics among champions of technological innovations including: ‘rational justification, repeated informal expression of enthusiasm and confidence about the innovation, and sharing of information with potential coalition members.’

## 2.4. The interplay between action and structure: the duality of technology

The exercise of power can produce favorable (or unfavorable) side effects (Cobb, 1984). This ‘aftermath’ may be important as an antecedent condition for the future exercise of power. Successful technology-based projects can enhance organizational support, credibility and visibility, enlarge traditional roles, increase interaction with other groups in the organization, or change the boundaries between the responsibilities and knowledge of professional groups (Bloomfield and Coombs, 1992). These effects can facilitate the future exercise of power. For example, when an organizational player has heightened visibility and reputation after successfully implementing technology-based projects, it is more likely that future negotiations for the implementation of other projects will be easier. Drawing from Giddens, this notion of information technology, both as a product of the exercise of power, and as medium that influences the exercise of power has been labeled as the duality of technology (Orlikowski and Robey, 1991).

## 2.5. An integrative framework for the study of power

A comprehensive understanding of power requires an integration of the structural and behavioral perspectives of power (Krackhardt and Porter, 1985; Molm, 1990; Brass and Burkhardt, 1993). The two perspectives offer complementary rather than alternative approaches to the study of power. Surprisingly, most empirical studies on power focus either on a structural or behavioral perspective (one of the few exceptions that focuses on both is by Brass and Burkhardt, 1993).

Giddens' structuration theory offers a resolution for this ‘artificial partitioning of research attention’ by providing an integrative theory that links the realms of social structure and human action (Orlikowski and Robey, 1991). Structuration theory recognizes that structure, being a product of human action, is also the means for action (Giddens, 1976; Orlikowski and Robey, 1991). Power is exercised both through human actions and through the use of resources, which are structural elements.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Research design

Multiple case studies were conducted to explore the process by which leaders of support units developed and exercised power as they pursued their goals: the implementation of organization-wide information based services. The case study design used a ‘literal replication’ logic (Yin, 1994). That is, if all the three cases support the same theory, with the same results, literal replication can be claimed (Yin, 1994, p. 31, 46). Yin (1994) recommends two or three replications. Three case sites were selected for the results reported here.

## 3.2. Selection of the cases

The research examined how power is developed and exercised for the implementation of technology-based projects. The major criterion for identification and selection of the libraries was their reputation for having implemented successful technology-based projects. The three organizations selected were libraries in medical centers participating in the Integrated Advanced Information Management Systems (IAIMS) program of the National Library of Medicine (Special Review Committee of the Extramural Programs (SRCEP), 1989). Libraries in the IAIMS project were selected because of the technological focus of the program and to ensure a similar context among the projects in the case study sites. The IAIMS project made financial resources available for the development of an organization-wide information technology network and provided shared applications in the health science centers of which the libraries were a part. $^{3}$ IAIMS introduced similar opportunities for the libraries. At the time of the study, there were 16 IAIMS sites. The three cases were chosen based on their reputation as extraordinary successes in the implementation of information technology. Interviews with the directors of several medical libraries and literature reviews indicated that the three libraries had played outstanding roles in IAIMS. Due to their success and accomplishments, it was assumed that the use of power had been necessary.

## 3.3. Unit of analysis

The unit of analysis was the director of the library and his/her actions to implement technology-based projects.

## 3.4. Data collection

Data collection had two phases. In the first phase, the organizations were asked to provide general documentation containing background information about the library, the organization, and the technology-based projects. In the second phase, the sites were visited and interviews conducted. Site visits ranged from three to five working days. In each site, 9–18 respondents were interviewed with interviews lasting from 30 min to 2 h.

Through interviews, as well as bibliographical, documental and archival sources, data were collected about:

\- Evidence of power of the leader of the library (the successfully implemented technology-based projects, other episodes of power exercise, perceived power of the leader in the organization);

\- Indications of success of the technology-based projects (evidence of the use of new services, user and organizational satisfaction with the services);

\- Power tactics used by the leader of the library for the introduction and acceptance of technology-based projects; and

\- Perceived effects of technology-based projects (including the influence on future power exercise).

Bibliographic sources included published articles about the technology-based projects, their characteristics, development, and impacts. Documentary data consisted of brochures and formal reports, which provided further information about the technology-based projects and their development. Bibliographic and documentary sources also provided general contextual information about the libraries as well as about grants obtained by leaders of the libraries. Organizational charts were the only archival data used. They provided information about the structural position of the leader of the library.

The primary source of data was focussed interviews with open-ended questions. The interviews elicited perceptual data about power and the tactics of power. The individuals interviewed were identified with the cooperation of each organization on the basis of an initial list sent to the contact person at the organization. Most interviewees were in high positions within the organization and were able to provide a broad perspective about issues related to power. Appendix A lists the interviewees at each site.

Prior to visiting the sites, an interview guide was prepared. For qualitative research, some scholars (Miles and Huberman, 1984; Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin 1994) recommend predetermined research questions, themes, and data collection plans. Especially when multiple case studies are used, having predetermined data collection plans is helpful to make data collection more systematic and enhances comparability of results (Miles and Huberman, 1984). Following these recommendations, the relevant literature in the area of power, information systems and support units was reviewed in order to understand the major ways in which power is manifested, developed and exercised. This review was the basis for the development of the interview guide.

The interview guide asked respondents to elaborate on (1) the vision of the leader of the library, that is, what the leader wanted to achieve, (2) power of the leader, (3) power tactics that were important for the leaders to achieve the implementation of the technology-based projects, and (4) the effects of the implemented technology-based projects. The respondents were also asked to establish details and complement the factual data in the documentary and bibliographic sources (e.g. other grants obtained or when a particular event occurred).

## 3.5. Within-case data analysis

For within-case analysis, all field notes and interview tape recordings were transcribed. The data from interviews, bibliography, documentation and archival sources provided the material for writing individual case write-ups, which were then analyzed. These write-ups were 10–15 pages long. A site summary was created for each site that synthesized the existing technology projects, context, evidence of the exercise of power, and the leader's tactics. The site summaries were in a common format for all cases in order to facilitate cross-case comparisons.

Additionally, the interview transcripts themselves were analyzed and coded. $^{4}$ The initial codes were created from the research questions, literature review, and a pilot site data collection (Miles and Huberman, 1984, p. 56, 57). Additional codes were added when the data did not fit the initial codes. In a second level of analysis, we looked for repeating themes (for example, a positive view of power was a theme that frequently emerged) and attempted to group initial codes into fewer categories (Miles and Huberman, 1984, p. 67).

There was a high degree of congruence among interviewees. $^{5}$ Factual data mentioned in the interviews were cross-checked with the documentation available. Data were discounted if it did not reflect opinions that prevailed the interviews and were manifested only by a single person. The interviewees who described the processes by which the objectives were achieved established the links between the use of power tactics and outcomes (i.e. the implementation of technology-based projects).

## 3.6. Cross-case data analysis

Cross-case comparison of data explored the commonalities and differences of the processes and outcomes across cases (Miles and Huberman, 1984, p. 151). Data from all the cases were gathered in summary descriptive meta-matrices (see Cendon, 1996). To facilitate comparison, data were further reduced in checklist matrices (Miles and Huberman, 1984, p. 80, 95), where, for each category, lables (categorical values) were applied. The following labels were used: Present Strongly, Present, Present Partially, Absent Mostly and Absent. The labels Increasing and Decreasing were used to indicate a strong tendency.

After the initial coding, the data were further analyzed in a number of different iterations. Categories were refined of arrive at higher levels of abstraction. Some categories were split and other grouped. For example, the discursive tactics and interaction tactics categories resulted from splitting the initial category networking, which included all formal and informal channels of communication. Other categories resulted from clustering several of the initial coding categories (for example, the category ‘non-threatening negotiation’ grouped several other tactics). With each iteration, we went back through the individual case write-ups and the original interviews to ensure that the concepts were consistent with the data. Categories of codes, which reflect the final analysis, are in Appendix C.

The following sections present the results of the final analysis. First we provide a brief description of the three exploratory cases. Then, we provide an overview of the analysis of the data. A description of the process of developing and exercising power to implement the technology-based projects follows, with an account of the use of each tactic. Next, we discuss the interaction between structural elements and actions, with an emphasis on the impact of the technology-based projects. We finally deal with the nature of power that underlay the actions of the leaders and their exercise of power.

## 4. The cases

The National Library of Medicine launched the IAIMs program in 1983 to achieve the integration of fragmented electronic information in health science organizations. The goal was a seamless electronic environment in which a variety of sources such as bibliographic, full text, clinical, administrative, educational, and research databases could be accessed via an organizational network. The three libraries selected were part of large university medical centers participating in the IAIMS program. We will call them sites A, B and C.

## 4.1. Overview — site A

The medical library under Leader A's administration had been, since the 1970's, a pioneer in the application of information technologies. Leader A's initiatives to automate the library in the late 70's resulted in the independent development of a library automation system. In the early 80's, the library was automated using this in-house developed system and provided the medical center with remote access to medical databases. At that time these were novel developments and instant successes. The library became a model for medical libraries throughout the United States.

In 1993, Leader A convinced the chief executive officer of the medical center to accept the IAIMs challenge. Leader A was appointed the principal investigator for the prestigious grant. In site A, the library became the central focus of IAIMS and its role was enlarged to include an academic computer center.

At the time the study was conducted, the library provided a variety of technology-based services for its users, the library developed software for educational and clinical decision-making applications, provided technical support for the medical center, acted as a consultant for development of computer projects in the medical center, and was a vendor of its own integrated library software. The library obtained an array of grants to fund its projects and enjoyed high visibility, leverage and credibility in the organization.

## 4.2. Overview — site B

The library leader at site B joined the organization four years prior to data collection. Under her predecessor, there had been a general frustration with the library's services. Up to this point, the library's role was limited to providing books and journals and was lagging behind in the use of technology. Leader B shifted the priorities of the library, intending to use information technologies as an opportunity to provide extended, responsive services. Leader B started by communicating her beliefs about the extended usefulness of librarians' skills and the concept of librarians as ‘equal level, peer’ professionals to other health science professionals, to the library staff and the health science center's top managers and faculty. When site B decided to apply for the IAIMS grant, Leader B was appointed as one of the co-principal investigators. As work progressed, she was considered a focal point in the project.

In 1992, after four years of Leader B's administration and two years after the start of the IAIMS project, interviewees reported that there was increased respect and credibility for the library. Leader B emphasized obtaining grants and establishing a network of supporters and allies, including the development of joint projects with other units. The library did not have strong technical expertise and sought association with the university computation center for the skills needed in the development of joint projects. The strongly user-oriented services and joint projects affected user satisfaction and added to the credibility, visibility and influence of the library.

## 4.3. Overview — site C

In the beginning of the 1980's, the library at site C was not advanced in the use of information technologies. But Leader C, like Leaders A and B, understood the broad organizational environment and had a vision of integrated information access and of an extended role for the library. She saw IAIMS as an avenue to implement her vision, and worked on creating the infrastructure that could make it feasible. Redefining the role of the library in an electronic environment was part of creating this infrastructure. Her ideas and work were instrumental in the fact that the health science center was selected as one of the four first recipients of the IAIMS grant in 1983.

Within eight years of the IAIMS grant, the library was almost fully automated. A microcomputer and media center was created within the library with funds from the health center. Library clients could access MEDLINE, a database of medical information, over the network from the hospital floors. There were plans to add other medical databases to the system. The library expanded its scope to include a research and development unit. Other units in the medical center began to cooperate with the library in joint projects that included a hypertext electronic textbook of medicine and research on methods to link clinical and bibliographic data located in separated databases. There were also plans to house educational technology facilities in the library.

## 5. Discussion of results

Although we derived our results from initially conducting a within-case analysis followed by a cross-case analysis, for brevity we will report only the cross-case analysis. Details of the within-case analysis are summarized in several tables within the text. Below is a brief overview of major similarities and differences among the sites. The next sections describe the process by which power was developed and exercised.

As the structural theories of power would predict, the libraries were not associated with power. In site A, a medical professor and member of the library committee explained why the library was in a weaker position than the academic departments. Being a support unit, the library did not participate in the structure of organizational decision-making.

The Executive Faculty Committee is a governing body for the Medical School where issues related to the medical center are voted. If you do not sit in the Executive Faculty, you are out of the loop in terms of decision making. You tend to pick up information from other sources. The library, as a service facility, is not involved in daily issues that become important, does not share the same lines of informal communications that people at the Executive Faculty would. (Quote from faculty member, part of the Library Committee of the medical center in site A.)

Nevertheless, as expected, the leaders of the libraries showed the ability to produce the outcomes they wanted, particularly the implementation of technology-based projects. The leaders could obtain support and approval for the project they proposed; they were able to expand the technology-based products and services offered by their libraries. Leaders were able to become influential partners in the prestigious IAIMS grants. Table 1 shows a summary of results for all sites. The labels used in the columns are explained in Appendix C. Tables A1–A3 in Appendix B provide more information about the major technology-based project in the sites.

The process by which power was developed and exercised was similar in all the cases. The leaders used two groups of power tactics to implement technology-based projects. The first group of tactics created the organizational climate that ensured acceptance of the ideas proposed. Grounded on discourse and interaction, these tactics (which where labeled discursive tactics, interaction tactics, and negotiation tactics) were used on an on-going basis to continually gather information about the organization, obtain a redefined image of what the libraries' function and domain of action should be and emphasize the benefits the library could bring to the organization and individual players. Through the use of discourse, compliance was obtained by means more subtle and sophisticated than the use of formal authority.

The second group of tactics used by the leaders was labeled structural tactics and had the objective of building structural sources of power such as obtaining external resources and creating alliances and partnerships with other units. The structural tactics helped build an infrastructure that enabled the proposed ideas to come to fruition.

Thus, whereas most research on power focuses on the use of either behavioral or structural sources of power, the current study showed that to achieve their objectives, the leaders used a combination of behavioral tactics and tactics which built on structural factors.

Underlying their power tactics was a cooperative, non-possessive notion of power. Their actions seemed to be guided by a sense of mission and a vision of use of IT to better serve their communities. Consistently with the view that power can be exercised in ways that do not necessarily reduce the power of other players, the secret of their success was the fact that the results of their exercise of power empowered other units and the organization.

Finally, the successfully implemented projects, in addition to being a manifestation of power, reinforced power development. Technology-based projects extended the boundaries of the libraries' traditional services and increased dependence of the users and the organization on the library. They also reinforced discursive tactics promoting new understandings about librarians' skills, and a new image of the library as an institution. The visibility of the projects facilitated the future exercise of power in the implementation of other technology-based projects. Other units would seek the cooperation of the libraries for the development of other projects.

Sites A and B closely replicate each other. Site C also supports the results, although some of the elements in other sites were not detected in the site C interview data, and some of the project's effects were not as strong as in the other sites. Contextual differences, such as the departure of the leader in site C and the retrospective data collection, could account for these variations in intensity. In site C, the leader of the library had left the organization a year and a half before the study was conducted. Possibly Leader C, having made the decision to leave the organization, removed herself from commitment to her job long before she left. A quote from the IAIMS project manager who was hired in 1989 shows this:

When I came (Leader C) essentially said: “IAIMS is all yours.” So I was representing the library in the IAIMS meetings. Perhaps she was burnt out or she was looking around at other things. She was involved in so many areas already.

Upon her departure in 1991, the existing projects stagnated, new projects and services were not started, opportunities for cooperation, although present, were not identified and nurtured.

5.1. Discursive tactics: use of formal and informal discourse to change views about the mission of the library

Discursive tactics consisted of engaging other groups in a dialog that helped change their perception of the role of the library and gathered support for its new role. At all sites, intense use of discourse in interactions with other units was a near daily effort. Participation in committees was seen as a tool for informing and being informed. In both formal and informal situations, contact with top managers and key individuals throughout the organization were sought. The quotes below illustrate this process:

(Leader B) does a lot to make herself visible and available. If you want her to come and talk to your class or to your faculty, or to brainstorm at a committee meeting, she will come. So, what has happened is that lots of more people have had their eyes opened to what goes on in libraries. (Leader B) and her staff promote that in a billion of different ways: newsletters, active outreach, they train people, they bring new people in, they always develop new services, and they are very attentive to their customer base. (Quote from a faculty member in site B, head of one of the IAIMS committees.)

I think (Leader C) perhaps was very effective in reaching out, in going into the medical school, the nursing school, realizing that potential customers were outside and had to be brought in. The way she did it was by having herself and her associates actually lend themselves to be available much more than they had been before. Several programs were developed which attracted the users and the people to come into the library, or they went out and made those services available to them outside the library. (Quote from the Chairman of the Library Advisory Committee in site C.)

A variety of avenues were used to convey the leaders' vision and change the image of the library. All the sites produced publications, newsletters, sponsored conferences and lecture series, and sent information to outside agencies. In site A, for example, the library sponsored conferences on collaborative work and work developed in the library. Site A also held parties to commemorate outstanding events such as the selling of systems developed in library A to other institutions. In site B the leader sponsored lecture series with guest speakers. Outside agencies were informed of major events that built publicity around the library's activities. The educational efforts paid off as players in the core units of the organizations started to modify their views of the libraries, as the quotes below illustrate:

The kind of discussion I have with the director of the medical library or the associate directors covers a wide range of topics. They are generally useful resources to me in any realm of information systems. (Quote from a faculty member in site B, head of one of the IAIMS committees.)

Today I think that the library and computation center are in the same business as providers of information. This is a new idea among technical computing units. (Quote from the director of the University Computer Center in site B. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

I do not think (the IAIMS co-principal investigator) had even thought of the library very much except as a place where you get databases and medical information and all that. He said “I did not really understand libraries or really even care much about libraries until I came here.” And it was (Leader C) who educated him (or started to) about libraries. (Quote from the Head of the IAIMS unit at the Health Science Center Library in site C.)

## 5.2. Interaction tactics: interacting to identify and win supporters, allies and users

The second group of tactics was used to construct an understanding of organizational needs, politics, potential partners, allies and supporters. The data collection allowed leaders to define projects that would resonate strongly with top managers, other units and library users. Table 3 summarizes the results on the interaction tactics, through which the leaders collected information that guided their actions. This tactic was used uniformly in all sites. Sites differed in delegation of functions. Leaders A and B used more collaboration with their associates. Again, Leader C appeared to have worked mostly by herself.

The leaders found people in the medical center interested in information technologies for health sciences. They identified key people with whom they could develop projects. The leaders made sure they knew the kinds of research that faculty was involved in and how the library could assist. Help was offered in the form of library services, grants or joint projects:

In my contacts, I was also looking at the broader organizational picture to find out what the information dissemination problems of the institution were and what role the library could play in that. I was part of several committees and took every opportunity to meet people at the different units of the medical center, and to understand their work. I understand what are the problems they are facing to the best extent that anybody who is not in that job could. (Quote from Leader B. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

I attend the quarterly meetings of the board of directors and participate in other committees. This gives me an opportunity to hear about programs other people are doing. And I make sure I know if the library can play a role on that. (Quote from Leader A. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

The interactions gave the leaders an understanding about organizational needs and politics. Leader B provided an illustrative quote about the initial collection of data necessary for the successful application of negotiation strategies:

In fact, I have this as a hobby: to understand the political framework, the organizational and political structure. In a huge and decentralized organizational like (site B), decisions are made by consensus and there is no concentration of authority. I would not be able to do a good job if I did not understand the political structure. People wearing the titles are not necessarily the people who make decisions... (Quote from Leader B. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

The use of the first two groups of tactics, discursive and interaction tactics, paved the way for the leaders to influence organizational decision making in areas beyond their scope of authority. This helped the libraries build a positive image, an expanded understanding within the organization of the library's scope, and a broad base of vertical and lateral support.

## 5.3. Negotiation tactics: being non-threatening

The third group of tactics was non-threatening negotiation tactics that emphasized benefits to users, other players and the general organizational good. This third group helped secure support and approval for the projects. Table 4 summarizes the results for the three cases.

The over all non-threatening nature of negotiation tactics was similar in the three sites. Leader C's tactics differed from those used by leaders A and B by focusing mainly on the high organizational level and less at the direct library users. Contextual differences could account for this. Site C had a history of conflicting goals among units, and Leaders C believed that work on the organizational issues was fundamental for the success of her ideas. Another difference was that Leaders A and B, due to the grants obtained (see next section) were better positioned than leader C to offer tangible benefits to other units.

A key element in the exercise of power by the library leaders was building the negotiation arguments in a non-threatening manner. In selecting new projects to develop, the leaders actively sought out needs that were not being addressed by other units. They were seeking ‘voids’ in service:

Success has to do with the ability to identify voids: nobody else in the Medical Center was offering technology education. The library took that role. The library started supporting the role of fulfilling computer education and being more related to education. (Quote from a faculty member and Chairman of the Library Advisory Committee at site C. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

The areas that we chose to carve out for ourselves for IAIMS were areas in which nobody was working. The secret is to find the vacuum, the voids and then move in to fill the void. The kind of educational projects that are being developed in the library simply did not exist before. Nobody was teaching people how to use computers. They would have to go outside and pay \$125 to take a course. We moved in and we give the courses. The toxicology information was being provided only for the Poison Center people. Now we provide it for everybody. (Quote from Leader A. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

Leaders A, B and C pointed out they were careful not to build turf. For example, in site A cooperation occurred when other departments understood that the new library system would only create links to the medical center's systems. Computers would be maintained where they were and nobody's turf would be touched. Leader C elaborated on the non-defensive attitude:

Success has to do also with whether there are other people already fulfilling similar roles. And whether then you end up going and stepping on somebody else's turf and whether it ends up being a question of territory. Building turf is not a way to succeed. Do not take a defensive attitude of thinking that somebody else will take it away from us. (Quote from Leader C. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

The focus on neglected needs reduced the potential for turf fights and increased the other units' perception that the library initiatives were complementary rather than competing. The quotes below show how other information system units in the organization saw the library's initiatives as complementary rather than threatening to their activities:

Having this interface ready to be placed in the workstations was ideal for me. I do not have the time to develop a sophisticated information retrieval interface like this. This is not my business. (Quote from the head of the hospital systems unit in site B. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

People are delighted when somebody else, in this case the library, offers to put a database that they have on-line or offer some other services. People do not want to do what the library is doing. (Quote from the IAIMS project manager in site C. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

In cooperative ventures with other units, Leaders B and C recognized that in working towards their long range goals, the tactic of letting other participants take the credit worked to the library's own advantage:

(Leader B) is meticulous in making sure that everybody else gets the credit. Never have I had the sense of the library seeking power or library aggrandizing. (Quote from faculty member in site B. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

There is lots of glory to go around. Let other people take the credit, and that will just help the system grow and succeed. Do not say, “It has to be identified as my baby”. (Quote from Leader C. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

Additionally, the negotiating strategies emphasized how the library could benefit the users of the library services, the overall good of the organization, or how other units might work with the library in cooperative projects. This focus on others rather than on the libraries' own needs made the behavior less threatening, even though there were occasions when the new library services invaded other unit's territories. For instance, in site A, the library took over some databases that were being offered by other units because of its ability to provide information generally rather than to a specific group.

In combination, the three groups of tactics created conditions in the organizations for the achievement of the leaders' visions and plans. These tactics enhanced structural sources of power that provided the resources necessary for the implementation of the information technology projects.

## 5.4. The tactics of power in use: enhancing structural sources of power

The fourth group of tactics consisted of actions that resulted in more tangible outcomes and affected structural sources of power. The library leaders incorporated other units, obtained external resources, and created joint ventures with other units in the medical center. These structural sources of power were essential for building a base for the development of the new technology-based systems and services the leaders wanted to provide. The fourth group of tactics built on the results of the previous ones, namely the new vision of the library (discursive tactics), the knowledge of organizational politics and needs (interaction tactics) and an attitude of collaboration (non-threatening negotiation tactics).

## 5.4.1. Incorporating technical units

In all the sites, the libraries incorporated technical units. In site A, voids in services in the organization were the justification for creating two new units within the library. With grants obtained by the library, a technology laboratory and an information resources center were created to fill these voids. In site B, the microcomputer and media laboratory had been physically located in the library although it reported to different units at different times. By demonstrating that it could do a better job in meeting the needs of its constituency than other units on campus, the library gained control of the laboratory:

The library resumed the control of the computer laboratory in large measure because the library understands, better than anybody in the computation center could, what the specific needs of the health science center are. It was part of the negotiation tactic, not to say, “we want this, this is ours.” Rather, emphasis was placed in the fact that the library wants the best possible service for its constituency and would be happy to make the lab more responsive to the institutional needs. (Quote from a faculty member and Chair of the IAIMS Patient Care Subcommittee at site B. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

In site C, when it was decided that the health center needed a microcomputer laboratory, Leader C used voids in service to convince top managers to house it in the library,

One of the major needs for the health sciences campus was to have a microcomputer lab for students. The university's central computer center provided such computer labs on the main campus but not to the health sciences campus. So, we did it in the library. There was nobody else to compete. Instead of letting somebody else do it, I said, we have the space and expertise, just give us the money. (Quote from Leader C.)

## 5.4.2. Providing external resources

Grants from external sources were essential to the technology-based projects the library leaders sought to implement. In sites A and B, the leaders acquired grants from government and corporate sources to fund the technology-based projects. $^{6}$ Leaders A and B were principal or co-principal investigators in a variety of grants that supported projects that benefited the entire organization, allowed the development of joint-projects with other units, or sustained the libraries' own projects. The library was the grant recipient, so that the money would come to the library and, in some cases, be subcontracted to other units. Because most of the projects were being developed in conjunction with other units, the discursive and interaction tactics were essential for the grants to succeed. A quote from site A provide an example:

This is an example of going out to the departments, identifying people who would be willing to work with her. She would approach them, for example and say: we can make a proposal to the National Library of Medicine (NLM) to create a database about drug information. And they would put together a proposal and send it in as a grant application. Leader A is very resourceful in attempting to generate funds for information related projects. (Quote from faculty member, member of the library committee in site A.)

Site C differed by not obtaining its own grants. In site C, political tactics were used to receive portions of grants obtained by the health science center.

## 5.4.3. Creating links with other units

Many of the technology-based projects were the result of collaborative efforts with other units. The libraries were considered essential to the mission of the medical centers, but had tended to the isolated administratively from the rest of the organization, particularly in terms of key organizational decision making committees. The joint projects removed the library from its isolation in the organization. Using the favorable image of the library, their knowledge of the organization and negotiation tactics that emphasized benefits to other units, the grants they had, and resources from their technical units, the leaders were successful in creating collaborative ventures. Especially in sites A and B, joint projects were very successful and promoted direct interaction with units with which the library did not have a prior working relationship.

## 5.5. The interplay between action and structure

From one perspective, the structural position of the library constrained the power of the leaders. On the other hand, being part of a support unit helped their ability to achieve outcomes. In site B, the fact that the library was seen as a non-challenging and neutral unit worked to the leader's advantage as demonstrated by the following quotes:

Why not Medical School as the center for IAIMS? The choice of the library was a political one. The library is not owned by anybody. It was not owned by the schools, it was its own self. So it could be a more neutral broker among the various Health Sciences schools. (Quote from Member of IAIMS Patient Care and Clinical Research Committee in site B. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

My neutrality as a librarian, the fact that I am not seen as having an axe to grind or being favorably disposed towards medicine vis a vis pharmacy or any of the other schools, contributed to the support I get. (Quote from Leader B. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

The interplay of action and structure is also seen in the emergence of roles. The library in site A emerged as a medical academic computing center. In site B, the medical library obtained a role in instructional support and curriculum consulting through the microcomputer and media laboratory. In site C, because of the creation of the computer laboratory, the library for the first time received a budget directly from the health center.

## 5.5.1. Impacts of technology-based projects

The successfully implemented technology-based projects had a catalytic effect on the future exercise of power to implement other projects. In sites A and B, there was a positive user response to the new systems:

You can hear students saying to each other: hey, you should try such and such database, it really helped me a lot. Or you are helping one person and somebody else will come and enthusiastically suggest another package. (Quote from library staff in site A).

The usefulness and quality of the new services, and the satisfaction of the users with new systems increased the dependence of the organization on the library and gave the leaders more bargaining power:

What we have given to the users is what has really helped us establish a power base. In designing systems, for them to have a lasting influence, it is important to give the users something they are not going to be able to do without in the future. If those services that we gave to the users were to go away today, there would be screams throughout the medical center. (Quote from Leader A. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

User enthusiasm, one element of the impact of technology-based projects, which was key in sites A and B, was not happening with the same intensity in site C. Many of the services and products planned by Leader C were not finely tuned with the needs of the users. For example, a high emphasis was placed on the provision of electronic textbooks. There is no indication of any success of this project with the users. The same can be said of the databases that were developed by the library. The site C leader, about to leave the organization, removed herself from the process of continuously scanning the environment to keep the focus on changing user and organizational needs. The goals remained unmodified despite the fact that it became evident that these projects were not successful.

Nevertheless, in all the sites, the implemented technology-based projects complemented the strategic efforts of redefining the role of libraries and librarians. The successful initiatives contributed to the libraries’ ‘visibility, credibility, and top managers’ support. The projects extended the boundaries of the libraries traditional services. They also reinforced the discursive tactic of promoting new understandings about the library. As the perception of the library was modified, there was a major modification of the traditional demands on librarians:

Around the medical center, people look for us to provide expertise in database development, and automated systems. They also depend on us for the technical aspects of linking and getting on the local area network, and in using some of the larger systems like the Internet to get to outside resources. People also think we are a bottomless pit. They think we have all this money. (Quote from an associate director of the library in site A.)

There were about 20 people sitting at the table, only 3 or 4 from the library. An issue came up about a project to manage and develop a research database. A computer person said: “Well, the library should do it.” That meant recognition that all the expertise that we have in cataloging, and thesaurus, and technical services have an application much broader than on books. (Quote from Leader C.)

## 5.6. The nature of power: sharing rather than guarding

Underlying the use of power tactics above, was a cooperative rationale consistent with the purpose of a support unit: it is by developing new and better services that support units strengthen their power position. Leaders A and B invested their work with a sense of service to the user and to the organization. Leader A was most eloquent in communicating this:

My mission in life has always been to bring information to the physicians and to the health care providers because I feel that it is so critical that patients get the best care. This is my mission, whether it involves training new physicians, nurses, providing information to researchers, or providing practitioners information. I want to be there and want to able to play an important role in that. But I do not care about power. (Quote from Leader A.)

Interviews with Leader C did not reveal this sense of mission. Another evidence of lack of this sense is the fact that the Leader left in the middle of the project, jeopardizing the achievements that had been accomplished:

In addition to the sense of a mission, an essential determinant of the action of the leaders seemed to be the presence of a vision. Leaders A, B and C shared in their vision of broad, integrated information access in support of clients' needs and the notion of technology as the way to achieve this. For example, Leader A said:

I had ideas for services I would like to provide long before the technology that allowed them was available. (Quote from Leader A.)

The leaders seemed to believe that one increases a support unit's power by sharing power with others rather than hoarding it. By taking a non-aggressive and collaborative approach to get what they wanted, they were not threatening to reduce the power of other players. In seeking to explain their success the project leaders referred to maintaining a cooperative rather than possessive attitude:

We did not say, “this is a library project and the library is the leader.” If the library wants to play an important role, you have to think of yourself as supporting a greater organizational good. The library has to be directly related to the organization’s values. (Quotes from Leaders C. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

We have to help people solve their problems. We cannot go all the time with our problems to them. We give the departments of the medical center support for their projects, or equipment, or staff time. You have to be constantly doing for others. This is part of the role of the library anyway. We just expanded it a little further. (Quote from Leader A. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

A possessive approach to power would have had a negative effect on their ability to achieve their objectives, and might have even threatened their survival:

I think that one of the biggest mistakes one could do in their career is to go after power just for power. Such power does not survive. What survives is your ability to provide the right kind of service at the right place and the right time. I really think if people focus on what the mission of the library is, and do the best they can to fulfill that mission, than there is no concern about whether the library will survive. Good services will survive and do well. (Quote from Leader A. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

If the library had assumed a position of saying “I want more resources” then, by definition, Nursing or Medicine gets less. This attitude would have been the kiss of death to the library. (Quote from a faculty member and Chair of the IAIMS Patient Care Subcommittee at Site B. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

The cooperative, non-possessive view of power was manifested in the types of negotiation tactics selected and in use of the structural power obtained. Rather than restricting access to the grants they got, the libraries made them available to other units for the development of joint projects. Thus, ‘sharing’ rather than ‘hoarding’ resources contributed to the power of the libraries. This sharing attitude is exemplified by the following quote:

In site A, Departments of Radiology, Neurology, and Emergency Medicine, as well as other departments in the health sciences center, have all benefited financially as well as with equipment from the grants obtained by the library. We gave them computer classrooms, staff time, support for their projects, or computers and connections. We sponsor instructional programs for the faculty. (Quote from Leader A.)

The joint projects initiated by the libraries in sites A and B were non-threatening to the other units. They provided ‘win–win’ experiences:

The development of this interface in and of itself is a unique experience for the university. (Leader B) got the funds and gave them to the person who could make value out of it (the head of the computation center). Usually, the library director would get funds and hire some programmer that would develop a weak application that would never work. By giving her funds to the computation center, she and the organization received more value back. (Quote from the director of the Medical Center Information Systems Unit in site B. The italic font is added for emphasis.)

The emphasis placed on sharing helped the leaders to achieve success in the accomplishment of their goals, led to cooperation with other units, and helped them get support for the projects they proposed. These leaders obtained power by empowering the organization rather than acting to obtain power for themselves.

## 6. Implications, limitations, and conclusions

## 6.1. Implications

Few researchers have studied power from the perspective of a support unit. However, ‘the core/support dichotomy’ (Saunders, 1990; Hackman, 1985), is important to explain organizational power. Not only does it explain asymmetries in the distribution of organizational power, but also the process used to develop power. The cases provide opportunities to analyze the type of power that leaders in support units develop and exercise. The cases also provide insights into the combination of tactics used to develop power and achieve the implementation of technology-based projects. To achieve their objectives, the leaders had to use a combination of behavioral tactics and tactics built on structural factors.

Additionally, the current study points out the interplay between the actions taken by leaders and structure in the exercise of power to implement technology-based projects. 'Structure does not merely emerge out of subjective human action; it is also objective because it provides the conditions for human action to occur. Structure thereby provides the means for its own sustenance, and structure and action constitute each other recursively' (Orlikowski and Robey, 1991, p. 147). The structural position of the library as a support unit constrained the leaders' power but also empowered them. The behavioral tactics created the organizational climate that ensured acceptance of the proposed projects and were used to enhance structural sources of power. Finally, the successfully implemented technology-based projects, themselves a structural element, facilitated the future actions of the leaders.

Although the study was conducted from the perspective of library leaders, the results should be useful also to IS department leaders since many of them belong to support units in organizations. Like libraries, IS departments are generally not perceived as powerful by user departments (Lucas, 1984; Saunders and Scamell, 1986; Markus and Bjørn-Anderson, 1987). Librarians, like IS professionals, assess the needs of users and propose solutions, which frequently involve the implementation of IT projects, and act as advocates for the successful implementation of these projects. In spite of their support position, they need to exercise power to attain their objectives. Thus, several implications to the work of IS leaders can be drawn from the results obtained here.

First, our study builds and extends the work of Markus and Bjørn-Anderson (1987) by substantiating the process by which technology champions develop and exercise power. It also complements the work of Lawler (1992) who investigated the situations that triggered the use of conciliatory versus hostile tactics. The behavioral power tactics, grounded on discourse, interaction, and negotiation, were used on an on-going basis, to continually gather information about the organization and to alter and maintain a redefined image of what the libraries' function and domain of action should be. The non-possessive concept of power and non-threatening negotiation tactics used by the leaders contrast with tactics such as coercion, sanctions or aggressiveness reported in studies of power (Kipnis et al., 1980; Mowday, 1978). Only two other studies were found that report on the use of similar tactics of power. They point out that these tactics are beneficial for support units (Hackman, 1985) and also for women, another group that often deal with the issue of limitations in power (Rosener, 1990). Non-possessive power builds trust, or willingness to be dependent on the other party. Trust has been touted to be the most critical component of partnerships and alliances in the IT literature (Henderson, 1990). Non-possessive power can alleviate fears of opportunistic behavior. By contrast, possessive power is related to opportunistic behavior because it assumes that parties pursue independent and conflicting interests.

This study also extends the work of Howell and Higgins (1990), in which they demonstrated that champions of information technology are informal transformational leaders (p. 337) and that they use a variety of influence tactics to influence subordinates, peers and superiors. The current research results seem to indicate that when the champion is in a support unit, influence tactics should be guided by the notion of cooperation and non-threatening negotiation tactics.

A second implication of the study relates to the roles of the technology champion. Markus and Benjamin (1997) note how managers often hold onto narrow, failure-promoting beliefs about their roles in information technology projects; technology champions often see their role as merely initiating a project with little follow through. Success, however, requires that managers practice multiple roles (Markus and Benjamin, 1997). In our case sites the leaders played different roles at different times: change advocate, sponsor, design team member, transition management team member, coach, etc. Mason et al. (1997) describe how A.R. Zipf at Bank of America served in the multiple roles of champion, project manager, technologist, and so on. The partnership literature similarly emphasizes the importance of the ‘squiggly lines of responsibility’ and blurred roles in successful IT partnerships (Lasher et al., 1991; Henderson, 1990). Changing roles also means a leader must view the development and exercise of power as ‘contact sport’ (Markus and Benjamin, 1997). The library leaders engaged in multitudinous contacts to help people change their minds and attitudes.

The third implication relates to the structural position of a leader in information technology projects. As Markus and Benjamin (1997) note, ‘Academics of the change school frequently write as if change advocacy is the exclusive domain of line executives with the formal authority to command. Yet consultants and innovation theorists know that effective change advocates can be found in every organizational role…’ (Markus and Benjamin, 1997, p. 65). The current paper sheds light on how a change champion can develop and exercise power despite a non-strategic organizational position.

The fourth implication is the importance of building a coalition of support in the leader's own unit. The site C leader did not build a network of internal support in contrast to the leaders at sites A and B. Without a support coalition in their own unit, a leader can get too far ahead, become disillusioned, and experience burnout and self-doubt. In essence, too large a gap between the leader and the rest of the unit can cause the leader to become isolated and frustrated.

Finally, the findings might shed light on ways technology champions can improve their credibility in an organization. The libraries were not perceived initially as experts in technology and had no track record of technological innovation. Yet, through prolonged and consistent dialog and interaction with the other units, library leaders were able to change the image of the library. Fulfilling their IT related promises was a critical component in sustaining their credibility over time. The library leaders stayed in touch with other units' problems, which in turn allowed them to produce pragmatic ideas for their counterparts as well as negotiate in mutually beneficial ways. IT professionals saddled with low credibility (Bashein and Markus, 1997) might find the tactics discussed in this paper useful.

## 6.2. Limitations and suggestions for further research

A criticism of this study is that the cases describe particularly successful project implementations and implementation environments. There is a likelihood that organizational actors select a particular combination of positive and negative behavioral tactics based upon the circumstances they face. One possible explanation is the selection criteria used. We sought case sites that had experienced extraordinary success in the implementation of technology-based projects. In these sites, a fortunate combination of individual, timing and contextual factors may have occurred. Another explanation might lie in the non-threatening type of power used. The high congruence among interviewees, who would repeat the leaders' stories and sometimes even their words, could reflect the pervasive indoctrination process. Future research could explore these explanations as well as challenge current findings with a set of cases where disconfirming evidence is sought. More specifically, evidence that the use of cooperative tactics can also lead to failure under certain circumstances should be researched.

The exploratory retrospective nature of the study is another limitation of the present research. The data collection focused on finding power tactics used to implement technology-based projects after the fact. Future research should deploy longitudinal concurrent designs focusing on what triggers the exercise of power tactics, what hinders the tactics, as well as the short-term and long-term ramifications of the tactics.

This study relied largely on perceptual data. This was especially an issue in site C, where the leader had left one and a half years before the study was conducted. $^{7}$ Perceptual data is not always reliable. People depend on memory and subjective judgment. Yet, prior research indicates that issues of organizational control and unit influence can be effectively studied by the use of perceptual data (Tannenbaum, 1968; Child, 1973; Hinings et al., 1974; Ouchi and McGuirre, 1975; McMahon and Ivancevich, 1976; Jemison, 1981; Lachman, 1989; Astley and Zajac, 1990). Future studies are encouraged to rely more on direct observational methods.

In addition to the use of tactics, the literature reports on individual traits related to the exercise of power (House, 1988). In the current study, the effects of individual differences in power were insufficiently explored. Future research should consider which specific characteristics of the leaders were relevant to the exercise of power and to the implementation of technology-based projects.

Finally, the study did not explore in depth the downward-influence tactics used by leaders in support units. According to Howell and Higgins ‘...innovation adoption is largely a process of influence, both with subordinates, as indicated by leadership behavior, and with peers and superiors, as indicated by influence tactics’ (Howell and Higgins, 1990, p. 320). Howell and Higgins concluded that champions are informal transformational leaders and are likely to use a variety of influence tactics, both upward and downward in the organizations. The current research unveiled mostly upward-influence tactics used by leaders of support units who championed technological projects. Other research is encouraged to explore their leadership behavior and the downward-tactics used to influence their subordinates.

## 6.3. Conclusions

The use of power plays a significant part in the adoption of technological innovations and in the implementation of IT projects. It is a specially challenging issue for technology champions and leaders of support units who do not hold formal power positions or are not involved in key organizational decision making. For these organizational players, the process to develop and exercise power projects might differ from that of leaders in core units. Although it has been recognized that the complexity of power demands comprehensive approaches, most research on power remains fragmented (Brass and Burkhardt, 1993) and adopts either a behavioral or a structural perspective. The present research attempts to show the specific characteristics of the process of power development and exercise by leaders of support units involved in the implementation of IT projects. It also shows how behavioral actions at the individual level interact with macro structural resources to achieve desired objectives. The study supports recent works (Brass and Burkhardt, 1993; Orlikowski and Robey, 1991; Markus and Robey, 1988; Giddens, 1979) that argue for more integrative frameworks for the study of power. Further research should benefit from similar approaches that consider the intricate process of power exercised in organizations, in which behavioral and structural sources, at micro and macro levels of analysis and integrated.

## Appendix C. Categories of codes resulting from final analysis

Technology-based projects implemented. $^{9}$

\- Basic library services (BASIC SERVICES) related to library automation (including an integrated library system, databases, and on-line catalogs.

\- Additional library services (ADDITION. SERVICES) related to other technology-based library projects usually addressing specific user needs.

\- Joint projects (JOINT PROJECTS) referred to technology-based projects developed by the library in collaboration with other units in the organization.

Exercise of power by the leader on episodes other than the implementation of technology-based projects:

\- Expansion (EXPANSION) referred to the expansion of the arena of action of the library (such as information policy-making, research in information systems, or the provision of resources other than information).

\- Influence (INFLUENCE) related to other episodes in which the library demonstrated ability to obtain desired organizational outcomes.

\- Setting trends (SETTING TRENDS) consisted of evidences that the actions and projects emanating from the library were influencing the direction in which the parent organization was moving.

Tactics used to implement the technology-based projects:

\- Discursive tactics (DISCURSIVE) consisted in engaging other groups in a dialog to change their perception of the library.

\- Interaction tactics (INTERACTION) consisted in interacting to identify supporters and users.

\- Non-threatening negotiation tactics (NON-THREATEN) included tactics such as negotiation strategies based on the needs of the organization and the user, use of non-threatening approaches, not building turf, being a catalyst of actions, and being a problem-solver to the organization.

\- Incorporation of other units (INCORPORAT. UNITS), such as microcomputers and media centers.

\- Obtaining grants (GRANTS).

\- Links (LINKS) referred to breaking the traditional isolation of the library by establishing work relationships with other units.

Perceived impacts of the technology-based projects:

\- Capacity for power (CAPACITY FOR POWER) referred to factors which provided the library with the potential to influence organizational decision making, such as credibility, reputation, a record of success, top management and allies support, and visibility.

\- User satisfaction (USER SATISFACTION) encompassed evidence of increase in user satisfaction and enthusiasm with the services and systems provided by the library and increase in user demand for more services.

\- Expanded image (IMAGE) of the library implied an enlarged understanding of librarians' skills and library roles and a change in expectations among top managers about how the library could serve the organization.

Notion of power:

\- Vision (VISION) included an extended understanding by the leader of the library of broad, integrated information access in support of clients' needs, and the notion of technology as the way to achieve this vision.

\- Mission (MISSION) referred to the exhibition by the leader of the library of a sense of mission to the users and the organization, which guided their actions.
