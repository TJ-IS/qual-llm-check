# Competing with CRS-generated information in the airline industry

J.D. Pemberton $^{*}$ , G.H. Stonehouse, C.E. Barber

Newcastle Business School, University of Northumbria, Newcastle upon Tyne, NE1 8ST, UK  
Received 26 April 1999; accepted 20 January 2001

## Abstract

For over 20 years, computer reservation systems (CRSs) have been cited as a source of competitive advantage for the airlines that developed them. This paper reviews the developments that have given rise to such advantage, emphasising the use of CRS-generated information as a competitive tool within the airline industry. New evidence is presented suggesting that the control, dissemination and manipulation of CRS data by owning airlines continued to allow them to capitalise on their investment at the expense of competitors during the 1990s. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Computer reservation systems; Information; Competitor intelligence; Competitive advantage

## 1. Introduction

This paper presents, for the first time, the findings of a detailed study investigating the ways in which CRS-owning airlines have sought to maintain an advantage over their competitors in relation to CRS data, and the subsequent information and knowledge created from it, when other channels, notably regulation and legislation, have contrived to erode their earlier competitive advantage.

The growth of the airline industry has been one of the most well documented success stories of the past few decades (Clemons and Weber, 1990; Richer and O'Neil-Dunne, 1998). Technology, whether aviation, information and communication based, has been central to such developments, as has the changing nature of the regulatory and business environment, and the increasing sophistication of the consumer. Arguably, the introduction of computer reservation systems (CRSs), originating in the 1950s but now an integral part of what are now termed global distribution systems (GDSs), has been one of the major catalysts for change, and has resulted in improved efficiency and competitiveness within the airline sector (Porter and Millar, 1985; Poon, 1993; Balsamo, 1996; Powell and Dent-Micallef, 1997).

Ostensibly, while investment in, and development of, CRSs arose largely in response to a desire for improved operational efficiency, the utilisation of CRS-generated information has become a powerful means of creating new strategic knowledge to enhance competitive performance (King et al., 1989; Dordick and Wang, 1993; Kenney, 1996; Buhalis, 1998; Pemberton and Stonehouse, 2000). However, the sensitivity surrounding the issue of CRS information, exemplified by the reluctance of airlines to divulge details of activity in this area, has ensured that little published material has appeared previously.

The research presented in this paper concentrates specifically on the role of information derived from, processed and managed by CRSs. In particular, issues relating to the use of CRSs by their owning carriers to enable the acquisition of competitive advantage are reviewed by reference to developments that have taken place within the airline industry. New evidence, arising from a variety of sources and methodological approaches, is presented to indicate how CRS-owning carriers have continued to have the upper hand despite regulatory safeguards. This 5-year study represents a departure from the usual emphasis on technology per se and focuses on the uses and manipulation of technology generated information and knowledge, methods of dissemination, and the influence of CRS-owning airlines in controlling the flow of commercially important information to competing airlines.

## 1.1. CRSs and competitive advantage in the airline industry

Schulz (1992) notes the airline sector was among the earliest of industries to explore the potential of information technology through its pioneering development of real time computing, creating, at one stage, the largest privately owned networks in the world. Today, sophisticated global distribution systems (GDSs) provide travel agents in remote locations with the facility to make on-line, real-time bookings or, increasingly, empower individuals to make bookings directly via the Internet or other digital media (Richer and O'Neil-Dunne, 1998).

The ability to operate in geographically dispersed markets, coupled with the information intensive nature of the airline business, give some indication of why carriers have been at the forefront of developing and exploiting new technologies designed to create and sustain competitive advantage (Feldman, 1987; Wardell, 1987; Humphreys, 1990, 1994; Richer and O'Neil-Dunne, 1998). Furthermore, CRS technology has been a critical element in shaping the industry's strategies and operations to the extent that industry and government watchdogs have imposed regulatory safeguards in recognition of CRSs' potential to influence airline competition (Hopper, 1990; Barber et al., 1993; Humphreys, 1994; Barber, 1998; Richer and O'Neil-Dunne, 1998).

CRSs have, in their lifetime, provided their owners with six main sources of competitive advantage as shown in Fig. 1.

Barber (1998) gives a detailed account of the various issues presented in Fig. 1 which, broadly speaking, correspond to the following three phases in the development of CRSs:

\- Internal seat inventory systems (1958–1960s),

\- Subscriber products (1970s and 1980s),

\- Global distribution systems (1990s).

In response to inefficient manual administrative systems, phase one was characterised by the development of real-time systems which resulted in internal operating efficiencies and new market opportunities (Truitt et al., 1991; Button, 1991; Archdale, 1993). However, high development and investment costs ensured that only a handful of the larger US airlines proceeded to develop their own in-house seat inventory systems, essentially the forerunners of the CRS (Feldman, 1987; Barber et al., 1993). Recognising the cost reduction and efficiency benefits of these automated systems, smaller carriers without the resources to finance such developments began to contract out their seat inventory and reservation function to the CRS-owning airlines (Humphreys, 1990). Such contracting was the first evidence of CRSs being used to generate external revenue for their owning carriers. At this stage, competitive advantage arose for the CRS-owning airlines through more effective operational and tactical management of its value adding activities. In contrast, later use of CRS information by owning carriers centred on creating new knowledge and attempting to disadvantage their competitors.

Phase two saw the development of subscriber products where CRS owners allowed other airlines to display information on their systems for which a charge was made (Barber et al., 1993). This provided CRS-owning airlines with an opportunity to regain their earlier competitive lead which had been eroded by increased competition following deregulation in the US (Wardell, 1987; Button, 1991). Revenue generation from the CRS, via booking fees for competing airlines, allowed CRS-owning airlines to increase profits, at the same time as increasing competitors' costs. Differential pricing policies based on charging discriminatory fees to competing carriers, weighting fee structures inequitably between distributors and subscribers, charging excessive fees for the services rendered and structuring prices so that high initial costs prevented smaller carriers from accessing services, were all contributory factors in ensuring CRS-owning airlines capitalised on their investment.

Two contentious issues also surfaced at this stage, namely:

\- Screen display bias—a deliberate ploy to display a CRS-owning airline’s flight information ahead of that of its competitors in an attempt to distort consumer choice. Such bias within CRSs was highly significant, leading to legislation to prohibit bias of primary screens i.e. those screens designed for use by the majority of travel agent subscribers (Civil Aeronautics Board, 1985; United States Department of Transportation, 1988).

\- The ‘halo effect’—incremental bookings achieved for an owning carrier via their CRS because of their status as systems provider and the natural tendency of travel agents using the CRS to book flights of the carrier providing the system (Wardell, 1987; Humphreys, 1990).

Various regulatory and other initiatives have attempted to deal with the issues of unfair advantage given by screen bias and the halo effect. Barber gives a thorough analysis of these developments over the last 20 years (Barber, 1998). However, while many of the more blatant examples of discrimination have been eliminated by legislation, incremental revenues have proved an enduring form of advantage for CRS controlling carriers.

The third phase relates to the 1990s during which time the airline industry was dominated by strategic alliances that spanned international boundaries (Feldman, 1992; Barber et al., 1993; Humphreys, 1994; Richer and O'Neil-Dunne, 1998). Mergers and alliances between US and European CRSs, coupled to closer links with Asian vendors, transformed the industry and its CRS technology base spawned a number of global distribution systems (GDSs). Throughout this phase, attention focused on the potential use of data contained within the GDSs to create knowledge-based advantage, with the issues of personalisation, customer targeting and the sharing of information figuring prominently (Stonehouse and Pemberton, 1999).

Arguably, a new phase is emerging, characterised by advances in the Internet-related technology including mobile telephony and digital television, which are altering the traditional channels of airline product distribution, and providing new ways of interacting with potential customers (Richer and O'Neil-Dunne, 1998). GDS owning partnerships are reluctant to relinquish their previous advantage and concerted efforts are already visible in the quest to keep abreast of developments in information, knowledge and technological applications. Third parties, not historically linked to the airline industry but with Internet expertise, are shaping the ways in which airlines conduct their business. For example,

Microsoft's Expedia, an Internet based booking facility, has resulted from partnerships with several airlines, while the UK's Flightbookers has resulted from partnerships with Germany's TISS and AA's Sabre GDS (Richer and O'Neil-Dunne, 1998). In terms of information and knowledge management, the ability to by-pass traditional intermediaries and gain direct access to customers represents a potential opportunity for CRS owning airlines to exploit their position.

## 2. Research focus

Technological and partnership developments are ongoing, with business-to-business and business-to-customer initiatives changing on a daily basis. However, while Internet-related activities will have important ramifications on the way in which airlines compete with each other in the future, the research presented in this paper relates to phase three and examines the competitive uses and applications of CRS/GDS-generated information. Based on research data collected over the period 1992–1997, new evidence is presented illustrating how CRS-owning airlines have attempted, often covertly, to disadvantage their competitors by inhibiting their ability to generate strategic information through the control, dissemination and manipulation of CRS data.

Hard evidence relating to how information derived from CRSs has been used to affect competitive positioning is difficult to quantify. However, both the US and European governments recognised the seriousness of the issues involved and, policed by the regulatory bodies of the Civil Aeronautics Board, the US Depart of Transportation and the European Commission Aviation Committee, agreed to incorporate clauses relating to the use of competitive data within CRS rules (Humphreys, 1994).

However, despite such controls, CRS-owning airlines have been adept at overcoming regulatory loopholes and devised ways of disadvantaging their competitors. These are the focus of this paper which, broadly speaking, relate to the:

\- format and media used for communicating CRS data;

\- frequency with which such data are distributed;

\- pricing structures associated with CRS data;

\- influence of ownership on access and control of CRS data.

## 3. Research approach

Despite considerable attention relating to competitive issues and the airline industry in academic literature over the years, the focus has predominantly centred on the notions of screen display bias and the halo effect, together with the accompanying regulatory developments. However, the use of CRS data has also played an important role in influencing competitive behaviour in the airline industry. Unfortunately, the sensitive nature of many of the issues surrounding this subject has posed a problem for researchers and, as a result, little has been published on the actual and potential use of CRS data.

To assess and further enhance understanding of this complex area, research conducted at Newcastle Business School has, among other things, focused on the role of CRS data in the creation of information and knowledge to gain competitive advantage. The findings, published here, are based on a pluralistic methodology and include:

• participatory observation;

\- the establishment of a key informants network;

\- a questionnaire based survey; and

\- personal interviews with key players in the industry.

Naturally, the controversial nature of much of this work necessitates that the respondents' anonymity is preserved.

## 3.1. Exploratory work

In order to gain a clearer insight into the issues surrounding CRS data and competitive behaviour, a 6-week period of participatory observation at a European CRS vendor gave valuable first-hand experience of the pertinent issues of concern within the airline industry. The benefits were threefold. Firstly, contacts were established with senior personnel knowledgeable about sensitive and sometimes controversial internal and external issues within the industry. This, in turn, led to contact with airline representatives, legislators, other CRS vendor organisations and travel organisations, creating an industry-wide ‘key informants network’. Secondly, grass roots exposure to the industry allowed the research team to focus on the most significant areas of CRS-generated information and competitor activity. Thirdly, face to face contact helped to establish credibility of the research team from an often-sceptical industry reluctant to discuss areas of commercial sensitivity.

While several topical issues of the day surfaced, attention was devoted to the perceived impact of the role played by CRSs in generating and sustaining competitive advantage. Broadly speaking, the following issues and themes emerged, among other things, as meriting further investigation:

\- availability and use of billing support output i.e. the information provided by a CRS vendor to airlines in relation to details of fee status based on reservations taken, and lease of hardware and software;

\- availability and use of management information derived from CRSs i.e. the information provided by a CRS vendor to airlines in relation to details of the volume of business taken;

\- influence of ownership status on access to billing output and management information.

In each case, the emphasis is placed on the use, access and information derived from such systems, rather than the technology.

## 3.2. Questionnaire survey

To broaden understanding of the issues highlighted previously and examine them on a more widespread basis, a questionnaire survey, based on the findings arising from the participatory phase together with issues discussed with key informants, was conducted.

This was designed to validate the concerns raised and assess the extent to which a competitive lead had been established and sustained by CRS-owning airlines.

The questionnaire was distributed to 145 of the 217 airlines registered with the International Civil Aviation Organisation (ICAO) at the time of the survey, covering those airlines with sufficient volumes of business to necessitate a technology-based reservations function (Humphreys, 1990; ICAO, 1990). Barber (1998) provides more details of the selection procedure.

The survey questionnaire included a range of system specific and general questions. In particular, analysis centred on the distribution status adopted by the responding carrier, categorised as:

1. Display Schedule Only—details of the carrier's schedules are available on the CRS but on-line reservations cannot be made though the system;

2. Participating—details of the carrier's schedules are available on the CRS and on-line reservations can be made through the system;

3. Ownership Stake—the carrier indicated that they either wholly owned or held an ownership stake in the CRS in addition to participating status.

A pilot survey was conducted and the sampled airlines cross-checked to ensure that each of the three above carrier types were represented on the major CRSs.

The pilot resulted in a poor response which, upon investigation, was attributed to the sensitive area under discussion. After some amendments, and with the aid of key informants, the questionnaire was distributed via a meeting of the Civil Aviation Systems Management Association (CASMA), a group of senior personnel representing over 100 international airlines with interest in CRSs. While reducing the numbers in the original target group and acknowledging a bias towards airlines with CRS involvement, analysis showed representation of airlines adopting various different distribution options across the range of available CRSs.

At the time of the survey, nine CRSs with a substantial foothold in the market place existed. These included Abacus, Amadeus, Apollo, Fantasia, Galileo, Gemini, Sabre, SystemOne, and Worldspan.

Analysis also centred on

\- the general level of involvement the responding carrier held across all of the CRSs covered by the survey;

\- ownership (analysed as two categories: owning or non-owning).

The general level of involvement of a carrier was assessed by examining the respondent's distribution status across each of the nine CRSs. A carrier with an ownership stake was considered to display a higher level of involvement than a display schedule only carrier. An 11-point numerical score was attached to each carrier for the purpose of analysis (Barber, 1998).

A less sophisticated measure of the involvement of the respondents with the CRSs was based upon ownership alone. This simple categorisation differentiated between ‘owners’ and ‘non-owners’, with the ensuing analysis treating this variable as a dichotomous response. An owner was considered to be any carrier who had indicated an ownership stake in any of the CRSs covered by the questionnaire, regardless of its size.

Closed-ended questions were used predominantly, but respondents were given the option to add additional comments towards the end of the questionnaire, common concerns being followed up in the interview programme.

## 3.3. Interview programme

In the light of the questionnaire analysis, the key issues surfacing from the survey, specifically the link between CRSs and the advantage derived for their owning carriers, were pursued in further detail with face to face interviews. Having already gathered information relating to a European CRS vendor, the interview programme centred on US CRS owning carriers, responsible for the majority of developments in world CRS distribution. Four US CRS vendor organisations were covered namely, Worldspan, Sabre, Covia (Apollo) and SystemsOne, controlled by Delta, North West, American, United, US Air and Continental airlines, the focus of the interview programme. Asian CRS vendors were not included as developments were in their infancy at this stage. However, Abacus, the largest Asian CRS vendor, subsequently formed links with Worldspan, operating its core through Worldspan's CRS, which was covered in the interview programme.

The interviews were conducted in the US with senior personnel at the level of Chairman, CEO, Directors of Marketing and Distribution. Interviewees were sent a list of topics of interest to the research team and, for commercial reasons, anonymity guaranteed. In addition, an interview with a senior adviser on CRS-related issues at the US Department of Transportation (DOT) was arranged to provide further insight and an alternative perspective of the effect of CRSs on airline industry competition. Common themes, and links with the questionnaire survey, are reported in this paper.

## 4. Research findings

The results and discussion given here relate predominantly to the questionnaire survey and interview programme, linking the responses together as the various issues arise. However, reference to the exploratory phase, noticeably the participatory phase and key informants network, is also made at appropriate junctures.

On a technical note, responses were coded appropriately and analysed using MINITAB software. In terms of the analysis presented in this section, as most variables consisted of two or three categories, tests of association are based on the chi-squared distribution, with parametric tests considered inappropriate. The hypotheses upon which these tests were performed are also given, with both p-values and number of respondents $(n)$ reported, the latter occasionally varying due to incomplete or missing data. Significant results are reported using a 0.1% significance level, unless otherwise stated. To avoid excessive use of contingency tables for each of the CRSs under consideration, graphs and tables arising from the aggregation of the data are utilised to clarify the trends apparent in the data. Barber (1998) provides fuller details of the statistical analysis.

The response rate to the questionnaire was high with sixty-four questionnaires returned representing over 65% of the membership of the CASMA network. The profile of respondents confirmed the bias in favour of carriers active in CRSs. More specifically, 90% of respondents indicated active participation in at least seven of the nine CRSs. However, participation rates were not equally divided across the five North American, two European and two Asian systems. Not surprisingly, the North American based systems were represented most strongly, with European CRSs having a higher level of uptake than their Asian counterparts, reflecting the dominance of US CRSs in airline product distribution.

The findings of the survey provide weighty support for the notion that the control, dissemination and manipulation of CRS data have played a vital role in sustaining competitive advantage for CRS-owning airlines. Evidence for such statements is varied and discussed here.

## 4.1. Format and frequency of billing support output

During the period of the survey, formats for billing support included the use of magnetic tape/cartridge or MIDT (Magnetic Information Data Tape), as well as microfilm/fiche and traditional paper output. For magnetic media, access to data is relatively swift due to the inherent compatibility of the medium used with the existing systems of carriers. Microfilm/fiche output presents a problem, however, as analysis depends on conversion to paper output that must then be transcribed into appropriate computer format via optical character recognition or manual keying. This clearly has significant ramifications on a carrier's ability to analyse their data in a timely fashion.

There is evidence to suggest that CRS vendors have operated differing benchmarks dependent on ownership status. Ignoring those airlines solely displaying their schedules on a particular CRS (in which case no billing support was provided), the survey results show that when aggregated, nearly 90% of participating carriers received their data output from vendors in microfilm/fiche format with only 11% of owning carriers receiving output in this form. For magnetic tape/cartridge, the trend was reversed, with two thirds of owners receiving it in this format compared to just 8% of participating carriers. One interviewee confirmed this directly, stating that ownership stake ensured that information was provided in a format requested, rather than having to accept the ‘standard’ format offered to participating carriers. A non-owning airline respondent emphasised the time value of CRS generated information suggesting that CRS vendors deliberately supplied non-magnetic media in an attempt to delay and dilute another competitor’s ability to utilise CRS data output effectively.

Interviewees shed further light on this issue. Three carriers remarked that CRS vendors frequently changed the format in which the data were made available to hinder competing carriers in their attempts to analyse and make use of it, although none admitted to their CRS engaging in such activity. One respondent made specific mention of the introduction of frequently changing codes and formats once systems were in place, as well as incomplete data sets, as deliberate ploys by CRS vendors to delay a competitor's ability to respond in a timely fashion. The regulatory safeguards were also called in to question with one interviewee commenting, ‘...it’s very difficult to get across the significance of what’s missing...even to people in the business...so it’s safe to say regulators don’t stand much of a chance’. When presented with this statement, the US DOT interviewee conceded that media and format of presentation were potentially causing difficulties for participating carriers. This suggests that in spite of continual regulation, CRS-developing carriers, while technically adhering to legislation, may have sought to find alternative ways of capitalising on their investments.

In terms of the frequency with which carriers were provided with billing support, there was evidence to suggest that, virtually all participating carriers (98%) received their billing support on a monthly basis, with a third of owning carriers in this situation. Conversely, over two thirds of owning carriers received billing support on an ‘as requested’ basis; less than 2% of participating carriers were afforded this privilege.

More formally, under the null hypothesis of independence between distribution status and frequency of provision of billing support, a chi-squared test based on these two variables gave statistically significant results across eight CRSs (all P-values <0.001 with n ranging from 54 to 61, dependent on the CRS). The alternative hypothesis that CRS ownership status and frequency of provision of billing support are not independent of each other is accepted, with CRS-owning airlines receiving billing support on a more frequent basis than participating airlines. (Note that one CRS was omitted as only 11 airlines had participating or owning status, the remaining 53 having display status only.)

Thus, with timing a key element of the airline sector and the ability to respond quickly a vital feature of the industry, potential advantage for owning carriers was again apparent. One interviewee confirmed this explicitly stating that as an owning airline, CRS data were available in any format desired and available on an ‘as required’ basis, a facility not available to non-owners.

## 4.2. Data access and cost

Questions relating to cost structures reinforced the notion that CRS vendor organisations adopted different strategies for charging participant and owning airlines. Furthermore, the idea that the owning carriers could attract revenue from participating carriers by charging for information relating to bookings taken through the systems also provides further ammunition supporting a suggestion of inequality between, and bias against, competitors. Interviewees, while keen to emphasise their adherence to government regulations insisting that CRS vendors make data available on a non-discriminatory basis to all participating carriers, acknowledged that providing CRS data output was costly. One carrier that had a CRS ownership stake but participated in other CRSs, quantified their MIDT bill as nearly \$700,000 per annum. With most CRS vendors charging a flat rate per MIDT, many small to medium sized airlines, with considerably smaller volumes of business than those of larger airlines, were unable to afford to access potentially useful information about their own business. Interviewees from non-owning airlines commented that fees were designed to ‘...price everyone out of knowing their own business’, and one European carrier described the pricing policies and billing support procedures of North American CRS vendors as ‘anti-competitive’.

The huge sums required to secure vast amounts of data, often in a form that makes analysis and interpretation difficult, effectively acted as a barrier to anyone other than the owning carriers, even though vendors had technically acted in observance of government regulations and guidelines.

## 4.3. Data content

Billing support encompasses not only financial information pertaining to fee status, but also includes access to different types of data of potential use in planning and trend analysis. Fig. 2 gives a breakdown of the degree of detail provided to carriers and their ownership status.

Virtually all carriers, irrespective of their owning status, received details of their own airline's business taken through the system (the two respondents claiming that such details were not provided had only recently subscribed to one of the systems and had not received billing support at the time of the survey). However, a quarter of non-owning and half of the owning carriers reported that they also received details of other airlines' business taken through the system on competing routes. Statistically, under the hypothesis of independence between ownership status and, correspondingly, each of the two types of billing support mentioned here (A and B), neither are significant with p-values of 0.7501 ( $n = 64$ ) and 0.0615 ( $n = 64$ ) respectively. Thus, the alternative hypotheses that ownership status is a factor in determining whether an airline receives either detailed summaries of its own business or other airlines' business on competing routes are not accepted here. However, over $30\%$ of owning carriers actually received details of all other airlines' business taken through the CRS, with only $6\%$ of non-owning carrier reporting such a privilege. Under the hypothesis of independence between ownership status and the third type of billing support (C) provided, a statistically significant result ( $P = 0.00058$ , $n = 64$ ) is recorded, leading to acceptance of the alternative hypothesis that where a detailed summary of all other airlines' business is provided, this is dependent on owning status, with owning carriers advantaged over their non-owning counterparts.

Thus, the findings confirmed an association between ownership status, in favour of owning carriers, and the more strategically useful information available to them about the business of other airlines on all, and not just competing, routes.

## 4.5. Management of CRS data and information

The approaches to management of CRS data, as well as the application of these data, are key elements influencing how well an airline performs, both operationally and strategically. Fig. 3 gives a breakdown of the differences in approach of owning and non-owning airlines to the dissemination of such information throughout their organisations.

Three quarters of owning carriers indicated that a specialist department existed for supplying managers with information derived from CRSs, with only 8% of non-owning carriers reporting such a facility. A statistically significant association, based on a null hypothesis of independence between ownership status and the existence of a specialist department, is noted $P = 0.0000, n = 62$ , leading to acceptance of the alternative hypothesis indicating that there are differences between owning and non-owning carriers, with specialist departments more prevalent amongst the former. There is no discernible statistical difference in the way in which owning and non-owning CRS carriers make such data available to authorised individuals within the organisation $P = 0.5013, n = 62$ , and the same is true for general departmental access $P = 0.1241, n = 62$ , under the hypothesis of independence between ownership status and the respective variables.

On balance, owning airlines appeared more likely to disseminate CRS-generated information to any department requiring it, rather than limiting access to certain authorised individuals. This appears indicative of the information culture pervading many of these organisations.

Further analysis also indicated that CRS owning carriers were more likely to supply an analysis of reservations data to members of their senior management teams than their non-owning counterparts.

More specifically, the following areas and issues are relevant when discussing the potential advantage derived from CRS data:

\- Format of billing support—the use of magnetic media greatly advantaged a CRS-owning carrier when compared with the paper and microfiche output provided to non-owning airlines.

\- Frequency of billing support—the ‘on request’ status given to CRS-owning carriers, as opposed to the typical monthly distribution available to non-owning airlines, permitted improved responsiveness in reacting to market conditions and competitor activity.

\- Costs of billing support—improved revenues for CRS vendors while, at the same time, increasing costs for competing airlines that acted as a financial barrier, particularly for smaller airlines.

\- Control and access—access to details of competing carriers' business, together with a greater perceived degree of control, gave a competitive edge to CRS-owning carriers.

\- Existence of specialist departments—CRS-owning carriers were more sophisticated in their approach to data and information handling, indicative of the importance attached to business and competitor intelligence by these airlines.

\- Empowerment of senior managers—greater emphasis was placed by CRS-owning carriers on ensuring senior managers had access to CRS-generated information, recognising its value in developing new strategic knowledge.

Collectively, the results presented in this paper suggest that CRS-owning carriers sought to develop new ways of capitalising on their investment by exploiting CRS data more effectively, when regulatory safeguards had eroded previous sources of competitive advantage.

However, recent developments have conspired to limit the potential advantage arising from CRS data. Traditional channels of distribution via travel agencies are now under threat from new platforms such as the Internet, mobile telephony and digital television. CRS-owning airlines, under the guise of GDSs, are adapting to this challenge, but the facility to book directly with individual airlines may redress the balance. While alliances and code sharing exist, so will integrated reservation systems, but the ability of airlines to directly capture on-line business may result in less reliance on CRS/GDS vendors providing billing support information, as patterns and trends become more readily identifiable as part of routine operational business. Currently, however, business-to-consumer Internet reservations account for a relatively small percentage of total airline reservations and thus, in the short term, the issues related to the competitive use of CRS-generated information are likely to remain pertinent and relevant during the next phase of airline-based reservations systems development.

The Internet has captured the imagination of researchers and practitioners alike, and the monitoring of Internet usage and applications, and the alleged business advantages that result, will continue to be a source of enduring interest and research in the coming years. Furthermore, the developments in technology-based information systems, the applications of information and knowledge management, as well as regulatory legislation, have seen the airline industry continually re-inventing itself. On the evidence presented in this paper, key players have succeeded in capitalising on their investment and retained an edge over competitors. On these grounds alone, the airline industry is likely to be the focus of much of the future research on developments in technology, information and knowledge management, and the strategic advantages that such advances may bestow on GDS-owning airlines.
