# Building outsourcing relationships across the global community: the UPS–Motorola experience

Moshe Zviran $^{a,*}$ , Niv Ahituv $^{a}$ , Aviad Armoni $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Management of Technology and Information Systems Program, Faculty of Management, Tel Aviv University, Tel Aviv 69978, Israel

$^{b}$ College of Management, Rishon Lezion, 75910, Israel

Received 31 October 1998; revised 28 February 2001; accepted 1 July 2001

## Abstract

The United Parcel Service (UPS)–Motorola case study revolves around two major areas of information technology (IT) management: IT as a competitive tool and global outsourcing of IT projects. It provides an opportunity to become familiar with the decisions and actions taken by UPS to assure their competitive advantage in the turbulent courier services industry. In the IT outsourcing area, the case addresses managerial and technical issues associated with outsourcing IT projects across the global community. The case presents the specific factors that made this outsourcing project successful and discusses the development of the outsourcing relationships between UPS and Motorola (Israel), from initial negotiations to the creation of a strategic alliance between the two companies. A major point in this case is the gradual development of trust between the two sides, trust that led the two firms from being committed to a contract to becoming strategic partners. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: United Parcel Service; Motorola; Competitive advantage; Outsourcing; Strategic partnership; Communications

## 1. Introduction

The United Parcel Service (UPS)–Motorola case study focuses on issues associated with managing information technology (IT) projects across the global community. The case traces the development of the outsourcing relationships between UPS and Motorola (Israel) during the period 1989–1997. It discusses how the initial outsourcing decision was made, the factors considered by each party, and the elements negotiated and agreed in the contract. The case then tells about the planning and implementation of the outsourced development, and, more specifically, how the project was managed and executed by Motorola Israel. Its flexibility in accommodating to UPS' changing design needs coupled with its technical capability and solid delivery record led to increasing levels of trust and openness between the two companies over the years, and eventually transformed their relationship into a strategic alliance.

## 2. UPS background

UPS is the world's largest parcel and document delivery company. The company's headquarters is located in the United States, but it operates many branches worldwide. The company was founded in 1907 in Seattle, Washington, to provide private messenger and delivery services and the transportation of packages for retail stores. Despite competition from other organizations offering similar services, UPS flourished and thrived. The company gradually assembled a fleet of vehicles, and, beginning in 1953, UPS expanded its services to include air delivery between US cities. In 1980, the company commenced international commercial activities and parcel delivery outside the US, opening offices in Central and South America, Europe, the Middle East, Africa, Australia, and Southeast Asia.

In 1988, UPS obtained a license to operate its own fleet of planes, and quickly experienced tremendous growth in air delivery, breaking major records in the American airline industry. By 1989, UPS was using a vast network of company offices and local affiliates to reach more than 200 countries and territories. The company's revenues stood at 12 billion dollars. It employed about 250,000 workers, operated 122,000 trucks and 470 planes, and delivered 11.5 million items daily. UPS was essentially one of the world's largest airlines.

## 3. The role of IT in UPS

During its expansion, UPS placed great emphasis on the field of information systems. Following the increase in competition, primarily from Federal Express and DHL, the company's management decided to focus on customer service as well as operational efficiency. Following this strategic shift, the company adopted the slogan: to leapfrog the competition with IT. The company invested 1.5 billion dollars in IT during 1984–1989, and, indeed, succeeded with accomplishing its goals, both in terms of infrastructure and applicability. Nevertheless, the rapid growth of Federal Express, DHL, and other competitors threatened the predominance of UPS in the parcel delivery industry (Bradley, 1990).

At the end of 1989, Frank Erbrick, the then senior vice-president and CIO of UPS, opened a senior staff meeting of the company's information systems department by emphasizing the need for UPS to use IT for competitive advantage:

Ladies and gentlemen, UPS is facing stiff competition in the parcel delivery business. The key to our success will be effective utilization of advanced technology and information systems. Our goal is to assess UPS' information systems and the ability of the company to compete, and in this way to prepare a strategy for future information systems. Most of you are familiar with the company's system; however, in order to provide the larger picture I will outline briefly the company, its activities and the information network that has been developed and established during the last few years. After this summary, we will have an open discussion and brainstorming session as we attempt to chart our course in this area. Please note that information systems are critical to the existence of the company, and failure to address our needs in this field could result in disaster for us all.

After a series of intensive meetings with members of our executive board. We have decided that we can gain a significant competitive advantage if we can devise a method of on-line tracking of deliveries. This tracking system will require a constant flow of information between the driver who makes the delivery and the company's central computer. We must therefore determine how to achieve simultaneous linkage and tracing. Naturally, such a technology shift requires decisions regarding the means of data transmission and selection of the required hardware. Further, an operational decision must be made as to whether the project will be performed internally or outsourced. To this end, UPS management has decided to invest the sum of 3.2 billion dollars over the next five years in information technology, in addition to amounts already invested.

Following Erbrick's opening comments and a discussion of the opportunities and possible avenues for action, a task force was established to devise a proposal for the next generation information system at UPS. The goals of the special task force were to evaluate the existing situation, analyze its weaknesses, and propose possible directions to transform UPS IT into a strategic tool which would strengthen UPS' competitive advantage and maintain its market predominance.

## 4. Deciding on a new information system for UPS

Motivated by the company's management belief that the key to success was communication with the customer, the task force agreed at the outset to concentrate on the customer communication process. They evaluated the various stages in the service chain and identified the information on package tracking, from the point at which it is collected until it is delivered to the recipient, as the weak link in the chain. Since a chain is only as strong as its weakest link, the task force decided to focus their efforts on strengthening UPS' performance in package tracking.

The process of package tracking in the late 1980s was based on portable terminals manufactured by the Japanese company Inforite and referred to as DIADs (Delivery Information Acquisition Device). During the course of the day, the courier utilized the DIAD to enter package details pertaining to the recipient, the package destination, time of package pickup or delivery, and more. At the end of the day, all the information collected in the portable terminal were transmitted to the company's central computer and updated on the central database. The use of the DIADs in the delivery process and the flow of information are illustrated in Fig. 1.

These portable terminals, however, only partially met UPS' need for current information, and suffered from several deficiencies. First, they experienced a high failure rate, causing tracking information lapses and requiring non-computerized tracking of deliveries. In addition, since the process of updating the central database occurred only once a day rather than continuously throughout the day, the company was unable to provide continuous package tracking. These two weaknesses impaired UPS' efficiency in terms of parcel tracking, and also raised a concern about the loss of the company's competitive advantage.

After lengthy discussions among the task force members and consultations with outside experts in the field of IT, the committee was clear on the need to forge a better link between the courier, who carries the portable terminal, and the company's computer center. Moreover, since the driver is perpetually in transit and does not remain in a single location, the practical answer for such a link was wireless communication. The following alternatives were considered:

1. Integration into a cellular communications network, which is a voice-based network. This requires the use of a modem for dialing and transmitting data.

2. Use of satellite communications.

3. Integration into a private packet-switching data network owned by a third party, such as the ARDIS network owned by Motorola and IBM or the RAM network owned and operated by Ericsson.

In the process of evaluating the alternatives, the task force members agreed that the use of satellite communications and the establishment of a UPS private communications network were not feasible at that stage. Therefore, both were dropped from further discussion. When evaluating the use of a cellular network versus joining the packet-switched ARDIS system, there were different opinions. The proposal to use the ARDIS network appeared feasible and practicable. However, in the late 1980s, the ARDIS network only had 42,000 subscribers and UPS was concerned that by joining the network it would double the volume of use, with UPS as the primary user. On the other hand, cellular network use was still in its infancy. Cellular services were furnished by local companies who offered local coverage, which necessitated dealing with hundreds of service providers to ensure complete coverage. Furthermore, telecommunications experts at the time did not predict a bright future for cellular communications.

The participants decided to focus on the ARDIS network as the preferred alternative, but to leave the cellular option for further consideration. In addition, it became clear that a universal communications solution was not possible, since different countries possessed different technology infrastructures. Nevertheless, they decided to test the technology in the United States and then to expand to other countries while evaluating the available communications technology in each country.

After considering another issue — methods of development and production — the task force team decided to rely on engineers for programming and design. At the conclusion of the discussion, the following recommendations were proposed:

\- UPS should focus on improving communication between the courier and the company's database via near real-time data transmission between the portable terminals and the company's central computer.

\- Implementation of a ‘DIAD Vehicle Adapter’, or ‘DVA’. Each company vehicle should be equipped with a DVA, a stationary device that, together with a suitable data radio, allows the courier’s portable terminal to transmit and receive information between the courier and the UPS computer network.

\- Implementation of the communications solution, using a public communications network (such as ARDIS) or cellular communication. Since a global solution was not feasible, an independent solution for each country using available technology should be devised.

\- Design of the system by UPS engineers and its affiliated companies (Roadnet and IIMorrow) and outsourcing of the manufacturing portion of the project.

In order to promote uniformity, whenever possible, the plan delineated the development of two separate systems:

\- A package for the DIAD portable terminal connected to the network for data transmission.

\- The data transmission network itself.

After the decision was made, the IIMorrow engineers began planning the DVA. At the same time, a project team was established comprising UPS representatives and IIMorrow experts. This joint team considered various methods to construct the DVA, and the sophisticated computer at the heart of the system.

## 5. In search of the right supplier

The field of wireless data transmission and mobile hand-held terminals bridges the fields of computing and wireless communications technology. UPS sought a company with expertise in both technologies, and the search proved difficult. Major suppliers of high-end computer equipment, including IBM, Hewlett-Packard, Toshiba, NEC and Digital, as well as major suppliers of wireless communications equipment such as Motorola, GE,

Ericsson, Philips, EF Johnson, Kenwood and ICOM, were considered. While IBM is a major seller and supplier of computer terminals, including those equipped with wireless modems, contact with IBM revealed that they purchased their wireless communication components from Motorola.

UPS also considered smaller companies who possessed expertise in wireless data transmission technology, such as portable computers capable of collecting data and transmitting them via radio waves. Among those companies were Grid, Telxon, Psion, and even Toshiba. However, following this search it became apparent that these companies also purchased their wireless communication components from Motorola.

The project team then approached Motorola and requested a proposal addressing possible methods to solve the communications component of the DVA. After analyzing the situation, Motorola decided to attack the problem from two different angles and submitted two competing bids. The first proposal entailed utilizing the company's Paging Division to build a system based on the ARDIS network. In the second bid, the Cellular Division at Motorola offered a solution based on cellular communication. In both bids, Motorola aimed to win the portion of the project involving wireless communication, since that was their field of expertise.

In October 1991, the UPS project team convened a staff meeting with representatives of the Paging Division at Motorola. Present at the meeting were UPS' Roger Dingus, IIMorrow's Norman Rhodes, Zahir Abji, a senior member of the Paging Division at Motorola, and Menachem Diamantstein from Motorola Communications (Israel), who developed the modem as an end unit for the ARDIS network. Roger Dingus summarized the status of the project: A DVA prototype had been designed by IIMorrow, and two options, the ARDIS network and a cellular network, were being considered for data transmission. At this point in the development stage, an experimental network using ARDIS technology had also been established to test its capabilities.

Motorola then discussed their proposed solution and the inherent advantages of the ARDIS technology. During the course of reviewing the DVA design sketches, Motorola suggested that their team be authorized to design a total solution for the vehicles, including the DVA and the transmission of data over the ARDIS network.

However, after examining the coverage issues and the relatively low subscriber rate to the ARDIS network, which would have rendered UPS the predominant user, the UPS team decided instead to use cellular communications for data transmission. UPS proposed using Motorola to develop an end-to-end solution, including the design of a DVA for the UPS vehicles, but utilizing cellular communications rather than the ARDIS network.

This posed some manufacturing problems for Motorola, however. Based upon the potentially small scope of the UPS order and the fact that DVA production was outside the company's normal product line, Zahir Abji, Motorola's senior representative, indicated that Motorola's management would be reluctant to become involved in the project as proposed. However, Menachem Diamantstein discussed the meeting's main points with Elisha Yanay, CEO of Motorola Communications (Israel), whose initial interest in the project had been based on the opportunity to promote sales of modems to the ARDIS network, which had been developed by Motorola Communications (Israel). He considered the facts and the commercial context of the offer and identified the opportunity for his company. He contacted his parent company's executives in the US and told them:

It appears that UPS has decided to use cellular communications. Thus, it is nearly certain that you will recommend that we reject their offer and abandon the project. In my opinion, abandoning such a project would be a mistake. In view of the facts reported by Menachem Diamantstein, it seems that this project is only the tip of the iceberg, as I believe it is just the first of a series. It is clear that we are talking about the assumption of an enormous risk, since the development of the DVA is beyond the strategic goals of Motorola Communications (Israel). However, the opportunity presented by this project, in the event that it is the first in a series, outweighs the risk. Therefore, I am prepared to accept this extraordinary challenge, and to have Motorola Communications develop the DVA. The cellular division will develop the modem for data transmission, and together, at Motorola, we will formulate the desired solution.

Following a series of intense discussions, Motorola's management continued to debate the merits of accepting the deal. Elisha Yanay used all his powers of persuasion to lobby his colleagues and senior executives to accept UPS' offer.

Finally, Motorola was given the green light after advising UPS that they accepted one of the key conditions set by UPS, namely, to provide a comprehensive warranty for the DVA. They agreed to this condition despite the fact that the cellular communications modem for the network would be produced by Motorola, Inc., and the DVA was to be built by Motorola Communications (Israel).

## 6. UPS assessment of Motorola (Israel)

From UPS' perspective, the above agreement was significant because it covered an end-to-end solution across Motorola business sectors. UPS began to review Motorola's commercial dealings, range of activities and the expertise of Motorola (Israel) in general and their direct partner, Motorola Communications (Israel), in particular. The following paragraphs provide a picture of Motorola Israel at the time it was assessed by UPS.

Motorola (Israel), Inc. was established in 1964, as a wholly owned subsidiary of Motorola, Inc. (USA). The parent company was a multinational corporation primarily oriented towards telecommunications. Worldwide, its annual revenues in 1990 reached nearly eleven billion dollars, while enjoying continued growth.

Motorola (Israel) consisted of several subsidiaries in the telecommunications business. The subsidiaries acted as independent profit centers. Some subsidiaries were fully integrated operations with activities ranging from product design, through development, production, marketing, and after sales customer service, while others focused on a more limited range of activities. The following is a brief description of each of the subsidiaries:

\- Motorola Communications (Israel) — Motorola Communications was founded in 1985, and its focus was primarily on development, production and marketing handheld mobile radio systems, car phones, command systems, broadcast controls, and various electronic systems. The company’s products were geared to both the local market and export markets.

\- Motorola Semiconductor — Motorola Semiconductor was founded in 1982 for the purpose of supplying programming and design assistance to Motorola companies which utilize semiconductors. The company designed, processed, developed, and produced microprocessors designed for data communications, signal processing, multimedia, design boards, computer aided design (CAD) and more.

The management of UPS was impressed with this track record and agreed to award Motorola Communications (Israel) the project for the development of the DVA. The following sections discuss in detail the different phases of the DVA project.

## 7. The contract

The contract between UPS and Motorola involved the development of a total solution for data transfer between the DIAD terminal held by the UPS courier and the company's central computer. Motorola Communications (Israel) was given responsibility for the design and manufacture of the DVA. Motorola, Inc. was in charge of design and production of the cellular modems, as well as building the logistical infrastructure, which would facilitate integration of the various components and installation of the DVA in UPS vehicles. The first stage of the project involved production of 52,500 DVA units. The first order was due to be shipped at the beginning of July 1992, while complete DVA systems were due to be installed and fitted by November 1992, in order to ensure that the systems would be completely operational prior to the peak delivery season. In addition, pursuant to a request by UPS, the contract included a clause specifying the payment of a penalty for failure to meet the timetable. Essentially, Motorola Communications promised to pay this fine if the final shipment of DVAs was not delivered to the UPS facility in Louisville, Kentucky, by Monday, October 6, 1992, at 10:00 a.m.

The significance of this commitment, and the risk assumed by Motorola Communications was enormous. The project concept and preliminary details about the DVA emerged in February 1991. The period for development and production of a new project was generally accepted to be 18 months. Thus, the project deadline was set for October 1992. In reality, negotiations were concluded only in January 1992. In spite of this, and due to the importance of the system to UPS management, the company could not postpone the project deadline. In effect, the timetable for the design and production phase was shortened to nine months instead of the customary 18 months.

In addition, within Motorola Communications itself, internal dissension increased over the project as a result of several factors. The design staff maintained that this type of product did not fit the company's strategic goals. The financial people viewed the project as a one-time deal, and insisted that the entire endeavor was unprofitable. They maintained that it absorbed tremendous financial resources that would almost certainly not be recouped since this product line would not be continued. An additional objection was heard from the manufacturing facility in Arad. The production capacity of the factory at the time stood at 50,000 units annually. Undertaking the project entailed doubling the production capacity. Furthermore, they would be compelled to train and hire additional qualified personnel, or alternatively to redirect all available production resources in favor of this project, which, as mentioned, was received on a one-time basis.

Another provision of the contract, which was accepted by Motorola for the first time in its history, included a penalty or reward for product reliability. This provision meant in essence that Motorola guaranteed that it would fix any problem or breakdown in the DVA for 28 months from the time of delivery, without charge for the repair. The contract specified such things as failure rates, fines and repair agreements, as well as a bonus clause for exceeding expectations.

## 8. Project management

Given the tight production schedule, both sides worked quickly to prepare for the project. UPS appointed the following management team:

\- Dudley Land, a senior member of Frank Erbrick's team, supervised the project for the company.

\- Angelo Sampona was a key manager in the project's product acceptance testing.

\- Dave Salzman was designated project manager by UPS.

\- Norman Rhodes was appointed UPS' project representative in Israel.

Meanwhile, Motorola Communications appointed Udi Katina as project manager and assembled a team of professionals from various Motorola divisions as well as outside experts. With regard to control and supervision of the project, Norman Rhodes was chosen to monitor the progress of the project and to report to UPS management in the US. Weekly committee meetings were scheduled between UPS representatives in the US and the design team to discuss the progress of the project and any difficulties. The senior staff headed by Dave Salzman convened monthly meetings and discussed strategies and plans frequently. Senior UPS management representatives planned to come to Israel as needed to assess the progress of the project. During the organizational phase of the project, the first problem arose: the final contract had not yet been signed and Motorola had already spent considerable sums of money for additional salaries and other start-up costs related to the project. Following extensive discussions, and in order to prevent project delays, UPS agreed to underwrite expenses related to the start of work to provide a safety net for Motorola, and with the understanding that after the contract was signed all sums expended would be deemed as an advance against the final project payment. The contract was executed in February 1992.

## 9. Planning and design

The first phase of the project focused on planning and design of the product. Although the UPS team originally intended its own IIMorrow division to perform all planning and design, and to outsource only the manufacturing portion of the project, in fact, when they witnessed the technological capability of Motorola's engineers, UPS decided to allow them to completely redesign the product. The development team at Motorola Communications worked with UPS representatives to design the DVA according to UPS specifications. In March 1992, the design was completed and the first prototype of the DVA was sent for field-testing to the US. This phase caused some concern on the part of both UPS and Motorola. UPS preferred testing fewer units, since the assessment was complex and demanded time and input from engineers, auditors, and others in the delivery process. On the other hand, the Motorola Communications staff was adamant that extensive field testing of at least 300 units was necessary to identify the maximum number of performance and design flaws at this early stage. Ultimately, both companies agreed to conduct field tests of 100 units, which were done in April 1992. Following the testing, and in accordance with plans, the team rushed to complete development in an effort to meet the deadline.

## 10. Accommodating changing requirements

At the beginning of May 1992, when the planning and design of the DVA was in its final stages, the project management team, headed by Dudley Land and Dave Salzman, arrived in Israel for discussions regarding the project's progress. This visit was preceded by an important, yet secret, decision by UPS regarding the development of a new generation of DIAD terminals and preliminary work on this terminal by IIMorrow engineers. In order to support this concept, and to enable the new portable terminal to be compatible with the DVA being developed by Motorola Communications, a new request was made that a new connector be integrated into the DVA design so that the DVA could be easily converted for use with the new terminal that was under development. Motorola had real concerns regarding this new requirement, fearing the change would lead to further plan modifications, delays in the development period and failure to meet their manufacturing deadline. From the perspective of Motorola Communications, the company found itself at crossroads. The request entailed a fundamental change in the design of a product ready for production. During the discussion, Motorola CEO Elisha Yanay suggested a novel and creative solution to the problem. He proposed the simultaneous development of two different versions of the DVA: the current model which was ready for production and which included the original connector, and, in parallel, to revise the plans and develop a new model with the capacity to operate both the original and the revised connectors. The deadline was deemed critical to the success of the project. Therefore, the schedule would remain for the original model and it would proceed to production. However, once the new version with both connectors was ready for production, Motorola would cease manufacturing the original model, and produce only the new version. It is important to note that UPS' reluctance to amend the deadline jeopardized Motorola's ability to fulfill the terms of the contract. However, Motorola would do whatever was necessary to please the customer, in the words of the company's CEO.

In retrospect, when analyzing the events that occurred then, both companies concur that it represented the turning point in their relationship. UPS management headed by its CIO, Frank Erbrick, appreciated the enormous risk undertaken by Motorola Communications when the company could have rigidly adhered to the terms of the original agreement, and to the model that had already been developed. Motorola's tremendous flexibility, in addition to its proven technological capability, contributed to an improvement in the working environment and greater openness in the relationship between the companies.

## 11. Manufacturing and delivery

The project continued according to schedule while the relations and trust between the sides continued to grow. The planning and design of the new DVA was completed simultaneously with the original version. Thus, the entire manufacturing phase was performed according to the new specifications. The first shipments began in the middle of June 1992, at the rate of 700 units per day. In order to integrate the different components of the system, a special distribution center was established in Louisville, Kentucky. Project components, including the DVA, were assembled there, along with connecting cables (from Israel) and the cellular modem (by Motorola, Inc.). All of the components were combined, inspected, and integrated at the distribution center and then distributed as a single piece of equipment to UPS centers across the United States for installation in delivery vehicles. UPS personnel installed the equipment until the beginning of the peak delivery season, in November 1992. All told, Motorola sent UPS 52,500 DVAs according to the terms of the contract and on schedule. Additional units were manufactured and installed in UPS' growing fleet of company vehicles.

## 12. Development of the DIAD II portable terminal (stage II)

At the same time that the DVA was being developed by Motorola, a UPS team, along with IIMorrow engineers, began to prototype and plan the next generation of portable terminals for deliveries. The new portable terminal was called the DIAD II, and was intended to replace the original DIAD portable terminal.

Initially, the UPS product specifications reflected the primary concern with identifying a communications solution that performed accurate and timely parcel tracking. In accordance with this goal, the group identified the following criteria for the new portable terminal:

\- Data collection on parcels, including package identification, the name of the sender, the name of the recipient, and the destination.

\- Written confirmation of receipt, and storage of the signature for future reference.

\- The ability to transmit information in real time via radio communication, and storage in the UPS central computer.

\- The ability to upload the daily work schedule and to update information in real time, in response to changing needs.

\- Use of radio channels to transmit the most vital data, combined with completion via the UPS telecommunications network during the evening and night time hours.

\- An alternative source of data transmission via phone lines in the event that the cellular system is not operational.

\- Total reliability of the system even in difficult situations. The system must be able to function despite electromagnetic disturbances prevalent in cellular communications.

\- A system that can withstand falls of up to four feet (1.20 m).

In short, they conceived a unique portable terminal with specialized technology that could not be built using existing equipment but demanded special planning and design. UPS representatives turned to Motorola Communications to design and build the DIAD II portable terminal. As mentioned earlier, UPS favored Motorola due to the respect and trust which had been cultivated, following the solution of the connector issue with the DVA. During the initial phases of discussions, the Motorola Communications team proposed certain modifications to the original plans which had been designed by the IIMorrow engineers. These changes were consistent with the demand for absolute security and reliability. Following the successful collaboration on the DVA project, and given UPS' high regard for the professional expertise of Motorola's engineers, the UPS representatives agreed to the request on the condition that every proposed change be submitted for review and approval by their own engineers. The project progressed quickly and a preliminary understanding was reached. In January 1993, Motorola's staff were given the green light to begin the project despite the fact that the formal contract had not yet been signed.

When the new project began in January 1993, the first order was placed for 20,000 DIAD II hand-held portable terminals, 5000 new model DVA II units for use with the new terminals, and an order to convert 15,000 DVA units which had been manufactured in the prior project for use with the newly designed portable terminals.

## 13. New challenges, new solutions

A team of developers was assigned to devise technological solutions for the functional needs of the portable terminals. These engineers specialized in various technological fields: computers (hardware and software), radio, lasers, mechanical kits, information and planning of broad systems, planning for advanced production systems, and quality controls. Udi Katina, who was selected to direct this project as well, assembled the development team, described the goals and timetables, and concluded his talk with an emphasis on the challenge inherent in this project. He noted:

There is no question that our success with the recently concluded DVA project contributed greatly to the assignment of the current project to Motorola. Moreover, the challenge presented by the development and production of this new portable terminal is greater than the earlier project. This product differs from the traditional radio lines in which we have specialized until now. The structure of the product is complex, and it presents a number of unresolved technological challenges.

In addition, there were three specific requirements for the portable terminals:

\- the portable terminal must be strong enough to withstand falls from a height of four feet (1.2 m),

\- the portable terminal must be absolutely reliable, with a specified maximum monthly repair rate, and

\- the entire design process, including development, production, and testing, must conclude by the end of October 1993, in order to ensure that the system is operational prior to the holiday period in the United States.

“Given these challenges,” added Katina, “We must be prepared to work diligently and efficiently. Our success in this project will prove our capability, both to UPS and to Motorola, Inc., and will lead the way to future projects.” Despite the fact that the formal contract was signed only at the end of March, planning began in early January 1993 on the basis of the preliminary professional understanding reached between UPS and Motorola.

During the development phase, weekly staff meetings were convened between all the parties to the project with the participation of a representative from each team. The agenda for these meetings was selected in advance according to the schedule as well as other timely issues which arose. In this project, as in the prior project, UPS representatives and the Motorola team met monthly for expanded project meetings. The project progressed as planned due to the tremendous effort expended by the team. In April, 30 units were sent to the US for successful field tests. The second round of field tests was performed in May and June on 200 units. This test was deemed a complete success as well, and the team advanced to the final stages following receipt of positive feedback and clear signals that the work was satisfactory to UPS. The design and development phases continued for seven months, concluding in July, 1993.

The technological advance achieved in the project was based on the successful integration of multiple technologies in a single product. The development team succeeded in fusing the following technologies:

\- Integration of cellular radio channels with computers to transmit data in real time

\- Development of cellular communications protocols including the ability to correct errors

\- Digitizer technology permitting data collection (using a bar code) and broadcast

\- LCD monitors which display the data to users and provide appropriate operational instructions

\- Use of microprocessors which receive data, process them, and transmit them to the appropriate destination

\- Technology for collection and storage of information until dispatch

\- High-tech packaging using light plastic able to withstand difficult conditions and

\- Effective use of project management methods for advanced technology. These methods include design for manufacturability, comprehensive quality controls, quality control at the level of ‘six sigma’ $^{1}$ and more.

## 14. Reengineering production processes

In conjunction with product development, the contract specified development of a new product line. This was to be performed using a ‘pincer movement’ by reengineering the production process. While the design and development team concentrated on ‘product creation’ (engineers appreciate the frustration which results when a machine which is perfect on paper suffers critical failures at the manufacturing stage, when this phase is ignored by designers), the old warehouse in the Motorola South factory was emptied and quickly converted to a suitable production facility. The assessments recommended leasing a production line (at a cost of one million dollars), configuring the line and constructing it in the Arad facility, and hiring and training workers. The new production line was unique, and was specially designed to produce a high volume of units in a short period: 750 units per day, for three months (August, September, October). The production line utilized automated machinery, robots, computerized quality controls, specialized conveyors, and more. In addition to the standard equipment, many accessories and much specialized equipment were adapted for use. The customized production line was built to achieve maximum efficiency and designed to ensure a rapid pace of production to fulfill the terms of the contract while reducing expenses to the extent possible. In the non-manufacturing aspects too, Motorola attempted to promote efficiency, speed, and cost-effectiveness. For example, a truck was purchased and specially fitted to move and transport the final products in packing which could be loaded directly on to the plane (from an area designed for this purpose), resulting in lower costs for air deliveries. Hiring considerations for new employees for the production line also took into account the short production time (three months) as well as the desire to reduce turnover of the professional staff employed in the production facility in Arad or the need to hire new workers and to fire them after the peak season. Therefore, Motorola sought local high school graduates who had not yet been inducted into the military for their compulsory service, and offered them work on a seasonal basis on the assembly line. Motorola also approached the Israeli Defense Forces, and obtained permission to delay the induction of certain workers, explaining that by working in the factory, these students obtained skills which would be useful to the army. The students in the program were highly motivated and enthusiastic and the program was considered a resounding success. Many of the student workers returned to the factory after the completion of their military service and were given permanent jobs. The production and delivery of the new terminals began according to schedule, and at its peak 750 units were produced and shipped daily.

At the conclusion of Stage 2 of the project, all of the new terminal units were shipped on time including the DVAs, both the new model and the conversion kits. In addition, the project contributed to a deepening of the spirit of team work, professional appreciation, and the close personal relationship between the UPS team and the Motorola Communications team, and between the managers of Motorola Communications (Israel) and the senior management at UPS. In particular, a close friendship was cultivated between the CEO of Motorola Communications — Elisha Yanay and Frank Erbrick, senior vice-president and CIO of UPS, who was in charge of the project from UPS.

## 15. Accomplishments

The conclusion of Stage II and integration of the DIAD II terminals with the DVA II packages compatible with this generation of terminals, resolved the problem of computerized support in the delivery chain and represented the attainment of the project's main goals from the UPS standpoint:

• obtaining information on surface freight, and

\- obtaining immediate and constant data during the day.

The solution developed by Motorola for UPS operated on the basis of three principal components:

1. A mobile hand-held terminal (DIAD II) carried by the courier;

2. An adapter (DVA) connecting the hand-held terminal and the wireless modem installed in the vehicle;

3. A wireless, cellular telephone modem (CTM) connected to a cellular network.

The integration of these components in UPS' data transmission network is depicted in Fig. 4.

The new technology allowed the courier to enter and receive on-time data on matters related to deliveries: the contents of the package, identifying details, the address of the sender and the recipient, the time of collection and delivery and more. Details are indicated on the LCD display monitor, which verifies the information by means of a man machine interface (MMI). When a customer sends a package, receipt is confirmed by a signature on the digitizer found on the front of the terminal. This signature is displayed on the LCD monitor and encoded into the database.

At this stage, the DIAD microprocessor processes the details pertaining to the package's route and forwards them to the central computer in the customer's offices. When the courier returns to his or her vehicle, he or she reinserts the DIAD into the DVA installed in the vehicle. The DVA detects that the portable terminal has been placed, automatically records the information loaded in the terminal using optical communication, processes the information and transmits it via a cellular phone network using the wireless modem. It automatically dials the preprogrammed telephone number, connects to the computer and transmits only the most vital details. When the courier returns to the base at the end of the work day, he or she places the terminal in a fixed base station and, during the night, the remaining data are transmitted to the UPS data centers using UPS' telecommunications network. When that step has been completed, the terminal is prepared for the next work-day.

UPS corporate guidelines demand that couriers inform the central computer of a delivery within minutes after completion. Customers who wish to confirm receipt of a package (or to ascertain the time of delivery) may phone one of the company's regional offices. These local offices are connected to the central computer and are capable of locating the details of delivery in seconds. UPS agents can report the time of delivery and the identity of the recipient to interested customers.

## 16. Subsequent developments

In 1993, the DVA units were already working in the field, with a very low percentage of breakdowns, and Stage 2 of the project, development and production of the new generation of DIAD terminals, was under way at Motorola Communications. This phase led to further improvement in the trust and appreciation between the companies. The UPS team was convinced of the excellent engineering and technical capability of the Motorola Communications engineers, their understanding of the customer's needs, and their willingness to accommodate UPS requests. The results of the project, including meeting the goals and providing complete product reliability, paved the way for future strategic cooperation between the companies.

To continue to improve the design and reliability of the DIAD II product, in 1995 representatives of Motorola Communications submitted a new proposal to revise the DIAD II terminals using ASIC technology. UPS accepted the proposal, and in 1996 a new version of the DIAD II terminal was created.

In 1996, DIAD I, the original portable terminal developed by Motorola, was reaching the end of its technical lifecycle, and it was concluded that it would be preferable to replace the DIAD I terminals with DIAD II. Beginning in mid-1996, 50,000 of the new DIAD II terminals were manufactured for 1997 deployment; this new generation of DIADs completely replaced the DIAD I population in the field. At the conclusion of the DIAD II project, UPS designated Motorola Communications (Israel) as its engineering design resource for future generation development.

## 17. Discussion: from an outsourcing project to a strategic partnership

The project of developing the new DVA equipment and the portable terminals for UPS began as a classic example of a ‘Build-to-Specifications’ outsourcing project. As noted, UPS was initially committed to having the DVA designed by IIMorrow engineers, and to have the manufacturing portion of the DVA performed by an outside firm on an outsourcing basis. Such projects fall into the ‘exchange-based relationship’ outsourcing category, which represents the simplest and most limited sense of outsourcing (Lacity and Hirschheim, 1993; Lacity and Willcocks 1998, 2000).

The changes in product design in the middle of the product development phase coupled with a very tight project schedule put the project in the high-risk category (McFarlan, 1981). The IS literature suggests that such strategically important, yet risky, projects require top management attention (McFarlan, 1981; Ozz, 1994; Ahituv et al., 1999). Indeed, a critical factor in the project's success was UPS' close monitoring of its progress. This included appointment of a UPS' senior IS manager as a joint project manager and the relocation of a project representative to Israel for the duration of the project. In addition, UPS' CIO was periodically updated on the progress of the project and was also involved in critical decisions.

As the project progressed, the relationship between UPS and Motorola Communications constantly improved and deepened, eliminating any early apprehensions either company may have felt. For example, the issue of the connector and its design, and the successful solution that was devised, melted the ice and led to greater trust and openness between the upper echelons of the two companies. Consequently, by the end of the project the outsourcing relationship between UPS and Motorola Communications (Israel) upgraded from a classic, stand-alone, outsourcing project to a strategic alliance that represents the highest extent of outsourcing relationships (Nam et al., 1996; Willcocks and Lacity, 1997). Thus, when the decision was made to outsource the DIAD II project, it was awarded to Motorola on the specific condition that it would be performed by Motorola Communications (Israel).

The importance of this strategic alliance to both companies is demonstrated through the tight and symbiotic work relationships between the two companies. The UPS team was convinced of the technical capabilities of Motorola Communications engineers, their understanding of the customer's needs, and their willingness to accommodate UPS requests. In some cases, Motorola's engineers took the initiative to suggest design changes. For example, to improve the design and reliability of the DIAD II product, Motorola Communications submitted a new proposal to revise the DIAD II terminals. UPS accepted the proposal, and in 1996, a new version of the DIAD II terminal was developed. At the conclusion of the DIAD II project, UPS designated Motorola Communications (Israel) as its engineering design resource for future generation development.

The strategic cooperation between the two companies continues to grow, and Motorola Communications has become part of the UPS cross-functional team with respect to the overall structuring of UPS/Motorola proposals. UPS and Motorola now work closely in analyzing project costs and benefits, and Motorola is automatically included on all bid lists for UPS technical project opportunities.

## 18. Conclusion

The UPS–Motorola case study represents a strategic project that started as a classic ‘Build-to-Specifications’ outsourcing project and was so successful that it led to establishing a true strategic partnership between the two companies.

The case provides two important conclusions. First, from the client's perspective, the key to a successful outsourcing project is a clear definition of the project scope and specifications, and good project management. Close monitoring of the projects' progress and top management involvement are important success factors, especially in critical decisions (Ahituv et al., 1999; Willcocks et al., 1999). Second, outsourcing relations between vendors and clients can be either one-time or repetitive. Towards the end of the initial contract, many firms make decisions regarding continuation of the outsourcing relationship with the existing vendors and only a handful will shift to a strategic partnership (Nam et al., 1996). Thus, from the vendors' standpoint, in order to promote the chances of upgrading the outsourcing relationships to a strategic partnership, vendors should be perceived as trustworthy as well as technically competent (McFarlan and Nolan, 1995). Exhibiting flexible behavior can certainly help build trust. For the vendor, this means understanding and adapting to changing customer needs even if it involves deviating from the original specifications and terms written in the original outsourcing contract.
