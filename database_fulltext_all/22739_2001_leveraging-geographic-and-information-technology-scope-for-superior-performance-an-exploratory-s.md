# Leveraging geographic and information technology scope for superior performance: an exploratory study in international banking

Ken Peffers $^{a,*}$ , Virpi Kristiina Tuunainen $^{b,1}$

$^{a}$ Department of ISM, School of Business and Management, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clearwater Bay, Kowloon, Hong Kong, People's Republic of China $^{b}$ Helsinki School of Economics and BA, Information Systems Science, Helsinki, Finland

Revised 26 April 2001; accepted 26 April 2001

## Abstract

Here we investigate the relationship between the use of geographic and information technology (IT) scope as complementary factors to affect the value of products, the cost of operations and the overall performance of firms. We introduce the IT/Geographic Scope Matrix (IGSM) as a framework with which to study and understand the relationship in order to better manage the firm. We develop expectations that the consistent use of either high or low levels of IT and geographic scope may result in better firm performance than inconsistent use, where high levels of one are matched with low levels of the other. We apply the IGSM in a study of eleven firms, all of which provide financial services, specifically international cash management and trade finance, to firms in Hong Kong. The results of our study suggest that the IGSM may be a useful tool for IS planning and research and have implications for the co-ordination of business and IT planning. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: IT scope; Strategic information systems; Geographic scope; Financial services industry; IT/Geographic Scope Matrix

## 1. Introduction

International trade has grown explosively over the last 20 years, particularly within Asia and between Asia and the rest of the world (Fung, 1997). For example, trade between Asia and North America has increased at an average annual rate of more than 12% between 1980 and 1996. This has resulted in total world trade that is nearly an order of magnitude higher. For firms producing goods and services, this means that ‘foreign sales’ can no longer be treated as an unimportant by-product of domestic sales and it means that foreign competition, current or potential, must be taken seriously in every industry (Bartlett and Ghoshal, 1991; Jensen, 1993). Over the same period, firms have made heavy investments in information technology (IT) infrastructure, putting as much as 8% of revenue into IT in some industries (Rayner, 1995).

Who will benefit from the opportunities that result from the global explosion in trade? Who will benefit from heavy investments in IT? Strategic management theory suggests that firms should perform better if they align IT investments so that they fit the firm's business strategy (Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993). If so, should the scope of IT investment to support products and processes also echo the firm's strategy for the geographic scope of its operations?

For firms in the banking industry, trade expansion has resulted in a shift in the importance of services to support international trade (Holland et al., 1994). If international trade has become of central importance to firms, so also, it has become to the firms' bankers. Globally distributed manufacturing and marketing activities require financing across national boundaries. In addition, they must be supported by cash flows among operating units in different continents and using a variety of currencies, which do not necessarily match the natural cash flows from the lines of business. For firms in the banking industry, this may create opportunities to reap substantial rewards from expansion in the need for trade finance and cash management services.

However, not all firms in this industry may be positioned with the right combination of products and processes to take advantage of these new opportunities. Increased trade, the availability of relatively cheap IT, and the shift in perspective by many of the most important clients toward a more global outlook have led banks to implement more global strategies. Some banks have developed global assets, consisting of people and offices around the world that provide them with high levels of geographic scope (Holland and Lockett, 1995). In addition, banks are information-intensive and highly dependent on IT as a core technology; they were among the first industries to implement inter-organizational IS at both national and international levels (Broadbent and Weill, 1993). Some banks have made extensive investments to develop IT infrastructures to support processes and products for international trade. IT investments and branch locations, it has been claimed (Martinsons, 1992), are now companion competitive weapons in banking. In the banking industry, this growth in trade will create rewards. Will they mostly go to firms with the geographic scope and the IT infrastructure to enable them to take advantage of the new opportunities?

In the following sections, we introduce the IT/Geographic Scope Matrix (IGSM) as a framework to investigate the relationship between IT scope and geographic scope and its impact on the performance of the firm. We review prior research in organizational theory, strategic management, and information systems to develop expectations for the impacts of this relationship. We explore the complementary use of IT and geographic scope among eleven firms in the financial services industry to produce value for their customers and to reduce costs. We use qualitative and quantitative data about the firms' strategy, implementation, configurations and performance to try to understand the impact of this relationship on the performance of the firm. Are IT scope and geographic scope complementary characteristics in terms of how they affect firm performance?

The results of our exploratory investigation suggest that the IGSM is a useful conceptual tool for use in analyzing firm strategy. The results are consistent with the expectation that consistent use of both IT and geographic scope at high or low levels may result in better firm performance than where levels of one are inconsistent with those of the other.

The IGSM is a promising framework for use in research on the relationships between IT and the configuration of the firm. For managers, the IGSM extends the concept of strategic alignment from the alignment of business and IT objectives to include alignment of business and IT configurations.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

## 2.1. Geographic scope

Geographic scope is defined as the extent to which a firm competes in markets around the world (Carpano et al., 1994). By choice, firms compete along a continuum from local (one market) to global (all markets). Geographic scope is a dimension with complex characteristics. Depending on the nature of an industry, the competition can be homogenous across products or segmented by product (Carpano et al., 1994). It can be homogenous globally or it can be multi-domestic (Porter, 1986). An industry tends to be global if there is a competitive advantage to integrating activities on a global basis. Firms competing globally can either deploy resources globally or they can concentrate resources. They can co-ordinate activities or they can decentralize control (Porter, 1986). Commonly, firms use global business to exploit innovation and know-how produced in a home country, but, alternately, the global generation of innovations is a growing phenomena (Iammarino and Jonathan, 1998; Archibugi and Iammarino, 1999).

A key enabler of high levels of geographic scope is an innovation referred to as localization. A traditional globalized firm operates in the whole world as if it was a single market. Localization refers to a strategy for operating globally by adjusting to local preferences for practice and product in each geographic market (Ramarapu et al., 1999). The large Japanese banks were thought to have planned to adopt a localization strategy to expand globally, but were prevented from implementing it fully because of the Japanese banking crisis. In consequence, Citibank and HSBC were able to take the concept and make good use of it. HSBC has particularly used this strategy to expand, buying existing strong franchises in local markets and absorbing them into a global bank (Brady, 1998).

Why is geographic scope important to the firm? Several factors push firms toward global scope. Some of these factors include economies of scale, competitive advantage to integration, learning curves, location advantages, and integration advantages. Economies of scale favour globalization (Porter, 1986). Innovations that increase the portion of product costs that are fixed across many customers lead to high economies of scale (Ghemawat and Spence, 1986). Few national markets are large enough to take full advantage of such economies, particularly in industries where IT plays a large role in creating the product or service (Peppard, 1999). Such products typically have high fixed development costs and low marginal operational costs. Early-stage technology industries (Preece et al., 1999) and information intensive industries may be so characterized.

Steep learning curves favour globalization. If an industry requires firms to learn at a fast rate, then this can best be done by integrating this learning across many markets (Porter, 1986). In addition, if advantages accrue to integrating learning across major activities, such as R & D, product development, and operations, then this can be more practically done if these activities are performed globally (Kotabe, 1998). Integration of activities can lead to either lower costs or differentiation (Porter, 1986). For example, integration may improve responsiveness or the quality of services (Kotabe, 1998).

From the marketing perspective, scope is a necessary prerequisite to entering new markets and gaining access to new customers; if you want more customers, you go to more places. Customers may be global or their needs may be common across customers globally. Global customers tend to prefer global providers (Lovelock and Yip, 1996) because they value the availability of the same services at many locations. For services to support international trade, clients may find access at many locations to be particularly important, such that the value of services may be directly and disproportionately related to the geographic scope of the provider; less geographic scope may render services far less valuable.

Localization strategies can be developed to adapt global services to respond to heterogeneous local needs (Ghemawat and Spence, 1986). Where customer needs vary greatly across markets, firms move to position products to address those needs. Needs for very short lead times, to minimise the cost of co-ordination across markets, and to respond to local governmental policies also indicate localization strategies.

In contrast to the globalization or localization strategies for high geographic scope, a firm can choose to focus on a geographic niche (Hansen et al., 1994). Firms that have not developed the resources to compete globally may be able to achieve ‘best-in-world’ competence by competing with a focused geographic scope, i.e. in one or more specific locations, catering precisely to the values of customers in those locations (Bonk, 1996).

Are greater returns associated with higher levels of geographic scope? Researchers, e.g. (Kim et al., 1989; Delios and Paul, 1999), have observed such a relationship between high levels of geographic scope and such performance measures as five year composite averages for return on assets, return on equity, and return on sales. This is in marked contrast to the relationship between product scope and profitability, where there does not appear to be such a relationship (Montgomery, 1985). The finding should not be surprising. With the world economy moving swiftly toward integration (Barbalho et al., 1998), firms can gain cost and quality advantages by locating activities in the value chain where they can be most effectively carried out. For example, low skill work can be located in moderately priced economies, capital intensive activities in places where the appropriate skills are available, and R & D to locations in proximity to where creative people can be found (Nakahara, 1999).

However, geographic scope is not free. There is evidence that a firm's ability to operate effectively with high geographic scope is a capability that must be acquired by, among other things, the development of an organisational culture. Firms that have successfully developed a common firm culture across many international markets are able to achieve greater operational efficiencies, market products better, plan better and implement strategies more effectively (Palich and Gomez-Mejia, 1999). On the other hand, firms that can make use of cultural diversity are better equipped to create value for customers across many cultures (Lattimer, 1998).

In the case of the financial services industry, high geographic scope requires infrastructure in each location in which the firm does business. This means employees, offices, equipment, and management. Clearly more geographic scope costs more, rather than less. There are, however, economies of geographic scope. Efforts may be replicated in diverse geographic areas at less than the average cost of prior areas. The extent of these economies depends on the level of customization required in each area as well as the cost of coordination and communication across areas.

## 2.2. IT scope

Little has been said about IT scope. Most IS researchers that have investigated the role of IS investments in the firm have focused on IT spending (Turner, 1982; Cron and Sobol, 1983; Harris and Joseph, 1991), the adoption of technology (Dos Santos and Peffers, 1995), or the impact of innovative IT (Dos Santos et al., 1993). Henderson and Venkatraman (1993) refer to the “specific information technologies that support current business strategy initiatives or could shape new business strategy initiatives for the firm.” Venkatraman (1994) defines five levels of ‘IT-enabled business transformation’, from localized exploitation to business scope redefinition. To the extent that firms use IT to support products and their delivery, such use may lead to products that have higher value for customers (e.g. Dos Santos and Peffers, 1995; Banker and Kauffman, 1988; Clemons and Row, 1988).

By IT scope, we refer to the extent to which specific products and services contain IT elements, are delivered using IT, or are produced with activities supported by IT.

Stories aside, empirical research has not always found a systematic relationship between the use of IT and product value, cost or overall firm performance. Dos Santos and Peffers (1995) found that early investors in an innovative IT gained in terms of customer value but not necessarily costs, even when cost savings was one of the original purposes of the system. Some researchers have found positive relationships between IT assets and profits (e.g. Delios and Paul, 1999), while others have found the impacts of intense IT use to be ambiguous at best (Roach, 1987). Harris and Joseph (1991) observed that insurance firms that spent a larger portion of their operating expenses on IT were more efficient. Dos Santos et al. (1993) discovered a relationship between higher firm values and the use of IT for innovation. Brynjolfsson and Hitt (1998) determined that IT, coupled with complementary assets, strategy changes, or re-engineered processes, facilitated higher performance.

Organisational learning may play a role in the impact of IT scope on value and cost. Peffers and Dos Santos (1996) showed that it took many years following the implementation of a major innovative application for the firm to begin to realize a substantial positive benefit. Firms had to learn to change processes and products to take full advantage of the new application. In addition, firms' customers had to learn to change behaviours to take full advantage of the innovation. Consequently, it may not be surprising to find that a firm's costs actually increase with increasing levels of IT scope until the systems become complete and mature enough to begin to reduce costs.

Technological leadership by itself does not insure a competitive position, of course satisfying customers has to be the goal (Bonk, 1996) and using IT as the basis for a customer-centred value chain has the potential to create value for the customer and for the firm (Barbalho et al., 1998).

## 2.3. Geographic and IT scope

## 2.3.1. IT as an enabler of global geographic scope

IT has become an important enabler for firms seeking to operate globally and effectively (Takac, 1994). Such investments allow efforts to be replicated in additional diverse geographic areas at low marginal costs. For example, the cost of developing bank branch automation software is the same whether the software is used in one branch or one thousand (Clemons et al., 1993). Firms that use IS can realize increased economies of scope by replicating systems to deliver products to customers in new areas. In general, IS applications have high impacts on such economies, i.e. the investment required to serve an additional customer is small. In addition, processing, communications, development, and maintenance costs are largely fixed and not dependent on the number of customers or areas served (Bakos, 1991).

IT enables the support of globally distributed activities, the integration of global markets, and delivery of products and services to global customers (Palvia, 1993). Firms that have dispersed operations globally require effective linkages to support coordination and control of activities through the value chain (Ives et al., 1993). Using IT to coordinate and control these activities allows them to be placed to optimize their value for the customer and the firm (Barbalho et al., 1998). This includes out-sourced as well as in-sourced activities (Baker, 1999). Telecommunications technology has the potential to support the integration of global markets (Sirois, 1999). A number of markets can be treated as a single entity if marginal communication and coordination costs are reduced to near zero. Many corporate customers, themselves global businesses, place value on the availability of identical services globally. This is particularly true for industries such as financial services (Ives et al., 1993).

## 2.3.2. Geographic, IT scope commonality

As one might expect, the levels of IT scope and geographic scope are related in firms (King and Sethi, 1999). This relationship is particularly strong for firms where the business strategy emphasizes global, rather than local, products. One reason for this commonality is the affect that IT has on the nature of distance for the firm. IT innovations break down the barrier of distance between buyer and seller, reducing its importance (Moore, 1998). At the same time, it has the potential to improve the effectiveness of planning for the global distribution of the firm's value chain components (Tzu-chuan et al., 1998). Empirical studies have confirmed a link between IT investments and geographic scope (Dewan et al., 1998).

## 2.3.3. What should the relationship be between IT scope and geographic scope?

An important part of the firm's strategy includes its geographic scope. Included in this dimension of strategy are decisions about what resources are required, where value chain activities should be deployed, how the activities can be coordinated and managed, and how to operate in a variety of cultures (Peppard, 1999). IT infrastructure decisions, i.e. decisions about how information and IS will support strategic choices, are critical to support the resulting strategy (Peppard, 1999). If the firm is to operate globally, there is important value to be gained by managing information globally as well (Peppard, 1999).

While global businesses require high levels of integration and coordination, local businesses must focus more on direct, even personalized, responsiveness to customers. This suggests a normative relationship between geographic scope and IT scope. Global businesses require high levels of integration, coordination, centralized management of innovation, and some amount of product standardization. Local businesses must focus on direct customer service, individual differences in customer needs, local government policies, and personal relationships (Prahalad and Doz, 1987).

## 2.4. Impacts of IT and geographic scope

Strategic decisions about IT and geographic scope result in performance impacts for the firm. Seven such impacts, which we introduced earlier (Peffers and Tuunainen, 1998), are described here. The first five are positive performance impacts of particular decision combinations and the last two are costs of high scope levels.

1. Economies of geographic scope. The extent to which producing products for the next geographic market is less costly than for existing markets. Global information systems can increase economies of geographic scope, increasing the portion of fixed costs across markets and reducing the marginal cost of entering new markets (Palvia, 1996). The suggested consequence of this impact is that low levels of IT and geographic scope result in low costs, higher levels of just one result in high costs, and high levels of both result in low costs after a learning period.

2. Economies of scale. The extent to which marginal costs for the next unit of product are lower than average costs. Producing and delivering products using IS results in higher economies of scale as firms use IS to replicate products for many customers at low marginal cost (Bakos, 1991). This effect results in firms with high levels of IT scope having lower marginal costs. This suggests that the lowest cost firms should be large firms with high IT scope, followed by small firms with low IT scope. Firms with high IT scope and small size or large size and low IT scope should tend to have higher costs.

3. Product value. Systems that integrate the firm's internal processing with that of customers create customer value by increasing customers' operating efficiency (Jelassi and Fignon, 1994) or convenience and flexibility (Peffers and Dos Santos, 1996). Firms that combine globally integrated infrastructure through IS with global service strategies can reduce customers coordination costs (Jarvenpaa et al., 1995). Putting together systems that combine networking, processing, and intelligence to provide geographically ubiquitous services can be very valuable for the customer. The customer value of such products depends on network externalities. For customers that value global integration, the highest levels of global availability and functionality create high value. For those customers, any less scope results in much less value. High geographic scope with low IT scope, or the reverse, would appear to create a mismatch, where the firm is unable to meet global customers' service demands. For customers that do not value global integration, low geographic scope, e.g. domestic only, and low IT scope may be satisfactory.

4. Efficiency and organizational effectiveness. Global IS can reduce costs for the firm by integrating globally dispersed value chain activities and reducing coordination costs (Seidmann and Sundaranjan, 1997). In addition, global IS can help firms achieve superior coordination and control (Palvia and Lee, 1996). In itself, dispersion of activities raises costs, so the lowest costs firms have activities concentrated in one location, not requiring much integration. Additional geographic scope increases costs. Increasing integration support through IS reduces the impact of geographic scope on costs.

5. Barriers to entry. Network externalities affect product value when the benefits to users are a function of network size. Such network externalities can create barriers to new entry by other firms (Palvia, 1996). Firms that have used the highest IT scope and geographic scope to develop effective global operations may have established systems that create sustainable advantages because new competitors would have to replicate them in their entirety.

6. Cost of geographic scope. The lowest cost configuration for a firm is to produce and deliver all products and services from one location. Every activity performed at another location adds cost. In addition, costs are increased for each additional market in which a firm competes. Using IT to replicate the production of goods and services across markets tends to limit the increased cost.

7. Cost of IT scope. High levels of IT scope add costs. Networks, communication systems, and integration all add cost.

## 2.5. IT/Geographic Scope Matrix

The expected impacts suggest that the relationship between IT scope and geographic scope systematically affects firm performance. The combination of low IT scope and low geographic scope is expected to result in the lowest operating costs for the firm. It is not expected to affect product value on a positive manner. High levels of both IT and geographic scope result in the highest customer value and moderately high costs, although such firms may learn to use IT to reduce costs over time. The highest cost firms may be those where high geographic scope is combined with low levels of IT integration. Firms with high IT scope and low geographic scope may have moderately high costs and little IT contribution to customer value.

Fig. 1 shows the IGSM, a two dimensional model of the relationship between geographic scope and IT scope. The horizontal dimension is IT scope. As a firm makes new investments in IT designed to improve the value of the product to the customer, operations costs increase because new IT applications are expensive. Eventually, costs might actually decrease substantially, depending on the nature of the IT, but prior studies have found that it may take several years for a firm to learn how to use a new application well enough to begin benefiting from its potential (Peffers and Dos Santos, 1996). The IT product investments may also increase the value of the firm's products to the customer. If the firm is able to retain some of this value through competitive advantage, it may result in improved revenue or margins.

The vertical dimension is geographic scope. For a given level of business, geographic scope may also be expected to be costly, for example, the more countries in which the firm operates, the higher may be its expenses for staff and offices. But geographic scope may also add value for the customer. This is true only to the extent, however, that systems are in place to use geographic scope to the customer's advantage. This suggests that geographic scope and IT product investments are complementary.

If we divide the space in the IGSM into four regions, we might expect to find:

\- Global high-tech. Firms in this region may generally be expected to have the highest revenue because of customer value as well as high expenses because of high IT and geographic scope. Eventually, the firm may learn to achieve reduced expenses because IT investments increase economies of geographic scope and scale. Firms in this cell are expected to have the highest and increasing margins.

\- Local low-tech. Firms in this region should have the lowest operating expenses. They have invested neither in high levels of IT nor are they paying for far-flung premises and staff. Because they are confined to a local area, they cannot easily provide the additional value that comes from well co-ordinated activities in globally disperse premises. If our expectations are correct, then the firms that perform the best may be firms where the level of IT scope is appropriate for the level of geographic scope. This suggests that firms located along the diagonal between low levels on both dimensions and high levels on both dimensions may experience higher overall firm performance, resulting from either a low cost strategy or a high cost–high value strategy. Firms where the levels of IT scope and geographic scope are extremely unbalanced may find themselves in low performance domains at the upper left and lower right of the framework.

In this study, we seek to explore how managers might use the IGSM to think about the strategic position of the firm.

1. Will low levels of IT scope and low geographic scope lead to low costs?

2. Will high levels of geographic scope and high levels of IT scope lead to high firm performance, based on high product value?

3. Will firms where geographic scope is not matched with IT scope tend to have less favourable overall performance, based on high costs and low margins?

In the Section 3, we describe our research methodology, measures, and sample.

## 3. Research methodology and data

## 3.1.1. Methodology, firm characteristics, and industry

To demonstrate the use of the IGSM, we studied the strategic use of IT and geographic scope among firms in the international cash management and trade finance services business. We selected 17 banks and bank holding companies for study, based on their known presence in the specific banking sector, of which eleven (64.7%) agreed to participate, including banks headquartered in Hong Kong, US, Canada, Singapore, and UK. Firms that declined to participate cited resource constraints and the sensitivity of desired data as reasons. The studied banks all provide services to support firms engaged in international trade, including two major components of those services: cash management and trade finance and all have a business presence in Hong Kong. Table 1 describes several characteristics of the participants, at the time of our study, including name, total assets, home country, the number of countries in which it has offices, and the number of its offices. The firms vary dramatically in size and scope, a characteristic of the industry (Berger et al., 1993).

In this study, we focus on two financial service product areas that are intended to support international trade: cash management and trade finance. These product segments are well suited for the study of the relationship between IT scope and geographic scope because firms use these products to support globally distributed operations and the services are essential for such firms.

Cash management is the movement of funds among currencies and countries to support operations. Multi-national firms tend to accumulate cash in some locations and need to have it available in others. For example, if a firm manufactures in Costa Rica and sells in Japan, the Japanese operations may accumulate excess cash, while the Costa Rican operations regularly consume cash. Additionally, cash may be temporarily invested or borrowed in various locations. Important cash management activities include:

• monitoring cash position in domestic and foreign accounts;

• reconciling accounts in foreign branch and subsidiary banks;

\- making payments to domestic and foreign third parties by check and by funds transfer;

\- obtaining foreign exchange rates and advice.

Trade finance involves lending funds to finance the purchase of goods internationally. If a letter of credit (LC) is used, the purchasing firm applies to its bank for a letter of credit for the purchase price. When approved, the LC is transmitted to a correspondent or subsidiary bank in the seller's country. The seller ships the goods. When the goods arrive and are accepted, the correspondent bank releases the funds to the seller, funds are transferred to the correspondent bank, and the purchaser owes the money to its bank as a trade loan. Important trade finance service activities include:

• receiving a letter of credit application;

\- transmitting an approved letter of credit to a branch, subsidiary or corresponding bank in a seller's country;

• monitoring outstanding trade loans and shipping guarantees;

\- obtaining insurance services.

Commercial banking is an industry in which IT capabilities have long been considered important, both for efficiency and to produce customer value. Geographic scope is also an important element of strategic choice for producers of these services because a critical element of these services includes the locations at which they are offered. The IT capabilities and the level of geographic scope applied to these products vary dramatically among firms. In general, banking is an industry in which firms vary dramatically in size, geographic scope, level of technical integration, etc. and the smaller, less sophisticated industry members are still able to compete by positioning products to customers who value product attributes differently. Consequently, firms with very different characteristics, in terms of the dimensions of interest, can be successful members of the industry.

For several reasons, Hong Kong is an excellent location in which to study these concepts. Hong Kong is a financial center for Southeast Asia and it is a city where business people cannot easily escape thinking about geographic scope; the domestic market consists of one city. Consequently, when we study firms in Hong Kong, we can be certain that managers have considered the issue of geographic scope and have, in some fashion, decided what it should be. Hong Kong is a city for which the principle business is international trade, so these financial services are extremely important in its economy. It is also an economy that is rich in IT resources and with a sophisticated technological infrastructure. Finally, Hong Kong is an economy with a rich mixture of Asian and Western business cultures, a place where competing business models and business cultures test each other in an environment of intense competition.

Our intent was to use intensive study to understand the business and IT strategies of each of the firms and to use quantitative data to observe the firms' performance on several dimensions. Our method was to determine the firm's strategy in terms of the IGSM, to see how well it works, and to make simple comparisons with its industrial peers.

To study the firms, we searched for news articles and other publicly available data using ABI-INFORM and a database of Hong Kong news articles, as well as press releases and other information found on the banks' Internet sites and other locations. We conducted lengthy structured and unstructured interviews of bank managers. We collected internal bank documents and annual reports for each bank for the three-year period, 1995–1997. All of the descriptions, data, and analysis that follow refer to this period. In all, we accumulated more than 10,000 pages of interview transcripts, notes, and other documents.

Another type of measure observes the extent that value chain components are dispersed (King and Sethi, 1999). These measures have the advantage of being straightforward and easily computed. A limitation of such measures is that they implicitly assume that all markets have equal importance and that the utility of increased dispersion follows some functional form, e.g. linear. As an alternative, one could differentiate firms' scope categorically according to a model of geographic scope, such as whether the firm's intended scope is local, continental, or global (Lovelock and Yip, 1996).

We chose to measure geographic scope as an aggregate of several of the available measures. These include three measures of market dispersion, number of countries in which the bank operates, the percentage of the banks assets that are deployed outside the bank's home country and outside the bank's home continent; a measure of value chain component dispersion, number of offices world-wide; and a measure of intended geographic scope, the bank's geographic strategy, as stated by managers in interviews.

To avoid making any assumptions about the expected distributions for each of these measures, we computed our overall measure for geographic scope by rank-ordering the banks on each of the five measures and averaging the ranks for each bank. As one might expect, observation of the values for the five measures suggests a high level of consistency among their rank orders. Table 2 shows the values we observed for each of the measures for each firm and our resulting measure for geographic scope.

Information technology has generally been measured as an expense, such as data processing expenses (Turner, 1982), ‘IT-ness’, a ratio of IT spending to capital (Tzuchuan et al., 1998), or IT intensity, the proportion of expenses allocated to information systems (Harris and Joseph, 1991). Using expense measures, researchers sought to simplify what was happening when IT resources were applied to processes and products so that they could observe whether performance impacts occurred across the firm or across firms for such investments, regardless of their specific purposes.

Here we are concerned with IT scope, the extent to which specific products and services contain IT elements, are delivered using IT, or are produced with activities supported by IT. Our purpose is to explore whether the IGSM can be used to understand a firm's strategy and how it might be expected to affect firm performance. Consequently, we are very interested in how IT is used to support specific products and services in the firm.

In our interviews with managers, we developed a list of the most critical activities necessary to provide international cash management and trade finance services. We measured IT scope for each firm by counting the number of these activities that were provided to customers through the use of computers and computer based communication systems. Table 3 shows a list of the activities that we included in our measure. The list includes activities where important interaction occurs between the client and the bank or between the bank and a third party.

The resulting measure for IT scope is shown in Table 4. We compared the resulting measure with statements made by the banks' managers in interviews about the banks' IT strategies and the results are consistent. Note that the measure does not measure IT scope for the entire firm. A firm-wide measure of IT-scope, incorporating hundreds or thousands of activities, was not feasible because of resource limitations. Our measure provides the basis for us to explore the use of the IGSM as a conceptual tool for managers to think about the position of the firm.

In Section 4, we examine the performance of these banks in light of the banks position on each of the dimensions in order to determine whether the IGSM framework helps us to understand the relationship between geographic scope and IT scope and its impact on the firm.

## 4.2. The groups

The placement of the eleven firms on the matrix suggests that they fall into four distinct groups. Three firms fall near zero on both IT and geographic scope. We can think of these as ‘local low-tech’ firms. Four firms fall into the upper right hand corner. These firms have high measured levels on both IT and geographic scope ('global high-tech'). Three firms have lower measured IT scope but moderately high geographic scope. Their geographic scope is quite far from global, but they have much higher geographic scope than the local low-tech firms. We will refer to them as ‘transcontinental low-tech’. The last firm, Nanyang, does not clearly fall into any region on the IGSM, however, an inspection of its characteristics suggests that it can be considered a ‘local high-tech’ firm.

Next, we introduce the banks belonging to each of the IT/geographic scope groups and review the group's performance at the time of our study.

Local low-tech Asia Commercial, First Pacific, and Wing Hang are similar banks in several respects. Each of the banks is located in Hong Kong and is focused, particularly for trade finance and cash management, on Hong Kong and adjacent areas, particularly other areas in China. First Pacific is located entirely in Hong Kong, Asia Commercial has one branch and several representative offices in China and a representative office in Taiwan, and Wing Hang has one office in each of China and nearby Macao. Each of the banks operates entirely within the region dominated by the Chinese language. $^{2}$ Each of the banks emphasizes its personal relationships with clients. Asia Commercial thinks of itself as an ‘aggressive local bank’ with many old fashioned customers uninterested in using sophisticated IT. First Pacific targets small companies, who themselves have regional focus and are very price sensitive, and competes on price for trade related services. Wing Hang emphasizes ‘relationship banking’ with its customers, most of which, it says, are of very long-standing. These are the three smallest banks in our sample.

All three of the banks have plans to introduce more IT-based service delivery. Asia Commercial offers a software package for bills processing that is used by some of its larger clients. First Pacific's customers can access some services by way of a telephone interface. Wing Hang plans automation of some service delivery. None of the three regard such initiatives as strategically critical.

To evaluate firm performance, we extracted key performance measures from the firms' financial statements for the three years ending in 1997. The results are shown in Table 5, averaged among banks in each of the four groups. To evaluate costs, we observed staff, premises (office space and equipment) and overall operating expenses. Each of the measures is normalized by dividing it by the firm's assets. The local low-tech firms had lower than average costs using each of the three measures. The results suggest that the firms are effectively pursuing their stated low-cost strategies. One might wonder whether staff costs may be substantially influenced by regional salary differences. Hong Kong, however, is one of the world's most expensive markets in which to own or rent office space. Consequently, the success of the local low-tech firms to minimize premises expenses is particularly convincing and impressive.

Global high-tech. The four firms in this group appear to have similar strategies. They each provide cash management and trade finance services, facilitated by IT. All four firms are broadly focused money-centre banking firms; they target retail, corporate and institutional customers.

Two of these, Citibank and HSBC, are truly global banks, operating in 97 and 79 countries, respectfully. They both offer customers branded PC-based trade, cash management, securities and information services (Citibanking and Hexagon). Each of them has expanded globally, implementing a system of localized global banking, where adaptation to local culture and national policy overlays a common IT platform and global banking products. More than 500 other banking firms use HSBC's Hexagon system to provide cash management services for their customers. The two banks have played a seesaw race for technological leadership for nearly twenty years.

Bank of America and Standard Chartered are somewhat less global competitors. Bank of America has the bulk of its offices in US from which it derives nearly 80% of its revenue. Both banks offer customers proprietary systems for cash management and trade services, neither as complete as those of the two leaders.

The global high-tech banks have, on average, the highest staff and overall operating expenses. This is inline with our expectations about the costs of geographic scope and IT scope. That their premises expenses are not the highest suggests that they have achieved some savings from increased economies of scale and scope from their IT investments.

This group of banks reports the highest operating income, suggesting that customers are willing to pay substantial premiums for the services that can be delivered by global firms supported by global IT. HSBC's exclusion from this group is thought to be an anomaly related to other business conditions for the firm for the particular three-year period. These firms also report higher than average pre-tax profits, consistent with their position along the profitable diagonal of the matrix.

Transcontinental low-tech. The three firms in the upper left of the IGSM are less similar to each other than firms within the two groups above, but nonetheless, they do have characteristics in common. Each of these firms has a broad geographic scope that is clearly not global. The two Canadian banks each have nearly 80% of their assets in Canada. It feels that sophisticated electronic interfaces are largely irrelevant to its competitive position and it does not feel an urgent need to push in that direction, although it does offer an on-line package for processing LCs. Royal, on the other hand, considers itself to be a technology leader in Canadian domestic banking. It wants to extend its use of telecommunications technology and electronic customer interfaces. United says that it values the potential for its electronic interfaces for trade services and it has determined that it is important for the firm as a whole to move toward more use of on-line services.

The transcontinental low-tech banks have higher than the overall average staff and overall operating expenses. They have the highest premise expenses, suggesting that they make use of physical offices to carry out communication and coordination tasks that the global high-tech firms provide through IT infrastructure. It seems that the firms with high geographic scope, but without high IT scope were unable to take advantage of economies of geographic scope like the global high-tech banks have done. Operating income levels of the transcontinental low-tech banks are slightly less than the average for the four groups, consistent with the idea that with less than global presence and without IT scope to support global integration, these firms are unable to provide the most valuable service levels to global customers. Finally, these banks have the lowest average pre-tax profits, consistent with their position at the margins of the profitable diagonal on the matrix.

Local high-tech. Nanyang Commercial Bank, part of Bank of China Group, is in a group by itself on the IGSM. It has a network of branch offices in Hong Kong, one branch in US and six branches in China, so like firms in the first group, it is almost entirely located in Hong Kong and adjacent areas. About 94% of its assets are in Hong Kong. The development of business in emerging economies, such as Thailand, is conducted through correspondence banks. In Hong Kong, its customers are primarily local and Chinese businesses, and in China mainly joint venture companies with foreign capital. For internal operations, Nanyang utilizes the centralized computer systems in Bank of China's computer center. In addition to telephone banking, its corporate customers are offered electronic banking services through Bank of China's Corporate Banking System (CBS), which is used by a PC and a modem at the customer's end. CBS, however, only covers the geographic area of Hong Kong.

The local high-tech bank has the lowest staff, premise and operating expenses. This is consistent with its position as a local firm. Nanyang's operating income levels are similar to those of the other local banks, suggesting that it also competes largely on the basis of price. It has the highest pre-tax profit as percentage of total assets, suggesting that it is the most successful in making this strategy work. Its performance suggest that it has used IT scope largely to become the low cost producer in the business rather than to provide valuable services for premium prices and that it has made good use of the IT support that it receives from the Bank of China to accomplish this objective. It has also avoided the costs associated with higher levels of geographic scope.

## 5. Conclusions

In this paper, we introduced the IGSM. We used it to develop concepts about the relationship between geographic scope and IT scope and how the relationship between these two factors might affect the performance of the firm. We then studied a sample of eleven firms, all engaged in providing financial services to firms in international trade and all doing business in Hong Kong, to see if we could use these concepts to explore the performance of the firms in a meaningful way. The results of our analysis support our expectations that the ISGM is a meaningful and potentially useful strategic management concept. They support the idea that IT and geographic scope are complementary in providing value for the customer and the firm.

In our sample, firms that maintained low levels of IT scope and low levels of geographic scope had lower than average expenses, particularly with respect to employee, building space and equipment, but also for operating expenses overall. Aside from the most straightforward costs of geographic scope that operating in additional geographic areas requires additional employees and offices, increased geographic scope also increases the cost of communication, co-ordination and governance. Firms with moderate to high levels of geographic scope had the highest levels of such expenses. Firms may not realize cost saving benefits from investments in IT, even if the purpose of the IT are to save on costs, for several years following implementation until they have learned to use the new applications to their full potential (Peffers and Dos Santos, 1996).

Firms with high levels of IT and geographic scope showed higher levels of revenue, relative to total assets. This seems to be consistent with the bankers' expectations. As an executive of HSBC told us, "the systems were originally intended to benefit the customer $80\%$ and the bank $20\%$ ; later the ratio was $90\%/10\%$ ." In other words, most of the IT investments made by the bank were not intended to save on costs, but to increase revenue by providing customers with more value.

Did IT scope and geographic scope together affect overall firm performance? The results of our analysis provide some support for the idea that they did. Firms positioned along the profitable diagonal of the matrix, where geographic and IT scope levels are consistent, performed better than average while firms with high geographic scope and low IT scope performed more poorly than average. One interesting exception to our expectations was Nanyang. It used IT scope to lower costs to the lowest levels in our sample. This suggests that for a local low-tech firm to increase IT scope may be a good strategy, even without a corresponding increase in geographic scope, particularly, if the firm has access to IT developed with sufficient scale so that it can use IT to reduce costs even further. This suggestion merits further study.

In our sample, we had no firms that fell deeply into the corners of the framework that suggest low performance. This is not surprising. All of the firms in our sample were successful, going concerns. To the extent that our framework is valid, we would not expect to find successful firms occupying the lowest performance regions. Of course, firm strategies and performance change. Nothing about our study should be inferred to predict current or future performance for any of the firms.

The results of this study, while very demonstrative, do not provide strong evidence that the performance results enjoyed by the investigated firms were primarily the result of the phenomenon of interest. The number of firms in our sample is too small for the purposes of statistical hypothesis testing. We wanted to understand the intent of the firms and to understand what they did with IT to provide value for the customer. Consequently, we were limited to the small number of firms that were willing to participate in our interviewing process. Since our investigation depended upon intensive study to obtain data on IT scope and firm strategies, it was not feasible for us to obtain a sample size sufficient to conduct an empirical test of the expected effects related to the IGSM.

Additionally, the model of performance impacts that we used to examine the affects of IT scope and geographic scope was quite simple. We wish that we could have measured the direct impact of each of the features in each of the banks in the manner of Mukhopadhyay et al. (1995), but such an intensive study was not feasible here because of the multi-firm nature of our study and the level of sensitivity in this particular business culture toward internal firm data. Consequently, we had to be content with publicly available performance data.

The measures that we used for IT scope applied only to a particular product line, albeit a very important one, rather than to the entire range of product lines for each bank. Resource constraints and business culture limited the scope of our investigation. Consequently, there is some mismatch between this measure and the measures of performance, which would be affected by process activities and product features in all product lines. Nonetheless, the use of the measure for IT scope is reasonable, given the high level of importance that the banks' customers were expected to attach to these features. In addition, discussions with managers lead us to believe that the scope of the banks' IT investment to support cash management and trade services was fairly consistent with their IT investments to support other products. In no case did any of the interviewed bankers or the literature provided by the firms suggest that the intensity of IT investment to support trade finance and cash management services differed markedly from investment to support other product lines in the firm.

The results of this paper suggest to managers that the highest performance may result from a match between geographic scope and IT scope. IT investments are not necessarily systematically beneficial for all firms. An industry sector may provide room for firms to focus on niches for which high IT scope may not be necessary or important. A word of caution is in order, however; a particular low-tech niche may not be long lived. Intense IT structures, when they become mature, may also lead to low cost processes. Indeed, the lowest cost firm in our study was a local firm with moderately high IT scope that used its IT to reduce costs. Consequently, a low-tech strategy may be dangerous because the advantageous niche may disappear, like a morning mist, when firms with high IT scope develop fully integrated systems and learn to use them to reduce costs.

To our knowledge, this is the first study that has examined the complementary impact of geographic scope and IT scope. This study has told a story that suggests that, at least in this particular industry segment, aligned levels of IT scope and geographic scope seem to produce higher levels of performance. Managers in industries where firms operate with varied geographic scope might use the IGSM as a planning concept for IT. Are the firm's plans for geographic and IT scope consistent with the firm's strategy as a low cost or a premium value producer?

Managers might benefit from future research that looked at related issues, such as the complementarity of product scope and IT or scale and IT. Future studies could also examine the issue of IT and geographic scope using field experiments with measures and data suited to statistical hypothesis testing.
