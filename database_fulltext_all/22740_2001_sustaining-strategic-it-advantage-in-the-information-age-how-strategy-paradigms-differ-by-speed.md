# Sustaining strategic IT advantage in the information age: how strategy paradigms differ by speed $^{☆}$

Gezinus J. Hidding

Graduate School of Business, Loyola University Chicago, 25 E. Pearson Street, Chicago, IL 60611, USA  
Received September 1999; accepted 3 September 2001

## Abstract

At the dawn of the Information Age, the competitive dynamics in numerous industries are more intense than ever before. We look at the implications of competitive dynamics to IT strategies. We summarize the basic logic of various well-known (IT) strategy methods, particularly regarding sustainability of an advantage and conclude that (IT) strategy theory incorporates precious little about competitive dynamics. We then explore a recent strategy framework, Sustainability Analysis [Calif. Mgmt Rev. 34 (1992) 29; Strategy and the Search for Rents: The Evolution of Diversity among Firms, 1994; Renewable Advantage, 1998]. Sustainability Analysis distinguishes three ‘ecologies’ that entail important differences in strategies and timelines for sustaining (IT) advantage. We review these ecologies and draw insights for IT strategy. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Strategic information systems; e-Business; Information Age; Sustainability Analysis

## 1. Introduction

In today's Information Age, and with the development of e-Businesses, we see technology transforming industry after industry, as well as creating new industries. Within such dynamic environments, how can firms achieve, and for how long can they sustain, a strategic IT advantage? Conventional thinking about strategy assumes implicitly that change is gradual and predictable, that market shares are stable and that share erosion is bad. This type of thinking does not apply universally, however. Market shares of fast changing ‘Information Age’ products, e.g. Intel Pentium II chips, erode rapidly. For those products, the ‘management of erosion’ is not a sign of failure, but of success. Such short-cycle competition is not replacing the traditional ‘Industrial Age’ type of competition; they co-exist. Reasons behind the differing paces of change are addressed in the second half of this paper. Clearly, crafting appropriate IT-based strategies and e-Business strategies $^{1}$ is becoming more difficult in the face of such wide diversity in competitive dynamics.

In this paper, we recognize two aspects within competitive dynamics: ‘Change’ (referring to evolving customer tastes, products, technologies, organizational capabilities, etc.) and ‘competitive interactions’ (referring to actions and reactions among competitors). Increasingly, strategy research is focusing on competitive dynamics, see, e.g. Day and Reibstein (1997), and Rumelt et al. (1994). Sustainability Analysis (Williams, 1992, 1994, 1998) has extended strategy thinking by analyzing different competitive dynamics related to a given product in order to determine: (a) the appropriate strategy paradigm, (b) different (IT) strategies consistent with that paradigm, and (c) a prediction for how long the resulting strategic (IT) advantage may be sustained.

In contrast to the process focus of recent IT literature, Sustainability Analysis (SA) assesses strategic advantage from a product perspective. It starts from the premise that no advantage lasts forever; the question is how quickly the advantage will erode. SA recognizes that product advantages come and go with different speeds, in different environments known as ‘ecologies’. Different ecologies imply different strategy paradigms, regarding, e.g. critical resources and key metrics. Consequently, IT strategies, as well as a product advantage’s sustainability, differ significantly dependent on the particular ecology.

Section 2 of this paper reviews well-known IT strategy methods $^{2}$ and classifies them according to the underlying strategy theory and its basic logic. Section 3 summarizes Sustainability Analysis, examines each of its ecologies, and draws insights for IT applications and core IS capabilities. Section 4 presents conclusions from our examination of the SA ecologies, and raises questions for additional research.

## 2. IT strategy logic

Several early IT strategy frameworks attempted to synthesize the experiences of various successful leading-edge cases. Examples of such frameworks include Critical Success Factors (Rockart, 1982), the Customer Resource Life Cycle (Ives and Learmonth, 1984; Neo Boon, 1988), Competing in Time (Keen, 1988), and matrices or ‘grids’, e.g. the Opportunities Matrix (Benjamin et al., 1983). Others, e.g. Kemerer and Sosa (1988), derived frameworks from cases where IS failed to be strategic. Such early frameworks, although useful in describing how IS was strategic, could not predict how long an advantage could be sustained. They were not typically rooted in established theory of strategy.

Strategy theory aims to explain or predict a competitive advantage based on specific factors, e.g. manufacturing, marketing, or IT. Thus, ‘sustaining competitive advantage using IT (or e-Business)' is a special case of ‘sustaining competitive advantage’. Therefore, we believe that IT/e-Business strategy methods should be specializations of general strategy theories.

To allow us to predict how long any advantage may be sustained, we argue that strategy reasoning should explicitly incorporate competitive dynamics, i.e. the speed of change and competitive interactions. In our view, the form of such reasoning ('logic') should be equivalent to: "If $\langle conditions\rangle$ , then the firm will obtain a competitive advantage that can be sustained for x period of time". Cyert (1988) called this a "control theory" view of strategy. Based on this control theory perspective, we review well-known IT strategy methods, presented by the strategy theory on which they are based.

We recognize two types of strategy-theory based methods: dynamic and static. We call strategy methods ‘dynamic’ if their logic explicitly incorporates speed of change and/or competitive interactions: “If $\langle$ speed of change characteristics $\rangle$ and/or $\langle$ competitive interaction characteristics $\rangle$ , then the firm will obtain a competitive advantage that can be sustained for x period of time”. We call strategy methods ‘static’ if their logic incorporates neither change nor competitive interactions. We will first review static methods.

## 2.1. Static methods

IT strategy methods, e.g. Parsons (1983), Cash and Konsynski (1985), Porter and Millar (1985), (Wiseman, 1985), and Bakos and Treacy (1986), extended the well known conventional strategy method: Porter's (1980) Industry structure analysis (ISA). ISA is a qualitative method that focuses on the industry and describes five forces (e.g. buyer power, rivalry) affecting competition. Its basic logic is: “The goal of competitive strategy for a business unit in an industry is to find a position in the industry where the company can best defend itself against these competitive forces or can influence them in its favor. If industry forces are decreased, then that will be in the firm’s favor, and possibly change industry structure” (Porter, 1980).

A firm generally does not have much control over many factors in the method, however, because the factors are external to the firm (e.g. actions of new entrants or competitors). Even though Porter (1980) used the term ‘sustainable’ competitive advantage extensively and clearly incorporates the existence of competitive interactions, ISA does not explicitly incorporate speed of change nor any characteristic of competitive interactions in its logic. Consequently, it cannot, and does not, draw inferences about how long a competitive advantage might be sustained. It may be possible to extend ISA with notions of dynamics, but to our knowledge, this has not been done and is not further addressed in this paper.

Rockart and Scott Morton (1984), Porter and Millar (1985), and Clemons and McFarlan (1986) extended another well-known method for analyzing competitive advantage, the Value Chain (system). The Value Chain, originally developed by Bower (1972) and popularized by Porter (1985), $^{3}$ is a qualitative method that focuses on the strategic business unit (SBU) and analyzes internal discrete value-adding activities. It incorporates no factors external to the firm. The basic logic is: “If the firm controls cost drivers, becomes more unique in performing its value activities, or reconfigures its value chain, then the firm can gain an advantage". A firm can also "broaden scope without broadening the firm by contracting with an independent firm to perform value activities (e.g. a supply agreement) or teaming with an independent firm to share activities (e.g. a marketing joint venture)", i.e. if it exploits linkages with other firms through inter-organizational systems (IOS), see Cash and Konsynski, (1985). The logic of the Value Chain method does not incorporate the speed of change in the value-adding activities or any characteristic of competitive interactions. Consequently it cannot, and does not, draw inferences about how long a competitive advantage might be sustained. Williams (1998) has extended Value Chain analysis with notions of dynamics, but that extension is not discussed in this paper.

Instead of looking either at a firm's (internal) activities or its environment, Teece (1984) argued that the “whole concept of strategy involves matching” the firm’s internal resources and its environment, which is the concept of competitive fit. Ansoff (1969), Johnston and Carrico (1988) agree. The logic says “[If] the better the fit, [then] the better a firm’s performance”. Several authors have described various models of fit involving IT: Rockart and Scott Morton (1984), McFarlan (1984), Henderson and Venkatraman (1993), Keen (1993), Jarvenpaa and Ives (1993), Reich and Benbasat (1996), and Lederer and Salmela (1996). Cash (1985) applies McKinsey’s 7S framework (Waterman et al., 1980) to analyze fit in the context of inter-organization systems (IOS). Note that competitive fit is a static concept. Competitive fit describes a state of (mis)match at a point in time. Over time, however, competitive dynamics (encompassing change and competitive interactions) will turn fit into a mismatch.

Static strategy methods take a snapshot picture of a point in time. Their logic does not incorporate any characteristics of change or competitive interactions. Consequently, these methods cannot, and do not, draw inferences about how long a competitive advantage might be sustained. Any analysis of sustainability requires a ‘motion picture’, i.e. an explicit consideration of competitive dynamics.

## 2.2. Dynamic methods

Competitive dynamics are addressed in recent strategy thinking, which adopts assumptions that are different from conventional economics-based strategy thinking. For example, the firms' resources (e.g. assets or capabilities) are heterogeneous (Cyert, 1988), change (e.g. technological innovation) is a fact of life (Nelson and Winter, 1982), and disequilibrium is the key to strategic advantage (Rumelt et al., 1994).

Following is a review of a number of dynamic IT strategy methods, grouped by the strategy theory on which they are based. First, we review several methods that explicitly take into account certain characteristics of competitive interactions (competitor's actions and reactions) but not speed of change. One method (Feeny and Ives, 1990) explicitly takes into account competitive interactions and speed of change.

Recently, Arthur (1994, 1996) has advanced the (economic) theory of “Increasing Returns”. Closely related is the concept of network externalities (Katz and Shapiro, 1985) and ‘positive feedback’ cycles in Forrester (1961) Industrial Dynamics. Increasing Returns are particularly relevant in e-Business and IT-intensive industries, e.g. eBay, where each new user adds to the total value of the system for everyone while costing very little extra to serve. The theory has been cited extensively in the findings of fact in the recent US anti-trust suit against Microsoft. $^{4}$

The basic logic is: “If the firm has a sizable (dis)advantage, then the greater that (dis)advantage will become”. Such self-reinforcing cycles (virtuous or vicious, respectively) can lead in certain markets, e.g. operating system software, to ‘tipping’. They leave only one product in a ‘pole position’ (Feeny and Ives, 1990), which can be sustained for quite a long time.

Hagel and Armstrong (1997) have extensively applied Increasing Returns to show the role of ‘communities’ as a source of competitive advantage in the Information Age. Clemons (in Day and Reibstein, 1997) describes how firms might lose their advantage in negative self-reinforcing cycles ('death spirals') due to use of IT by nimble entrants. Increasing Returns imply that success (or failure) feeds on itself, an important dynamic indeed. It does not explicitly analyze the speed at which such dynamics might occur, however, nor how competitors (re)act regarding such dynamics.

Inter-organizational systems (IOS) lower ‘transaction’ cost between firms, and internal information systems lower ‘coordination’ cost within the firm. Transaction Cost Economics, see Williamson (1975) states: “If [IOS cause] transaction cost decreases faster than coordination cost [then] the size of firms (‘hierarchies’) will shrink and their activities will be outsourced more (‘markets’ will expand)”. Using Transaction Cost Economics, Malone et al. (1987) predicted correctly that IOS would cause a shift towards electronic markets. Aside from pointing out specific opportunities enabled by IT (e.g. electronic brokering), the theory has mainly been used to describe changes to the economy overall, e.g. Benjamin and Wigand’s (1994) study of the effect of the National Information Infrastructure (NII). Transaction Cost Economics does not analyze a specific firm’s competitive advantage, let alone how a firm’s competitive advantage might be sustained.

IT Strategy theories, as described in, e.g. Clemons and Row (1991), and Mata et al., 1995, are based on the Resource-Based Theory of the firm as proposed by Wernerfelt (1984), and also described in, e.g. Barney (1997), and Dierickx and Cool (1989). Closely related is Ansoff's (1969) concept of “competency profile” (popularized by Prahalad and Hamel (1990) as “Core Competence”) and “Core IS Capabilities”, see Feeny and Willcocks (1998a,b), and Sambamurthy and Zmud (2000). Resource-Based Theory views the firm (or SBU) as a bundle of resources, which includes tangible or intangible assets, capabilities, and skills. Resource-based methods (which are qualitative) focus on the internal resources of the firm, and on competitive reactions, particularly imitation.

The basic logic is: “If there is resource heterogeneity (resources are different across firms, i.e. unique to a firm) and resource immobility (competitors find it hard, or impossible, to imitate resources), then an advantage will be ‘sustainable’”. Resource immobility can be reinforced by ‘isolating mechanisms’, which delay imitation by competitors, see Rumelt’s chapter in Teece (1987). An example of an isolating mechanism is ‘complementary’ resources, i.e. synergistic combinations of particular assets (Teece, 1987).

Through the lens of resource-based methods, several IT-related resources were found to contribute to sustainability: capital to invest in IT (Kettinger et al., 1994); proprietary IT (Morrison and Ferguson, 1993); patents (Dunford, 1987), e.g. Amazon's 1-click ordering technology (Bezos, 2000); information itself (King and Grover, 1991); capabilities regarding IT, e.g. technical or management (Powell and Dent-Micallef, 1997); or information (technology) resources that are complementary to unique non-IT resources, (Ross et al., 1996) or (Venkatraman, 1997). Several of these authors, and also Hopper (1990), and Keen (1993) have found that IT management skills are the most likely source of sustained IT-based advantage.

Resource-Based Theory gives some indication about the sustainability of a competitive advantage. Its logic explicitly recognizes imitation of valuable resources by competitors and indicates how long an advantage might last: ‘Sustainable’, essentially forever, or ‘temporary’ (if resources are heterogeneously distributed across firms but mobile). It does not, however, determine how long temporary might be in terms of months or years, other than implying it is shorter than forever. It also does not determine how fast changes occur in, e.g. customer tastes or technologies, or the impact of such changes on the sustainability of a strategic advantage.

Feeny and Ives (1990) developed a framework to analyze (IT-based) sustainability, combining insights from resource-based theory (particularly resource heterogeneity), first-mover advantages (particularly preemption), and ‘project life cycle analysis’ of how long a competitor will take to respond. The basic logic is: “If the firm can exploit unique resources to target an ‘exploitable link’, and competitors take x time to imitate, then the firm will enjoy a sustainable competitive advantage for $x + ?$ time”. This logic takes into account competitive interactions as well as speed of change, and indicates how long an advantage can be sustained.

The adjective ‘sustainable’ has been extensively applied to competitive advantage. We have argued that, in order to predict how long an advantage can be sustained, (IT) strategy methods must contain explicit logic regarding competitive dynamics: speed of change (in, e.g. customer tastes or technologies) and/or competitive interactions (e.g. imitation). Various strategy theories do, such as Increasing Returns, Transaction Cost Economics and Resource-Based Theory. They help us make qualitative inferences about the sustainability of a competitive advantage. Feeny and Ives (1990) have come close to the ideal: Their logic contains the ‘x + ?’ (unit of time) indicating how long an advantage can be sustained.

Feeny and Ives's (1990) ‘x + ?’ factor, it turns out, can be different from one business to the next, i.e. different in how long an advantage can be sustained. It turns out that such differences also imply differences in key success factors.

## 3. Sustainability Analysis for IT strategy

Williams (1992, 1994, 1998) observed that differences in learning curve slopes, which Spence (1981) had observed, correlate with differences in competitive dynamics (speed of change and competitive interactions) and in competitive strategies. Mendelson and Pillai (1999), in a study of the electronics manufacturing industry worldwide, also found that “industry clock speed” correlates strongly with various operational factors.

Williams argues that, based on differences in dynamics, different strategy paradigms (key resources, success factors, metrics, etc.) are appropriate. Compare, for example, the slowly evolving strategy consulting industry (e.g. McKinsey) or the fast-evolving personal computer industry (e.g. Dell). If different strategy paradigms are appropriate (based on certain conditions), different IT strategies may be appropriate as well. We will explore the implications for IT strategy of different strategy paradigms after a general introduction of Williams' Sustainability Analysis theory.

Sustainability Analysis considers advantages at the product level. $^{5}$ Products generate revenue when customers buy them, and thus a firm makes profits and gains or sustains an advantage. No product lasts forever, however. Thus, no product advantage lasts forever. Instead of focusing on how a strategic advantage can last forever, we focus on the question: “How long can an advantage, once obtained, be sustained?” To answer that question, Williams (1998) posits three ‘laws’ underlying competitive dynamics: Alignment, Renewal and Convergence, see Fig. 1.

Embracing the literature on Competitive Fit referenced earlier, Alignment means to maintain competitive fit between external conditions (e.g. customer needs, industry forces) and internal conditions of the firm (e.g. resources, skills and attitudes of management, organization structure or core IS capabilities). Resource-based analysis focuses on only one side of the coin: resources are required for a firm to produce products that customers buy. Bad alignment leads to poor profits and possibly the loss of a strategic advantage. Good alignment is necessary, but not sufficient, to capture full profit potential. Customer needs, the firm's resources, and competitors' offerings will change. Alignment must be maintained while the external environment, products, and resources are changing; it is a dynamic process. See the chapters by Burn, and by Sauer and Burn (Sauer and Yetton, 1997).

To maintain Alignment within a changing environment, the firm should innovate and find new sources of uniqueness in order to prosper (Renewal). Renewal builds on the notions that disequilibrium is the key to strategic advantage (Rumelt et al., 1994), and that change and technical innovation are facts of life (Nelson and Winter, 1982). Its roots lie in Schumpeter's (1934, 1950) “gale of creative destruction”. Christensen (1997) explores how many firms lost their advantage and even existence due to their difficulties in incorporating new ‘disruptive’ technology in products such as disk drives or mechanical excavators.

Incorporating the Resource-based view and Feeny and Ives (1990), both discussed earlier in this paper, Convergence states that competitors will react to and imitate successful innovations, causing product advantage (and profits) to dissipate. If not countered by Renewal, Convergence would ultimately lead to firms offering identical products (commodities) in perfectly competitive markets in equilibrium, a premise of traditional Industrial Organization economics.

From the view of Sustainability Analysis, business strategies must foster continual Renewal of products (and the internal resources) at a speed that maintains Alignment with the speed of change in external conditions (Convergence of competing products and changing customer needs). The scope of Sustainability Analysis is the ‘ecology’. An ecology is the firm and the set of actors, e.g. customers, competitors, suppliers, and government, with which the firm needs to be in Alignment for that product. In a way, ecology is the set of players that, with respect to a particular product, operate at a similar speed of change and competitive interactions. For example, for high-end strategy consulting, the ecology consists of firms such as McKinsey, DiamondCluster, BCG, and numerous boutique firms, as well as the senior executives at their clients.

## 3.1. Three types of ecologies

Williams (1992, 1998) has found that in different ecologies product advantages come and go at substantially different speeds. He distinguishes, for ease of explanation, three types of ecologies, which we call “long cycle”, “standard cycle”, and “short cycle”. $^{6}$ In long-cycle ecologies, product advantages last 7–10 years (or more). In standard-cycle ecologies, product advantages last 4–6 years; in short-cycle ecologies, product advantages last 6 months–3 years. Products classified as long cycle generally include operating systems, investment banking, business strategy consulting, local-telephone connections, hub airline service and patented prescription drugs. They typically have high per-unit margins. Standard-cycle products generally include fast food, appliances, mid-range automobiles, $^{7}$ standard automobile tires, mass-produced food, long-distance telephone services, and credit cards. They are typically high-volume, low-margin products. Short-cycle (“hot”) products generally include women’s fashion, toys, laptop computers, Personal Digital Assistants, cellular phone handsets, and microprocessor chips. They typically have high per-unit margins early in their life cycle that rapidly decline towards the end of their (short) life cycle.

Most importantly, Williams has found that different ecologies are equivalent to different strategy paradigms, i.e. different key success factors and (IT) strategies. For example, drastic price reductions (say 20%), implemented possibly overnight, are key for short cycle products, e.g. personal computers. Such reductions would be disastrous for standard-cycle products, e.g. car rental services, where the norm is moderate annual price reductions (say 3–6%) implemented gradually. For long-cycle products, e.g. prescription drugs, prices steadily increase (say 2%) even adjusted for inflation. Clearly, IT applications supporting business activities, e.g. marketing and sales, as well as a firm's overall IT architecture must support the specific focus of the firm's product ecology: Overnight price reductions require that the workflow for changing prices can be completed within, say 24 h. Gradual price increases put less stringent requirements on the workflow for changing prices.

For a given product, Sustainability Analysis's basic logic (which assumes good Alignment) is:

Conditions consistent with ecology $x \langle = \rangle$ Product advantage sustainable for y period of time,

where ‘conditions’ means customer needs, competitors’ (re)actions, technological change, the firm’s resources, key success factors, etc. $^{8}$ Particularly,

Conditions consistent with Long-cycle ecology $\langle = \rangle$ Product advantage for $7 - 10 +$ years Conditions consistent with Standard-cycle ecology $\langle = \rangle$ Product advantage for 4-6 years Conditions consistent with Short-cycle ecology $\langle = \rangle$ Product advantage for 0.5-3 years

Note the logic is an equivalence relation. The If...Then... works in both directions (assuming good Alignment). If we know an ecology's conditions, we can predict the sustainability of a product advantage. If we know how long a product advantage can be sustained, we know which ecology conditions must exist. Firms achieve appropriate Renewal, Alignment and Convergence by analyzing the various conditions and focusing on the key success factors (including how IT is used) relevant for their ecology.

We explore the implications of different ecologies for IT Strategy in the next several subsections. We examine, in particular, implications for IT applications and core IS capabilities (Feeny and Willcocks, 1998a,b). Similar to viewing products as the interface between external conditions (e.g. customers) and the firm's resources (e.g. assets and capabilities), we view IT applications as the interface between IT users and IT resources (assets and capabilities). In our view, resources affect strategic advantage indirectly: Resources yield products, which customers value (or not). Likewise, IS capabilities yield IT applications, which users value (or not).

Feeny and Willcocks (1998a,b) identified nine core IS capabilities that are organized into three overlapping groups: Business and IT Vision ('Vision'), Design of IT Architecture ('Architecture'), and Delivery of IS Services ('Services'). The general strategy discussion of each ecology will subsume capabilities regarding 'Leadership' and 'Informed Buying' (part of all three groups), 'Relationship Building' (part of Vision and Architecture), and 'Business Systems Thinking' (Vision). Implications for the capabilities 'Architecture Planning' and 'Making Technology Work' (part of Architecture and Services) and ‘Vendor Development’ and ‘Contract Monitoring’ (part of Services) and ‘Contract Facilitation’ (part of Vision and Services) are described for each ecology.

## 3.2. Standard cycle

We start with the standard-cycle ecology because it reflects familiar, conventional thinking in business and strategy, e.g. Porter (1980, 1985). Again, standard cycle products are high-volume, low-margin, with product advantages lasting 4–6 years. Williams points out that standard-cycle organizations must standardize their products and resources to exploit economies of scale. Over time, the firms typically become large and complex. To counteract the resulting diseconomies of scale, a key management skill is ‘orchestration,’ the coordination and optimization of numerous activities in the organization. For example, careful marketing to not hurt other products; balancing manufacturing capacity; (re)training many workers to achieve high volumes at low unit cost; training many sales people, resellers or business partners. In a standard-cycle ecology, other important strategy elements include exploiting bargaining power, cost control (called efficiency improvement, productivity improvement, rightsizing, etc.), differentiation of (standardized) products and services, and market segmentation to offer slightly different versions of the standard product. Generally, the most important measure of success is market share. Large companies fight ‘wars’ over small changes in market share, e.g. Burger King’s Whopper versus McDonald’s Big Mac.

## 3.2.1. Strategic IT applications

Strategic IT applications for standard-cycle ecologies must focus on the key success factors. Services are standardized through IT, e.g. scripts for telephone operators, menus in Automated Voice Response systems, or product support via web-based help desks. CAD/CAM is used in different ecologies, but for standard-cycle ecologies it is strategic that the focus of CAD/CAM is on standardizing and managing product modules and platforms. By ‘strategic’, we mean that an application enables the product to generate profit margins that are higher than the competition’s during the product life cycle.

Another example of a strategic IT application for standard-cycle ecologies is configuration software, which originated with Digital's (now Compaq) XCON/R1. This software is used to specify numerous products from standardized modules (mass customization), e.g. mid-range computers or earth moving equipment.

IT also supports traditional, strategic IS activities to help control costs of the business: inventory control, distribution control, or manufacturing control. ERP packages such as SAP or PeopleSoft now fulfill such functions. Other IT applications (e.g. data warehousing/mining) can help to segment markets (Clemons and Weber, 1994) and enable ‘micro’ segmentation.

For customers, IT enhances their bargaining power, yielding substantial consumer surplus (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 1996): Rosenbluth's reservation systems, for example, show how much its customers have flown on a particular airline, and thus negotiates discounts for its customers. Customers use comparison shopping on B2C sites (e.g. Auto-by-Tel) or spur competition on B2B Exchanges (e.g. FreeMarkets).

## 3.2.2. Core IS capabilities

Core IS capabilities in standard-cycle ecologies must also focus on the ecology's critical success factors. The IT architecture should enable standardization of a firm's products and businesses processes (e.g. production, marketing, accounting, HR). The IT architecture should enhance negotiating power by tracking how much product was purchased, sold, etc. across the many activities of a firm. Additionally, it should enhance efficiency improvements by tracking of expenses across a firm's many activities.

In a standard-cycle ecology it is also strategic to standardize the IT applications themselves and their interfaces with the IT architecture (e.g. organization-wide PC configuration standards). Software can be developed from standardized components (Meyer and Zack, 1996). Note that standardization is inherent in IS. Databases contain standardized data. Programs are standardized processes.

Finally, it is strategic to standardize the IS delivery services, e.g. help desk skills and responsiveness, or contract monitoring and facilitation. Of course, differing national standards, e.g. for communications network connections, may hinder standardization.

## 3.3. Short cycle

We now shift our discussion to short cycle ecologies, where the key success factors are generating and delivering ‘new idea’ products that rapidly become extremely popular globally. A basically identical product is distributed quickly, in large quantities: MiniDisk players, fashion (e.g. Benetton), or microprocessors.

Short-cycle organizations, to an outsider, may seem out of control, but they require management skills and attitudes appropriate for risk taking and fast-paced change, see, e.g. (Eisenhardt, 1990). Extremely creative people design the next new idea. Standardization, while beneficial operationally, may not be strategic. Timing is critical, for both market entry and market exit. Prices fall dramatically, as new ideas are copied fast, and as hot new products quickly become old.

The value of inventory depreciates substantially every quarter, because of rapid price declines. Significant increases in inventory of products in decline (e.g. a hot product fizzles out earlier than expected) cause substantial earnings write-offs. Inventory management is so difficult that even well-managed firms occasionally stumble.

Branding of individual products and market segmentation are less relevant in short-cycle ecologies because a specific product has come and gone before conventional branding strategies could make a significant impact. By the time a brand was valuable, the product would already be “yesterday’s news”. The branding that does occur in short-cycle ecologies is ‘umbrella’ branding of the firm (Sony, Dell) and/or of a product family (Macintosh, Pentium). Umbrella branding may be valuable on the margin, i.e. when there are competing hot short-cycle products. But even a good umbrella brand (e.g. IBM) will not be able to salvage a product that is old or too late in a short-cycle ecology (e.g. Aptiva).

## 3.3.1. Strategic IT applications

Applications that are strategic in short-cycle ecologies enable rapid change of the organization's products. For example, CAD/CAM systems are focused on enabling fast product changes (e.g. new designs of product bodies), to be manufactured rapidly in large quantities. Note that standardization (the focus of standard-cycle CAD/CAM) is not excluded in short-cycle, but likely is not strategic. A hot new product enjoys a certain pricing freedom, which can hide some cost inefficiencies due to lack of standardization.

Rapid development of new ideas across departments and geographic boundaries can be enabled by groupware and video conferencing designed to enable speed. Workers at financial houses, Disney, and Dreamworks use super-fast computers to design new products that are hoped to be hot.

Other applications that are strategic for short-cycle are focused on managing the timing of entry and exit: For example, IS to gather and disseminate competitive intelligence and information about the firm's own sales or IS to spot market trends within a few days, e.g. for Benetton sweaters (Davidow and Malone, 1992). Systems to enable quick price changes are strategic as well in short-cycle.

For information products, wireless technology allows instant downloads, i.e. distribution, of hot items on a global scale. Services, particularly their pricing, e.g. for air travel, can be made available rapidly via global reservation systems. Supply chain technology to speed up global distribution of physical goods is strategic, for example, in facilitating customs clearance. Standard-cycle inventory management tries to determine an ‘optimal’ (non-zero) inventory level, given expected demand and the probability of losing future sales due to stockouts. Short-cycle inventory management tries to maintain zero inventory because vast amounts of product move quickly, with any left-over inventory depreciating rapidly.

The key in short-cycle ecologies is that IT applications enable rapid change (in all business functions), regardless of the speed with which such applications change themselves.

## 3.3.2. Core IS capabilities

The IT architecture supporting a short-cycle organization must focus on supporting speed of design, marketing, production, distribution, etc. The IT architecture must also enable the firm to exit the market of one product and to rapidly enter the market with a new one. Such market exits and entries routinely cause huge spikes in volume, which standard-cycle operations find uncommon and difficult to handle. Short-cycle IS services also need to enable and support the organization's need for speed. Procurement and contracts for services may be short-lived and therefore need to allow for rapid changes and spikes in product volumes.

From time to time, new IT products appear on the market that can enable speed in short-cycle firms. Note that these products initially may not fit an organization's IT standards, e.g. when web browsers were first introduced. For the short term, the need to enable speed and IT's propensity for standardization may conflict, but, in short-cycle ecologies, speed should win. Standardization takes time, which short-cycle organizations do not have.

## 3.4. Long cycle

Conventional approaches might view long-cycle ecologies as fragmented and (mistakenly) consider them unimportant. In long-cycle ecologies, however, per-unit margins are typically quite high, due to ‘lock-in’ with the customer, which is the key of the long-cycle strategy paradigm. Lock-in is reinforced by ties to location, high complexity of the product, close trusted personal relations or regulation by the government or professional organizations. These four factors can be independent (e.g. location advantage of restaurants), or mutually reinforcing (e.g. complexity and relations reinforcing a consultant’s advantage).

Locations such as airport hubs are often controlled (10–20 year contracts) by one airline (Bailey and Williams, 1988), e.g. Delta in Atlanta. Even with certain deregulated services (such as producers of electricity or gas), customers continue to be locked into the one distribution company that ties to their house.

Trust fostered by long-standing close personal relations between a buyer and a seller (high-end strategy consulting or investment banking) is critical in selecting a vendor, and very difficult for competitors to imitate. For example, when a trusted advisor switches employers, clients follow him/her to the new firm. Also, such relations are highly sensitive to cultural differences, making it even more difficult for foreign firms to compete against established domestic firms.

Lock-in can also be reinforced by the product's complexity, requiring an extremely high level of expertise in the seller (e.g. jumbo jet airplanes). Trusted relations and complexity together can keep competitors out for long periods of time (e.g. EDS running other firms' IT operations). Particularly where high levels of complexity are valued, customers often view standardization negatively. Clients want providers of, say, strategy consulting or manufacturing plants, to develop solutions that are not ‘cookie cutter’, but truly unique to the client’s specific situation.

Complexity (e.g. the need to convert files or retrain employees) and regulation (e.g. intellectual property protection) can result in reluctance to switch to a different product (e.g. Microsoft Windows). Even though PC software products (operating systems or application software) frequently need new versions in order to be successful, customers want the product's underlying technical standards to remain the same (and at least compatible) for long periods of time. Such increasing returns effects due to network externalities (Shapiro and Varian, 1999) lock customers into the product to which the market has ‘tipped’.

Government regulation can result in extremely long product cycles. For example, before a new drug can be marketed, government-regulated testing takes years without guarantee of success. Patent protection can shield the firm from competitors, assuming they have not developed a different product with similar benefits.

Advantages based on these four factors (location, relations, complexity and regulation), or a combination of them, generally cannot be easily (i.e. profitably) imitated by others. Convergence takes a long time, which yields product advantages of 7–10 years or even longer.

## 3.4.1. Strategic IT applications

Applications that are strategic in a long-cycle ecology enable key success factors. To reinforce location-based advantages, IT products such as web crawlers and databases are strategic when they help to keep track of potentially desirable locations. Such IT may not require a large part of the IT budget, but is still strategic. A gas-pipeline company we know keeps track of all for-sale announcements of land with pipeline right-of-ways. Even a small, seemingly insignificant right-of-way might become an important link in their pipeline network. Note that location in other types of ecologies may be important (e.g. where to locate a factory), but not strategic.

Long-cycle firms focus their use of CAD/CAM not on standardization, which would suppress the unique value-adding (complex) product characteristics, but on managing the necessarily extreme complexity of their products (e.g. jumbo jet airplanes). We worked with one firm in the manufacturing industry whose new products will only be considered for purchase if extremely high performance requirements are proven after five years of continuous testing.

Communication among geographically dispersed experts can be enabled with video conferencing or with Knowledge Management technology/ groupware, which is focused on handling extreme product complexity, not development speed. Data mining can help to keep track of much more information about personal relations than the standard contact management and sales history applications. Such detailed information can then be used to further cement a bond over time.

In order to keep track of or influence regulation, the Internet can be used strategically to analyze voting patterns of elected government officials in committees or districts that are relevant to a firm's business. Such information can then be used to target lobbying efforts and investments. Also, e-mail is increasingly being used to quickly rally people around a certain issue and encourage them to communicate with their representatives in government.

## 3.4.2. Core IS capabilities

For long-cycle ecologies, IS capabilities must focus on the ecology's key drivers: Lock-in due to regulation, location, complexity or trusted relations. Particularly for the latter two, the IT architecture as well as the IS capabilities must to be able to handle high, i.e. non-standard, levels of complexity. One custom engineering firm has a highly customized version of an ERP package to process and track complex one-of-a-kind orders (that are essentially design-and-build projects). Databases to keep track of numerous details about a customer will be complex to design and to integrate into an overall IT architecture. Highly specialized IT-based tools for complex design or production are likely to be unique and difficult to inter-operate. The effort of making (and keeping) such IT working is itself complex. Other IS services, such as contract facilitation and monitoring, have to be oriented to the high levels of complexity required in certain long-cycle ecologies (and not so much the degree of standardization or speed of delivery).

Even IT architectures and services that support standard- or short-cycle ecologies are likely long-cycle products themselves (from the point of view of their vendor). Such architectures and services (even if made of standardized components) are unique, complex, and subject to high performance characteristics (with dramatic consequences of downtime). They are highly custom-engineered for the organization, making their design, delivery and maintenance a long-cycle business, which may explain certain frustrations that business departments have with corporate IT departments: The IT architectures may, for good reasons, be on a longer cycle than the rest of the business.

## 3.5. Transitions between ecologies

Within a given ecology, things are constantly changing, but the competitive dynamics stay qualitatively the same. A change in the speed of change (similar to a non-zero second derivative) is a transition from one ecology to another. Transitions are a change in industry structure, a change in the rules of the game, traditionally a topic of great interest in (IT) strategy. For example, deregulation (e.g. airlines, gas, electricity) generally means a shift from long-cycle competition to standard-cycle competition. The Smart car, by Mercedes and Swatch, is a transition from standard-cycle (Mercedes) to short-cycle (Swatch), just as the original fashion oriented Swatch watch was a transition from traditional Swiss Watches. Junk bonds, on the other hand, moved from the short-cycle to standard-cycle. Microsoft's overarching strategy is to transition, fast-changing IT applications into features of long-cycle PC operating systems.

Maintaining alignment in a transition means to adjust products, strategies, and internal resources (including IS capabilities) to fit with the new ecology. Companies misinterpret the signals from the new ecology when they do not recognize the shift from one strategy paradigm to another. They dismiss the new signals, because in the old strategy paradigm they were not significant.

## 3.6. Hybrid ecologies

To ease explanation we have first presented the three pure ecologies. In practice, a product, may well be in a ‘hybrid’ ecology: A blend of the pure cases. $^{9}$ As with the pure ecologies, the core IS capabilities of a hybrid ecology have to be aligned with the other internal resources and with the external environment. Due to the mixed and conflicting signals about the competitive dynamics (some things must stay the same for many years, and some things must change rapidly), hybrids are more difficult to manage. They present good opportunities, however. For example, long/standard-cycle or short/standard cycle ecologies can benefit from higher prices and profits, due to lock-in or hot products, respectively, and simultaneously enjoy cost efficiencies due to high volumes of standardized products.

An example of a long/standard hybrid is Amazon.com. Amazon.com is a (self-)service organization that, in the words of Jeff Bezos (CEO), is designed for “helping people make purchase decisions”. It sells standard products through standardized processes, and has a large, and increasing, scale of distribution operations to reap economies of scale, all pointing to standard cycle. Over time, Amazon can expect to derive substantial bargaining power (over suppliers) from its size. In addition, Amazon.com is attempting a long-cycle strategy: Lock in customers by exploiting increasing return effects from collaborative filtering. By offering customers superior suggestions (based on the interests and purchasing history of the customer and of other customers), it entices customers to purchase more, enabling Amazon to offer even better suggestions.

If this lock-in is ultimately successful, Amazon.com will have a ‘pole position’ (Feeny and Ives, 1990) as a consumer-purchasing portal and simultaneously be very cost-efficient due their scale of distribution, i.e. it will be a long/standard cycle hybrid. If so, it will be the dominant player, face mild price competition, and enjoy above-average margins compared to the industry. The lock-in strategy may not be successful, however, if customers fear privacy issues and do not provide enough information about their interests for collaborative filtering to yield interesting recommendations. If so, Amazon.com will be (reduced to) a pure standard-cycle distributor, i.e. one of 3–4 major players (oligopoly), with intense competition over small changes in market share, and margins around industry average.

## 3.7. IT strategy: differences across ecologies

Sustainability Analysis is about explicitly recognizing different dynamics of different ecologies, and deriving recommendations for strategy in general, and IT in particular, that reinforce the key success factors of the relevant ecology. Table 1 summarizes key success factors by ecology, and gives examples of the type of IT applications that reinforce those success factors.

Any particular IT is not inherently strategic. Systems are strategic when they support the ecology's key success factors. Table 1 presents conclusions from our earlier analysis: IT applications can be strategic in one ecology but not in another. For example, a system to keep track of (potential) locations is strategic in long-cycle ecologies where location is a key success factor, but not in short- or standard-cycle. Also, the (seemingly) same IT may be strategic or not depending on whether its focus is appropriate for the ecology. We discussed, for example, how strategic CAD/CAM systems focus on managing extreme complexity in long cycle, standardizing products in standard cycle, and enabling speed in short cycle.

Core IS capabilities differ across ecologies, not in their importance, but in their focus, i.e. in the success factors they reinforce: The capabilities regarding IT architecture and delivery of IS services in short-cycle ecologies must enable extremely fast changes in design, production, and distribution of products. Long-cycle IS capabilities must enhance lock-in with the customer. The differing key success factors of each ecology yield different requirements for core IS capabilities, which we described for each ecology.

## 3.8. Corporate strategy: managing multiple ecologies

The most difficult situation for executives occurs when multiple ecologies (and inherent strategy paradigms) must be managed simultaneously in one firm, because that firm commercializes products that are in different ecologies (e.g. Sony, HP, Philips, NEC, Samsung, or Bosch). Executives need tremendous mental agility to simultaneously apply different strategy paradigms for the different ecologies. Similarly, IT executives need tremendous agility as well. Different applications have requirements that are fundamentally different, possibly even conflicting (long-cycle complexity versus standard-cycle standardization versus short-cycle speed). The multi-ecology firm's IT architecture and its IS delivery services must support all such demands.

## 4. Conclusions

In our view, IT strategy frameworks are specializations of general strategy theories. We reviewed well-known IT strategy frameworks, particularly with respect to how they incorporate competitive dynamics. Static methods do not incorporate competitive dynamics and thus cannot predict the sustainability of a competitive advantage. Dynamic methods do incorporate competitive interactions and/or speed of change. The most comprehensive (Feeny and Ives, 1990) proposed a strategy logic that indicated the sustainability of a competitive advantage.

Sustainability Analysis (Williams, 1992, 1994, 1998) extends strategy dynamics thinking further by recognizing differences in sustainability, based on different competitive dynamics, and how they are equivalent with different strategy paradigms (in different ecologies). Its terminology (ecology, evolution, renewal, etc.) borrows heavily from biology, reflecting a broader trend in management thinking, see, e.g. Hidding and Catterall (1998), De Geus (1997), Kelly (1994), Carroll's chapter in Rumelt et al. (1994), or Hannan and Freeman (1977).

Even though all ecologies are becoming more dynamic over time, some ecologies are, and will remain, much more dynamic than others. In pure ecologies, there is usually no confusion about the competitive dynamics and how the organization should be managed. Executives from many organizations operating in pure ecologies found SA's insights and suggestions (including those in Table 1) self-evident and confirming. In hybrid ecologies or multi-ecology firms, Sustainability Analysis resolves confusion. Executives operating in hybrids or multi-ecology firms, and executives managing a new business, found SA's insights and conclusions illuminating and clarifying, because it classifies signals into different paradigms.

Sustainability Analysis is, effectively, a contingency framework for strategy. Based on competitive dynamics, it suggests a strategy paradigm that is appropriate for a given product. By focusing on competitive dynamics, Sustainability Analysis allows us to classify the ecology, to predict the length of time for which an advantage can be sustained, and to determine the conditions (technology change, customer needs, competitors' (re)actions, the firm's resources, IT strategies, etc.) that are consistent with the ecology.

This paper argues that different ecologies imply that IT applications differ in their importance to strategy as well as their focus, i.e. which key success factors they support or enable. Core IS capabilities differ across ecologies in how they support or enable the relevant key success factors.

## 4.1. Implications for further research

The exploration of the implications of Sustainability Analysis for IT strategy as presented in this paper is a first step. This paper raises additional questions for research. For example: Are the differences in IT Strategy, as dependent on ecology, occurring systematically in practice? Can it be proven quantitatively (e.g. in PIMS-like studies) that certain strategies, IT applications or IS capabilities lead to superior strategic performance when applied in ecologies where they are appropriate, but lead to inferior strategic performance when applied in ecologies where they are inappropriate? Do the differences in focus (standardization, speed, etc.) of certain applications (e.g. CAD/CAM) occur systematically in practice? What are the implications of the different ecologies on common IS issues, e.g. development methods used (e.g. waterfall, RAD, etc.), or organizational IT standards? How do multi-ecology firms manage their applications portfolio, their IT architecture, and their delivery of IS services?

A number of developments regarding competitive dynamics in the strategy literature also warrant evaluation for the IT strategy domain. Examples are: Ghemawat's (1997) research into ‘commitment’ and lock-in evolutionary views of strategy (Nelson and Winter, 1982), or ecology theories of competition (Carroll in Rumelt et al., 1994, or Hannan and Freeman, 1977); Santa Fe Institute's research into complex adaptive systems (Kelly, 1994); D'Aveni's (1994) framework regarding hypercompetition; Teece et al.'s (1997) research into dynamic capabilities; absorptive capacity (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990) or other learning-based research (Herriott et al., 1985 or Ingram and Baum, 1997); or the knowledge-based theory of the firm (Kogut and Zander, 1992). Further research should explore such new developments, consider implications and extensions for IT strategy, and conduct empirical testing of new insights.
