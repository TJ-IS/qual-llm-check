# Managerial information system success factors within the cultural context of North America and a former Soviet Republic

Michael D. Ishman $^{a,*}$ , C. Carl Pegels $^{b}$ , G. Lawrence Sanders $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Commerce, College of Business Administration, Niagara University, New York, NY 14109, USA $^{b}$ Department of Management Science and Systems, School of Management, Jacobs Management Center, State University of New York at Buffalo, Buffalo, NY 14260, USA

Received 1 September 1999; accepted 1 June 2001

## Abstract

Due to the magnitude of organizational resource requirements for the development of managerial information systems, both practitioners and researchers have attempted to identify those factors that impact on information system success. This paper presents a cross-cultural analysis of a hybrid model of information systems success within the cultural context of North America and a former Soviet Republic, Latvia. The results of the analysis indicate that some of the factors that impact on managerial information system success have built-in value biases reflecting the value orientations of the culture in which they are developed. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Managerial information system; Success factors; Managerial issues; Cross-cultural

Using data provided by the International Data Corporation, the World Information Technology and Services Alliance (WITSA) estimates that the “worldwide ICT (information and communication technology) market surpassed the \$2 trillion mark in 1999”. WITSA projects that the industry will surpass the \$3 trillion threshold by the year 2004. Between 1997 and 1999, growth in ICT spending (spending on computer/telecommunications hardware, software, and services) was at the astounding rate of 42% for Eastern Europe and Latin American while growth in the more mature ICT infrastructures of Western Europe and North America was at a 13 and 15% rate, respectively (WITSA, 2000). The magnitude of these investments has focused managerial attention on obtaining successful outcomes from these resource commitments to IT. In particular, both practitioners and researchers have attempted to identify those factors that impact on managerial information system success. Two of the most widely studied factors have been user involvement and user participation (Garrity and Sanders, 1998). In a cursory review of the literature between 1974 and 1986, Pettingell et al. (1988) identified over 250 papers that addressed the relationship of user involvement $^{1}$ and system success. Over the last decade, the number of papers on user involvement/participation and managerial information system success has continued to be substantial.

There has also been a stimulation of research into linkages between system success factors and other managerial/behavioral issues. For example, Hunton and Price (1997) have linked user involvement/participation with procedural justice and decision control. Using equity theory as a foundation, Joshi (1990) has linked perceptions of resource equity to variations in the user's reaction to change caused by the implementation of information systems. Equity has been suggested as one of the more useful constructs in explaining successful implementation. This paper introduces a new linkage based on equity sensitivity research from the behavioral sciences (Huseman et al., 1987; King et al., 1993) and perceptions of equity in management information systems (MISs) resource allocations. Almost without exception, these studies, and those mentioned in the previous paragraph, have taken place in the domestic homogeneous cultural environment of the US and Canada. The reality of the competitive marketplace is, however, an environment composed of global heterogeneous cultural dimensions. Firms desiring to compete in such an environment must develop information technology applications that offer effective and efficient linkages for the complexities of a global marketplace.

Roche (1992) reports that relatively simple domestic developments of IT “can turn into cultural, administrative, and logistic nightmares when attempted internationally”. Kumar and Bjorn-Anderson (1990) have concluded that information system design (ISD) methodologies have built-in value biases reflecting the value priorities of the culture in which they are developed. The systems developed using these methodologies may or may not be acceptable in cultures with value orientations different from the one in which the system was developed. A number of studies have focused on whether cultural differences have had an impact on the global application of information technology and how great those impacts have been. For instance, Watson et al. (1994) have considered cultural factors in the application of group support systems in Singapore and the US. Straub (1994) has studied the effects of culture on information technology diffusion in the US and Japan. Kettinger et al. (1995) have investigated the cultural effects on the global measures of information service quality in Korea, Hong Kong, the US, and the Netherlands. Nelson (1996) has identified the ‘people’ dimension as a key issue in global information systems quality and more recently Zaheer and Zaheer (1997) have studied national cultural effects across 25 countries on information seeking in global electronic networks.

Other IT researchers have found that global similarities far outweigh any differences and the unintended consequences are minor in nature (Emery, 1990; Watson and Brancheau, 1991). The reality may be somewhere in between the two positions of similar/different. Tractinsky and Jarvenpaa (1995) report that many important factors that influence the applications of IT appear to hold across both domestic and global environments. However, there are also a number of contingency factors (e.g. variety, familiarity, and complexity) that cannot be ignored. The degree to which any of these factors may support the ‘Global is Similar’ school, or the ‘Global is Different’ school, or some position in between, can be situational dependent. Accordingly, IT application models should be tested in a variety of cultural situations in order to increase the validity of the model in both a domestic and global context.

The purpose of this research study is to analyze the validity, in a domestic and global context, of user involvement, user participation, perceived equity, and equity sensitivity as critical factors in managerial information system (MIS) success. In sum, these variables have been linked to successful information systems in Western countries (Garrity and Sanders, 1998; Segars and Grover, 1993, 1998). The specific question addressed in this study is to what degree does the general model of information success presented in Fig. 1 hold across the cultural settings of North America and former Soviet Republics, such as Latvia? This is an important question since the individual factors of the model have only been measured and tested in North American context. If international practitioners and researchers are to adopt the findings of the previous domestic research and the premise of the general model, then they need to know if those individually based factors hold across other cultural settings and in particular, in former Soviet Republics, such as Latvia. In Section 1 the research model is presented along with literature that substantiates the research hypotheses.

## 1. Background to the study

Fig. 2 represents the hybrid model of the relationship between the independent variables, user involvement, user participation, perceived equity, equity sensitivity, and the dependent variable, user information satisfaction. User information satisfaction is the most widely used surrogate for measuring information system success. DeLone and McLean (1992) cite numerous journal articles that address empirical studies of user information satisfaction. Three reasons are given by DeLone and McLean for the wide use of this variable: (a) satisfaction has a high degree of face validity, (b) several reliable instruments have been developed for measuring satisfaction, and (c) most other measures of information system success are problematic.

The primary research question to be addressed in this study is whether the hybrid model is valid in two diverse cultural and economic environments, North America and Latvia. The model is derived from the work of: (a) Barki and Hartwick (1989, 1994) who measured user involvement/participation, (b) Kappelman and McLean's (1991) who evaluated the impact of those two factors on MIS success, (c) Joshi (1990) who has evaluated user reactions to change caused by the implementation of information systems (perceived equity) and its impact on MIS success, and (d) Huseman et al. (1987) and King, et al. (1993) who have evaluated different perceptions of equity based on an individuals equity sensitivity classification. There is evidence (to be presented) from previous research that the research model is valid for North America. However, little research has been conducted on the individual constructs of the model in other cultural environments. Accordingly, from the model, one main hypothesis is developed for study and several sub-hypotheses emerge as the discussion proceeds.

## 1.1. User involvement/participation

North America. Historically, it has been an accepted premise that user involvement is a critical factor for successful information systems development/implementation. However, early research in this area yielded inconclusive results. In 1986, Baroudi, Olson and Ives reported on a number of problems with previous user involvement research. One key problem they identified was the lack of an operational definition for user involvement. They differentiate between attitude issues, such as satisfaction toward an information system, and behavioral issues, such as the usage of a system.

Barki and Hartwick expand on this theme of differentiating between user attitudes and behavior by establishing two separate constructs for involvement. One construct is user participation, which is defined as a set of behaviors or activities performed by users in the system development process and the other is user involvement, which is a subjective psychological state reflecting the importance and personal relevance of a system to the user (Barki and Hartwick, 1989). Kappelman and McLean (1991) found that when previous research on user involvement was reviewed from this psychological and behavioral perspective, the majority of the research had been based on behavioral theory. Their research also demonstrated that user involvement and user participation directly influence user satisfaction and that user participation has a direct impact on user involvement.

Latvia. In a recent study by Ishman (1998b) on Latvian attitudes towards information technology, it was observed that Latvian managers and professionals (accountants, engineers, public administrators, educators, etc.) who have worked within the framework of the Soviet management model $^{2}$ are likely: (a) to be involved and participate in the development and implementation of information systems and (b) to be satisfied with the outcomes of those systems. It is speculated that the Soviet model, with its emphasis on group harmony and working for the good of the group as a whole, regardless of individual rewards, motivates high levels of involvement/participation and consequently satisfaction with the resulting information systems (Ishman, 1998b). Those Latvians with less exposure to the Soviet model generally consider communism, and its concepts of equality, as obsolete values of the previous generation. They equate capitalism with personal financial success (Neimanis, 1997). Accordingly, their levels of satisfaction will be largely influenced by the degree of the individual reward to be derived from their participation and involvement.

It is therefore proposed that the outcomes for both cultural groups, in regards to the model constructs of user involvement, participation and satisfaction, are fundamentally the same. However, the underlying reasons maybe different. For North Americans, individual behaviors and personal relevance of systems impact on satisfaction levels. For many Latvians, especially those with extensive work experience under the Soviet management model, the emphasis is on work group impact rather than on the individual. The hypotheses that follow for both cultural groups are:

H1a. The greater a user's involvement in the development/implementation of an information system (I/S), the higher is his/her level of satisfaction with the system.

H1b. The greater a user's participation in the development/implementation of an I/S, the higher is his/her level of satisfaction with the system.

H1c. The greater the user's participation in the development/implementation of a system the greater is his/her involvement in the process.

## 1.2. Perceived equity and equity sensitivity

North America: Using equity theory as a foundation, Joshi (1990) developed an equity-implementation model to explain variations in the user's reaction to change caused by the implementation of information systems. Individuals evaluate the ‘fairness’ of those changes based on how it modifies the outcome/input ratios in their relationships with others. Those relationships are often measured in terms of resource allocations. When the ratio declines, the individual perceives inequality and is distressed. The higher the perception of inequality the greater the level of distress and the more likely the individual will take action to restore equity. Such actions can range from an attitude of indifference to an outright sabotage of the information system. When outcome/input ratios increase, however, the opposite effect is observed. High levels of perceived equity lead to high levels of user involvement, participation and satisfaction (Joshi, 1992; Ishman, 1995).

An aspect of equity theory not considered in Joshi's equity implementation model, and introduced as a new construct in the hybrid model presented in this paper, is equity sensitivity. In the equity implementation model, it is assumed that everyone is uniformly sensitive to equity. From the behavioral sciences it is proposed “that individuals react in consistent but individually different ways to both perceived equality and inequality because they have different preferences for (i.e. are differently sensitive to) equality” (Huseman et al., 1987). Individuals are classified along a continuum of equity sensitivity that ranges from ‘benevolent’ to ‘entitled’. Only those in the middle of the continuum, classified as ‘equity sensitive’, are uniformly sensitive to equity in that they prefer outcome/input ratios to be equal in their relationships. As implied by the classification, entitled individuals prefer their outcome/input ratios to exceed those of others and benevolent individuals prefer them to be less. King et al. (1993) have also observed that there is a propensity of benevolent individuals to report greater job satisfaction across various reward situations. Accordingly, it is proposed that the higher an individuals’ benevolent classification, the more likely they are to perceive equity in information system development and implementation.

Latvia. Soviet egalitarianism included “not only equality of opportunity, an acceptable principle in Western democracies, but also equality of outcome” (Lenches, 1993). Among workers, there was a general desire for equality of rewards and the security of one’s position, irrespective of one’s efforts. “Material success that elevated one above one’s neighbors constituted antisocial behavior, and popular egalitarianism justified any action taken against the successful” (Neimanis, 1997). In 1991, Neimanis asked a group of Latvian and Russian graduate management students to name the greatest social value. The general response at the time was ‘equality’. In a 1992 study in Cuba (Mesher et al., 1992), it was observed that “high-quality work (was) not preferentially rewarded within a given organization, and entrepreneurial activity (had) little outlet within the formal economy”. The typical individual trained in the Soviet management model was taught that it was more important to be ambitious, obedient and ideologically correct than it was to have talent. This resulted in a system where the powerful were “not necessarily a talented cream, but (rather) remnants of a failed elite who floated because of the fat of privilege” (Chepatis, 1994). That is to say the reality of Soviet egalitarianism was a “genteel poverty for the masses and relative affluence (privilege) for the numerous officers, officials, and bureaucrats” (Neimanis, 1997). It was the inequality reality that individuals came to expect while at the same time maintaining an illusion of equality.

Ishman (1998b) found that Latvians tended to be more ‘benevolent’ in their equity sensitivity classification and generally had increased perceptions of equality in the distribution of information technology resources as their number of years of work experience under the Soviet management model increased. Similar correlations between work experience and sensitivity classifications, as well as perceptions of resource equalities, were not observed in a similar group of North American managers/professionals. In an earlier study (Ishman, 1995), both cultural groups exhibited increased levels of satisfaction with information systems as their levels of perceived equity in information technology resources increased and individuals within both groups were more likely to perceive resource equity if their equity sensitivity classification was ‘benevolent’. Increases in perceived equity were also observed to increase involvement and participation levels.

It is therefore proposed that the outcomes for both North American and Latvian groups in regards to the model constructs of perceived equity and equity sensitivity are fundamentally the same. However, once again the underlying reasons are different. For Latvians, Soviet egalitarianism drives a desire for equality in outcomes regardless of individual efforts. As long as equality of outcomes is perceived to exist within the group, involvement/participation levels increase and overall satisfaction results. Equality for North Americans is influenced by input/output ratios where increases in individual efforts are expected to result in higher individual rewards, which, in turn, leads to increased involvement, participation and satisfaction. Latvians, with extensive work experience under the Soviet model, came to expect the reality of under-reward (or inequality) inherent in the Soviet system while at the same time maintaining an illusion of equality. Accordingly, the longer they were in the system the more likely they were to become ‘benevolent’. The hypotheses that follow for both cultural groups are:

H1d. The greater an individual's perceptions of equality in information technology resource allocation, the higher his/her level of satisfaction with the resulting system.

H1e. The greater an individual's perceptions of equality in information technology resource allocation, the higher his/her level of participation in the development and implementation of information systems.

H1f. The greater an individual's perceptions of equality in information technology resource allocation, the higher his/her level of involvement in the development/implementation of information systems.

H1g. An individual is more likely to perceive resource equity if his/her equity sensitivity classification is ‘benevolent’.

The remainder of this paper will present the methodology used in, and discuss the results derived from, investigating the above propositions.

## 2. Methodology

It is the purpose of this research study to assess the validity of the hybrid model across two heterogeneous cultural settings. From a pure research perspective, the validation of all factors should take place in a variety of cultural settings. For IT research studies, such an approach is especially important because of the globalization of IT applications.

The research design of this study is centered on an empirical approach using a survey research methodology, factor analysis, and causal path analysis. The following sections partially describe how an instrument was developed to measure the constructs of the model. Using standardized procedures, the instrument was satisfactorily tested for reliability and validity (see Appendix A).

## 2.1. Instrument development

The survey instrument was originally developed in English and translated into Latvian and Russian for use in Latvia and into French for French speaking Canadians. Adler's (1983) work in cross-cultural comparative management research was adopted in the preparation of instrument translations. Particular attention was given to the issues of language differences through forward and backward translations and scaling equivalency. (For a more complete description of the instrument development, see Ishman (1998a)).

## 2.2. Sample selection

Two distinct geographic/economic regions were selected for this study; North America (including the US as well as the English and French speaking geographic regions of Canada) and Latvia (including both native Latvians and those of Russian origin). The US and Canada were selected because of their long histories of an open market economy while Latvia was selected based on its recent transition from a Soviet style centrally planned economy to an open market economy. As previous discussed, it is speculated that these diverse cultural/economic histories have an impact on the constructs of the model under study.

## 2.4. Data entry

Since each construct in the research model is a composite of several measurements, a composite score for each group of items was calculated for the analysis of variance (ANOVA) and causal path analysis. Equal weight was given to the individual scores for each item in a composite.

## 2.5. ANOVA

An ANOVA was used to test for differences with respect to the constructs of the model (user involvement, user participation, perceived equity, equity sensitivity and user satisfaction) as dependent variables and the two cultural groups (North America and Latvia) as the independent variables. The result of the analysis showed that there were no statistically significant differences between the cultural groups for all but one of the constructs. The one exception was for the construct of user participation (F value of 11.628, significance level of 0.001). There were no within group differences when a further analysis was conducted on all of the sub-cultural groups present. The within sub-cultural groups for North America were: (a) Americans, (b) English speaking Canadians, and (c) French speaking Canadians. For Latvia, the within groups were: (a) Latvian speaking Latvians and (b) Russian speaking Latvians (see Table 1). The implications are that the sub-cultural groups of the two major groups under study can each be pooled for further study using causal path analysis. However, based on the ANOVA results one can expect to find some model path coefficient differences between the two major groups when analyzing the user participation construct.

## 2.6. Causal path analysis

The research model lent itself to causal path analysis because it is a simple recursive model. The recursive model states the theory in terms of the relationship of the constructs. Each successive construct in the model can be determined from one or more of the preceding constructs. Regression strategies were used to accomplish the path analysis. The goal was to determine if the research model was consistent with the sample data and to make comparisons of the relative strength of association along the various paths (beta weights). The model was treated as a linear model with the exogenous variable, equity sensitivity, being entered first into the regression analysis. Successive steps involved selection of endogenous variables based on their linear sequence in the model. Since there are four such variables, four separate regressions were run. From the standardized beta coefficients of each separate regression that was run, the total, direct, indirect, and non-causal effects were calculated for each path relationship. The complete causal path relations are reported in Figs. 3 and 4 for each statistically significant beta coefficient $p < 0.05$ .

It is possible some of the path relationship is a manifestation of the effects of another variable, such as Involvement or even Perceived Equity. It should also be noted that there are significant spurious effects (when compared to the direct causal effects) in the relationships between the variables participation and involvement (0.115) as well as involvement and satisfaction (0.212). Again, the implication is that the path relationships are a manifestation of the effects of other variables in the model.

A closer examination of Fig. 3 indicates that there is an excellent fit between the causal path model and the North American data. The adjusted R squared value for the model is 0.47. All the paths are statistically significance (p < 0.05). The paths involving Perceived Equity are particularly strong and significantly expand on earlier research in this area. These findings are also consistent with the core research studies previously conducted on other portions of the hybrid model (see Section 1). Finally, these findings give support to the addition of a ‘new’ construct, equity sensitivity, to the field of information technology from the behavioral sciences.

The adjusted R squared value for the Latvian data, presented in Fig. 4, is substantially weaker than the adjusted R squared value for the North American data (0.13 versus 0.47). It is also observed that only three of the path coefficients are statistically significant (p < 0.05). Even the path between Participation and Satisfaction (-0.048) is in the opposite direction of that predicted. This was not entirely unexpected since the ANOVA screening indicated that there were statistically significant differences between the two cultural groups for the construct of user participation. It is also interesting to note the important role that the constructs participation and perceived equity play in the hybrid model. For both the Latvian and North American data sets, these are the only constructs that involve significant path relationships.

## 3. Discussion and conclusion

The focus of this research was to promote an understanding of managerial information system success within the cross-cultural context of North America and Latvia, a former Soviet Republic. The hybrid model presented in Fig. 2, which joins together the work of a number of information systems researchers and introduces a new construct from the field of organizational behavior, was found to be most strongly supported by the data set collected in North America. That is to say, the majority of the constructs presented in the model appear to have built in value biases reflecting the value orientations of the culture in which they were developed (North America). The one exception is the construct of perceived equity, which has a major impact on information system success in both the cultural groups under study.

A partial explanation as to why the causal path model is not as conclusive within the cross-cultural context of Latvia lies in the length of work experience for Latvian participants. When the exogenous variable of work experience is added to the Latvian model, it is found to have a negative path coefficient to participation (significant at p = 0.027). In the North American model, this path is not significant. The implication is that Latvian managers, with extensive work experience under the Soviet management model, are less likely to participate in information system development. Neimanis (2000) has observed that “not everybody enthusiastically embraced market capitalism or the divorce from the old empire that offered a higher degree of social security and equality…by and large members of the old nomenklatura (those appointed to their position by the communist party) continued filling the leading positions in industry…moreover, many senior executives and officials lived in considerable fear of losing face in front of subordinates and foreigners. One minimizes the risk of appearing incompetent by doing little (participating) and saying less”.

Since participation is a central endogenous variable in the model, the causal path model fit is ‘distorted’ for the Latvian data set by approximately half of the participants having extensive Soviet management experience (members of the old nomenklatura). An interesting follow-up study would be to collect a larger data set in Latvia and test for within group difference based on length of work experience. This would be conceptually similar to the study done by Ralstron et al. (1999), where managerial values in China were assessed based upon the extent to which a manager experienced (based upon their age) certain significant periods of societal change (the Republican, Communist Consolidation, Great Cultural Revolution, and Social Reform Eras). Similar significant periods of societal change in Latvia would be the Russification, Glasnost/Perestroika, and Post-Communist Eras.

Another shortcoming of the study lies in the fact no attempt was made to distinguish the validity of the model among different types of information system development. Because of Latvia's relatively recent transition to an open market economy, it is speculated that the majority of the information systems encountered were transaction processing type systems. As the economy develops it is anticipated that additional systems, such as group decision support systems (GDSS) and executive support systems (ESS), will come into use. At that time, it may be found, for example, that managers, deeply steeped in the Soviet tradition of placing the needs of the group above the needs of the individual, will accept GDSS more readily than others (see Ishman, 1998b for further discussion).

In spite of its shortcomings, the results of this study can be of significant value to both practitioners and researchers attempting to identify factors that impact on managerial information system success and their application in cross-cultural environments. Within the cross-cultural context of North America and Latvia, perceptions of equity in the allocation of information technology resources have an important impact on successful system outcomes. Increases in perceptions of equity increase the level of satisfaction with related information systems, and consequently with the success of such systems. International managers need to be aware that when individuals perceive that they and/or their work group have not been treated fairly in the resource allocation process, the consequences can be disruptive. Such individuals can become uncooperative in the development and implementation phases of the information systems. In more extreme cases, they may even sabotage the systems (Joshi, 1990).

Within the domestic environment of North America, the hybrid model of user satisfaction presented in Fig. 2 is offered as a valuable tool in the development and implementation of managerial information systems. This hybrid model supports the extensive research that has previously been conducted on the importance of user involvement/participation in system success. The evidence is now very substantial that as the importance and personal relevance of a system (psychological involvement) to the user increases, their satisfaction with, and the ultimate success of, the system increases. Likewise, from a behavioral perspective, increases in one's participation in the actual process of developing and using a system, directly and indirectly (via involvement) increases their satisfaction with the system and thus increases the likelihood of system success. Thus by increasing end-user involvement and participation, the development and implementation manager can substantially influence the successful outcome of their projects.

Other important factors for the North American development/implementation manager to consider are perceptions of information technology resource allocations among end-users and the equity sensitivity index of those users. Previous research has shown that increases in perceptions of resource allocations increases user satisfaction with related information systems. The research presented in this study supports that research and also reveals that increases in perceived equity bring about increases in user involvement/participation. A new dimension introduced in this research study is the role that equity sensitivity plays in perceptions of information technology resource allocation. Managers need to be aware that individuals with a ‘benevolent’ equity sensitivity classification are more tolerant of inequality than individuals classified as ‘entitled’ and may even extend to the point where they will judge an under allocation situation as equitable.

These results raise a number of resource allocation issues for managers. First, they must establish fair procedures for the allocation of information technology resources. Second, those procedures must be perceived by the users as being equitable. Third, managers need to be aware of individuals with a propensity toward an ‘entitled’ sensitivity classification. Such individuals will be less tolerant of perceived inequalities of resource allocations than individuals classified as ‘benevolent’. The former individuals may be the most uncooperative in the development and implementation process when inequalities are perceived.

Because of the magnitude of the international investments in information hardware, software and services, and the limitations of the sample in this cross-cultural study, the factors impacting on global systems success need further investigation. In particular, the hybrid model presented in this study should be tested in other cultural environments in order to evaluate its usefulness outside North America.
