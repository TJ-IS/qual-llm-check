# B2B e-Commerce in emerging economies: i-metal.com's non-ferrous metals exchange in China

Paul S. Hempel $^{a,*}$ , Ying Ki Kwong $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Management, City University of Hong Kong, 83 Tat Chee Avenue, Kowloon, Hong kong, People's Republic of China $^{b}$ i-Metal.com Limited, Hong Kong

## Abstract

Existing examples of Business-to-Business (B2B) e-Commerce in the literature tend to be drawn from the experience in developed economies, and existing theories are also implicitly grounded in this context. Emerging economies present a significantly different business context, owing largely to their less developed financial, legal, and physical infrastructures. Emerging or non-western economies also have different business philosophies and cultures, which influence the relationship between companies and the marketplace. As an example of the challenges facing an electronic marketplace in an emerging economy, the experience of i-Metal.com Limited in setting up a non-ferrous metals exchange in China is examined in detail. It becomes apparent that setting up a successful B2B marketplace in emerging or non-western economies may require the creation of basic services that are taken for granted in developed economies, as well as requiring a careful consideration of the underlying business philosophy and culture. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Electronic commerce; Business-to-business; China; International business; i-Metal.com

It has become widely accepted that electronic commerce is transforming the state of business-to-business (B2B) transactions, it seems not so much a matter of whether organizations should explore how B2B will fit into their business operations, but rather when and under what conditions B2B will be appropriate. Currently, there are large numbers of publications, consultants, and conferences, all with the objective of providing aid to companies making the transition to e-Commerce based B2B. Despite the seemingly wide range of viewpoints and recommendations offered, there is one common element: recommendations are implicitly based upon developed (and Western) economies. In practice, we believe the implementation of e-Commerce outside of the developed Western economies cannot be borrowed directly from the Western experience. This view appears shared by others in the Asian business community. A recent conference in Hong Kong on e-Commerce in which presenters repeatedly draw upon American experiences has even been described as ‘the blind leading the bemused’ by an Asian commentator (Pang, 2000).

As both observers and participants of B2B e-Commerce within Asia, the authors have experienced difficulty in reconciling the recommendations emerging from the West with the business environment of Asia. At times, discussions concentrate upon issues that appear inappropriate or out-of-date due to rapid economic development. At other times, recommendations appear to be based on assumptions about business and culture that do not apply in Asia. As this paper will argue, we now believe this difficulty of applying Western best practice in e-Commerce to Asian economies is fundamental.

E-Commerce, especially Internet-based e-Commerce, is widely believed to be an important force in international business that transcends national borders. At the same time, the discussion of international business cannot be decoupled from geography and culture specific issues. At present, theories of global B2B e-Commerce that can help guide practice around the world are lacking. To this end, a case study of a Chinese B2B marketplace is presented that serves two objectives. The first objective is to show some of the particular difficulties faced when designing and implementing an Internet-based B2B exchange in an emerging, non-Western economy. The second objective is to use this example in order to suggest some of the research issues that need to be addressed so that truly global theories of e-Commerce can be developed. Before the case is presented, existing research into electronic marketplaces and Chinese business is reviewed. The case is then presented, followed by a discussion and conclusion section that raises the need to expand current theories of electronic marketplaces to cover emerging economies and attendant cultural differences.

## 1. A culture-bound B2B marketplace paradigm

The existing theorizing and cases on electronic marketplaces is primarily drawn from the Western context, where there are already mature legal, financial, and physical infrastructures. In this literature, there are a number of implicit assumptions on B2B marketplace design about the business environment that are primarily appropriate for developed economies. These assumptions are reflected in the choice of topics and examples that are discussed in the academic and management literature (e.g. Bakos, 1997, 1998; Kaplan and Sawhney, 2000), as well as in textbooks (e.g. Turban et al., 2000). Contrasting the existing literature on electronic commerce with the international management literature will help to motivate the importance of expanding current theories, especially where non-Western and emerging economies are concerned.

## 1.1. Theorization on electronic commerce

Early papers on electronic markets have adopted an economic view of markets, using transaction economics as the primary theoretical model. Bakos (1991) and Malone et al. (1987) both rely heavily upon the economics of transactions in theorizing about marketplaces. One specific prediction made is that as market costs decrease, the operation of markets would start to displace the role of managerial decisions in allocating the flow of materials. Note that this prediction is grounded solely upon the costs of transactions, and not on other basis (such as cultural values regarding business relationships and control). More recent theorization about electronic markets continues to emphasize the economic view, and explore the way in which reduced search costs change buyer and seller behaviors (Bakos, 1998).

Firms must compete differently within these new markets (de Figueiredo, 2000), and recent research topics have concentrated on ways firms can compete effectively as markets change. Research related to the operation of electronic markets, and to the firm-level effect of electronic markets, have concentrated upon a few specific topics: business alignment across market participants; the relationship between re-engineering and e-Commerce, and the role of intermediaries in markets.

The degree of alignment in business procedures between market participants is a key organizational level issue when adapting to electronic markets (Lindemann and Schmid, 1999). The close alignment of procedures is seen as necessary for the greatest reduction in costs. Supply chain integration is one means through which market participants can seek to reduce operational costs, not only through a reduction in transaction costs, but through a better management of the supply chain to reduce inventory and stocking costs.

A market based upon tight supply chain integration requires that members participate in non-casual ways. As Johnston and Mak (2000) point out, less sophisticated members of the supply chain pose a major impediment to complete integration. Fundamentally, supply chain integration presupposes that participants are willing to modify, or otherwise re-engineer, internal business processes. Also, supply chain integration presupposes that participants are willing to align business practices and share information with other market participants. The latter entails a fairly high degree of trust among participants (Lindemann and Schmid, 1999), as well as being influenced by supply chain politics (Johnston and Mak, 2000). Indeed, within developed economies, e-Commerce is frequently considered intertwined with business re-engineering and supply chain integration.

A final aspect of electronic markets that needs to be addressed is the role that intermediaries play in both traditional and electronic markets. It has long been assumed that electronic markets will lead to disinter mediation as buyers and sellers identify each other directly through the electronic market (Bakos, 1991). It is now becoming clear that as firms and traditional intermediaries adjust strategies to suit the new electronic market, in the long run reintermediation will occur (Chircu and Kauffman, 2000).

The existing literature on e-Commerce does not usually address the role of business philosophy and culture. Although not specifically referring to emerging economies or international business, Markus (2000) points out that terms such as Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) and Supply Chain Management (SCM) refer to both a philosophy and an application. Indeed, the existing literature on electronic marketplaces covers a set of applications (including tools and associated business process models) that reflect an implicit set of underlying business philosophies. Evolution in business philosophy leads to changes in business practices and, consequently, different ways in which business tools and applications are designed and used.

It is crucial to expand beyond statements like ‘With such sound underlying economics, electronic markets are not a fad. They are in fact inevitable.' (Malone et al., 1989, p. 170). More recent works have continued to concentrate on economic forces when examining electronic markets, but we believe it is also necessary to recognize that business relationships are influenced by values and culture. Since electronic markets tend to change the nature of business relationships between market participants, the role of business philosophies and cultural values underlying the user community cannot be ignored. To understand ways in which electronic commerce might vary globally, B2B e-Commerce will be reconsidered from the viewpoint of an emerging, non-Western economy, China.

## 1.2. Business and IT infrastructure in China

Understanding electronic marketplaces outside of the Western economies requires a consideration of the needs of emerging economies as well as an understanding of how culture influences business practices. Lu and Farrell (1990) have identified factors that influence information systems development in emerging countries, and these factors can be aggregated into two broad categories: infrastructure and business culture/practices. The importance of considering culture has also been emphasized by other researchers (e.g. Azad et al., 1998), although Korpela (1996) rejects a cultural explanation in favor of political economy that may vary across geographies.

China differs from the West both in the state of economic development and in national and business culture, and so provides a useful example in examining the effect of these factors on electronic marketplaces. Prior research has examined some of these issues within the Chinese context, but the pace of economic development in China is so rapid that much of what is known is quickly rendered outdated. Despite these limitations, the literature does provide some valuable insights.

Rosen (1999) provides an overview of five key impediments to electronic commerce in China: the lack of telecom facilities; privacy and security issues; the lack of a payment network; the lack of a legal infrastructure; and the lack of clear government plans. Rosen's (1999) highlighting of the lack of a government recognized digital certificate authority provides one example of how the rapid growth experienced by China renders published information quickly obsolete. Only months after the publication of the Rosen article, China enacted new contract law that provided recognition of digital certificates (Kennedy, 2000). A relatively poor telecommunications infrastructure in China has also been identified by others (Hou, 2001; Triolo and Lovelock, 1996), but it is important to realize that the rate of growth is tremendous. In 2000, the number of mobile-phone users doubled, to $7\%$ of the population, to become the world's second largest wireless communication market, while fixed telephone lines increased by $33\%$ (Asian Wall Street Journal, 2001, p. 3).

The telecommunications infrastructure is key to the development of electronic commerce, particularly with e-Commerce shifting from closed systems to the Internet, but simplistically relating Internet usage rates in the general population (c.f., Sprano and Zakak, 2000) with a country's suitability for electronic commerce overlooks many other important factors. As has been pointed out before (Rosen, 1999; Trappey and Trappey, 2001), other factors such as financial infrastructure are also important. A primary obstacle preventing the growth of e-Commerce in China has been ‘the lack of a well-functioning electronic payment system' (Schlevogt, 2000, p. 208), and the fact that ‘too few businesses use credit and too few customers hold credit cards’ (Trappey and Trappey, 2001, p. 203).

The development of secure on-line payment systems based on credit cards as used by most North American business-to-consumer (B2C) marketplaces has certainly not been trivial. However, the task have been considerably simplified by the existence of a well developed credit card payment authorization and billing infrastructure used by traditional retailers. In China, as in many other emerging economies, the development of an on-line payment system cannot take advantage of existing systems. This holds true for B2B marketplaces as well as for B2C marketplaces, since neither businesses nor banks are accustomed to the use of credit.

The creation of an electronic marketplace in China will, thus, require greater attention to infrastructure issues than would be true in developed Western economies. While B2C marketplaces are heavily constrained by the lack of consumer level access to the Internet, this is no longer true for B2B marketplaces. Internet access is now readily available and quite affordable for small and medium sizes enterprises in China (especially in the more affluent coastal provinces), although the low comfort level with computers among enterprise owners does result in low Internet use relative to the West. In respect to e-Commerce, business infrastructure issues have become more pressing than issues related to the development of technical infrastructure issues, and possibly even more difficult to change since it entails changing the way in which business is conducted. We will return to this point in greater detail later.

In addition to considering infrastructure related issues in China, which can apply in other emerging economies, it is necessary to take into consideration the underlying Chinese business philosophy and the way in which this influences business relationships. Lai et al. (2001) present an attempt to combine transaction economics with Chinese culture, and conclude that ‘at this point in time the e-market is less likely to be successfully deployed within Chinese organizations’ (p. 287). They conclude that a shift towards electronic markets would also require a shift in organizational culture, but do not provide specifics on the changes needed. Predicting specific changes needed requires closer attention to the existing body of research into Chinese business culture and relationships, management, and uses of technology.

Redding (1993) highlights a number of ways in which Chinese business relationships vary from Western practices. Key Chinese practices include: a lack of business process formalization; an emphasis upon flexibility and adaptability rather than on efficiency; a distrust of outsiders; and a patriarchal and authoritarian management style combined with a distrust of employees. These differences lead to marked differences in the way in which Chinese organizations use technology.

Chinese decision-making styles and business relationships influence the way in which information technology is used in the enterprise. Martinsons and Westwood (1997) explore the ways in which management information systems are used in Chinese businesses, and identify a number of ways in which information system use varies from Western uses. Chinese interpersonal communication tends to be highly dependent upon context (cf. Hall, 1976), and so Chinese businesses tend to find it more difficult to record communications in a form that can be placed into an information system. Also, since Chinese managers tend to rely upon more intuitive modes of decision-making, the collection of factual information is not emphasized, nor is the use of formal business planning. This lack of formal business planning also stems, in part, from a Chinese belief that it is better to maximize flexibility rather than to make a long-term commitment that is difficult to change. In sum, management information systems tend to be relied upon less heavily than in the West.

Martinsons and Hempel (1998) have noted that these cultural characteristics also influence re-engineering projects in China, leading to divergences from Western practices. The fact that Chinese re-engineering projects take on a unique form has important implications for the development of electronic markets, not only because internal re-engineering and electronic market use are interdependent (Johnston and Mak, 2000), but also because the creation of B2B marketplaces represents re-engineering at the market or supply chain level.

## 1.3. Electronic commerce in China: how it differs from the West

The infrastructure in China, as well as other emerging economies, can offer greater challenges than would be faced in developed economies. This has been noted to be a particular problem with B2C marketplaces, but is also an issue of concern with B2B marketplaces. However, infrastructure can be developed, and a greater challenge lies in addressing the ways in which business is conducted. Existing theories of electronic markets are grounded in economic theory, and do not give much consideration to culture or values. While consideration has been made of issues such as trust (Lindemann and Schmid, 1999) and supply chain politics (Johnston and Mak, 2000), culture and national models of business have not played a central role in electronic market theories.

The research in China has identified a number of ways in which Chinese practices differ from Western practices, and these differences have implications for electronic marketplace development and usage. Table 1 summarizes the main elements of the research into electronic markets and into Chinese business. Key sources of difference lie in the way in which information technology is used, as well as in the nature of business relationships. In particular, the Chinese tend to limit the amount and nature of information that is placed into an information system (Martinsons and Westwood, 1997), and to exhibit an unwillingness to share information (Martinsons and Westwood, 1997; Redding, 1993). Additionally, re-engineering is hindered by a lack of emphasis on formalization of business processes and communications (Martinsons and Hempel, 1998; Redding, 1993). All these factors will act as sources of resistance to tight supply chain integration provided through an electronic marketplace.

The amount of existing technical and business infrastructure in China differs from the West, as do Chinese business philosophy and business relationships. These differences influence both the use of information systems and the re-engineering of processes, and are thus likely to influence the development and use of electronic markets in China. In order to move beyond conceptual treatments of the topic, we will examine an actual example of electronic market development and use.

## 2. Research methodology

This paper presents a case study of a single firm that has set out to develop a Chinese B2B exchange. One of the authors is an executive at the company examined in this case, and has been involved since the inception of the business. The analysis of this case is based upon notes and recollections of the participant, as well as being based upon an analysis of internal company documents and records.

This case makes use of the participant-observation technique. This particular technique has both advantages and disadvantages. Advantages include access to information and groups that are not normally accessible to researchers, but a major disadvantage is the increased possibility of researcher bias (Yin, 1994). The company examined in this case is a closely held business, and given the secrecy norm for Chinese business noted by Redding (1993), the use of participant-observation offers access to the internal decision making processes that is extremely rare in the published literature on Chinese business. The disadvantage of potential researcher bias is also minimized due to the fact that the other author is an independent party, and not involved in the business being examined.

This study is based upon a single case, and the analysis is covered at a holistic level. The use of a single case can be justified in this situation, since it is a revelatory case (Yin, 1994). Revelatory cases are justified when a phenomenon previously unavailable to researchers becomes accessible, and given the rarity of internal access to Chinese businesses, as well as the recency of the emergence of Internet-based B2B, the case meets this standard of justification.

The case will be presented as a description of the actions taken in order to design and implement a B2B exchange in China. The statement of actions taken is supplemented with an accounting of the self-reported motivations underlying these actions. Once this statement of the facts of the case has been presented, the case is discussed in light of the prevailing (Western) literature on B2B e-Commerce.

## 3. The case

This paper examines the experiences of a company named i-Metal.com Limited (http://www.i-metal.com), which has set up a non-ferrous metals exchange primarily oriented to serving the non-ferrous metals industry in China. i-Metal was founded in March 2000 by Asia Aluminum Holdings Ltd., a publicly traded company on the Stock Exchange of Hong Kong. Based in Hong Kong with manufacturing operations in Nanhai in southern China, Asia Aluminum operates China's largest aluminum extrusion and fabrication business. The design of i-Metal's B2B marketplace leverages heavily on the vertical industry expertise of Asia Aluminum, before reviewing i-Metal's approach to designing a new marketplace, discussion will begin with the traditional market for non-ferrous metals in China.

## 3.1. The non-ferrous market in China

Aluminum is one of the most important non-ferrous metals globally. Pure forms of aluminum are commodities commanding high trading volumes and liquidity, and are traded extensively among metal producers, industrial users, traders, and financial industry players. A number of commodities exchange around the world support this market, with the London Metals Exchange (LME) being the largest and most important, while in China these commodities are traded on the Shanghai Futures Exchange (SHFE).

Between 1997 and 2000, price volatility for aluminum has been moderate by historical standards, but the price of aluminum futures has been known to fluctuate by 10% within hours (Penson, 1997). As a result of this volatility, large metal users typically hedge their physical market positions by buying futures contracts on one or more of the futures exchanges (Economist, 1998), while smaller users are more likely to turn to the spot market to meet their needs. In China, companies of all sizes in the non-ferrous metals industry have tended to rely heavily upon spot transactions. While China is a large producer of primary aluminum, China is a net importer of primary metals and scraps.

There are four important characteristics of the Chinese market for aluminum products: tremendous growth rates and potential; geographic concentration; producer decentralization and market fragmentation; and supply chain inefficiencies. Table 2 contains statistics that give an idea of the size of China's aluminum industry relative to the rest of the world. China is both a large supplier and consumer of aluminum, and the growth of the market tracks GDP growth. During the first eight months of 2000, imports of aluminum had increased by 62% from the previous year (Asiainfo, 2000). The per capita consumption figures indicate that there is tremendous growth potential as China continues to modernize and expand its economy. Table 2 also shows the amount of geographic concentration in the aluminum extrusion industry served by i-Metals marketplace. Nanhai, a city in Guangdong province near Hong Kong, is the largest center for the production of aluminum extrusions in China. Of the top 10 producers of extrusions in China, eight are located in Nanhai, and Nanhai is home to 380 out of 1000 extrusion companies in China.

The market is much less concentrated (more fragmented) than is typical in the West. In the US, 10 companies control 70% of the extrusion market, while proprietary research by Asia Aluminum indicates that in China the top 10 companies only control 30% of the market. Roughly 600 small companies account for more than half of the production of extrusions in China. The final characteristic of the Chinese market is that the market and supply chains are less efficient. One measure of efficiency is the number of intermediaries that take part in transactions. In China, the supply chain for standard commodities often involves multiple tiers. This is due, in large part, to the fact that many companies in the non-ferrous metals industry are small firms not in a position to consolidate procurement. It is this large pool of small to medium sized enterprises that makes the development of a B2B marketplace so attractive.

Asia Aluminum (the parent of i-Metal) occupies a prominent position in China's non-ferrous metals industry. The founder of Asia Aluminum is the chairman of the metal manufacturing association in Nanhai. Prior to founding Asia Aluminum, the founder and several senior managers of Asia Aluminum were traders of non-ferrous metals. Even now, Asia Aluminum occasionally sells excess inventory of metals and scraps to other companies in the Nanhai area. Since Asia Aluminum has traditionally provided a number of services to the Nanhai metal user community, a formal move into the B2B arena was viewed as a logical step by the executives of Asia Aluminum.

## 3.2. Designing the marketplace

It is against this background that Asia Aluminum set about developing an Internet-based non-ferrous metals exchange at the start of 2000. i-Metal was set up as a wholly-owned subsidiary of Asia Aluminum, and as is common in Chinese businesses, some executives at Asia Aluminum are also executives or advisors of i-Metal. Thus, key strategic decisions regarding i-Metal's marketplace design were guided by the parent, which has a great deal of experience with the non-ferrous metals industry in China. The user communities that i-Metal initially identified were the aluminum extrusion companies in southern China, which is reasonably compact and with business needs that are clearly known to i-Metal.

After working closely with an external consultant, the executives of i-Metal decided upon a comprehensive electronic marketplace. Rather than simply provide an on-line meeting place for potential suppliers or buyers, it was decided that a wide range of services to support the target business community would be offered. Table 3 shows the features that were selected for inclusion into the marketplace design. Collectively, these services constitute a comprehensive marketplace, and the executives indicate that they believed these offerings were necessary for i-Metal to succeed.

Decisions were also made as to what goals and functions not to pursue. The i-Metal executives did not set the goal of disintermediating the marketplace. Rather than cutting out existing middlemen, the i-Metal marketplace was designed to add new features and services to the existing marketplace. The provision of value-added services was identified as the key to revenue and growth opportunities rather than through disintermediation of the existing market. In any case, disintermediation was seen as unlikely, since the strong reliance on cash (spot) transactions entails a reliance on personal business relationships to minimize the chance of ‘bad debt’. $^{1}$ or substandard goods

Another important design decision was to create a neutral trading platform with enabling business services that provides for an arms-length relationship between participants, rather than to promote integration of supply chains among participants. There were a number of factors that supply chain integration was not selected as a business goal. First, potential Chinese users of the exchange, both large and small, tend to rely upon spot market transactions. While business networks do exist, they tend to be informal in nature, and it was felt that formalizing such relationships would not gain wide acceptance. Second, supply chain integration would require, as a precursor, the formalization of internal business processes, a situation that does not typically exist in member firms. For example, Asia Aluminum (i-Metal's parent) is the largest and most sophisticated extrusion manufacturer in China, but management still relies more heavily upon the chairman's intuition than upon formalized management processes. The executives of i-Metal believed that supply chain integration simply did not fit the way that firms within the industry conduct their businesses at present or in the foreseeable future.

## 3.3. Implementing the marketplace

The i-Metal executives identified the means for the on-line transfer of funds as a key element of the marketplace. In China, domestic funds transfers often involve currency, and personal or corporate checks are uncommon. When large transactions are needed, the banks will provide what is essentially a cashier's check. i-Metal found that while the financial services required were unavailable, the Chinese banks were willing to work with i-Metal to improve the range of services provided. So, i-Metal's first step was to work with banks to develop a system for members to more easily transfer funds on-line, since this real-time funds transfer system was to be a key to the trading processes underlying i-Metal's trading systems.

i-Metal worked with one of the four main Chinese banks to create a banking product called the Internet Commerce Debit Card, or the IC Card, and has been working with other banks to introduce similar products. In order to trade on i-Metal's trading platform, users will have to open an account with one of the partner banks, but this has not been a problem since most users already have an account with one of the partner banks. The use of the IC Card to support transactions enables the real-time on-line transfer of funds. In implementing this feature, underlying technological issues related to interfacing the web site's trading platforms with the banks' legacy systems presented a number of problems. The executives believe that the arrangements necessary for this interface work were only possible due to the prominence of Asia Aluminum within the Nanhai business community and nationwide.

Once it was possible to electronically transfer funds, i-Metal worked closely with futures brokerages trading on the SHFE to enable on-line trading, as well as electronic funds transfer between IC Card accounts and brokerage accounts held by i-Metal members. Three months of co-operation between i-Metal, the initial bank, the initial brokerage, and the SHFE were required to complete the integration of the various subsystems involved.

Before a futures contract is traded over the i-Metal exchange, the brokerage automatically examines the customer's accounts and determines whether funds are available to meet the initial margin call. If insufficient funds are available, the customer has the option of using the IC Card to electronically transfer funds into his account to meet the margin requirement, or to use other financial instruments in person. Once the margin call has been met, the brokerage forwards the order request electronically to the trading system of the SHFE, which is a real-time system. As an order is entered into the system, the amount of available fund in the brokerage account is updated immediately, and likewise for the IC Card account. Having real-time funds transfer system allows i-Metal users to quickly respond to changing market conditions, which may lead to variation margin calls. This Internet-based transaction process is distributed over a wide area network, with i-Metal members located virtually anywhere, i-Metal's servers located in Hong Kong, the brokerage houses and bank gateway servers located in Nanhai or elsewhere in China, and the commodity exchange located in Shanghai.

The executives at i-Metal indicated that developing the on-line system for the spot trading of physical aluminum, primarily in the form of pure ingots, presented even greater challenges. With futures trading, there is already a well-developed exchange in Shanghai with attendant trading rules and policies under the scrutiny of government regulators. With spot trading, an on-line trading process for physical goods had to be developed from scratch. The executives understand that market volatility of non-ferrous metals entails significant challenges in ensuring non-repudiation of on-line transactions. Legal remedies are considered difficult and, in general, impractical. After consulting with experts from the Shanghai Financial University (who also played a key consultative role in the creation of the trading rules for the SHFE), i-Metal decided to require a deposit equal to 10% of the transaction amount in the form of an account hold, or freeze, on available funds for both the buyer and the seller. This freeze of funds is to be released when both counter parties acknowledge successful delivery and acceptance of the physical metals in the transaction. The rationale given by i-Metal was that, due to the potentially rapid fluctuations in market prices, the on-line deposit system ensures confidence in transactions and guarantees ‘performance’ for both counter parties.

Requiring deposits on transactions proved to be technically more challenging than anticipated, since the Chinese banks did not initially permit an on-line hold of funds in a bank account. The initial bank proposed that the deposit requirements be effected by transferring funds from the user's IC card accounts to a neutral account administered by i-Metal. However, the possession of funds (even if temporary) was a role that i-Metal did not want to assume, due to i-Metal's stated objective of creating a neutral marketplace. After discussions with i-Metal and subsequent consultation with headquarters in Beijing, the bank agreed to implement internal procedures necessary to freeze a portion of the user's account equal to the amount of the required deposit.

## 3.4. Continuing development of the marketplace

At the time this case was written, i-Metal had just implemented the system for trading physical metals. The next market segment to be implemented will be the trading of extrusions, which will take two primary forms. Standardized extrusions, as well as custom extrusions, will be traded through the use of on-line catalogues and a request-for-quotations (RFQ) mechanism. Once this is completed, i-Metal will have developed a vertical B2B marketplace, covering trades for futures contracts, spot ingots, and extrusion products. Initially, the majority of the transactions is expected to be domestic, but the Chinese non-ferrous metals market is internationalized enough that i-Metal's marketplace will also involve international transactions.

Another issue regarding the financial infrastructure in China that i-Metal has been working on involves the use of warehouse documents as collateral for short-term business loans (inventory financing). The executives of i-Metal are aware that in the West, receipts or warrants for inventory stored in public warehouses are considered negotiable instruments, and can be used as collateral for bank loans or lines of credit. Unfortunately, in China, the notion of collateral financing is not yet widespread in commodities trading, and banks do not typically accept warehouse documents as collateral. Thus, i-Metal has been working with the banks toward introducing this concept. If successful, this will allow more flexible financial operations for industry players, as well as promoting further liquidity in the market. At the time this case was written, it appears that it will be possible to use these warehouse documents as collateral for financing if they are SHFE certified documents. Chinese banks, not yet comfortable with this form of documentary collateral, seek assurance that the collateral offered could be readily liquidated in the case of default.

In international business, fluctuations in factor prices and exchange rates provides strong incentives to adopt sophisticated hedging strategies, and the use of hedging strategies is covered in any MBA program. Unfortunately, managers with formal management training are rare in this industry in China. Since many of the companies in the i-Metal user community are small, most do not have a systematic hedging strategy. In fact, the i-Metal executives indicate that most owners of small companies presently associate futures trading with speculation, rather than risk management. In order to promote the use of hedging strategies, i-Metal has scheduled articles reviewing the use of hedging strategies in its members-only portion of the web site, as well as in its quarterly print magazine. i-Metal also plans to incorporate hedging analysis and management tools into the marketplace as market needs arise.

## 4. Discussion of the i-Metal experience

One aspect of this case that may have caught the reader's attention is that the case concentrated primarily on topics related to the business infrastructure, such as hedging strategies, warehousing, holds on bank accounts, and funds transfer. More typically, research on B2B might concentrate on topics such as trading models (cf., Kaplan and Sawhney, 2000) or supply chain integration (e.g. Johnston and Mak, 2000), but China's status as an emerging economy means that considerable attention must be paid to these more fundamental business infrastructure issues. There is also ample evidence in the case that suggests cultural factors, indeed, play an important role in marketplace development.

## 4.1. Business infrastructure

China has made tremendous advances in developing a thriving market economy, but much of the market and regulatory infrastructure taken for granted in the West is not necessarily present yet. The experience of i-Metal suggests that Chinese institutions are actively improving the infrastructure. It may have been reasonable for i-Metal to side step certain challenges and to focus more narrowly on creating a B2B marketplace that serves the roles of information provider, business directory, and bulletin board. Indeed, in emerging economies like China, these are valuable services to provide, but i-Metal has chosen a broader vision.

The role that i-Metal sees itself playing is that of a comprehensive supplier of business services for the vertical industry targeted, rather than purely that of a trading platform. The decision to include brokerages, logistics providers, and warehousing partners in the marketplace design is a direct result of this vision. The inclusion of futures trading support in the marketplace design provides a useful insight into the long-term role that i-Metal sees itself playing in aiding the non-ferrous metals community in China.

In designing its marketplace, i-Metal has chosen to integrate various services provided by different external (third party) intermediaries. Thus, a large part of the value that i-Metal's marketplace provides is the integration of services. The intention was not for i-Metal to displace the existing middlemen (i.e. disintermediate the market), but rather to improve market efficiency by allowing better integration of services provided by i-Metal's strategic partners (i.e. banking, warehousing, shipping, etc.). In so doing, one anticipated consequence is that the value-added by middlemen and the associated costs will become more transparent to market participants.

i-Metal's efforts should ultimately yield industry wide improvements. Consider the work behind: developing the IC Card product; encouraging banks to develop the online capability to freeze funds in accounts; getting banks to accept warehouse documents as negotiable; and promoting the acceptance of collateral loans. In all these cases, once the banking community has accepted these changes, the entire business community will benefit, including, of course, i-Metal's membership community. Even knowing that i-Metal may not be the exclusive beneficiary, i-Metal has worked on these projects as part of its business development efforts. There are two reasons for this. First, i-Metal believes that the first mover advantage it enjoys, combined with Asia Aluminum's status within the Chinese non-ferrous metals community, would establish for itself a secure market position. Second, and perhaps more importantly, these reforms are necessary in order to provide i-Metal's marketplace with the desired functionality, and to meet the objective of promoting market liquidity.

If i-Metal had not taken a lead in promoting these changes, the only feasible marketplace design would have been information and messaging services. Given the less developed business infrastructure in China, these services alone would have limited value in promoting liquidity of China's non-ferrous metals market. Sculley and Woods (1999) point out that one of the primary objectives of any marketplace, and particularly a B2B electronic marketplace, is to promote liquidity and market transparency, and i-Metal aims to increase both. More generally, while a messaging and directory service would be of definite value in an emerging economy, the provision of a full-service electronic marketplace would add much higher value.

i-Metal faces a potential uncertainty: government regulation. Many of the reforms enacted or being enacted with business partners were brought about through changes in administrative rules. It is possible, although impossible to predict, that as a part of the continuing economic reform, the government will choose to promulgate national regulations or laws that will influence these administrative rules. i-Metal has worked with national-level partners in developing support services for the marketplace, and as the first mover in designing these services, i-Metal and its partners will help serve as a reference model for future national standards or policy reforms. i-Metal has also deliberately chosen to design these support services in alignment with international practices in order to prepare this marketplace for China's entry into the World Trade Organization (WTO).

## 4.2. Business philosophies

The marketplace design that i-Metal has chosen reflects the business philosophy and management culture in China. The marketplace was designed to provide needed business services, while at the same time i-Metal executives decided to forego features that did not fit into the Chinese management culture. This decision is consistent with Markus (2000) assertion that underlying business philosophies affect the development and the deployment of business tools used in electronic commerce. i-Metal services were provided through a neutral trading platform that were intended to supplement, rather than replace, the traditional marketplace. The marketplace did not seek to promote supply chain integration, since i-Metal executives believe the underlying requirements for business process formalization and the target user community would reject the sharing of business information.

Lai et al. (2001) conclude that “the e-market is less likely to be successfully deployed within Chinese organizations” (p. 287), due in large part to the fact that in an electronic market, buyers don’t know if they can trust the sellers. This is perhaps an overly broad generalization, since there is ways to overcome this lack of trust, especially for commodities with widely accepted quality standards. i-Metal has incorporated various approaches to enhance or, in fact, to enforce trust. By partnering with licensed brokerages that are already members of SHFE, i-Metal members may trade in compliance with existing Chinese laws and regulations. Another approach was the deposit requirements for spot transactions. These and other approaches serve to promote the integrity of the underlying trading processes. In essence, i-Metal takes an active role in regulating the conduct of the user community.

The lack of business formalization and reluctance to share information helped drive the i-Metal decision to avoid integration with users' back-office operations. One particular example worth mentioning is that supply chain integration is hindered by the fact that the adoption of ERP or, for that matter, networked information technology systems, is extremely rare in the non-ferrous metals industry in China. In part, this is due to the small size of many enterprises, but there also appears to be a resistance on the part of Chinese managers to adopt these systems. This resistance stems from the fact that the small, entrepreneurial Chinese businesses have traditionally concentrated on adaptability and flexibility, rather than the formalization of business processes. Chinese managers that i-Metal has spoken with about this topic tend to believe the perceived rigidity imposed by ERP implementations does not fit well with the way they run their businesses. In addition, as noted in the case, few businesses within the industry have formalized business operations to the degree necessary for successful ERP implementation. Given the present low levels of interest in ERP systems and the prevailing business culture of secrecy and control (Martinsons and Westwood, 1997), the possibility of promoting a high degree of supply chain integration seems remote in the near term.

The lack of formalization in business processes is accompanied by a lack of formalization in business relationships. In the traditional market in China, even when individual companies rely primarily upon a single supplier, they would rarely formalize the relationship through contracts. Instead, these companies would treat the preferred supplier like participants of a spot market, in large part as a means of maintaining the possibility for flexibility, as well as maintaining relationships so as to avoid ‘bad debt’. Positioning the i-Metal marketplace as a complete procurement system would represent a fundamental change in the nature of business relationships. The management of i-Metal believes this type of e-Commerce model, as presently understood and applied in the West, is not consistent with the business philosophy prevalent among Chinese private enterprises, both within the non-ferrous metals industry and in general.

In many ways, the emergence of electronic markets that emphasize tight supply chain integration, with attendant increases in information sharing, is a particularly Western response to the desire of reducing transaction costs. It reflects a particular philosophy toward business relationships among market participants in the West, which is manifestly different from that of their Asian counterparts.

## 5. Globalizing B2B theory and practice

The i-Metal case serves to highlight the importance of considering electronic marketplaces from a more international perspective. The example offered shows that both the state of economic development and cultural values influence prevailing business practices, and that the development of electronic markets should take these factors into account. While some of the issues raised by this case might only require minor adjustments in the way we think about electronic markets, other issues may require fundamental rethinking. The issues that deserve greater recognition and research include the following:

\- Rapid economic development may cause accepted ideas and paradigms regarding an environment to become outdated rapidly.

\- Business infrastructure, as well as technical infrastructure, are necessary precursors to electronic marketplaces, and business infrastructure reflects both the state of economic development and national business practice norms.

\- Culture influences the use of technology, and differences in technology use will place constraints on the way in which electronic markets are developed and used.

\- Theories about electronic marketplaces must be based on culture and the nature of business relationships, in addition to transaction economics.

The case shows how quickly the pace of economic development in China is making what we know about China obsolete. In just a few years, the telecommunications infrastructure has grown dramatically. While it is still true that telecommunications infrastructure is not well developed enough for most B2C applications, the development of B2B applications is not appreciably constrained by technological considerations any more, at least for the coastal provinces where e-Commerce activities concentrate. In fact, in some respects, the technological infrastructure (e.g. wireless communication) might be superior to that of developed economies simply because the infrastructure in an emerging economy is newer and unencumbered by legacy issues. Besides technical infrastructure, the government is also working to extend legal and regulatory frameworks in a rapid pace. In a developing economy, what we knew last year might be well outdated this year.

This case also serves to highlight the fact that business infrastructure can be a more serious constraint than technical infrastructure. The IS community might traditionally concentrate on technology and its ramifications, but it must also keep in mind that electronic commerce is fundamentally a business application and, thus, requires careful considerations of business issues and their effect on technology use. Within emerging economies, business infrastructure might be lacking in ways that are taken for granted in the West. The electronic marketplace developer can choose to accept these limitations, and thus limit the scope of services offered, or the marketplace can choose to improve the business infrastructure as i-Metal has. Changing or improving business infrastructure is also much more difficult than changing technical infrastructure. This is because business infrastructure is tightly connected with the nature of business relationships, business practices, legal considerations, as well as organizational, local, and regional politics.

A related observation is that the value propositions of electronic marketplaces can differ between developed and emerging economies. When the business infrastructure is already well developed, and when business re-engineering has already been undertaken, an electronic marketplace must contribute toward surpassing a level of efficiency in the traditional marketplace which may already be quite efficient by the standards of emerging economies. By contrast, in emerging economies, an electronic marketplace can add significant levels of efficiency through the provision of basic business services lacking in the traditional marketplace. Researchers outside the developed Western economies need to make greater recognition of the extent to which available business infrastructure influences electronic marketplace development. Also, the ability of electronic marketplaces (indeed any marketplace) to deliver key business services should factor prominently into the assessment of their ultimate value to the industries they serve.

Electronic markets development has a heavy technology component, but the use of technology is ultimately influenced by business culture (Azad et al., 1998; Lu and Farrell, 1990; Martinsons and Westwood, 1997). The connection between electronic markets and the way in which technology is used has been subjected to limited examination, but this is a critical topic toward a comprehensive understanding of international e-Commerce. For example, an information system can be used for tactical management of transactions and associated records in a firm, or it can be leveraged as a strategic decision making tool. More sophisticated use of information systems in a firm would engender more sophisticated expectations of the electronic marketplace. Given that culture influences the way in which technology is used, culture will directly or indirectly influence the way in which electronic markets are used by participating firms of a marketplace.

As a final point, existing theories regarding electronic markets are grounded in transaction economics, which is assumed to be globally valid in scope. There seems to be an implicit acceptance of the proposition that economic rationality, as exemplified in a firm's desire to minimize procurement and other operational expenses, would overwhelm other considerations and ultimately lead to similar business solutions and practices throughout the world. This paper offers the alternative proposition that cultural factors will give rise to differences in electronic market development and their use in different geographies. The i-Metal example suggests that it would be useful to examine decision-making style (intuitive vs. rational), business process formalization (informal vs. formal), and supply chain integration (loose vs. tight) of the participants in the vertical industry being served.

Electronic markets are undoubtedly a ‘less personal’ way of conducting business. Many electronic market trading platforms, including those of i-Metal, enforce anonymity of market participants during the trading process. Another interesting area of research would be to examine how the reliance upon personal, rather than legal relationships, in different industries or parts of the world influences electronic market development.

Prevailing descriptions and prescriptions for electronic markets embody many implicit assumptions on the nature of business relationships from a decidedly Western perspective. Documented best practices from the West, therefore, cannot be directly applied in emerging economies without careful considerations. Likewise, the broader implications of electronic marketplaces on the long-range development of specific industries cannot be understood without including geography-specific and culture-specific considerations.

## 6. Conclusion

The creation of an electronic marketplace to service B2B transactions in an emerging economy presents a number of challenges that are not present in a developed economy. If a B2B marketplace is to provide business services beyond information and business directories, then the designers of the B2B marketplace may need to provide a degree of comprehensiveness in business support services that is less common in developed economies. This need for comprehensiveness is driven by the fact that the traditional marketplace in emerging economies typically is lacking basic business services available in developed economies.

While it has been recognized that culture influences the application of information technology (Azad et al., 1998; Martinsons and Westwood, 1997), existing theories on electronic markets paid scant attention to this literature. Research to date has concentrated on economics and technological infrastructure, which, in our view, are necessary but not sufficient. This case highlights the importance of considering business infrastructure and culture on electronic marketplace development.

The prevailing B2B paradigm in the West is implicitly based upon a business philosophy that may not readily translate into other cultures. In the Chinese example studied, a business climate characterized by a lack of process formalization and a strategic emphasis on flexibility led to a marketplace design where the members of the user community remain at arm's length. Relative to the West, B2B marketplaces are likely to be more difficult to design and to implement in China and in other emerging economies. At the same time, there exists a greater opportunity for improvement over existing practices of the traditional marketplace. Thus, an important contribution of an electronic marketplace in an emerging economy may be the enhancement of basic market infrastructure, in addition to the expected efficiency gain at the firm level. In fact, concentrating upon marketplace efficiency is likely to drive improvement in internal efficiency, as firms respond to the opportunities enabled by the new—likely more efficient—marketplace.
