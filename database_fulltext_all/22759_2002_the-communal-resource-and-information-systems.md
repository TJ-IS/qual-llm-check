# Viewpoint Paper

# The communal resource and information systems

Georg von Krogh<sup>\*</sup>

University of St Gallen, Dufourstrasse 48, CH 9010 St Gallen, Switzerland

Received 1 July 2001; revised paper accepted 1 April 2002

## Abstract

This paper discusses the problem of knowledge sharing in organizations and proposes a concept ‘communal resource’ to overcome this problem. Communal resources rely on opportunities for knowledge sharing and social norms. Implications for information systems are discussed. q 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Knowledge management; Knowledge sharing; Community; Social norms; Information systems

Knowledge management<sup>1</sup> research is grounded in various fields, such as organization behavior, organization theory, strategic management, information systems, technology and innovation management, marketing, and in the fundamental disciplines of economics, psychology, and sociology. Common to most of the contributions is their intent to demonstrate the power that knowledge has for explaining and predicting behavior, at the level of societies and economies (Stehr, 1994), industries (Arthur, 1997), firms and institutions (Nelson and Winter, 1982; Cohen and Levinthal, 1990; Kogut and Zander, 1992; Grant, 1996; Spender, 1996), networks (Dyer and Nobeoka, 2000), groups (Osterloh and Frey, 2000), and individuals (Czikszentmihalyi, 1988).

The way the knowledge construct has evolved, since the cognitive revolution in the 1950’s, it now bridges the chasm between cognition and action (von Krogh et al., 1994; Varela et al., 1991), and it integrates the individual and the collective levels of analysis (Nonaka, 1994). Although many definitions of knowledge are available in the literature, three characteristics of knowledge seem to emerge as central to theory building and research (Venzin et al., 1998). First, knowledge is ‘justified true belief’, meaning that an individual justifies the truthfulness of his or her observations based on observations of the world. This process depends on a unique viewpoint, personal sensibility, and individual experience (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995). Second, knowledge enables this individual to act in a situation, for example, resolving a task or responding to unforeseen challenges of work (Stehr, 1992, 1994). Third, knowledge is both explicit and tacit (Nonaka, 1994; Polanyi, 1958). Some knowledge can be put on paper, formulated in sentences, or captured in drawings. Yet, other kinds of knowledge are tied to the senses, movement skills, physical experiences, rules of thumb, and intuition (Merleau-Ponty, 1995). As a general rule, although sharing it might be rewarding, such tacit knowledge can be costly to describe to others (Grant, 1996).

Equipped with a construct of knowledge applied across a variety of empirical settings, the central quest for knowledge management researchers became performance enhancement through organizational enabling conditions, processes of knowledge capture/location, sharing, and creation, and information and communication technology tools (Kluege et al., 2001). The research opportunities on knowledge management are still vast, but in order to progress fruitfully, we need to pay more attention to one of the core-challenges that bridge the chasm between the individual and collective level, namely why and under what conditions people in organizations share knowledge. Commentators on knowledge management suggest this problem is yet to be fully understood (Dixon, 2000; Wenger et al., 2002). In this short essay, I will elucidate the issue of knowledge sharing through a collective action framework, and discuss the communal resource as a solution to this problem, and finally how information systems might impact on the development of communal resources.<sup>2</sup>

## 1. Knowledge sharing, interests, and agency

Underpinning knowledge management research is a basic idea that knowledge can be shared (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995; Szulanski, 1996).<sup>3</sup> Performance in various parts of the organization can be enhanced, when people communicate information, best practices, insights, experiences, tastes, lesson learned, as well as common and uncommon sense. Given the emerging construct of knowledge, knowledge sharing implies that individuals mutually adjust their beliefs and actions through more or less intense interaction. However, as mentioned, knowledge sharing can be costly (especially for tacit knowledge), and because performance enhancement is of interest, the nature of the process deserves attention. Often apprentice training is used as an example of tacit knowledge sharing, and it can be shown that this process entails as much self-observation, reflection, and indwelling in the routines of the master, as it does observation and imitative learning by the apprentice of the master (Polanyi and Prosch, 1975). Various authors have also argued that apprentice training involves considerable learning on part of the master, indicating a two-directional sharing rather than one-directional transfer (Perkins, 1981; Cohen, 1985). In effect, some authors claim that good apprenticeship training can only be achieved, when the routines of the master include the observation and reflection that comes with training (Dreyfus and Dreyfus, 1986).

However, if knowledge sharing is two-directional, it could be construed as a sequenced collective action problem. In its simplest form, for sharing to occur there must be a giver, e.g. master, and receiver or ‘recreator’ of knowledge, e.g. apprentice, and a sequence of activities. The giver must be interested in giving knowledge, and the receiver must be interested in applying the knowledge. In both cases, cognition and action change. The receiver turns into a giver, who, through combing the new knowledge with gained experiences, awards knowledge to the giver, who in turn becomes the receiver. The collective aspect of this process becomes clearer, if we compare it to individual learning: as Merleau-Ponty (1995) remarked, individual learning often aims at producing a ‘maximumgrip’ in a situation. Working in isolation, individuals direct their perception and position themselves in relation to a task in such a way that they can optimally control the task execution and performance. This also involves ignoring the presence of others during task performance. However, in a master–apprentice relationship, the ‘other’ becomes an integral part of cognition and action, by knowing that there is a master or apprentice observing, commenting, helping, or maybe even ignoring the other who performs tasks.

Had it not been for the variation in individual interest throughout an organization, knowledge sharing would have been a straightforward activity without much shirking, guile or resistance (Collard, 1978). A definition of interests is a specific motivational focus that determines, in a specific context, a person’s definition of the situation and her particular objective, purpose, or other guidelines of conduct (Schutz, 1970:319).<sup>4</sup> Individual interest influences the individual’s learning in a specific situation (Bruner,

1979). Such interests are varied and manifold and tightly connected to areas of tacit and explicit knowledge, history of action, and task performance (von Krogh et al., 2000). Throughout human history, we have seen how knowledge and interest are intertwined. For example, Bruno Latour’s work on how science proceeds as a collective enterprise, show that there are diverse and distributed interests behind knowledge creation (Latour, 1987, 1993). Since people invest in learning and creating a body of knowledge, this naturally affects their interests. At stake might be their reputation and ability to appropriate returns from those investments. Carlile (2002) has shown that people’s long-term investments in areas of expertise, for example, in engineering disciplines, make them reluctant to share knowledge with representatives from other areas, and they tend to be very conscious of ‘boundaries’ and diverse interests, which separate their work practices from those of other disciplines. Moreover, Darrah’s (1995) ethnography of a high-tech company showed that fearing the loss of authority, manufacturing engineers, who possessed full information about the product design and manufacturing specifications, were reluctant to share this with front line workers. As a negative result, the firm’s attempt at lowering quality costs in manufacturing by training led to more frustration than skill enhancement among the workers. These studies, that pay explicit attention to diverse and distributed interests in organizations, conclude that knowledge sharing is a fragile and uncertain activity.<sup>5</sup>

Organizations create opportunities for people to realize their individual interests through learning, whereby the organization’s system of tasks allows individuals to invest in learning and build expertise, that is, considered more or less costly, valuable, and legitimate. A theory that explains why and under what conditions the collective act of knowledge sharing occurs in organizations must therefore take into account a variety of interest and personality traits including self-interest and individuals, who seek to maximize their own utility (Ostrom, 2000).<sup>6</sup> Given the sequencing of activities and selfinterest, free riding on other’s knowledge is also of some concern (Olson, 1965). A free rider chooses to receive a higher payoff for a socially detecting choice than for a cooperative choice, even though all contributors will get a higher payoff if they all cooperate (Olson, 1965; Hardin, 1968; Mannix, 1991). Free riding on knowledge means that one individual learns, while hiding the learning process from the one, who bestows the knowledge. Under certain circumstances, the free rider will be able to obtain benefits from the knowledge that are equal to those a contributor can obtain. Hence, if someone gives knowledge, why should someone else be interested in reciprocating?

In general, organizations solve collective action problems by agency,<sup>7</sup> such as management, enforcing a regime of cooperation, while being exogenous to the process (McCaffrey et al., 1995). The agency can counter the problem of knowledge sharing in two ways. First, it can ascertain an increasing number of points of contacts between organization members (Kransdorf, 1998). Examples of this include problem solving groups and task forces (Grant, 1996), knowledge and technology transfer units that instigate, direct and oversee knowledge sharing (O’Dell and Grayson, 1998; Dixon, 2000; von Krogh et al., 2000), a multi-layered organization structure, with a business system layer that covers the main tasks and business processes of the firm, a project layer, where people collaborate to create new knowledge and innovation, and a knowledge-base layer, which comprises the memory of the organization (e.g. positions, data-bases, organization culture, etc.) (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995), or a matrix structure in which people share knowledge with a regional focus, as well as functional product/technology, or industry focus (Doz and Prahalad, 1981). Second, regardless of the structural solution chosen, local individual interests must be reflected in the organizational agenda, and an effective human resource management must be put in place (Govindarajan and Gupta, 2001). This resonates with the conclusions in much of the knowledge management literature. Because human interest and tacit knowledge are often involved, simply increasing people’s exposure to functions, projects, and knowledge, and to other people, does not safeguard the collective learning in the firm (Boisot, 1998; Grant, 1996). Well-functioning human resource management including incentive systems is imperative, too. Management can create monetary or career incentives for individual organizational members, and conduct employee performance appraisal that take into account people’s propensity for sharing knowledge (Pedler et al., 1991; Grant, 1996; Davenport and Prusak, 1998; Davenport and Probst, 2000; Kroenig, 2001).

In those instances, where tacit knowledge cannot be codified, sharing it between people is slow, costly, and uncertain (Kogut and Zander, 1992; Grant, 1996), and naturally there are limits to what the agency can accomplish with incentive systems. Osterloh and Frey (2000) discussed whether self-interested individuals are motivated to share knowledge extrinsically, through material incentives, such as money, or intrinsically, through the actual activity itself. Intrinsically motivated individuals act for their immediate need satisfaction. If knowledge to be shared is tacit, the role of intrinsic motivation outweighs the role of extrinsic motivation, since no material incentive can change people’s interest to codify and share tacit knowledge, and because no contract can assure effective and efficient knowledge sharing. Because there is no way for team members to monitor the sharing behavior of other team members, the ‘production team’ solution breaks down as well (Alchian and Demsetz, 1972). If monitoring and sanctioning of possible free-riding behavior is uncertain for team members, it is even more uncertain for the exogenous agency that must base disciplinary action on retrieved information about individual team members’ performance. Hence, intrinsic motivation, by which team members realize immediate need satisfaction by working together with others in order to solve complex tasks, is a prerequisite for (tacit) knowledge sharing.

Therefore, if an organization is populated, perhaps not by altruists, but by a stable group of intrinsically motivated experts, and has at its disposal a human resource management system that always provides new learning challenges, the problem of knowledge sharing might be resolved. However, intrinsic motivation might be contingent on many factors that may or may not be present in the context of work. One such contingency relates to cadence. Immediate need satisfaction might be a strong intrinsic motivator for experts, but the sequencing of collective activities involved in knowledge sharing depends on the type of knowledge to be shared, as well as other individuals’ rates of learning. For example, people differ strongly in their rate of acquiring tacit knowledge (Shanks and Johnstone, 1998), and as a result immediate need satisfaction might fail to evoke or change individual interests. Moreover, since tacit knowledge is associated with both physiological and cognitive structures (Merleau-Ponty, 1995), people may differ strongly in their capacity to act even after a long period of learning. Years may go by before the master sees her apprentice’s task performance come within reach of her own.

## 2. The communal resource for knowledge sharing

The term ‘community’ can be derived from a classical sociological premise that people form social bonds through shared norms, traditions, identity, and solidarity. Historically, the concept of community stands in contrast to society, where competition, individualism, and self-interest rule (Toennies, 1877; Weber, 1978; Durkheim, 1893). Communities have their own languages, rituals, norms, and values that are developed and refined over a long period of time.<sup>8</sup> By intense and sustained communication, members develop a shared sense of identity. Interestingly, this deep sense of identity, tradition, solidarity, and longstanding norms can make a binding commitment among community members to mobilize large-scale social changes. If people experience grievances or entrepreneurs see opportunities for action, feelings of solidarity can bind people together in collective action (Cohen, 1985; Calhoun, 1986, 1994; Fantasia, 1988). Building on a long tradition in sociology, anthropology, and economics, a growing number of authors have focused on the role of communities in knowledge sharing, including peasant communities (Foster, 1965), occupational communities (Van Maanen and Barley, 1984; Orr, 1990a), virtual communities (Rheingold, 1993; Castells, 1996) ‘imagined communities’ where people ‘imagine oneness’ with others they have never met or seen (Anderson, 1983; Calhoun, 1991), micro-communities for knowledge sharing and creation (von Krogh et al., 2000), and communities of work practice (Lave, 1988; Orr, 1990b; Brown and Duguid, 1991; Wenger, 1998; Wenger and Snyder, 2000).

A number of characteristics make communities particularly interesting for the problem of knowledge sharing. They exist outside the formal organization (Bennis et al., 1958), they are not regulated by formal structure and incentive mechanisms, control, and sanctions (Wenger, 1998), and they attract people around common tasks, work, knowledge and experience, as well as affect and empathy (Van Maanen and Barley, 1984). A community has a stability of affiliation over time,<sup>9</sup> it has multiplex and direct relations between the actors (Taylor and Singleton, 1993), and there is some level of common information (or cues) about the knowledge of the other members. However, because of the contingent nature of intrinsic motivation, not all affiliates’ needs are immediately satisfied in knowledge sharing. Nevertheless, the community could be a resource for this process, because the satisfaction of needs is suspended and therefore the natural sequence of activities well preserved (von Krogh, 2002).<sup>10</sup> I will come back to this in a moment.

Basing their argument on Coase (1960), Taylor and Singleton (1993) suggest that in the case where the community displays a high degree of collective interests, it can lower social (transaction) costs. First, affiliates of a community have access to information (cues) about others’ knowledge and the community reduces the affiliate’s costs of searching for knowledgeable people in the organization. Second, bargaining costs are lower in a community than outside it, because beliefs, preferences, and interests are shared among members. Stable membership makes it easier to conclude agreements among individuals, and relations on many fronts make it easier to make trade-offs that compensate for differences in cooperative gains between the parties. Third, the level of shared interests, the expectations of continued interaction, and the direct and manifold relations among individuals all reduce monitoring costs involved in enforcement of compliance in knowledge sharing. Enduring relationships allow affiliates to seek out with whom to share knowledge on a stable basis, and based on direct experience, affiliates are likely to share their ‘best’ knowledge with other cooperative individuals.

However, as research has shown, interests in organizations are very difficult to make meaningful, stable, and valid, and they are subject to constant bargaining and drift (March, 1962; Devine, 1999). Most likely communities are comprised of affiliates who are subject to changing demands and conditions of work. An affiliate may be faced with the doublebind of not being a responsible affiliate on the one hand, and not being a self-interested individual on the other (Hardin, 1968). In organizations, people are rewarded not only for sharing knowledge in communities, but also for being self-interested, rational, and free riding, in order to advance their career and improve their monetary position (Fife, 1977; McCay and Jentoft, 1998). In organizations, where communities for knowledge sharing are explicitly fostered, supported by top management, and endowed with slack resources, this double-bind could be potentially powerful. A community will be comprised of people, who may have contingent, diverse and distributed interests. In effect, I propose that the community should not be defined by its level of shared interests, but rather by its evolutionary stability. It follows that the community is not a communal resource per se. If stable and collective interests cannot be assumed ex ante this does not exclude stable, multiplex, and direct relations among affiliates. Rather, depending on the context of community interactions, a person’s definition of a situation, personal objectives, and guidelines for conduct, evolve over time.<sup>11</sup> Communities can be resources and perhaps even ‘liabilities’, and we need to examine the conditions necessary for communities to serve as communal resources for knowledge sharing. 12 At least three factors should positively impact on the effectiveness of knowledge sharing in a community: opportunity structures, care, and authenticity (von Krogh, 2002).

## 2.1. Opportunity structures

The opportunity structure refers to the occasion and benefits of sharing knowledge in the community. Since interest and knowledge are intimately connected, and since it takes more effort to identify sharing possibilities if affiliates have diverse interests (irrespective of community size), the opportunity structure of a community is a particularly important factor for the problem of knowledge sharing. Opportunities present themselves to affiliates in a manner that structures relationships for sharing within the community. These structures of relationships hinge on factors, such as individual and collective benefits from knowledge sharing, the cost of knowledge sharing, the sequence of knowledge sharing activity, the history of sharing activities in the community, the search for sharing opportunities, the diverse and distributed interests among affiliates, the diverse knowledge and experience of affiliates and bargaining behavior. The community is an arena, where affiliates can realize diverse interests over time as they see fit (Nonaka and Konno, 1998). If the opportunity structures are ‘narrow’, knowledge sharing benefits can only be realized through a limited number of relationships, sharing very specific knowledge at both a very specific time and place. In contrast, if opportunity structures are broad, more relationships are conducive to sharing in a continuous manner, and in several places, both virtual and physical (Diemers, 2001). Opportunity structures are within each affiliate’s immediate reach, both cognitively, in the sense that individuals within the community can imagine their interests being realized through cooperation with others, and manipulatively, which means that individuals can take concrete action to meet physically or virtually in order to realize these opportunities.

The concept of ‘opportunity structures’ does not assume that affiliates have ‘full knowledge’ of other’s knowledge, since this would rule out the problem with the social cost of monitoring compliance. All that is needed is a cue about what the other affiliates are capable of (MacKenzie, 1998). This cue is both a stimulus to perception and a hint on how to behave in certain circumstances. Whereas an individual’s choices and learning is beyond the direct observation of other community affiliates, these take cues from how the task is performed and how it differs from their task performance (Dreyfus and Dreyfus, 1986). It is also likely that much knowledge sharing will be improvised in the community. Tilly (1999) referred to such a system of cues, when he described a situation of collective action as ‘deep improvisation’, where people do not necessarily follow predefined behavioral scripts (like in a wedding ceremony), but where strong social ties and a shared interpretation of these cues allow people to collectively improvise (e.g. a jazz orchestra). A history of interaction among affiliates enables the evolution of cues that carry meaning in a particular context. A system of cues makes possible a deep improvisation of knowledge sharing, where people send and interpret cues as to when, where and how knowledge sharing is appropriate. A blink of an eye, a waiving hand, or a cough, invites the apprentice into the workspace of the master. A shaking head and a sigh urge affiliates to approach the master to learn what went wrong. Such a system of cues allows sharing to proceed without unnecessary interruption. In other words, it takes into account that interests are contingent by allowing individuals to coordinate the realizations of their interests at a particular time and place. Hence, there is a relationship between the system of cues in a community and the social cost, both in terms of searching for knowledge and in terms of bargaining about when sharing it is suitable. In particular, the system of cues guides the sharing of tacit knowledge, because it affects the level, time, and type of interaction needed for this sharing. Tacit knowledge must be shared on the fly, but as Tilly underscores, collectively meaningful cues that underpin this sharing take a long time to evolve and learn, and they easily mutate under conditions of discontinued interactions.

In order to sustain collective action of knowledge sharing under adverse conditions, such as a high turnover among community affiliates the opportunity structures rely less on a system of cues and deep improvisation, and more on behavioral rituals. Such rituals encompass the introduction of newcomers into the community, discussion platforms, weekly debriefs of tasks and activities, morning speeches and breakfast meetings, coffee breaks, trading posts, poster sessions, a ‘speaker’s corner’ where people can state what’s on their mind regarding the work of the community, etc. However, rituals like these do not foster the spontaneous and impromptu realization of interests, and might therefore work better for the sharing of explicit rather than for tacit knowledge.

As mentioned, because knowledge sharing is a sequenced collective action problem, free riding on other’s knowledge is of some concern to a concept of communal resource, and a question is how this plays out in the two cases of realizing opportunity structures through cues and rituals. First, the common knowledge of cues takes time to learn and might involve considerable investment on behalf of the individual newcomer into the community. The community decides on affiliation through the extent to which the newcomer has learned to interpret the cues necessary for knowledge sharing (Tilly, 1999). Since the free rider can only appraise the value of knowledge once she has learnt the system of cues, it is quite unlikely that a potential free rider would make this investment to engage in knowledge sharing that has uncertain outcomes. Hence, a certain self-selection may exist among extrinsically and intrinsically motivated people. Second, rituals give easier access to the community and formalize opportunity structures, and in this case, it should be expected that access granted to newcomers should be restricted and that the community will have clearer criteria for affiliation. Empirical studies show that people tend to classify themselves and others in terms of categories that relate to a social identity (Roy and Parker-Gwin, 1999). A collective identity can be defined as a ‘people’s sense of who they are in terms of some meaningful social category (e.g. occupational, gender, status, age, community of practice member, presence on the intranet, tech-clubs, etc. author’s example ) that distinguishes how they interact with those inside than from those outside the category’ (Roy and Parker-Gwin, 1999:206). Typically ritual-based communities can be expected to bestow credentials on affiliates, based on observed efforts and/or skills. Interestingly, individual affiliates derive utility from such credentials if these give privileged access to resources, knowledge, social relations, status, reputation, and so on. Hence, a value can be assigned to the social category that will be affected by past and current community affiliation. The higher the category’s value, the more attractive affiliation will appear to newcomers.

It should be expected that in the larger community, opportunity structures tend to be less robust, since social relationships become increasingly scattered, ephemeral, and interests become more diverse. Therefore, the value of social categories might decrease with the increasing community size (more people have access to scarce resources). As a side effect, in larger communities the impact of any individual’s cooperation in knowledge sharing is negligible and a self-interested, rational individual will choose to free ride under these conditions (Hardin, 1971). In larger communities, the cost of an affiliate’s decision to free ride is also spread over a greater number of people, and the cost of monitoring freeriding and taking punitive action increases as well (Marwell and Oliver, 1993). As the community gets larger, the monitoring costs outgrow the costs of sharing, and jointly they could outweigh the rewards from knowledge sharing. The expected outcome is that the opportunity structure will be underprovided and the value of the communal resource will decrease.

## 2.2. Care

Communities evolve social norms (Heckatorn, 1993). When the number of believers in the social norm increases, affiliates derive utility from behavior conforming to the norm, both due to reputation in the community, as well as the individual’s taste towards conformity (Hess, 1998). In any collective action problems, strong socialization and loyalty to the social norm could moderate free riding (Hirschman, 1970; Ferree, 1982; Ostrom, 1999). Care is a social norm in human relationships, and involves the dimensions of trust, active empathy, access to help, lenience in judgment, and the extent to which the former four dimensions are shared in the community (von Krogh 1998). On the one hand, care can but does not necessarily entail concrete action in ways of helping others. Depending on the needs of the care recipient, care can simply refer to presence and intimacy without action. On the other hand, care is sufficient to induce helping others (Egan, 1986). In caring for another, a care provider may bestow valuable knowledge for task execution, may integrate a person socially, give guidance, enhance social bonds, as well as helping to interpret learning experiences (Mayeroff, 1971).

Each dimension of care relates to knowledge sharing. The more the affiliates trust each other, the lower the social costs of knowledge sharing. The more lenience in judgment of other’s knowledge, experience, and behavior, the more likely diverse and distributed interests can persist among affiliates in the community. Interaction can happen in spite of diverse interest, and affiliates suspend their immediate realization of their own interests for the benefit of the community. Given different learning rates of tacit knowledge among individuals, and as well the learning to interpret cues, time might elapse before the giver can realize returns. As a dimension of care, lenience in judgment implies that affiliates accept diverse rates of learning in the community and it could be particularly important for integrating newcomers with different learning rates.

Active empathy is the attempt to ‘put yourself in the shoes of the other’, understanding his or her particular situation, interests, skill levels, successes, failures, opportunities, and problems (von Krogh et al., 2000). As such, it signifies a proactive attempt among affiliates to understand the interests of others observed in ongoing conversations in the community. Whereas active empathy prepares the ground for helping behavior, care in the community also can develop into ‘real and tangible help’. For example, the master will teach the design of a tool, how to use a tool, how to maintain the tool, where to acquire the tool, and so forth. Not only does she show by example how to perform tasks or listen to the apprentice’s concerns and questions, she is also actively involved in the apprentice’s task performance. Help among affiliates might be based on an affiliate’s experience, sharing the burden of the task, and thereby engaging in a joint learning enterprise. Helping behavior should moderate the free-rider problem in communities, where knowledge sharing relies on deep improvisation rather than ritual. Trust and active empathy, may provide too vague information about people’s compliance with the social norm, whereas the level of access to help and the level of help given can be directly observed. If opportunity structures narrow due to an affiliate’s lack of interest in helping, then his future community status might be endangered. Yet if the social category matters to him, he is likely to change his behavior.

## 2.3. Authenticity

When interests are shared in the community, one can assume that the ‘best knowledge’ will be shared. There is no reason why people should pass on false information, suboptimal procedures, unwarranted lessons, hide important details about task performance, etc. When interests are diverse and distributed, the social norm of authenticity could possibly impact on the communal resource—legitimate knowledge is shared directly with the source in a way that ensures its genuineness, accuracy, validity, and reliability. The master–apprentice serves the purpose of illustrating this idea. An apprentice directly observes the genuine know-how of the master. Accuracy in turn depends on the apprentice’s ability to observe a certain level of detail in the work of the master. Validity signifies the apprentice’s ability to appraise observations, interpretations, and understanding of task performance with the master. It also signifies the extent to which the knowledge shared with the apprentice can be extended to the same or different tasks. Reliable knowledge enables repeated action, and depends on affiliates’ application of the knowledge shared in the collective action process. Because care is a social norm from which people derive utility by giving, the community might end up with more knowledge giving and less learning and using. Coupled with authenticity, however, affiliates derive utility from searching out valid and genuine knowledge, but since sharing is a matter of collective action, people also derive utility from proving reliability by using it.

Simon speaks of docility, which in an archaic sense is a willingness to be taught, or in other terms, to defer to the superior knowledge of others (Simon, 1993). The best way to understand superior authentic knowledge in the context of this essay is to view it as cues about what others are capable of in terms of above-average performance in the community at large. At the extreme, communities can be self-preserving and conform, punishing affiliates, who try to branch out and be inventive. In groups with strong social norms and diverse interests, collective action can be highly unlikely due to the social costs faced by individuals pursuing their own courses of action (Massey, 1994). Proposing new ideas or criticizing the work of others could imply retaliation and exclusion from the social category (Hirschman, 1970). Rejection of new knowledge could be warranted from the cost resulting from bargaining about what is ‘really superior knowledge’, or the unlearning of previous lessons learned (Leonard, 1995). In this sense, community affiliates, who have worked hard and long to achieve a certain level of task performance, might find it extremely difficult to change routines that have proved successful in the past (Kluege et al., 2001). Docility might be a prerequisite for the evolution of authenticity as a social norm, and in fact, might underpin the communal resource.

To summarize the discussion thus far, a communal resource is influenced by a community’s characteristics, including the degree to which interests are diverse and distributed among affiliates. I reasoned that where interests are diverse and distributed opportunity structure, care and authenticity impact on knowledge sharing. Opportunity structures constrain knowledge sharing. The social norm of care positively impacts on the suspended satisfaction of needs and realization of knowledge sharing opportunities, and as well the search for opportunities in spite of diverse and distributed interests. The social norm of authenticity positively impacts on the ability of affiliates to realize ‘better knowledge’ within opportunity structures, to learn, and to improve. Even if interests are shared, the community can be characterized by opportunity structures, care and authenticity. However, I do not assume a priori collective interests in knowledge sharing, although the more collective the interests, the more powerful the communal resource for solving the social costs problem.

## 3. The role of information systems

Communities per se do not solve the problem of knowledge sharing—they range from being resources to liabilities. Moreover, there is some bad news for knowledge management research inherent in the discussion thus far. As should have become clear, the intention to manage communities and turn them into high-performing communal resources for the organization might easily fail. Communities are then resources, when they display a certain level of voluntary action and self-organization, and they do not fit well with outside authority, monitoring, and punitive action. However, there should be ways to enable the development of communal resources, of which one is the support of information systems. Information systems enable communities to overcome time and space constraints in knowledge sharing and increase the speed and range of access to information (Ramarapu et al., 1999). In this sense, information systems are imperative for creating an advantage for organizations (Ferratt et al., 1996), and the development of information systems should relate to the way information and knowledge is shared in a community (Swan and Galliers, 1996; Chalmers, 1999; Jarvenpaa and Staples, 2000).

First, opportunity structures broaden, when people are aware of possibilities to share knowledge and form communities, and information systems could be vital here. Basically, the coordination of expertise in the organization may require technologies in order identify, who has knowledge and where they are to be found (Faraj and Sproull, 2000). Beyond this, information systems can have various roles to play in the formation of a community. At first, people may be part of a loose gathering in which they share a location. This location could be virtual and technology-based, including bulletin boards, discussion groups or personal web-pages on the Intranet. In general, the information systems literature advocates a very broad participation and access to such networking tools (Howley, 1998), that input of explicit knowledge should have a high degree of user-friendliness in order to impact on effective technology-enabled knowledge sharing (Hall, 2001), and that systems supporting knowledge repositories should have the capacity to argue the rationale behind the knowledge retrieved (Gregor and Benbasat, 1999). As people start to interact more closely in the electronic medium, they may begin to form reciprocal relationships in which they exchange small favors and gifts, and perhaps decide on time, place and contents for further exchange. This in turn makes the group more coherent. Once they become a ‘fused group’,<sup>13</sup> affiliates recognize differences and similarities in interests, individual needs and different areas of expertise. At this stage, individuals might perceive strong benefits from forming social categories, restricted affiliations and stronger communal relationships. For example, one affiliate sees a great business opportunity in combining screen technology with the development of selfrepairing materials. Another affiliate has this kind of materials expertise and sees benefits from starting to share knowledge in order to explore a technical innovation. In terms of information systems, a fused group means that the networking tools provide a ‘affiliatearea’ in which access is restricted by credentials and passwords. Above all, a fused group is driven by a shared curiosity about what other affiliates know and what the group might eventually accomplish if they continue to explore their interests and knowledge. In order to establish further progress, it might be vital for the community to meet physically at this stage. The advantage of the information and communication technology, however, is that it keeps a record of previous interactions in the group.<sup>14</sup> For example, modern community platforms, such as Groove or Metalayer provide an effective memory of group interactions. These records can be of high value for both reflecting on the social norms evolving, and as well for making any kind of pledge needed for the communal resource. Such a pledge could be informal or formal, for example, outlining the basic expectation to reciprocal helping.

A popular way of facilitating knowledge sharing in many organizations is through socalled ‘technical share fairs’ or ‘knowledge fairs’ (Davenport and Prusak, 1998). These are large exhibitions over several days where research teams, engineering teams, technical groups, etc. can exhibit information about their projects, areas of expertise, and technical pursuits. For example, for several years the German technology group Siemens’ have organized knowledge fairs for information sharing between a thousand managers. The first meeting was held in a large exhibition area at Munich’s Olympic Hall, and was very participatory. With twenty to thirty separate spaces on each of the hall’s two levels, information markets were set up each focusing on a particular topic. At each market, managers came together to discuss the issues they were interested in (von Krogh et al., 2000). In such knowledge fairs, some people will already be affiliated with a community of technical specialists at a research site. Others will not be affiliated with any particular community, but will discover new opportunities for establishing a community and for sharing knowledge. Through these fairs, organizations enlarge the opportunity structure for knowledge sharing. The fair might also contribute to the evolutionary stability of the community, whereby social relations are reinforced and new ones are created. In some ways, knowledge fairs are extraordinary events that require considerable investment from the corporation. Possibly virtual knowledge fairs can help trim organizing costs and can make knowledge sharing accommodate individual needs more fully. A platform for such fairs could include community spaces, various levels of introduction to the community work, effective search facilities, on-line chats, or web-casts. Perhaps experiences in the physical fairs will serve as a meaningful template for creating virtual fairs.

Opportunity structures rely on cues and rituals. As mentioned, cues are particularly important for sharing tacit knowledge. Whereas a cue about what others are capable of accomplishing can easily be transmitted through information systems, cues for realization of opportunities for sharing, such as appropriate times and places require physical colocation to evolve and diffuse throughout a community. However, when using networking tools, a certain ‘netiquette’, style, or convention of communication emerges, which should be helpful for affiliates to realize knowledge sharing. For example, such conventions might include a ‘technical grammar’ needed for effective knowledge sharing, for example, codifying tacit knowledge (Argyres, 1999). Also, there might be certain discussion lists devoted to certain topics, and it might be considered imprudent to enter material into the lists that does not deal directly with the core-topics. Likewise, research on Open Source Software Development has shown there may be ‘Frequently Asked Questions and Answers’ lists or other repositories available that aid newcomers when entering discussions in the community (von Krogh and Spaeth, 2002). Such conventions might be found somewhere between rituals and cues—they are more explicit than cues, but less formalized than rituals.

Second, information systems can also be related to the evolution of care as a social norm in the community, with an emphasis on access to help and trust. Electronic networks should enable helping behavior at very low cost. For example, one large consulting company has an ‘issue posting system’ which allows consultants to post urgent and critical questions electronically to colleagues all over the world. Answering these questions is a matter of sending a reply e-mail, but whether such systems provide useful advice and answers in a specific organizational setting is dependent on such factors as people’s motives and positive feelings towards the organization in question (Constant et al., 1996). If a consultant knows well the answer to the question posted, the benefit in terms of enhanced reputation for generosity should outweigh the cost of preparing and sending the answer. Such issue posting has a great advantage over phone calls to community members, because it reaches out to a much larger group of potential experts and it works well across different time zones. However, investigating themes such as helping behavior in electronic networks, Wasko and Faraj (2000) argue that information systems will not be very useful in promoting knowledge sharing, unless a shift of mind-set occurs in the organization, from knowledge being a private good to knowledge being a ‘public’ good owned by the community. With a new mind-set, knowledge sharing is motivated by moral obligation and community interest rather than narrow self-interest. Their survey of three electronic communities of practice indicated that people participate out of community interest, generalized reciprocity and pro-social behavior. Such a shift seems crucial especially since many organizations lean too much towards individualistic, hyper-competitive mind-sets. But a word of caution may be raised: in organizations people are rewarded both for being self-interested, as well as collectively interested. Perhaps this tension in fact spurs creativity and drives innovation. The double-bind phenomenon outlined above, however, could potentially hamper this creativity. As a result, individual organizational members should be given the possibility of sharing in the community at their own discretion, when it fits their and the community’s particular circumstances, and the realization of opportunities for sharing needs to become as effortless as possible. What matters is not that interests are collective but that as a mechanism for knowledge sharing, the community is evolutionary stable in spite of diverse and distributed interests.

The relationship between trust and implementation and use of information systems has been much discussed in the literature (Kumar et al., 1998; Luftman and Brier, 1999). Investigating the relationship between organizational culture and the use of intranets for knowledge sharing, Ruppel and Harrington (2001) found that Intranet implementation is facilitated by a culture that emphasizes trust, a concern for other people, flexibility and innovation. In their view, management should ensure that proper values of trust are in place to facilitate intranet implementation and knowledge sharing by electronic means. A question still remains, however. Can long-term ‘deep’ trust needed for knowledge sharing evolve if the community must rely on electronic communication only? (Jarvenpaa and Leidener, 1999). In fact, the deep and binding relationships needed for sharing knowledge may take considerable time to develop if the community is confined to electronic communication only (Chidambaram, 1996). The concept of opportunity structures implies that affiliates will support the community if this provides some utility, and it is likely that if the benefits of knowledge sharing takes too long to realize, then affiliates might withdraw their support at an early stage. In conclusion, it could therefore be that communities that have some early record of physical interaction that establishes trust would more likely use information systems effectively than would communities without such records.

Third, information systems can also have a positive influence on authenticity. In particular, for explicit knowledge, direct and effortless feedback can be given to the creators of knowledge, for example, on validity and reliability as perceived by the affiliates. Furthermore, this process might allow for some form of self-censorship and selection that helps affiliates to retain valuable knowledge, while discarding knowledge that they find does not comply with the social norm of authenticity. Moreover, a voting and commentary scheme associated with a knowledge repository can allow affiliates to give direct feedback, but more importantly to see how other community affiliates react to the knowledge, and as well track the development of the acceptance of this knowledge. A useful extension of such systems could be to provide some access to affiliates of other communities as well, where awareness between communities of their local work and possible points of interaction and shared interests can be identified.

As mentioned, communities can be communal liabilities preventing new knowledge sharing. If there are strong norms in the community and people derive utility from conformity, if an established body of universally ‘accepted’ knowledge has helped community affiliates to solve tasks in the past, and if physical interaction is very intense in the community, then any attempt to share radically new knowledge is associated with high-social costs. Moreover, people may derive utility from punishing those who try to share new knowledge (exposed loyalty), and even those, who do not punish can be exposed to retaliation from other affiliates, who thereby force compliance with collective interests. Under such adverse conditions, information systems have a key role to play in the sharing of knowledge. Affiliates’ social costs can be offset by anonymity offered, for example, through electronic means of communication (Massey, 1994). Clearly, the focus on interests here raises a number of questions that call for more studies on how organizational politics is enabled or constrained by information systems use (for more details, see Romm and Pliskin (1997)).

The concept of authenticity might also have implications for the design and implementation of information systems. The process of information systems design has been dominated by attempts to create technical solutions to perceived problems of user groups across a variety of contexts (Tractinsky and Jarvenpaa, 1995; Barua et al., 1995; Stolterman, 1999). Typically, difficulties arise when one attempts to prove or verify that the information system solves the real problem. Often users’ ‘real problem’, work, social interactions and previous knowledge are difficult to capture and understand in a structured fashion (Newell et al., 2000). An improved way of designing information systems is to let users work on the authenticity of the systems for facilitating their own learning (Champion and Stowell, 2001). The social acceptance and effective use of such systems is sensitive to the participation of a user community and the spread of knowledge about such systems throughout the organization. However, there is a potential danger of information systems that a centralized department deploys community tools without really involving various affiliates in specific design decisions (Robey and Sahay, 1996). What matters for the successful implementation of information systems is that there is a high level of trust between the IS professionals and the user communities. When there is high level of trust between these groups, shared knowledge about information systems is likely to spread more effectively throughout the communities (Nelson and Cooprider, 1996). It is also worth noting that the interdependency between the affiliates’ satisfaction with the system and their participation in its design, is likely to be reinforced with increasing complexity of the information system (McKeen et al., 1994). In this context, the IS professional is more than a designer of an information system. Rather she is an agent of learning and change at various levels and in different groups throughout the organization (Lee et al., 1995).

## 4. Conclusion and implications

Knowledge sharing is a key process in many knowledge management activities, including the capture, transfer and creation of knowledge. It might be appropriate for future theory and research to consider knowledge sharing as a problem of collective action among actors with diverse and distributed interests. Two different solutions to this problem are worthwhile examining. The first is to instigate an agency that structures, provides incentives for and monitors knowledge sharing, and punishes defiant behavior. Several problems are associated with this solution, such as choosing the right incentives for sharing and obtaining sufficient information for overseeing sharing activities with some degree of certainty.

Second, the communal resource reduces the social costs of sharing knowledge without the external agency. However, where interests are diverse and distributed, the effectiveness of the community as a ‘communal resource’ depends on available opportunity structures and the social norms in the community. Opportunity structures for knowledge sharing, work on cues observed among people that cover both task performance and the appropriate ways, times and places to share knowledge. Communities can ‘deeply improvise’ sharing or provide rituals for this process. Care is a social norm that gives rise to trust, active empathy, access to helping and lenience in judgment. Authenticity is a social norm that gives credence to knowledge directly observed in action, as well as the willingness to be taught lessons of people with superior knowledge. If we assume people in an organization hold diverse and distributed interests that change over time, the evolutionary stability of the community matters for a theory of knowledge sharing in organizations. Several poorly understood factors gain significance for whether or not the community will reduce the social costs of knowledge sharing. Hence, the role of the community as a resource has still to be determined.

Following the discussion of the role of information systems, a number of research imperatives emerge. First, what is the relationship between information systems and the emerging opportunity structures for knowledge sharing? Do certain information systems characteristics tend to broaden or narrow opportunity structures? Second, how can a certain information systems design promote helping behavior in the community? Moreover, what is the appropriate balance between physical meetings and virtual communication for the development of trust in the community? Is there a way to better integrate information systems in the physical meetings as well, and thereby make the use of such systems an integral and natural part of the ways of working? What are the organizational consequences of giving affiliates anonymity when sharing (explicit) knowledge? How is the norm of authenticity affected by the use of information systems?

Communities need available resources and time to develop in a sound manner. Meeting in so-called ‘free-spaces’ where resources and time are available, affiliates can build social ties outside the existing social structures, and develop particular pledges and social norms (Polletta, 1999). Information systems play a significant role here, since some of these freespaces might initially be virtual (Townsend, 2000). What is the relationship between information systems and the emergence of free-spaces for community formation?

It is worth noting that over time, the agency can have great advantages over the community in terms of securing superior expertise and knowledge. Social norms that are maintained by the community, but not questioned by the outside agency could have an adverse impact on accepting and integrating superior knowledge (as argued above), and hence, draws attention to the potential negative impact of error-tolerance or lenience in judgment. What is the interplay between lenience in judgment and the cost of identifying ‘superior’ knowledge, and what is the role of information systems in this interplay? Does anonymity help to secure the sharing of superior knowledge? Also, with the implementation of information systems there might be a difference between tacit and explicit knowledge worth noting. Under conditions of a social norm of authenticity, how can affiliates deal with the difference in quality and robustness between ready-made explicit knowledge and tacit knowledge evolving through learning? People learn a lot from sharing an expert’s trial-and-error with a task, flawed lessons, systematic reporting of experiences, etc. However, cues diffused through information systems about superior knowledge are often recognized after the fact, that is, as ready-made knowledge. Furthermore, in terms of reducing the time of learning, which is one of the benefits claimed in the information systems literature (Ramarapu et al., 1999), preference might be given to explicit rather than to tacit knowledge, even at the expense of authenticity.

There is an ongoing debate in the community of information systems professionals about the way in which information systems should meet the contemporary needs of organizations to improve the effectiveness of knowledge sharing (Kakola and Koota, 1999; Lim and Klobas, 2000; Hall, 2001; Ruppel and Harrington, 2001; Stodart, 2001). Generally, a shared vision among IS professionals influence the design and adaptation of new technological solutions (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997). Perhaps a broader vision among information systems professionals about the role of the communal resource in knowledge sharing can help focus future information systems research on the social fabric of organizations that constrain and enable knowledge sharing. Some scholars also express dissatisfaction with the inability of the IS community to provide research on themes, such as interactive knowledge repositories and collaborative electronic networks, reflecting back on a somewhat inadequate research process (Hars et al., 2000). Whether or not such a claim is substantiated is not for me to say, but I would suggest that a heightened attention to opportunity structures, care and authenticity exercised and lived in the community of information systems researchers should positively influence the production and dissemination of scientific knowledge in this very important field.
