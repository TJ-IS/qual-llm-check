# Bridging communities of practice with information technology in pursuit of global knowledge sharing

Shan L. Pan<sup>a,\*</sup>, Dorothy E. Leidner<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Information Systems, National University of Singapore, 10 Kent Ridge Crescent, Singapore 119620, Singapore <sup>b</sup>Baylor University, P.O. Box 98005, Waco, TX 76798 USA

## Abstract

This paper explores the use of information technology to support knowledge sharing within and between communities of practice. In so doing, it presents a case of a multi-national organization’s efforts to implement an organizational knowledge management (KM) system. The case traces both the technological solutions and the KM strategy of the organization as it met with various challenges along a several year period of establishing KM as organizational practice. The study highlights several lessons, including the possibility of a flexible KM strategy, the necessity for multiple channels of knowledge sharing, the desirability of expanding communities of practice, and the evolution of the role played by information technology as KM strategies evolve. q 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: IT-enabled knowledge management systems; Knowledge management; Communities of practice; Global knowledge sharing

## 1. Introduction

In today’s fast changing environment, competitive firms can be seen as generators and transformers of different kinds of knowledge (von Tunzelmann, 1995). As firms increasingly compete with a different stock of knowledge (Stinchcombe, 1990; von Krogh and Roos, 1995), management of the firm’s knowledge base has emerged as a major challenge in maintaining sustainable competitive advantage (Spender, 1996). It can be concluded that we have entered a new era of knowledge management and are living and working in a knowledge society as knowledge workers (Drucker, 1993).

Various streams of knowledge management (KM) research have emerged. Early research focused on understanding the differences among data, information, knowledge, and the effects these differences have on KM (Vance, 1997; Machlup, 1980). Classifications were developed to differentiate organizational knowledge and analyze the interrelation of knowledge types (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995; Spender, 1996). Two common dimensions found in the classifications are the distinction between individual and collective knowledge (Spender, 1996), and tacit and explicit knowledge (Polanyi, 1962, 1966; Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995).

Other research viewed knowledge as a source of competence and as a competitive resource (Huang et al., 2001). Sometimes referred to as the knowledge-based view of the firm (Grant, 1996), this view seeks to explain the relationship between knowledge and the firm. It establishes a rationale for the existence of the firm, and analyzes knowledge integration within firms (e.g. Grant, 1996; Nonaka, 1994; Spender, 1996; Spender and Grant, 1996). Subsequently, research has sought to recommend tools to leverage knowledge so that organizations can identify, share, process, and capture more knowledge effectively. This will enhance the organization’s competitive advantage. Thus, more recently, a stream of research examining Information Systems (IS)-enabled KM has developed. These include descriptive studies of KMS in practice (Alavi and Leidner, 1999), conceptual articles discussing the role of IS in KM (Alavi and Leidner, 2001; von Krogh, 2002), theory testing articles examining motivations to use and contribute to KMS (Jarvenpaa and Staples, 2000; Wasko and Faraj, 2000), and field-based studies examining issues that contribute to KMS success (and organizational issues that inhibit KMS success (Schultze and Boland, 2000; Merali, 2000).

In addition, there is also an increasing number of studies linking the concept of communities of practice (CoP) with organizations’ strategic knowledge (Wenger and Snyder, 2000; Storck and Hill, 2000). A goal of many KM initiatives is to develop a global knowledge community where knowledge is shared and utilized across various CoP in the organization.

This paper explores the use of information technology to support knowledge sharing within and between communities of practice. The paper is organized as follows: after the introduction, Section 2 discusses CoP, Section 3 presents the case description; Section 4 highlights the key findings from the case study; and Section 5 presents the implications and conclusions.

## 2. The importance of communities of practice in knowledge management

The realization of knowledge as embedded and constructed from and through social relationships and interactions (Scarbrough and Swan, 1999; Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995; Blackler, 1995) has led some KM researchers to focus on the importance of communal resource (von Krogh, 2002) and the notion of evolving communities within organizational boundaries. In particular, community of practice is defined as people bound by informal relationships who share common practices (Brown and Duguid, 2001, 1998, 1997; Lave and Wenger, 1990). The notion of CoP suggests that organizational community boundaries do not correspond with typical functional boundaries. Instead, it includes practice- and person-based networks. Members of CoP typically spend time helping each other solve problems. The importance of CoP has been acknowledged in a number of earlier works: sensemaking (Weick, 1979), CoP (Lave and Wenger, 1990; Orr, 1990; Brown and Duguid, 1998, 1997), storytelling (Orr, 1990), knowing in practice (2002), and communities of knowing (Boland and Tenkasi, 1995).

CoPs are important as they weave the organization around competencies without reverting to functional structures. They facilitate an environment of ‘structured informality’ supported by knowledge, people, organizational processes and infrastructure. These CoP possess a vast base of knowledge ranging from theoretical concepts to practical experiences; they are the engines for acceleration in an organization. Socially, CoP are the fabrics of knowing as members of CoP acquire communal identity around a shared passion, relationships, roles and ways of intermingling common knowledge, practices and approaches (Scarbrough and Swan, 1999).

The importance of CoP stems from the fact that knowledge cannot be separated from its context. In all types of knowledge activities, even where technology is very helpful, knowledge contributors as well as seekers require a common community to share general conversation, experimentation, and experiences with other people who do what they do. When inside the community, knowledge workers are informally as well as contextually bound by a shared interest in knowledge sharing, and applying common practices. Yet a challenge encountered in KM initiatives is to bridge these CoP to enable sharing across, not just within, communities. This study seeks to explore this challenge by examining if, and if so, how, CoP can be integrated to enable global knowledge sharing. Specifically we look at the use of information technology to link communities in order to create global knowledge sharing.

## 3. The case

Buckman Labs, a US\$300 million chemical company with operations in 21 different countries, sells more than 1000 different specialty chemicals. It is a company with a 50- year history of developing, manufacturing and marketing proprietary chemical products. The specialty chemical industry is characterized by more stable demand, higher values of products, more restricted competition, lower financial commitment to plant capacity, lower output volumes, and a higher technological content than is normally the case with commodity chemicals. The products of the specialty chemical industry are fundamentally important to virtually most manufacturing industries as they contribute essential base materials. The total number of employees at Buckman Labs in the Tennessee headquarters is around 400. They include sales, research and development (R&D), manufacturing, technical and administrative support employees.

Given the increasing need to integrate and consolidate knowledge from operations worldwide, the importance of global knowledge sharing across Buckman Labs had long been recognized. According to an informant, ‘Traditionally, global knowledge sharing for us meant having experts who traveled around the globe trouble-shooting. We also used postal services to communicate best practices and keep people informed. However, both these methods were problematic, the first because it put pressure on the traveling individual experts, and the second because it was slow and inefficient.’

Before 1990, all requests for information were channeled through the Technical Information Center (TIC) at the corporate headquarters in Memphis. TIC members were responsible for making sure that requests received attention and that experts were located in the field to address problems. Solutions or suggestions were usually transferred by fax or post, but this method proved limited, inefficient and ineffective. For example, in December 1991, a Brazilian employee requested expert information about a particular chemical. His request took six weeks to arrive by post and the response; several more weeks (see Appendix A for details).

3.1. Phase 1: the beginning of global knowledge transfer initiative: IT as the overall knowledge infrastructure

In 1990, Buckman Labs’ vision for global knowledge transfer initially involved an attempt to establish an e-mail system based on an internal modem bank for the transfer of best practices around the world. In particular, the organization linked up with IBM’s global network and introduced its first formal and organized system (called Case History System) to share and capture knowledge within its subsidiaries. As a forum specialist explained:

A case history is an electronic CoP recording how a front line sales associate creates new knowledge within the organization. It is achieved by solving customers’ problems either by applying existing ‘Buckman knowledge’ (if it was a well-documented problem) or by developing an effective new solution (if it was a new problem). This repository, with full text search capabilities, was accessible by all employees worldwide through PCs connected via telephone lines. In total, we had 2472 case histories in English and the remainder in Spanish.

However, with the need for more mobility within the system, the demand for an enhanced technological capability grew. It soon became apparent that the desktop and mainframe-based approach would not work in the long run. Following a year of study on the feasibility of implementing a global KM system, Buckman Labs decided to use a CompuServe service to integrate two communication packages (one for e-mail and forum access, and the other for intranet access). Together, they formed the core of the global KM system.

## 3.2. The establishment of single global forum

The first project was to design and implement a global forum (TechForum), based on intranet technology. The resulting TechForum created a taxonomy of knowledge areas based upon business functions across all of the different systems and repositories with a common interface. Buckman Labs had, by the end of 1993, invested US\$8 million in laying the groundwork for its global KMS. All employees were able to make a single phone call using a modem to engage in global knowledge transfer activities. One informant explained:

Within a couple of months, CompuServe was chosen as the e-mail and forum provider as well as the network provider, largely due to its emphasis on the individual, dialconnected user. While this technology was not new, its use as a global business communication platform was a radical departure from the traditional IT solution for these services, especially in Buckman Labs. Buckman users around the world could use e-mail and forums to share knowledge via messages on the forums, company-wide discussions and documents authored using PC tools.

By then, the TechForum had become the central pivot of Buckman’s global KMS. TechForum (see Fig. 1) had 20 sections, each with its own message board, conference rooms to facilitate debate, and library section, where the communication threads and other pertinent knowledge were stored. Thirteen of the 20 sections were devoted to the business areas within Buckman Labs, e.g. Pulp and Paper, and Leather. These forums were expected to help improve client companies’ productivity. Six of the 20 sections were primarily internal and designed to improve the operational efficiency and effectiveness of Buckman Labs, e.g. Human Resources, plant operations, safety/environment, KT Topics/ Help). The Bulab News and Breakroom sections were general discussion sections in the TechForum where Buckman employees were free to discuss topics of their choice.

In terms of content design, the majority of the TechForum was devoted to business related activities, which was considered as the ‘push’ factor to provide employees access to relevant knowledge needed for their tasks. On the other hand, sections like Breakroom and Human Resources were considered as the ‘pull’ to bring the whole company together by encouraging people to communicate in a more socialized environment. The diverse topics discussed in the Breakroom ranged from support for sports teams, to requests sent to employees in foreign countries for vacation recommendations in their area, and other nonbusiness related activities.

## 3.3. Towards systematic global KM

In order to provide full access to the employees, Buckman’s management combined Information Services, Telecommunications, and the Technical Information Center (which included a full-fledged corporate library) to form the Knowledge Transfer Department (KTD). Ninety per cent of the 50 KTD employees were responsible for the design, development, implementation and maintenance of the software and hardware of the ICTbased KM system. The other five KTD employees were located in the knowledge resource centre (KRC), and their responsibilities included the monitoring and processing of the knowledge generated within the various sections of Buckman forums.

A knowledge processing team was put in place to reduce the need for each associate to retrieve and store the accumulated knowledge that each section captured as a result of discussions within the sections and stored as text-files in the appropriate section library. Members of the specialist knowledge processing team included a number of forum specialists and section leaders (two or more per section from various departments). Four forum specialists who belong to the knowledge-processing team monitored the activities on the KMS. Their job was to ensure that the knowledge generated from their respective regional forums was accurate.

Apart from the forum specialist, a section leader was also recruited to help manage knowledge activities. The latter was a highly trained chemist or microbiologist with specialized industry knowledge and experience. Section leaders were individuals already well-known to many in the organization as experts and leaders in their respective industries. In other words, they were authorized experts from different areas of the organization who acted as a key source for unanswered requests or unsolved problems. According to an informant,

Section leaders are selected initially to lay a foundation of trust in the solutions which employees are sharing. They are then trained on the system (either in person, or interactively online) and are expected to enter the forums daily to review any messages and respond accordingly.

The section leaders wrote weekly abstracts for a central database accessible to most employees. They were not only responsible for facilitating the knowledge-sharing process but also for assisting in ‘processing’ knowledge, e.g. by writing abstracts for storage, and facilitating the re-use of the obtained knowledge. Members of the specialist team assumed the additional responsibility of preparing a summary of the discussion points that occur in each section and posting the information at the end of each week.

If an on-line request went unattended for a few hours, a forum specialist would come in and facilitate the process. According to a forum specialist, two scenarios could emerge. First, one of the forum specialists would pick up the request and then identify the potential experts based on their previous industrial experience and reputation of their willingness to share knowledge. Alternatively, a team of experts with related industrial experience who had volunteered as ‘section leaders’ would also help answer any requests and prepare weekly summariestobestoredintheknowledgerepositoriesforlateruse.Whenaninformationsearch was completed, responses were then formulated and presented to customers for problem solving. The request was kept on the forum for as long as there was an active discussion, and would only be extracted for knowledge processing when the issue became ‘dead’.

As a result of the discussion of the request, which usually generated new knowledge with the help of the forum specialists and section leaders, the new knowledge then went through various processing activities. For example, the knowledge generated was often repetitive and inaccurate. In this case, forum specialists and section leaders would need to organize, validate and verify the knowledge before it was uploaded, stored into the knowledge base and was ready for distribution and use/re-use if a similar query resurfaced in the future. These processes enabled front-line employees to continue serving customers while a specialized knowledge processing team devoted time to capture their knowledge into a re-usable form.

## 3.4. Attempts to consolidate global knowledge: IT as linking mechanisms between different communities of practice

After the global KMS was put in place, Buckman Labs was able to consolidate its global knowledge and provide almost just-in-time solutions to many of their technical and organizational problems worldwide. Given the range of business activities Buckman Labs was involved in, there were frequent demands for knowledge in new or esoteric domains. In one instance, an employee who was based in Singapore as a Managing Director in Asia needed some technical expertise for a business proposal bid to an Indonesian pulp mill. He sent a message through the KMS asking for help on how he could go about preparing the business proposal. The first response came three hours later, from an employee in Memphis, and included a suggestion to use a specific chemical and a reference to a students’ thesis on the pitch control of tropical hardwoods. The second response came 50 min later from Canada, offering an experience on solving the pitch problem in British Columbia. Then another employee logged in with examples from Sweden; as did an employee from New Zealand; and others in Spain and France. In all, the request generated 11 replies from six different countries (including messages from Latin America), stimulated new discussions, generated new knowledge, and enabled the Managing Director in Singapore to secure a US\$6 million order from an Indonesian pulp mill (Buckman, 1998).

## 3.5. Difficulties encountered trying to bridge separate communities of practice

The system was initially designed to support a single global community. However, some of the non-American employees disliked this concept and resisted the practice. As pointed out by a sales manager from Brazil, the cause of the problem was two-fold: linguistic and cultural. ‘For our Latin American colleagues, their first concern is the language. In most cases, they had difficulties understanding the messages as well as doing a good job in sharing their knowledge globally’. On the other hand, ‘culturally, our European colleagues preferred having their own forums using European languages as they were not at all happy about using English as the medium language in the single global forum’, suggested by a German software engineer.

Therefore, to encourage employees outside the USA to participate, the management introduced a new policy:

Bulab employees should feel comfortable using any language they desire when posting messages on the message board. The Systems Operators will follow the addendum to the Sysops manual and see that the message is translated into English for all to read. Technical replies will be translated back to the originator’s own language in accordance with the addendum. (On-line archive, 1997).

Yet this solution too proved inadequate to deal with the problem. There was a deeper problem, that of a lack of shared language and understanding across the Communities of Practice that had created dysfunction and divisions in communication. To address this problem, a philosophy of ‘anything was discussible and anyone could participate in the Communities of Practice’ was introduced and made known to everyone. Top management took time to explain to employees that the company was made up of individuals with different capabilities and potentials, but all of who were necessary to the success of the company.

Despite the relatively popular reaction to the new philosophy as a flexible guideline to global transfer behavior, many of employees were still not very comfortable without specific guidelines on participating in global knowledge transfer. An informant said that such uncertainty was probably due to the ‘transparent’ nature of the global knowledge transfer system. She explained further, ‘employees were hesitant to communicate because of not knowing what was right and what was wrong’. Subsequently, management took notice and introduced better-defined guidelines—the ‘Buckman Code of Ethics’ to provide precise guidelines to employees on how to participate in global knowledge sharing. The guidelines were printed on a wallet-sized laminated card and given to every employee. It showcased a new operating philosophy that embraced a common language and understanding to facilitate participation in global knowledge sharing.

To improve the global knowledge transfer environment, the ‘waterline’ metaphor was introduced. Buckman employees were encouraged to think about the company as a ship, with the Code of Ethics as the waterline. According to the Chairman of the organization,

You do not shoot below the waterline, because you can sink the ship. However, you are free to be as innovative as you wish in changing the superstructure of the ship to meet the needs of the customer.

In making sure that employees understood the metaphor, great effort went into explaining the meaning and the practicalities involved. Top management, as well as some middle management, was encouraged to participate in promoting the metaphor on-line. An informant recalled:

I can still remember how the metaphor of waterline was promoted and explained online in an effort to encourage us to participate in knowledge sharing. When I was first introduced to the concept, although without the clear guidelines of do’s and don’ts, I thought it gave us a lot of flexibility in terms of how we should behave on-line as Buckman employees. It has made participation easier. I think.

On the other hand, ‘misbehaved’ participants were also singled out and ‘punished’ accordingly. A forum specialist said:

There was an employee who made some close-to-racist comments regarding our international employees. He was punished for his misbehavior. His comment was taken off-line at the suggestion of our management to protect the integrity of our employees in a particular region. He was further stripped of his right to use the K’Netix for a few weeks, despite the loss in productivity, in a hope that he and other employees could learn how one should not participate on-line.

Despite these efforts by senior management to encourage a global knowledge community, they were still unable to put their global philosophy into reality in a certain way. The management had no choice but to implement a region-based knowledge sharing approach.

In the case of the AAAForum (established in 1997) that served employees from Asia, Australia and Africa, English was used. ## 3.7. Phase 3: towards a global industry-based forum: IT as a support to different communities of practice

Notwithstanding the effort to overcome the communication inertia introduced by the single global forum, the communication problems did not disappear. Rather, the decision to move towards a set of regional on-line communities led to other difficulties, which became evident in 1995–1997. These were mainly concerned with the redundancy of solutions provided by different forum participants, and also the formation of different online communities contradicted the original philosophy of genuine global knowledge sharing. For example, for an employee in Brazil to obtain so-called ‘worldwide Buckman expertise’, he or she would need to post the question in four different forums. This caused inconvenience for all and was identified as the probable cause for the drop in the usage of on-line forums during this period (1995–1997). This was brought to the attention of the management when an US-based associate expressed his preference for a proposed single industry-based forum over multiple regional forums spread over five continents:

It is my understanding that the TechForum was created to provide a vehicle by which our global company could communicate and share information. Now I see that we have an Euroforum and an Asia forum, etc. This segregation appears to be a contradiction to the original intent of global communication. I like the concept of one forum and one company, sharing ideas, knowledge and wisdom.

This comment later triggered a series of long heated debates on the intranet involving employees of all ranks and regions. A manager recalled, ‘I remember there were more than 20 messages posted by members of different communities discussing whether or not the region-based arrangement should be continued. There was one regional director who argued strongly that the regional set up was really helpful as members from this forum found it more useful than the previous single forum arrangement. On the other hand, a sales person from the headquarters warned that if the region-based approach continues, less and less global knowledge sharing will take place. And after while, we are going to lose the kind of global knowledge sharing culture which we spent so much time building over the years.

By the 1998, the management decided to reorganize its forums into a single global industry-based forum, using new software and with an enhanced translation capability. In the long run, this shift was aimed at dismantling the communication barriers that had built up in the company. The Chairman explained:

We started with one global forum (TechForum), then we went separate, tried to meet individual needs. Now, we are going to bring it all back together which will make it easier for the people who are using it.

With the new arrangement in place, a single global industry-based forum divided into different lines of business, for example, an employee from the water industry in Asia now only needs to communicate in one forum instead of the previous regional forums.

## 4. Discussion

The purpose of the study was to examine the major issues related to bridging communities of practice with information technology in pursuit of global Knowledge sharing. The case does so by describing and analyzing the evolving approach used by Buckman Labs to support the exchange of knowledge within and across communities of practice globally. The following sections present and discuss the four main lessons learned from this case study. The four lessons include: (1) possibility of a flexible KM strategy; (2) providing multiple channels for diverse knowledge sharing needs and preferences; (3) continuous expansion of communities of practice; and (4) exploring issues and concerns regarding the changing role of IT.

## 4.1. Possibility of a flexible KM strategy

The first lesson learned from the case study is that technological solutions to knowledge management are flexible. As such, they enable management to refine the organization’s knowledge management goals and processes without drastic changes to the technological underpinnings. From this perspective, KM can be viewed as a dynamic process. Based on our case data, we found out that Buckman management started with a single KM forum hoping that it would capture global knowledge. Unfortunately, this approach met with difficulties when cultural and linguistics differences were encountered by participants from non-US countries. The management had to re-adjust and came out with a different design (multi-region design), so that the knowledge contributors and seekers could share their knowledge in the communities where they felt most comfortable. Just when the management thought they had the right focus and strategy in place, they encountered a renewed interest in global knowledge communities. This time, a common knowledgebased design was preferred over the ones that were regional-based. One might argue that the management of Buckman was too reactive and should have stood by the initial strategy. Alternatively, one can conclude that, because the technology is flexible, management is able to explore different options. The fact that the technology does not impose a significant constraint on future KM options enables a dynamic KM strategy.

A dynamic KM strategy might have advantages because of the fact that communities of knowledge practice are comprised of affiliates subject to changing demands and conditions of work (von Krogh, 2002). In other words, in an organization, it is less a question of whether or not the firm possesses the right knowledge culture and state-of-art KMS, but more a question of having the flexibility an evolution of knowledge needs. A knowledge organization not only requires focusing on different stages of the KM evolvement but also the flexibility to change focus as demanded by the user community.

Some research is needed to examine how successful KM organizations manage evolving changes within communities without losing the focus of their KM goals. Specifically, more effort is needed to identify the processes and mechanisms used to handle the dynamic changes of KM activities. Moreover, we also need to know about how different types of organizational culture and subcultures would interact with the changing conditions and environments of communities of practice in organizations.

## 4.2. Providing multiple channels/forums for diverse knowledge sharing needs and preferences

The second lesson learned is the need to provide multiple channels/forums for diverse knowledge sharing needs and preferences. As indicated in the early sections (initial single global forum effort began to see fragmentation, redundancy and lack of interaction), we learned that the oversight by Buckman Labs in recognizing the importance of having multiple region-based Communities of Practice, especially those dispersed across cultural and geographical boundaries, was cited as the main reason why the organization was unsuccessful in implementing a truly global KM initiative. As shown in the case study, all Communities of Practice from different countries/cultures were initially managed as if they were the same, without taking into consideration issues such as the need for a shared context, and linguistic and cultural differences.

The major implication of this second lesson is that global KM is about using KMS to help link various Communities of Practice across the globe—it is not merely about managing single Communities of Practice and then replicating the same practice across cultural boundaries as what Buckman Labs did and failed. From a community point of view, IT is a better used tool to link communities from different countries. A wide range of future research is needed in the area of examining the role of IS in the enablement of communal resources (von Krogh, 2002).

## 4.3. Continuous expansion of community of practice

The third lesson learned is the importance of expanding individuals’ community of practice. The community is an arena, where participants can realize diverse interests and objectives overtime as they see fit (Nonaka and Konno, 1998). After all, a community should be based upon common knowledge or interest. The specific involves enabling knowledge workers to identify people who share their interest. This implies that knowledge workers must continue to work towards bridging mini-communities into megaones.

It may be easier to communicate with each other if knowledge workers share common knowledge. For example, employees who work in similar divisions or are engaged in similar business activities may not be handicapped by the lack of language proficiency. Some of the existing KM studies have addressed the need to encourage individuals to share knowledge (Wasko and Faraj, 2000; Jarvenpaa and Staples, 2000), but studies have not yet addressed the question of encouraging individuals to share knowledge with people from different communities or to expand their communities. This was in fact one of the biggest barriers encountered by Buckman Labs in their global KM effort, and it was addressed by creating common knowledge Communities of Practice as opposed to a single global community or multi-region forum arrangement.

In the case, we also learned that the entire KM process had been carefully designed and implemented, from knowledge creation, sharing, storage, to dissemination, but what had not been considered was the question of whether or not individuals would share/seek knowledge outside their community. Such concern (employees would only share knowledge with people in their own communities) was highlighted as the major reason for the management to move its global KM practice from multi-forum design (geographically determined) to one that is arranged based on common knowledge shared globally (determined by degree of common knowledge shared).

Future research must give more attention to the formation, nurturing and impact of global Communities of Practice on global KMS. While most of the previous Communities of Practice-related studies have focus on the nature and nurturing of an individual CoP, very few attempts were made in exploring issues related to inter-Communities of Practice, in its role to facilitate global KM. One future research question is thus: what are the processes and mechanisms needed in order for employees to share their common knowledge with their peers in other Communities of Practice? And what are the processes and mechanisms needed in order for employees to seek knowledge from their peers in other Communities?

## 4.4. Exploring issues and concerns regarding the changing role of IT

Our case findings suggest that information technology can have different roles during the various phases of KM evolution and is a key part of establishing a global knowledge-sharing culture. The roles which IT had played in the three phrases can be considered as: IT as the overall knowledge infrastructure; IT as the linking mechanism between different Communities of Practice; and IT as a support to different Communities of Practice. This leads to the fourth lesson: the need to explore issues and concerns regarding the changing role of IT in global knowledge sharing. In many ways, it is IT that has made the organization of global KM a theoretical reality. Therefore, one cannot discount the importance of IT availability to the knowledge workers who participate in KM-related activities across the globe and are impacted by these processes. Two issues are then important to future studies on KMS. Firstly, there is a need to base such studies on an integrated understanding of the design of KMS and performance of KMS. This is important as we learned from the case that no one KMS design will fully satisfy the changing KM needs. From a KMS design point of view, Buckman Labs went through a number of different designs and approaches to ensure that best KM practices can be facilitated. An organization will have to re-adapt itself to different designs of KMS in order to bring out the best KM performance. Secondly, studies can look into other IT-based factors that have been highlighted in the literature as elements of the reasons why some of the KMS implementations have failed. Two factors in particular, are the designers’ failure to understand situated work practices (Grudin, 1994; Suchman, 1995), and the need to have a mind-set change— promoting knowledge as public good instead of private good in organization (Wasko and Faraj, 2000). Following this, KMS can only be effective if it is congruent with existing work practices rather than imposing new practices. However, if an organization desires to introduce a new practice—that of global knowledge sharing and use—then, IT is clearly a significant facilitator and an important aspect of the knowledge infrastructure.

## 5. Conclusion

Based on detailed empirical evidence of a globally operating organization, this paper has analyzed KM systems, processes and practices that support the development of global Communities of Practice. The findings in this study address the conditions surrounding the implementation of global KM as an organizational practice. Table 2 summarizes the key issues and findings, as well as implications both for management and for further research.

The case study focuses on the interrelationships between using KMS and nurturing global Communities of Practice in implementing global KM initiatives. The driving force of this study was the realization that most of the previous studies on KM focused on addressing issues of KM within a geographical area; almost none tackled the complex issues related to global KM implementation.

The research has closely examined knowledge initiatives implemented from 1992 to 1998 by Buckman Labs, focusing on the organization of global KM. The research traces Buckman Labs’ experience as a networked, global company organized as a set of linked Communities of Practice. The data used in the case study are derived from a variety of sources and help to create a retrospective picture of the company. The diversity of the issues raised in the case study also brings up important theoretical considerations about the manner in which global knowledge sharing is organized and some of the limitations of existing approaches. One of the major weaknesses in the existing KM approaches is the tendency to focus too much on either technological or cognitive issues. The present study, by addressing the imbalance between the uses of KMS and managing global Communities of Practice, seeks to make a novel and useful contribution to this area of research.
