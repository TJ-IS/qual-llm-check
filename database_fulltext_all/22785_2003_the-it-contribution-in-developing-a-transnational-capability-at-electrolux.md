# The IT contribution in developing a transnational capability at Electrolux

Sharm Manwani<sup>a,\*</sup>, Robert M. O’Keefe<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Henley Management College, Greenlands, Henley-on-Thames, Oxon RG9 3AU, UK <sup>b</sup>School of Management, University of Surrey, Guilford, Surrey, GU2 7XH, UK

Received 16 March 2002; accepted 8 May 2003

## Abstract

Transnational companies combine global integration with local responsiveness, catering to national and even regional tastes while obtaining efficiencies through global operations. What is the role of IT in enabling an organisation to become transnational? What actions ensure that IT helps, rather than hinders, the transition?

One major European company recognised as having a transnational disposition is Electrolux. Home Products Europe (HPE), the largest sector in Electrolux, has previously been investigated both by international business and IT researchers. This paper updates the longitudinal perspective of HPE, including an examination of the transnational change drivers. Using a tentative framework tha combines previous research into multinational and transnational IT policy, it shows that all of HPE’s IT strategies align with the framework. We identify a number of key obstacles in the HPE case that hindered the transition, most notably problems of data quality and integration, largely occurring due to acquisitions. We also note the slow changes in the working practices of distributed IT groups and suggest that future research and frameworks need to better address staff issues. q 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information technology; Policy; Transnational; Framework; Case study; Electrolux

## 1. Introduction

According to Bartlett and Ghoshal (1998), three strategies characterise the transnational company: global integration (efficiency), local differentiation (responsiveness), and worldwide innovation (learning). Whereas, global companies select integration policies, and multi-domestic companies focus on national responsiveness, transnational companies attempt to combine global integration with local responsiveness. Transnational strategies are being adopted by an increasing number of firms, as a result of the need to combine integration strategies, for example cross-border logistics or (in Europe) the single currency, with differentiation at the customer interface (Karimi and Konsynski, 1991). It is typically the largest companies in the world with multinational customers, products, and operations that need to implement these strategies.

A key theme of international researchers is the balance between the forces of integration and responsiveness (Prahalad and Doz, 1987; Roth and Morrison, 1990; Johnson, 1995; Bartlett and Ghoshal, 1998) and how to link IT to business strategies based on the multinational configuration (Karimi and Konsynski, 1991; Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1992; Ramarapu and Lado, 1995; Earl and Feeny, 1996). Previous research conducted by Earl and Feeny (1996) identifies aspects of IT policy that may help transnational capabilities, but concludes that work in this area is limited and that there is a need for longitudinal study of the evolution to transnational operation.

What is the role of IT in enabling an organisation to become transnational? What organisational actions ensure that IT helps, rather than hinders, the transition? This paper both contributes to our understanding of these questions and produces some initial answers. We use previous concepts and investigations to produce a tentative framework of IT transnational policy, which is then used to analyse the case of Electrolux Home Product Europe, the largest sector in Electrolux. Specifically, we identify some issues raised within this case that have not been addressed by previous research.

In the next sections, we briefly review prior research, explain our research methods, and then describe and analyse the case study before drawing conclusions for future research.

## 2. International business and IT

## 2.1. The drive to become transnational

Large international organisations face a range of global challenges such as defining target markets, products, and geography, introducing core technologies, and balancing growth, survival, and profit (Morris 1996; Roche, 1996). There is often a tension in multinational companies between global standards and local operations. This can be represented graphically by the integration–responsiveness (I–R) grid shown in Fig. 1 (Prahalad and Doz, 1987). I–R has become a fairly well accepted way of explaining the balance between integration and responsiveness across company types, and work based upon I – R is used later in this paper.

Key drivers in Europe have been the Single European Act (SEA 1988) which removed many economic barriers, and later the introduction of the Euro. Companies needed to respond to the challenges created by a market which for many industries has become increasingly pan-European.

As an example of a specific response to these drivers, 3M moved from a countryoriented organisation to one based on European product lines. In doing this, 3M recognised the challenges: ‘to run a business across 20 countries in Europe with multiple cultures, at least a dozen languages and different distribution channels, you have to understand Europe (Ackenhusen et al., 1996, 34). 3M was one of a number of companies that restructured during this period as evidenced by a report commissioned by the Conference Board Europe (Bleackley and Williamson, 1995). In this study, three dimensions of integration were categorised: rationalisation, centralisation, and geographic scope. Many companies had at the same time expanded their sales and marketing networks in order to be more responsive to the customer. One item that was particularly revealing and relevant to this research was the finding related to IT infrastructure. Evidence was found of:

## 2.2. IT strategic response

Transnational strategies require an effective IT Policy, which is an explicit set of mandates and directives and sets the criteria for the architecture (Keen, 1991). Daniels (1994) reinforced the importance of an IT policy to enable globalisation, stressing that the infrastructure takes a significant time to build.

Keen (1991) introduced the concept of reach and range to define the business functionality of the corporate IT platform. Reach refers to the locations that a platform can link to; it extends from internal to external locations. Range refers to the ability to share information directly and automatically across systems. The IT responses to globalisation include corporate networks and databases (Karimi and Konsynski, 1991). An extension of this view shown in Table 1 was developed by Ramarapu and Lado (1995) who created a contingency framework matching business strategy groupings to IT architectures, showing that managers need to adapt IT approaches based on the type of ‘global’ strategy adopted.

The base requirement for integration is the definition and communication of standard data. For organisations wishing to go further the recommended strategy is ‘to implement standard applications systems world-wide with the additional goals to ensure the integrity of information, facilitate the transfer of activities and people, and perhaps achieve scale economies in systems development and processing’ (Earl and Feeny, 1996: 78).

Brown and Magill (1994) developed a model of antecedents that could best explain IS transformation decisions. Variables included: extent of connectivity since an increase in connectivity may be required to provide enterprise-wide applications; data sharing and database architecture responding to pressures for company-wide data standards and potential investments in databases; and extent of application sharing as there may be requirements for common applications and central transactions. These are valid IT variables, however, they do not explain the business drivers that cause the change in the IT policy variable.

Brown and Magill (1994) used a classification of IT structures as centralised, decentralised, and shared to categorise six large companies. They found that the structure was not consistent across the IT groups, findings that support a contingency approach in defining the IT architecture policy.

## 2.3. Synthesising the IT policy architecture

IT policy is an enabler for companies to move to a transnational strategy dictated by industry pressures. We can conclude from the time it takes to implement an infrastructure that the IT policy should reflect the directional goals of the I–R framework rather than match the existing situation. Policy contents include technology and communications, applications and data, management practices and staff. Previous classifications of IT responses to transnational demands have provided important insights, albeit within a partial IT policy framework. This gap is addressed in this paper by constructing a composite comparison of multi-domestic and transnational IT strategies. The resulting tentative framework has been adapted and extended from Keen (1991); Brown and Magill (1994); Ramarapu and Lado (1995); Earl and Feeny (1996). Strategies are grouped into management of technology and management of applications using Brown and Magill’s classification (see Tables 2 and 3).

The transnational column reflects an intermediate position between multi-domestic and global. In the latter structure we could expect a highly centralised system whereas in the transnational the focus is on interdependency and sharing. This tentative framework potentially offers the ability to analyse the IT policy of a multinational company and establish if it will enable the organisation to move to a transnational positioning. It is not presented as a complete or finished framework, but rather a starting point from which a case can be analysed. In effect, it represents the IT policies either hypothesised or observed by the referenced authors. We use it as a starting point to analyse the HPE case.

## 3. Research methods

IT strategy and multinational positioning are two areas that have received much academic attention individually. Yet prior research on their interaction is less prevalent, leading to calls for proposed research from Earl and Feeny (1996) which are fully in line with the authors’ goals.

The critical questions seem to be ones of information management strategy …one way of doing such managerial research is through case studies. They allow multidisciplinary, integrative enquiry…Longitudinal case studies—not least further monitoring of events in the four firms reported here—therefore could be valuable if theory development is in part making sense of firms’ actions. Another direction for research is to extend investigations into the transnational organisation (Earl and Feeny, 1996, 97).

## 3.1. Overview of approach

Electrolux HPE was one of the four firms investigated by Earl and Feeny. They reviewed the IS strategy of HPE and noted that a simplistic solution of implementing common systems had been avoided in favour of a shared data response that met the business and the cultural need.

Our longitudinal study collected primary data from April 1996 to December 1999, although secondary data relates back to the 1980s. The HPE data derived from a number of rich and varied sources since there is considerable prior research on the industry and Electrolux. The first author had access to many historical documents as a senior Electrolux manager. Further, data were collected through a variety of data collection methods including business and IT executive interviews, supplemented by observation and documentation of key meetings (detailed in Table 4).

In order to validate and analyse the transnational stance of HPE, a questionnaire on business strategy was derived based on the I–R instrument. Additionally, semi-structured interviews were conducted with a range of stakeholders at multiple organisation levels— corporate headquarters, regional business sector, and local business unit. These taped interviews supplemented the review of key documents such as the corporate IT policy standards, and major European and national investment business cases. Table 4 presents a summary of the primary sources of data.

The perspectives generated by the diverse range of interviews provided a range of views on the policies and practices in HPE. Specifically, there was a hierarchical dimension (corporate, HPE sector, and three country business units—UK, France, Finland) and a functional view (business, IT). Together these views offered an in-depth understanding of the issues facing a multi-domestic company evolving into a transnational and the resulting IT policies.

## 3.3. Analysis of transnational IT policy using the framework

The corporate CIO and two members of his team were interviewed on the development and implementation of the IT policy. Discussion points included the drivers for the IT policy and also the creation of a business IT model designed to support HPE which was based on Enterprise resource planning (ERP) and integrated bespoke components. An analysis of the European IT strategy was supplemented by interviews with process and regional managers. This created a representation of how the IT technologies and applications supported the cross-border business initiatives.

Interviews with local business and IT managers in Finland, France, and UK provided the national perspectives. Views were sought on the IT investment drivers and the factors affecting compliance with the IT policy. Interviewees included business and IT managers involved in the implementation of the Electrolux business IT model. The results of the data collection were analysed and compared to the tentative framework to assess how the implementation of IT supported and hindered the transformation to a transnational.

In summary, the I–R instrument questionnaire was used to evaluate the business strategy, and consider the extent to which HPE was moving to a transnational position. Data were collected on the IT policies, and then the tentative IT framework was used to evaluate the IT policies in the selected case of HPE. Our presentation of the case now follows.

## 4. Case study

First we explore the development of Electrolux as a transnational company, its differential positioning in relation to its competitors, and specifically the HPE cross-border strategy as a response to the transnational drivers. We then examine the extent to which IT policy enabled this transition to a transnational.

## 4.1. Electrolux

The origins of Electrolux go back one hundred years and include the introduction of the vacuum cleaner and the invention of the absorption refrigerator. In 1997,

Electrolux was a multinational company based in Sweden with more than 100,000 employees and revenues in excess of \$10 billion, focused on three core areas: Electrolux Appliances, Commercial Appliances and Outdoor Products (Nordicum, 1997). Although labelled as a global company because of its worldwide presence, Electrolux has been assessed as multi-domestic by separate cross-industry research (Ghoshal and Nohria, 1990). Yet increasingly, Electrolux is recognised as embracing a transnational stance (Bartlett and Ghoshal, 1998; Earl and Feeny, 1996). In the late 1990s there was a move towards ‘fewer, but larger manufacturing sites’ and ‘considerable consolidation as the single European market pulled down national trade barriers, though not to so great an extent as in the US’ (Logistics Europe, 1998, 34). The somewhat conflicting positions of Electrolux may be reconciled partly by different definitions and partly through the gap between current positioning and aspiration.

David Whitwam, the CEO of Whirlpool, a major competitor of Electrolux, explained his philosophy of going global (Maruca, 1994, 137–145).

Our vision is to be one company worldwide…the only way to deliver (long-term shareholder) value is by focusing on the customer… I’ll know we’ve arrived as a global company when we have cross-border product business teams…running all our operations….

The philosophies expounded by Whirlpool were relevant to the development of Electrolux. In common with other Swedish multinationals, Electrolux had experienced strong domestic demand after the Second World War (So¨lvell and Zander, 1997). Later it grew by acquisition with Zanussi–Italy in 1984 and AEG–Germany in 1993 (Lynch, 1994), using mergers and acquisitions as a key strategic tool for growth or entry into markets (Illes and Clarkson, 1997). By the late 1980s Electrolux had a portfolio of over twenty major brands and more than two hundred factories in more than forty countries. It then embarked on a process of rationalisation moving from a multi-domestic position to a transnational one (Bartlett and Ghoshal, 1998).

Electrolux’s management style has been described thus:

in many international companies.there is a matrix structure…national management teams and international product teams…in Electrolux there is a third element as well: decentralised strategic business units. This has produced a highly complex structure with its own tensions. (Lynch, 1994).

Electrolux is considered as having had a different style to its competitors:

Electrolux’s global expansion has been accomplished by delegation and autonomy at the local level, an approach midway between the integration of Whirlpool–Philips and the narrow focus of Hotpoint (Baden-Fuller and Stopford, 1991, 65).

## 4.2. Electrolux–Home Products Europe

HPE, the specific subject of the case study, is the largest division of Electrolux and the European market leader in home appliances. Most households in Western Europe have a refrigerator, cooker, and washing machine resulting in a ‘fairly mature’ white goods market (Logistics Europe, 1998: 38). By the early 1990s, HPE had forty-three factories in fifteen countries with a turnover of more than US \$5 billions (Earl and Feeny, 1996). The Home Appliances industry has been examined from the viewpoint of increasing product standardisation globally. In particular, Hoover was accused of a failure of imagination for reacting to local research instead of aggressively selling a simple, standardised high quality washing machine at a low price (Levitt, 1983).

As the largest sector, HPE led the way in major business and IT developments with an annual spend in excess of \$50million. Strategy and Marketing executives in HPE were interviewed on their perceptions of the European White Goods industry using the questionnaire based on the I–R instrument. Respondents identified integration pressures as: concentration of trade channels, increasing scale economies, and European Union influence. In contrast, national responsiveness pressures included a greater demand for trade partner service and a wide variation in costs. Responses demonstrated a clear trend away from the multi-domestic position towards a transnational position.

## 4.3. Cross-border strategy

In 1991 the HPE organisational structure consisted of: product divisions responsible for development and manufacturing, international marketing units controlling specific brands, national sales companies, country organisations and centralised service functions such as IT. Subsequently the role of the country manager was downsized to a holding company function. Structural challenges were significant requiring, according to Bartlett and Ghoshal (1998), three different roles from the CEO: global business strategist, configuration architect, and cross-border coordinator. The AEG acquisition added a third major (German) culture and brand to the Swedish (Electrolux) and the Italian (Zanussi) set-up. These acquisitions provided a developing context for the three roles.

Global business strategy, the first role, evolved with the dual aims of positioning brands to target the increasingly segmented marketplace and balancing cross-border brands with the local brands. This strategy recognised that while more standardised products were needed, local responsiveness was also important; sometimes for local tastes such as frontloading versus top-loading washing machines but also where consumers favoured a brand, sales channel, or promotion medium. One difficulty with setting a cross-border brand strategy is the inherited positions of a brand, which in the case of Zanussi and Electrolux varied both across countries and across product categories in the same country. As evidenced by PIMS (profit impact on market strategy) studies, it is not easy to dramatically change consumer perceptions and a company may not have the time or money to do so (Buzzell and Gale, 1987). Hence while pan-European brands were a strong feature of the strategy in the early-mid 1990s there was considerable debate internally on the feasibility of this strategy during the mid-late 1990s.

Configuration decisions such as where to locate key plants or other strategic resources as a critical second role. Three guiding principles for these decisions in the early-mid 1990s were: dispersal of facilities, building on existing capabilities, and matching to competency. Perhaps the most contentious of these asset configuration decisions was to build on existing plants even if they were in high cost locations. When Electrolux initiated a restructuring program in the mid-late 1990s some of these high cost plants were targeted for closure.

The third role of cross-border coordinator in the early-mid 1990s involved issues such as setting transfer prices between the thirty-five different national marketing companies and the twenty-nine different factories. Business units were initially measured on local results and therefore took an internal negotiating stance. In the mid-to-late 1990s the operating locations were measured on integrated profit and stock. This change was lengthy and painful given both the limitations of the information/accounting systems and the cultural change required.

One element of the cross-border strategy that became increasingly important during the 1990s was the merger of key customers in Europe. The retailer Kingfisher expanded from its UK Comet base acquiring companies in France, Benelux and then Germany. As with the manufacturers, retailers adopted different strategies. For example, while Comet was positioned as a discounter, Darty in France had a service and quality image and was given the autonomy to maintain this position (Horovitz and Kumar, 1998). This diverse positioning was in contrast with another international retailer of white goods, IKEA, which had grown organically and had a more standard offering. An HPE Marketing senior vicepresident explained the importance.

(Retailers) are more open to the idea of a partnership with a company like Electrolux which offers an international and global business solution, and is prepared to share that knowledge with a retailer in exchange for more real time market data about their consumers’ purchasing demands (HPE Senior VP, Jan 1998).

## 4.4. Sector IT policy

Cross-border and cross-sector customers were one of the driving forces behind the new IT policy. Prior to the creation of the HPE business sector there was no identifiable Sector IT Policy; sectors relied heavily on the Corporate IT Policy, known in Electrolux as the ‘Basic IT Directives’. This included application and technology standards complemented by group purchasing agreements. For example, JDE was the ERP standard and IBM AS/400 was the commercial mid-range computer standard. The Swedish Corporate CIO commented on the policy.

When we look upon the standardised components, if we look upon cost parameters, of course we have group purchasing agreements with the potential in that area …we can also go further here and look upon not only standardised components but also standardised shared data—not only for cost potential reasons but also business reasons—look upon our present and future customers and customer segments in this area-where they are looking for a lot of commonality from a group like Electrolux even with different business sectors (Corporate CIO interview, January 1998).

The Corporate IT Group initially had responsibility both for policies and services. These roles were split in 1997 with the set-up of a new IT Services organisation designed to consolidate the decentralised operations and development groups into cross-location teams. The sectors were asked to create business-oriented IT responsibles who could act as buyers of IT Services while retaining the benefits of corporate IT agreements. In HPE, the CEO set a goal to create a network of IT buyers across the different processes and operating units. A network was an important concept to signify a balance between integration and responsiveness, in effect a one company, customer-driven approach. In a similar vein, a new architecture was defined together with corporate IT. The concept behind the IT model was a process-driven set of standard and business-specific applications within a shared data environment.

An example of this approach was an in-house developed system called REX that was employed by HPE for spare parts ordering and stock control although other business sectors used it for finished goods logistics. After much internal debate, REX was set up by HPE as a common application on a central computer. This was a major breakthrough in demonstrating that a single, central application could support multiple companies across Europe. The path was not an easy one and it took from 1995 until 2000 to reach the stage where all HPE units were using the central computer system for spare parts management.

A different application, the Electrolux Forecasting System (EFS) was a key tool for managing the finished product supply chain between factories and sales units. In a review of EFS, Earl and Feeny (1996) commented favourably that this was an IS initiative that was closely linked to a business initiative. They noted that EFS was interfaced with local order processing systems instead of HPE trying to replace every local system by a common package.

In a similar vein, the Electrolux Profitability System (EPS) was designed to address the cross-border issue of integrated profitability. The challenge as with EFS was to ensure that the local data entered into the systems was accurate. This was not easy with many different order processing systems and this together with maintenance costs and year 2000 issues became drivers for JDE to be implemented as the order processing and accounting standard. The goal was to interface with EFS and EPS to form an IT model.

A variation of the IT model was created to support the development of a new business model for pan-European strategic accounts. The new strategic accounts model was piloted with a major retailer. This involved moving from a make-for-stock to a make-to-order model. Speed of implementation was critical and therefore the national sales company system was bypassed with a new Internet front-end data entry module used directly by the strategic account. There were security policies for use of the Internet but no defined application development standard as it was an emerging technology for business-tobusiness systems in Electrolux in the late 1990s.

Product development, in common with the supply chain, was a process that connected the market to the factory hence sharing data was again important. This was achieved through workflow applications developed on Lotus Notes which was an office automation standard in Electrolux. Management information needed to be accessed from the operational systems, often across countries. Users had become accustomed to local query tools and these were retained, however, a standard database format was implemented to ensure an exchange capability.

The IT model was installed in a shared IT environment to enable a shared services centre for the Region West countries of Belgium, Netherlands, Luxembourg (collectively Benelux), UK, and Ireland. This required a central IT services operation and a more powerful data communications network. Concerns were expressed by the UK IT manager about having to participate in a shared services environment with Benelux as part of Region West.

We have to be very careful here—as soon as we go down the route of a shared environment with Benelux you introduce a whole new host of political issues… (UK IT Manager, August 1998).

France and Finland were able to avoid the first phase of this centralisation as their projects had already commenced. Both companies had significant challenges implementing JDE without adding another dimension.

The CEO had two major concerns about the IT strategy at that time. Firstly that EFS was front-ended by a forecasting system that solidified the process whereby the sales company tried to second-guess the market rather than using actual market data from retailers. Secondly that the JDE order processing system was reinforcing the sales company role, which would be changed in the future. His goal was to transfer data from the market source to all the active partners in the supply chain, allowing decisions to be made in real time by people with knowledge to use the data.

In summary there were serious concerns at various levels in HPE that the sector IT policy would lead to a heavy centralised, common approach driven by a remote corporate group. The challenge was to ensure and demonstrate that the HPE policy would both help to create one company and be responsive to customer needs.

## 5. Analysis

In this section we consolidate the IT-enabled evolution of HPE to analyse the contribution of the IT policy to a transnational positioning. We then use the tentative IT policy framework to assess the management actions of HPE, and the extent to which these helped or hindered the move to a transnational.

Table 8 similarly maps the actions at HPE in the areas of applications and data onto the tentative framework. Our data suggests that data quality and integration was a major challenge for HPE. Although the technological infrastructure could be created, getting the right data in the right place was far harder.

One major barrier to data integration was the starting point of a large number of acquired companies and hence different applications and inconsistent data. HPE had over 800 different applications and databases as a result of acquisitions. Large companies typically take several years to implement new systems internationally and we can regard the acquisition heritage as a contingent factor in the speed of transition in HPE. The case of HPE, exemplified in Table 8 by data management and transaction processing, suggests that data must be given a high focus in terms of defining both the business data entities and the technical database items to ensure that sharing and conversion of data are enabled.

Application standards were set in the corporate IT policy and reflected in the sector IT policy. However, component standards were not enough in HPE. An integrated IT model covering both the new application packages and the retained legacy systems was needed. This highlights the need for organisations to develop a target architecture and a realistic plan to achieve it in stages (HPE demonstrated a limited success in Region West with this approach).

The tentative framework does not include IT staff as a major factor. Within HPE, staff changes were another challenge. Movement towards cross-location teams was painstakingly slow, in part due to the time taken to create the new consolidated IT services structure across multiple countries in Europe. IT staff, while familiar with implementing change, were no more receptive than any other group to being the receiving entity.

In summary, HPE was able to address the technology issues more easily than the application solutions which were hindered by data and people issues. A contingent factor in HPE was the acquisition heritage moving from a multi-domestic to a transnational.

## 6. Conclusions

What is the role of IT in enabling an organisation to become transnational? The journey taken by HPE shows IT can help balance the constructive tensions in an organisation between integration and responsiveness. IT can be used to centralise applications, but local modules can be used to make areas or regions more responsive. Similarly, IT can be used to centralise data, but common query facilities can make that data available locally. Our analysis of HPE suggests that there is no single IT perspective that characterises a transnational, rather it is the combination of the of IT solutions that support the goal.

What organisational actions ensure that IT helps, rather than hinders, the transition? Within the HPE case, technology issues were addressed relatively easily. Shared IT services were created, and common platforms identified. However, data quality and integration proved to be a far more difficult issue. Large numbers of different databases and applications hindered HPE in moving to a transnational position, and this was exacerbated by the need to integrate acquisitions. This perhaps suggests that organisations need to focus more on data integration and quality.

Our tentative framework, as a summary of existing theory and literature, was useful in analysing the case. The application of the framework confirmed that technology issues are easier to address than applications and data issues. We do not posit the framework as new theory in any sense, but it perhaps points to where future theory is necessary. In particular, there is a need to better understand what happens to people when a company transitions to a transnational. In HPE, a move towards cross border teams and working was very slow. Thus any future framework or model of IT policy differences required by various strategic positions, including transnational ones, should better address people.

Our conclusions need to be understood within the context of Electrolux HPE. Electrolux was able and willing to invest in IT. It exhibits a management style that is derived from its Scandinavian roots, with respect for consensus. Irrespective of this, our position is that all organisations moving to a transnational position need to understand the challenges inherent in their starting point, and the necessary commitment to technology, applications, data and people. We recommend, in line with the Earl and Feeny (1996) call for future research, that further longitudinal case studies are developed to broaden the database of transnational companies, and provide additional guidance for practitioners.
