# An extended platform logic perspective of IT governance: managing perceptions and activities of IT

A. Schwarz<sup>\*</sup>, R. Hirschheim

Department of Information Systems and Decision Sciences, E.J. Ourso College of Business Administration, Louisiana State University, Baton Rouge, LA 70803, USA

Accepted 19 May 2003

## Abstract

Over the years, Information Technology (IT) has struggled with how to create an effective structure and processes. It is our main thesis that if organizations focused more on implementing a sound IT governance strategy,itmighthelpsenior executivestomanagenotonlythe IT-related activities,butalso the perceptions between IT and the rest of the organization, and, in doing so, foster a more successful IT organization. Using six case studies conducted within the oil and gas industry, we explore differences in perceptions toward IT and in the organization of IT activities. Using an Extended Platform Logic Perspective, we note differences and similarities between the firms, with respect to IT capabilities, relational and integration mechanisms, measures of success, and relationships with the business units. Our results suggest that our colleagues-in-practice have evolved from focusing on one-way architectures within a centralization/decentralization context toward a two-way relationship-oriented approach to managing the IT structure. We conclude by offering some thoughts on how IT executives can help to shape perceptions of IT within their firms and explore how academics can begin to help our colleagues-in-practice as they struggle with the governance of the IT function.

Keywords: Information Technology Management; Oil and gas industry; Governance Platform Logic Mode Relation-Based Management

## 1. Introduction

One of the most enduring problems faced by the Information Technology (IT) function is how it should organize and structure itself.<sup>1</sup> The term ‘governance’ has been used to broadly describe the policies, structures, and management processes involved in managing the IT function (Brown and Sambamurthy, 1999; Weill and Broadbent, 2000). Indeed, implementing a sound IT governance strategy is believed to be key for having a successful IT function in organizations (Brown, 1997). It is our main thesis that if organizations focused more on implementing a sound IT governance strategy, it might help senior executives to manage not only the IT-related activities, but also the perceptions between IT and the rest of the organization, and, in doing so, foster a more successful IT organization.

In this paper, we use six case studies that were conducted within the oil and gas industry to explore the broad issue of IT governance. In operationalizing ‘IT governance’ we chose to use the notion coined by Sambamurthy and Zmud (2000)—that of ‘organization logic for the IT function’ and especially their ‘platform logic model’. This allows us to move beyond the more basic centralization–decentralization issue of governance, to focus on the practical IT capabilities, relational architectures, and integration architectures that are currently being used in ‘successful’ IT organizations. By focusing on these capabilities and architectures, we hope to elucidate new opportunities for the IT function.

We should like to add that the motivation for our work comes from the Sambamurthy and Zmud (2000) call for researchers to better understand the challenges and discoveries of contemporary IT management. They argue that the academic community should begin by examining the logic for organizing IT activities. With the call in mind, we shall apply the platform logic model to IT governance. However, as we discuss in Section 2, we will first extend the original model and add a new dimension to the model: a metric of success. Our ‘extended platform logic model’ will then be used as a theoretical lens to interpret the governance arrangements within six organizations. We believe that by understanding how IT responds to environmental and business imperatives in the structuring of required IT capabilities, and the integration and relational architectures that derive from those capabilities, we will then be able to explain the differences in perceptions that appear to exist between IT and rest of the organization (Ward and Peppard, 1996; Enns and Huff, 2000; Peppard and Ward, 1999).

## 2. Governing the IT function

Historically, IT governance has been strongly associated with the structure or configuration of the IT function, thus reflecting the locus of responsibility for making IT management decisions. Three possibilities are typically identified. Organizations in which the locus of responsibility for making IT management decisions belongs to a corporate or a central unit are said to be centralized; if it belongs to a business unit or department it is termed decentralized, and if it is divided between these groups it is termed shared or federal or hybrid (Camillus and Lederer, 1985; Tavakolian, 1989; Main and Short, 1989; Fiedler et al., 1996). However, we believe that such a view of governance is inadequate. Focusing on IT structure alone ignores the sphere of IT activities that take place within these structures. To this end Sambamurthy and Zmud (2000) defined governance as “an organization’s IT-related authority pattern” including the activities that are governed within the structures. They argue that researchers need to understand the organizing logic for the IT function which they define as “the managerial rationale for designing and evolving specific organizational arrangements in response to an enterprise’s environmental and strategic imperatives”.

For the purpose of this paper, we have defined governance as the “IT related structures or architectures (and associated authority pattern) implemented to successfully accomplish (IT imperative) activities in response to an enterprise’s environmental and strategic imperatives.” In defining governance in this way, we are including three essential elements to governance:<sup>2</sup>

1. Strategic and environmental imperatives that define a necessary response from IT.<sup>3</sup>

2. Structures designed to support the response.

3. An imperative for IT to be successful in this design.

The definition does not depart from previous research into governance choice. The first element of the new definition, strategic and environmental imperatives, embraces research that attempted to understand the governance structure by examining business and environmental factors (Olson and Chervany, 1980; Ein-Dor and Segev, 1982; Earl, 1989; Brown, 1997). Further, our approach to governance is also consistent with previous definitions of governance as ‘an organization’s IT-related authority pattern’ (Sambamurthy and Zmud, 2000, 1999), for it recognizes the authority structures of IT.

Nonetheless, our definition embraces new notions of governance as well. The proposed definition of governance includes recognition of the source of the authority pattern and the result of that decision. While past research has isolated individual factors that help to explain why firms engage in governance arrangements, none has attempted to link governance choice to a metric of success. While in a recent survey of governance research, Agarwal and Sambamurthy (2002) note that “there is no single ‘best’ IT organizational structure or governance arrangement because IT needs to respond to the unique environments within which it exists,” we argue that it is imperative for IT to examine its own success at creating a governance arrangement that includes the structures and policies of IT. We believe our definition moves governance research toward this end, for it allows us, and by extension other researchers, to examine the result of the arrangements that are in place.

The concept of IT capabilities is linked to the first element of our definition of governance: strategic and environmental imperatives that cause IT to define the capabilities that the firm requires—does IT need to provide value creation and innovation, leverage knowledge work, or a combination of the capabilities for the firm to be successful?

Next, according to their platform logic view, IT organizational architectures are “the means for enabling and harvesting a stream of valued IT capabilities” and include two types of architectures: relational and integration. Relational architectures differ from typical governance models of IT in that they focus on how IT interacts with, and is related to, the business units (Kern and Willcocks, 2001). Examples of such relational structures include account managers and alliance management units. Thus, relational architectures are formed in order to source, configure, and deliver IT capabilities through the formally established relationships within the organization. We argue that there are two types of relational architectures: intraorganizational, or architectures that operate within the firm to deliver capabilities (e.g. account managers) and extraorganizational, or architectures that extend beyond the firm to deliver the capabilities (e.g. outsourcing).

Integration architectures are the organizational overlays (or the infrastructure that facilities the implementation of the activities within the IT function) used to integrate IT planning into the business. Integration architectures serve two functions: coordination and identity. Coordination is the orchestrated interplay of IT and business capabilities (Barua and Mukhopadhyay, 2000), or the mechanisms that IT uses in order to increase its perception within the firm. Identity refers to how the meaning of IT is established within the corporation.<sup>4</sup>

The two architectures reflect element #2 of our definition of governance—the structures designed to support the response to the required capabilities. These architectures mark key differences in the conceptualization of governance, for they do not focus upon the locus of decision making (centralized, decentralized, or shared), but rather on how IT delivers the capabilities. It should be noted, however, that while the structure of these architectures is typically in the form of centralized, decentralized, or shared, the focus in studying governance from this perspective it not to understand the form, but in the mechanisms themselves and how they are linked to the capabilities.

Once these three elements of Sambamurthy and Zmud’s platform logic have been combined, they offer a model that can be used to investigate the organization of IT activities. The model is displayed in Fig. 1.

According to the model, for researchers to investigate the organization of IT activities, the critical IT capabilities must first be examined. Then, the relational architectures that have been built to handle the capabilities should be examined. Finally, the integration architectures that are used between the IT function and the business units should be investigated.

Implicit in the platform logic model are feedback loops that allow executives to constantly update the capabilities and architectures decision. Within the discussion, one of the research questions that Sambamurthy and Zmud offer is “What criteria are most important in assessing the effectiveness of IT-enabled transformation process?” Yet, despite the question, their model does not delineate how or where the measure of ‘success’ is made. We believe that such measurement is critical to understanding IT, and thus offer, as an extension to the platform logic model, a fourth dimension: success metric.

Although the term ‘success’ has been used within the literature to depict different concepts, we leave the defining of success open since different companies will have different meanings of the term and what metrics they have in place to gauge the success of their organization of IT activities.<sup>5</sup> Indeed, one of the secondary objectives of our research is to determine precisely what organizations mean by ‘IT success’ and how they measure it. Since the intent of both the integration and relational architectures is to help shape management’s perception of IT, it makes sense to have the managers that interact with IT on a daily basis define the success of IT. Thus, the responsibility of IT is to create mechanisms that determine if IT is being successful in the minds of their users. Previous research on IT success focused on the economic benefits of IT (Brynjolffson, 1993; Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 1996; Hitt and Brynjolffson, 1996; Bharadwaj, 2000). However, now ‘many organizations have progressed from elementary cost–benefit analysis, akin to book-keeping, towards a more entrepreneurial approach which seeks to deliver firm benefits while considering intangible aspects and elements of risk and uncertainty’ (Serafeimidis and Smithson, 2000). The progression means that IT organizations are now utilizing a multi-faceted success metric, not limited to just financial benefits of IT, but how IT is being perceived. An example of a multi-dimensional approach is the balanced scorecard, which has attracted much academic attention (Van der Zee and Jong, 1999; Martinsons et al., 1999; Nolan, Norton, and Co., 1991). However, this research seeks to understand what approaches are currently being used in practice, recognizing that the business unit assessments of the value of IT services may be different across the organization and a structure must be in place to assess the ultimate success of IT (Ross et al., 1999). This extension reflects the third element of our definition of governance—the metric to gauge success. We thus offer an extended platform logic model for organizing IT activities (Fig. 2).

In this paper, we explore how large organizations go about governing the IT function. While Sambamurthy and Zmud (2000) provide some examples of this, we believe a more detailed description is warranted. Thus, using the extended platform logic model, our research investigates the IT capabilities, relational architectures, integration architectures, and success metrics in six firms in the oil and gas industry. We believe that by analyzing firms in the same industry, a richer understanding of IT governance can be found, since the IT issues will essentially be the same for each firm. Moreover, we seek to show the importance of ‘success metrics’ when attempting to understand how firms create a governance structure. It is our belief, that in studying six firms in the same industry (where IT is critical), we will be better able to explore the existence (and possible resolution) of any mismatch of expectations between IT and the business units. This research thus supports the call put forth by Sambamurthy and Zmud (2000) for academics to explore how IT organizations respond to their companies’ environmental and strategic imperatives, and in so doing offer some possible answers to the problems facing the CIO (specifically) and the IT function (more generally).

## 3. Methodology

In seeking to examine how companies go about creating a governance structure, we conducted six case studies in the oil and gas industry focusing on each company’s central IT department. We used our extended platform logic model as the basis for interpreting each company’s IT governance pattern. A qualitative approach based on case studies was considered appropriate given the small amount of prior empirical research on the broad area of IT governance, and the sensitive nature of the data needed (Benbasat et al., 1987; Yin, 1984). Moreover, the focus of the study was on the types of IT governance patterns each organization engaged in and how they differed or stayed the same across organizations. In order to understand the thought processes underlying these patterns, it was essential to incorporate the perspectives of a variety of IT executives. At the same time, in order to achieve some understanding of the possible differences, we needed to examine them through multiple cases.

## 3.1. The case sites

The oil and gas industry was chosen for this research because of the extremely important role that IT plays in the success of the companies in this industry. The selection of the case sites was based on a combination of accessibility (to the company’s IT executives), and interestingness (in the sense that each chosen company’s IT organization was considered as being within the ‘top performer’ category in the oil and gas industry’s (American Petroleum Institute: API) benchmarking database). We use the pseudonyms<sup>6</sup> Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, Epsilon and Zeta to represent the six organizations in this paper.

## 3.2. Data collection

Each case involved the examination of four primary areas: IT capabilities, relational architectures, integration architectures, and success metrics. These areas were studied retrospectively through non-directive interviews with the executives involved in strategic IT management at each organization. In an effort to address the potential limitations of examining evolving phenomenon through retrospective interviews, we interviewed multiple informants from different backgrounds and at varying hierarchical levels. This use of a variety of informants, along with the examination of internal company documents, provided multiple perspectives on the four elements of the extended platform logic model and enabled cross-checking of the perceived interactions among these elements.

We asked the informants to discuss the various activities they perceived IT was engaged in their respective organizations. (Appendix A provides a list of questions asked.) We did not privilege one interpretation over others—any contrasting interpretations discovered were of considerable interest in their own right. Each interview was taperecorded, with additional notes being taken when necessary, and then transcribed. A total of 10 interviews, lasting about 20 h, were conducted with 18 informants.<sup>7</sup> At each sited we typically interviewed between two and four people. All interviewees were senior IT managers<sup>8</sup> responsible for the delivery of IT products and services to the various business units. The interviews were carried out during the period April and May 2001.

## 3.3. Data analysis

Being based on only six cases, our results may seem somewhat particularistic. However, we tried to produce more general explanations (Eisenhardt, 1989) through ‘analytic generalization’ (Yin, 1984), where ‘the generalization is of theoretical concepts and patterns’ (Orlikowski, 1993, p. 310). As recommended by Glaser and Strauss (1967), the concepts and patterns were linked to the proposed extended platform logic model. A three-step process was followed.

Step 1: initial analysis of transcripts. We read each transcript carefully, highlighting the interviewee comments on IT activities, what they were, whether they were successful, if so why and if not, why not, and what they meant by successful. Additionally, corporate material in the form of IT strategy documents, organizational charts, company brochures, annual reports, IT department presentation materials, etc. were requested and studied.

Step 2: formal interpretation of transcripts. During this step, the interview transcripts for the cases were analyzed in a more structured fashion. In order to facilitate consistent interpretation, we used a common set of brief definitions of the various types of IT capabilities and architectures (Tables 1 – 3). We used this common form to analyze the transcripts.

Step 3: analysis of the formal interpretations. After studying the interviews and supporting materials, comments were divided into five areas, including quotations containing information about IT capabilities, relational architectures, integration architectures, success metrics, and insight into IT – business unit relationship (for a detailed account of these interviews and associated quotations, see Appendix B). Our protocol was to first analyze the interviews to determine the capabilities of IT at all six firms. After determining the IT capabilities for each firm, the next step was to determine the relational architectures adopted. Again, we did this for all six firms. Next, we looked for integration architectures, and finally, success metrics.

Together, Steps 2 and 3 helped us to decontextualize interviewee comments and then recontextualize them (Tesch, 1990). In other words, they helped us to take the interviewee comments out of their original context (i.e. an interview), and present them in a different context (i.e. assemble all comments that all the interviewees in a case made about a particular IT activity).

## 4.2. Relational architectures

After determining the IT capabilities for each firm, the next step was the analysis of relational architectures. Relational architectures were found by examining the transcripts for specific governance mechanisms that were created for the purpose of delivering the capabilities. Beta provides an example of relational architectures. Having undergone a recent change, Beta’s Intraorganizational’s Relational Architecture called for a CIO within each business unit. To ensure that the relationship between IT and the business unit is integrated, three steps are being taken:

† Account executives will be placed in the business units. The account executives are in charge of relationship management and not selling the IT product;

† The bonus structure of the IT staff within the business units is tied to the profitability of the business unit. If the business unit improves, IT also receives a bonus;

† The CIO of the business unit has a dual reporting relationship: to the CEO of the business unit and to the group CIO. Both the CEO of the business unit and the group CIO are in charge of hiring the CIO of the business unit, but either one can fire him/her if the needs of the business unit or the needs of IT are not being met.

In addition to the governance steps, Beta holds conferences for business units that help to inform the business units about technology issues. By helping to identify the business drivers, the project will be driven up through the business unit instead of down through Beta to the business units.

For extraorganizational relational structures, Beta was not highly reliant on those outside of the firm. Since Beta (previously in their history) had been striving to become a service provider for other organizations, much of the IT sourcing is internal. Nonetheless, the company is currently using IBM to help with e-architecture and is looking at telecommunications now to determine how much to keep in-house versus outsource. Beta demonstrates how IT has created a structure that delivers the capabilities to the business units. While previous governance research would have interpreted Beta as having a shared governance structure, this interpretation would not have accounted for how these mechanisms operated in delivering the capabilities, as well as the variety of shared structures that were found in our study. Thus, Beta demonstrates the second element of governance—structures designed to support IT capabilities that derive from strategic imperatives.

## 4.3. Integration architectures

Following an analysis of relational structures came the investigation of integration architectures. Integration architectures were found by examining the transcripts for specific governance mechanisms that were created for the purpose of integrating IT into the business. Gamma provides an example of integration architectures. The upstream and downstream information management (IM) managers are members of the global leadership team, along with the corporate CIO, and all directly report to the corporate CIO. The global leadership team meets monthly to discuss global strategy. The corporate CIO then reports to the Vice Chairman of the Board and sits on the Management Committee, which consists of the CEO, CIO, and representatives from the treasury, public relations, new business development, and research and development business units.

Gamma has established a global common infrastructure, including a common operating environment (COE), consisting of Windows 2000, Microsoft Exchange, and a core set of products. If a local business unit desires to not use the common infrastructure or set of applications, they must petition the global leadership team for certification. Now that Gamma has created a common infrastructure, they have also adapted a set of corporate mandates to all of the business units, including:

† All business units must have a common operating environment;

† All business units must have a WAN that connects to corporate;

† All business units must have a core set of applications.

The mandates allow corporate Gamma to instantly upgrade the entire network and offer remote connectivity to its’ employees. Corporate then pays for the connectivity between the corporate headquarters and the local business units as well as the common infrastructure. From this example, the two dimensional nature of integration mechanisms can be seen—both organizational, non-technical structures and the technical architectures. Both serve to integrate IT into the business.

## 4.4. Success

Finally came an analysis of the success metrics. Success structures were found by examining the transcripts for specific metrics that were created for the purpose of judging a ‘successful’ IT department. Delta uses a particularly interesting metric of success. To measure success, Delta uses a balanced scorecard. The balanced scorecard is then used to create a weighting system with six main categories:

† Customer service (24% of the score), measuring overall customer satisfaction and help desk satisfaction;

† People (12% of the score), measuring employee retention and employee alignment;

† Reliability (16% of the score), measuring key application availability and uptime of e-mail and servers;

† Internal operations (12% of the score), measuring safety and security;

† Projects (24% of the score), measuring project performance;

† Financial (12% of the score), measuring the operating cost versus the actual.

## 4.5. Summary of results

The first interesting result that was found was the diversity of capabilities within the same industry. Further, the capabilities were not related to the size of the firm. Delta, the biggest firm, possessed only two of the IT capabilities listed by Sambamurthy and Zmud, while Beta, one of the smaller firms, possessed four IT capabilities. Of the six organizations, four firms claimed two core IT capabilities.

The IT capabilities found within the firms reflect a variety of approaches that IT adds value to enterprises and should not be viewed negatively, where the absence of a capability reflects a failure of IT. Rather, IT capabilities, from the extended platform logic perspective, means that IT has chosen a set of routines within each of these six firms to adapt to their environment and provide service in these specific areas. The capabilities are dynamic in nature and thus shift as the organization places new demands upon IT.

The implication of this discussion about IT capabilities is that firms, in reacting to similar environmental forces (including competition from the other firms studied here) have chosen a variety of different approaches to IT.

It is clear from the capabilities that there is no clear consensus across these firms about how to react to the external environment and support the firm within this industry. Four of the six firms are focusing upon operational excellence as a capability, with this capability receiving the most focus. In contrast, only one of the firms (Delta) has chosen to use IT to leverage knowledge work. Three firms have chosen to focus upon an IT-enabled business platform and solutions delivery system. Two firms are concentrating upon value-chain extension and value innovation. Within different industries, researchers might find that companies value different capabilities. Yet, the companies within this industry believe in these specific capabilities to leverage IT. Nonetheless, the capabilities are just the first element of the model. Next are the relational architectures.

In terms of intraorganizational relational architectures, all six firms have a form of account executive within the business unit to help manage the IT–business unit relationship. Firms have chosen a variety of approaches to accomplish this objective, using a variety of titles, including customer relationship manager, account executive, IS coordinators, or IM management within the business units. The differing levels within the hierarchy for these titles reflect different methods to ensure the integration of the business with IT.

Further, for extraorganizational architectures, four of the firms had outsourced a component of the IT function, with two (Delta and Epsilon) choosing to insource. Firms were most likely to outsource infrastructure, with five of six firms choosing to allow another firm to handle this component. E-business was also handled differentially by the firms, with two firms moving this responsibility outside of IT.

Success metric choices varied across the six firms, with three firms choosing the balanced scorecard, two firms using components of the scorecard, and one working toward standard objective measures. Yet, the focus upon success was not only technologically based (examining ‘up-time’ and financial metrics), but also examining the user as a customer. The difference between viewing the end-user as a ‘customer’ rather than a simple ‘user’ has implications that will be further explored in the Discussion section.

The IT–business unit relationship also varied across the firms, with Gamma, Epsilon, and Zeta enjoying positive perceptions. Beta, in recognition of its strained relationships, has begun implementing a new structure to improve perceptions; Alpha and Delta have different perceptions within the business units and are struggling with the perception of IT as an overhead cost.<sup>9</sup>

The results suggest that there is little correlation between the presence of architectures and success metrics and the business unit perception of IT. Thus, one interesting finding is that it is not the presence of a structure, but rather how the structure works within the firm that helps to create a positive IT–business unit relationship. This point is exemplified in that an examination of the data from a centralization/decentralization perspective, finds that all of the firms exhibit a shared structure, with IT sharing responsibility with the individual business units. Yet, the form that this structure has taken varies. For example, while Delta has account executives within the business units, these account executives also make IT decisions on a global level. In contrast, many of the other firms give power to the account executives to make decisions at the local level. Thus, simply categorizing firms into one of three forms by looking at the structures themselves would have been limiting in these cases. Nonetheless, this finding also allows us to examine the data to determine what is working within the six firms. This requires us to return to Table 3.

We have previously defined IT capabilities as the IT-based assets and routines that support business conduct in value-adding ways, arguing that IT capabilities derive from the business strategies. Thus, the IT capabilities ostensibly become a surrogate for IT strategy and the combinations of capabilities represent a key part of the overall strategy for IT within the firm. For example, in Alpha, the capabilities are value innovation, operational excellence, and solutions delivery. With the firm focusing on these three capabilities, this means that the IT strategy should focus on IT assets for emerging business opportunities. Table 4 lists the results of the analysis across the six firms.

These results show that three of the firms were focusing on IT architecture (Beta, Gamma, and Epsilon), while the other three were relying on IT assets for knowledge workers and for the value chain (Alpha, Delta, and Zeta). An interesting finding is that in all three areas where there is an architecture focus for IT, there is a positive perspective toward IT, while in two of the three firms where the focus is on IT assets, there is a strained relationship (with Zeta representing the anomaly).

Another interesting finding is that implementing an IT strategy focusing on cost containment does not necessarily translate into an effective IT–business unit relationship. Thus, an IT strategy focusing on cost as part of the equation does not necessarily translate into success; instead, an IT strategy must be linked to the business and environmental imperatives (i.e. it must be in alignment) in order for IT to be successful (Sabherwal et al., 2001).

An additional analysis that can be conducted is to examine the combination of capabilities (or the strategy) and the architectures in place. First, it is evident that no combination can explain the presence of relational architectures, with all firms having a presence within the business units. However, by examining the relational and integration architectures together, an interesting pattern emerges.

The key pattern that explains the successful firms in this data are those in which IT is centrally controlled (e.g. central locus of decision making for budgets and strategy) and locally managed (e.g. local ability to dictate specific responses to business unit imperatives). If IT defines its necessary capabilities as focusing upon using IT assets for strategic opportunities, while carefully watching bottom-line costs, the most effective structures are those without heavy centralized control. This finding thus explains the negative relationship in Alpha, where the IT accountability matrix is heavily controlled and complex. It also explains Delta, where all IT decisions are made upon the global level, taking control away from local management. Further, in Beta (where the focus is on architecture as the asset and there is a CIO in each business unit), Gamma (where the focus is on architecture for processes and there is an IM Manager in each business unit), Epsilon (where the focus is upon architecture for emerging opportunities and there is an empowered account executive within the business units), and Zeta (where the focus is on extending the value chain and there are IS coordinators and a CIO in each business portfolio), IT has a positive relationship.

## 5. Discussion

By using the extended platform logic model, IT governance is moved away from a discussion of structures toward the realm of relationships. While discussions of the importance of relationships are not new in the IT area as can be seen in research into outsourcing (Klepper, 1995; Kern, 1997; McFarlan and Nolan, 1995) and project management (Beath, 1991), the extended platform logic marks an important shift in governance research. The emphasis is no longer on the mechanisms organizations use to control business units (e.g. the dictating of IT policies), but rather on how to manage the relationships that exist within the firm (e.g. partnering with the business units on IT policies). As Gordon and Gordon (2000) argue, ‘little work has been done on the relationship between IT and the business units’ in regard to IT structure. Rather, past work has focused upon discussions about governance approaches and their determinants.

The benefit of the shift in focus can be seen in the analysis of the results of this research. Instead of an inward focus on the internal workings of IT, the extended logic model takes an outward focus, examining how IT can manage its own perception. Bashein and Markus (1997) note:

‘Many IT specialists believe that other people’s ignorance leads to negative stereotypes of IT people. The obvious solution is for other people to change their negative views…IT specialists should ask themselves what they do that might contribute to their low credibility in the eye of their business counterparts and what they can do differently to change negative perceptions.

The shift in emphasis to integration mechanisms in the extended platform logic is a move toward helping bridge the cultural gap between IT and the business units (Ward and Peppard, 1996; Peppard and Ward, 1999). In our cases, we found that IT had indeed taken on the problem of credibility, leading most CIOs to spend approximately 50% of their time outside the IT function. This is important for attaining credibility, understanding, and support for IT (Feeny and Willcocks, 1998). Such activity also makes it possible to help shape the image of IT within the organization. Given the often negative perception by business units that IT is an ‘overhead’ such CIO visibility is important. The relationships created with the business units through the visibility of the CIO and his/her management team creates a positive image and support for IT.

Beyond the integration mechanisms, relationship management had taken on a familiar face, with relational architectures reflecting something of a backward trend. A number of years ago (in the late 1980s), account executives were adopted to ensure that business unit interests were effectively served by IT. The account executives would be housed in the business units, paid by the business units, and be responsible for translating business unit IT needs into IT deliverables. The early 1990s brought a downturn in the economy and a subsequent reduction in organizations’ employees. IT suffered these cutbacks too, and the account executives were often the first to go. Apparently, they have made a comeback as each organization visited had such account executives in place, although they were sometimes referred to as customer relationship managers (CRM). CRM—or the account executive—is clearly perceived as an important piece of an intraorganizational relational architecture.

Our study participants also recognized research and development undertaken by the IT unit as an important component of a firm’s intraorganizational relational architecture. For example, the development of a common operating environment (COE) was a key for all visited companies. Some had it in place already; others were close to having it implemented. The key point was that the development of such as COE and other infrastructure/architecture strategies, could only seriously be considered within the context of an internal IT R&D effort. Such effort had to be part of the cost borne either by the business units or the corporation itself. Companies differed in the way such R&D was funded, but all realized the need for it.

In focusing outward toward the business units, IT departments typically shared responsibilities for IT activities. Despite the commonality in the shared model, in all of the organizations, IT was strategic to the business and had close ties with senior management. This occurred either through the direct reporting relationship between the CIO and his/her superior, and/or the senior management team of the corporation that IT was part of. We believe that these organizations felt IT was too strategic to their survivability not to have IT as either a part of executive management, or directly reporting to it. This sharing of IT responsibilities with the business units emphasizes the importance of relationship management. This is consistent with Ross et al.’s (1996) call for: ‘(1) Business partner ownership of and accountability for, all IT projects, and (2) Top management leadership in establishing IT priorities’.

An additional benefit of the outward focus in the extended platform logic is the ability of the model to capture the variety of IT capabilities within the same industry. Not surprisingly, we found that each firm had their own unique capabilities in response to similar competitive pressures. Nonetheless, this is important within this context, for the extended platform was used as the basis for this finding and thus can be used in the future for researchers investigating the strategic composition of IT capabilities. Further, no firm had all of the capabilities that Sambamurthy and Zmud (2000) proclaimed as being the critical IT capabilities. However, firms were able to use their capabilities to design architectures to successfully support the business units.

In addition to finding commonality of intraorganizational relational architectures, we also found that extraorganizational relational architectures were also consistent across firms. Consider, for example, the case of applications development. Organizations all felt the need to buy rather than build their own. The applications area was considered too important to outsource (although this view is not shared by some outsourcing pundits; cf. Quinn and Hilmer, 1994; DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani, 1998). All had implemented some form of enterprise package, mostly SAP, and felt it incumbent upon them to continue buying rather than building applications internally. When no package was available that satisfied their needs, they first looked at the possibility of reusing software before making a decision to build from scratch.

Finally, all companies agreed the importance of continuously monitoring the satisfaction of those receiving IT services. Such monitoring had to be visible, had to be serious, and had to be seen to be fair. It was also realized that everyone in IT must make customer satisfaction their number one goal. Case site participants noted that the business users of their services had to be referred to as ‘customers’ or ‘clients’ because such terms connote the fact business users have a choice in service providers. Internal IT must be the preferred vendor of choice, not dictate.<sup>10</sup> As part of the recognition of the importance of the customer, IT not only monitored customer satisfaction but also how well it did relative to others. Consultants such as Gartner Group, Meta Group and others were employed to ensure that internal IT performance was satisfactory. This was done through regular benchmarking exercises. The use of well-accepted practices such as the balanced scorecard was also commonplace amongst our interviewed companies.

## 6. Implications for researchers

The extended platform logic model marks a shift in IT governance research. For researchers that embrace the extended platform logic model as a basis for studying IT governance, the meaning and purpose of organizational structure changes. Instead of focusing on structure as a static form, it should be viewed as dynamic and evolving (Brown and Magill, 1994). From this point of view, structure is not about configurations of roles and responsibilities. Rather, structure ‘describes both the prescribed frameworks and realized configurations of interaction, and the degrees to which they are mutually constituted and constituting’ (Ranson et al., 1980).

Structure, viewed from a static view focuses on organizational charts, including prescriptions for formalized roles within an organization. Structure, viewed from a dynamic point of view, focuses on building architectures and platforms for interactions between IT and the business units and allows the individuals within this framework to elicit the appropriate resources needed. The individuals within these structures create the necessary roles and responsibilities as they interact, in order to accomplish the task before them. Findings about the effectiveness of such informal structures are beginning to be seen in the IT academic literature (Chan, 2002). Such research reinforces our arguments about the importance of flexibility and relationship-oriented governance arrangements.

Adopting this perspective on structure involves looking at the ‘processes and systems that connect and activate structural frameworks’ (Greenwood and Hinnings, 1993), looking beyond how the organization chart depicts IT governance toward how IT seeks to create relationships with the business units. In doing so, this would likely require ‘combining traditional organization design with more sensitive, micro-level interventions designed to open up and reconstruct the organization’s underlying working structure (Bate et al., 2000).

Within these six cases, we have gone beyond the organization chart to determine how IT works with the business units, understanding how IT relates and integrates with the business. We have shown how six organizations measure the success of their ‘structure,’ realizing that the structure is dynamic and is formed through daily interactions. With all six firms employing success metrics that examine customer satisfaction as well as IT performance (e.g. the balanced scorecard), the results of this study suggest that our colleagues-in-practice have evolved from focusing on one-way architectures within a centralization/decentralization context toward a two-way relationship-oriented approach to managing the IT structure.

For researchers, this means a shift toward examining structure as social in nature, realizing that the design of the IT organization is a skeleton, against which the daily life of the employees gives life and flesh to the organic entity of the organization (Bate et al., 2000). Researchers must first re-orient themselves toward a social view of structure, examine the architectures as we have done, and then note how firms react to shifts in capabilities and, ultimately, begin to manage the perception of the IT function.

Additional research can begin to understand the combination of architectures that exist once organizations define their required capabilities. As this research has shown, the presence of architectures does not guarantee a positive perception of IT. More work is needed to examine what makes the architectures work. For example, research from the extended platform logic model perspective can examine what ‘successful’ account executives do within the business units or how and what integration architectures enable positive perceptions to be achieved from top management.

By its very nature, the extended platform logic model is highly practical in nature. Yet, underlying the model (as was discussed previously) are theoretical issues that mark a shift in the way that IT governance is viewed. Further, in developing such a practical, yet theoretical model, questions about the relevance of IT research may begin to be answered which in turn might help IT academics establish their value in the broader IT community (Benbasat and Zmud, 1999; Applegate and King, 1999; Paper, 2001).

## 7. Implications for practitioners

The extended platform logic model not only offers opportunities for researchers, but also for practitioners. In describing and analyzing the cases, we have discussed the different elements that help shape the perceptions of IT within the firm. We argue that the adoption of the model would help IT practitioners determine opportunities for changing, shaping and then managing the perceptions of IT within the organization.

For practitioners seeking to determine the ‘best’ governance structure to achieve the goals of creating better perceptions, we are not advocating either a decentralized or centralized approach. Instead, we have found that practitioners should implement structures that allow their IT employees to interact with the business employees on a daily basis and that IT executives should approach their governance structures as ‘an architecture’. The difference between the two approaches is that governance creates formalized hierarchies while architectures create spaces that are designed to provide the resources that employees need to offer their ‘customers’ the best possible service. In an architecture, an IT employee is free to move within the designated space instead of being constrained to specified roles and responsibilities. While this does not mean that there are no formalized processes within IT, we are suggesting that practitioners take a relationshiporiented perspective toward IT management. IT practitioners should look toward their current arrangements and examine carefully if their governance encourages the IT employees to understand the business or if they are shielded from the business units. The results of this study suggest that ‘successful’ IT organizations know their needs of their customers because they interact with them often and that these interactions allow the IT employees to better the position of the IT function.

We believe that the strategic success of IT is very much tied to the way IT ‘carries itself’—in other words, that it is positively perceived by both executive management and the business units. This is done formally with the integration architectures. However, we further believe that taking more of a ‘marketing perspective’ can help shape perceptions about IT. We define a marketing perspective as: ‘determining the needs and wants of the target customers and then delivering satisfaction more effectively and efficiently than the competitors’<sup>11</sup>.

Such a perspective can help: First, to manage expectations of the customers, IT must ensure there are realistic expectations about what IT can truly deliver at a certain cost. In the case of Delta, the IT organization defined their mission as to deliver services “in a secure, reliable, timely, and cost effective manner.” Thus, they were attempting to manage their expectations of security, reliability, timeliness, and cost. Yet, all IT was distributed as an overhead cost to the business units. Thus, the business units themselves had no idea about the cost of delivery associated with their projects. The result was a strained relationship. Had Delta organized their IT department so that the business units were able to know their costs, then more realistic expectations could have been achieved.

Second, change the focus of the IT organization from delivering finished products to services. This involves migrating the IT function over to a service function with a service mentality. The key to this mentality is the view of the end-user. We believe that successful IT organizations view the end user as a customer. All of the firms in this study used some measure of customer satisfaction in their metric of success. However, Gamma is the exemplar, including project satisfaction studies, overall satisfaction studies, and a customer education center within their metric of success. This view of delivering services to a customer is a key component of a successful IT organization.

Third, embrace the notion of being the preferred choice of business units needing service through competition with the external world. In order for IT to become the preferred provider it must afford high quality service. A respondent from Beta, which had recently transitioned from an IT department seeking to become a profit center to being internally focused, noted that “We got so excited about going to the external environment that we neglected, to some extent, our internal customer, the (company name) group customer, so obviously they weren’t very happy about that so now you’ve got the worst of both worlds.” Thus, in the case of Beta, they had forgotten that they were competing with the external world and took for granted their internal relationships. Beta almost lost their own customers as clients, for the internal business units almost outsourced the entire IT function. We argue that, for IT to be successful, they must continue to court their internal customers.

Fourth, realizing that given the wealth of shared services organizations, the internal IT function will be one of many options open to the business units. IT must recognize that with the proliferation of outsourcing options IT is constantly being benchmarked by the business units. Within Beta, a methodologies and practices group benchmarked the IT organization against outside outsourcing vendors. Thus, the internal IT function must remember that it is not the only option that is available and it must perform or risk being outsourced.

And fifth, with IT services increasingly facing the ‘real’ customer of the organization, the need for a positive experience on the part of the customer becomes critical. In many cases, IT has become responsible for how customers directly contact the organization, i.e. through web pages and electronic data interchange. In four of the six firms studied in this paper, organizational excellence, or enabling the firm to meet the needs of their customers, were defined as key capabilities for IT. Thus, the IT function is a key to the customer experience and IT must ensure a positive experience for the customer.

## 8. Conclusions

In this paper, we have presented an extension to the platform logic model and discussed the new model through the eyes of six case studies. Nonetheless, the discussion does not come without a few limitations. First, all of the responses gathered were from the point of view of IT. Thus, when examining perceptions, we are examining how IT believes that the business unit perceives them. Yet, we argue that, while this is not ideal, the results from Table 3 suggest that the interviews were able to illuminate the key perceptions that these organizations were struggling within their structuring of their governance arrangements. The respondents from the organizations were able to suggest how they were perceived, in that the success metrics that they used allowed them to gather the data to effectively answer questions regarding perceptions.

A second possible limitation is the relatively small number of interviews. Yet, the key criterion for choosing respondents was their ability to effectively communicate the structures and the rationale for the governance arrangement. This resulted in a limited number of available respondents that could participate in the study. Nevertheless, each interviewee was a key decision maker within his/her organization, thus allowing us to gather rich insights into the organizations.

A third limitation is that we have used the sum of IT capabilities as ostensibly a surrogate for IT strategy. This has two potential problems. One, we have argued that IT capabilities derive from the business strategy, which they may or may not do. While we agree that this is a potential limitation, we have consistently argued that, in our view, governance arises from strategic and environmental imperatives. Thus, at the very foundation of our conceptualization of governance is this notion. Two, we have limited IT strategy to just IT capabilities. We acknowledge this shortcoming and are hopeful that this limitation becomes the basis for future researchers to investigate the link between IT strategy, capabilities, and governance.

Despite these limitations, we believe that focusing on the core underpinnings of successful IT organizations in practice should help other organizations successfully adapt to the new reality unfolding before us. More specifically, by examining the relational and integration architectures of the model, we have documented how IT interacts with the business units. By investigating IT capabilities, we have seen how IT views itself and its position within the firm. By examining the various success metrics, we have seen the variety of methods that IT can use to determine its impact on the firm. Our results suggest that researchers need to change their views of IT ‘structure’ to embrace a more social and dynamic existence. Fundamentally, IT governance has moved beyond structure to embrace relationships. For the practitioner, there is no longer the need to focus on centralization/decentralization, but on how relationships are formed and managed. Such relationships can benefit through IT adopting a more marketing orientation. In sum, we believe that we have answered the call of Sambamurthy and Zmud to better understand the challenges and discoveries of IT management.
