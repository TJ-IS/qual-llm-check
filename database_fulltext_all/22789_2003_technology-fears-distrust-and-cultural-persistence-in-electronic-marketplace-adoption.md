# Technology fears: distrust and cultural persistence in electronic marketplace adoption

Ruey-Lin Hsiao<sup>\*</sup>

Department of Decision Sciences, NUS Business School, National University of Singapore, BIZ 2 Building (#03-14), 1 Business Link, Singapore, Singapore 117592

Received 1 June 2001; accepted 1 June 2003

## Abstract

This study interprets fears towards technology use to account for the challenge of e-marketplace (electronic marketplace) adoption. The adopters’ fears can be understood by analyzing both reliability-related and value-oriented distrust. While technical remedies may reduce reliabilityrelated distrust by removing adoption barriers and restoring online trust, they may still be ineffective in overcoming value-oriented distrust. To analyze value-oriented distrust within the context of e-marketplace adoption, it is essential to examine how a society’s socio-cultural structure affects economic activities. An insight derived from this study is that when an e-marketplace is adopted, such adoption includes not only the use of technology but also the transfer of the embedded trustproduction mechanisms as well as the transformation of economic assumptions and cultural beliefs about how a market system operates. As distrust is rooted in cultural beliefs persisting over generations, this requires us to be sensitive to the need to reduce value-oriented fears so as to maximize the possibility of e-marketplace acceptance. © 2003 Elsevier B V. All rights reserved

Keywords: E-marketplace adoption; Reliability-related distrust; Value-oriented distrust; Trust-production mechanisms; Cultural belief

## 1. Introduction

The issue of trust has been the Achilles heel of e-marketplace (electronic marketplace) adoption. Although previous studies highlight the importance of adoption barriers with regard to technology, organization, collaboration and infrastructure (Farhoomand et al., 2000; Kumar and Crook, 1999), they seem generally to be insensitive to the trust issue. The growing online trust literature suggests useful remedies for restoring trust in terms of institution-, reputation- and technology-based control mechanisms (e.g. Ba and Pavlou, 2002; McKnight et al., 2002). Nonetheless, complex issues of trust in online commerce remain a subject requiring continual examination and interpretation.

The study offers an alternative interpretation of trust by examining the issue of distrust through a socio-cultural analysis at the societal level. Although scholars have defined trust/distrust in various ways, a common understanding is that distrust is not only the absence of trust but also the active expectation that other actors will behave in ways that endanger our safety and security (Kramer, 1999). Lewicki et al. (1998): 439) explain distrust as ‘confident negative expectations’, which mean a fear of another’s propensity to attribute harmful intentions. More importantly, Sitkin and Roth (1993) urge us to be watchful about the difference between reliability-related distrust (relating to technical competence) and value-oriented distrust (relating to cultural assumptions). Both types of distrust may engender fears that lead to resistance to purely legalistic remedies. Value-oriented distrust may be particularly difficult to invalidate through experience, which prevents people from engaging in productive social collaboration. Even worse, it stimulates behavior that can bring about escalation of distrust (Luhmann, 1979). Sitkin and Roth (1993) caution us that, although legalistic remedies may be effective in overcoming the reliability-related distrust/fear, they may be counterproductive when it comes to addressing the value-oriented basis of distrust/fear.

In this research, the challenge of e-marketplace adoption is addressed in the framework of distrust/fear constructed socio-culturally (Fukuyama, 1995; Yamagishi et al., 1998). Distrust and fear are here explored in relation to the societal culture and the mechanism of trust production (Zucker, 1986). The analysis is situated in the context of a selected Chinese SME (Small and Medium sized Enterprises) community within which resistance to an e-marketplace is observed. It interprets the concept of technology fears with reference to both reliability-related and value-oriented distrust by expanding the work of Sitkin and Roth (1993). Based on this research strand, an explanatory framework is proposed to outline four possible paths of e-marketplace adoption. Additionally, by extending Kumar et al. (1998), the study examines resistance to e-marketplace in terms of adopters’ fears stemming from the transfer of an incongruent trust-production mechanism (embedded in the technology). In these circumstances, the adoption of an e-marketplace requires Chinese merchants to transform their cultural beliefs and to shift their frame of reference in conducting businesses, which may not be easy.

Section 2 introduces the existing literature with regard to adoption barriers and online trust; the trust problem situated in societal culture is highlighted. Section 3 explains how the interpretative method is incorporated in the research design. The case description consists of three analytical points: case background (Section 4.1), reliability-related distrust/fears (Section 4.2), and value-oriented distrust/fears (Section 4.3). This interpretation explains how distrust/fear may arise from two contrasting sources, leading to adoption difficulties. Finally, the contribution of distrust/fear analysis is discussed in relation to the e-marketplace adoption literature and the practical relevance of this study.

## 2. Literature review

The range of e-marketplaces covers many functions of online market arrangements such as auction, procurement, catalogue sales, and clearances of excessive stocks, greatly reducing transaction costs and increasing efficiency (Johnston and Vitale, 1988). E-marketplaces consist of vertical exchanges (by different industries) or horizontal exchanges (as in MRO: Maintenance, Repair and Operation) which bring various sellers and buyers together to share information and transact products. Although there are various ways to classify e-marketplaces (e.g. Kaplan and Sawhney, 2000; Pavlou and El Sawy, 2002), practitioners generally recognize three types of e-marketplace: private, consortia and independent exchanges (Morgan Stanley Dean Witter, 2000; Raisch, 2001).

Private exchanges are hosted by key buyers to exchange purchase orders, inventory reports, and other trading information electronically. One example is Trading Partners Network, led by General Electric. Consortia-based exchanges can be buyer-led (e.g. Covisint, led by an automobile consortium) or supplier-led (e.g. Cisco Connect offers integrated services for buyers). They are established to achieve the aggregation of purchasing volume and supply chain integration. Independent exchanges, also known as net market makers, are hosted by neutral intermediaries (e.g. SESAMi, Gate2Biz, and Com2B). Companies which do not want to build an e-marketplace can subscribe to net market makers and benefit from their services, including technology implementation, telecommunications infrastructure, logistics, product-content management, credit-rating information, business surveillance, pre-shipment inspections, financial payment, etc. However, net market makers face more difficulties than private and consortia exchanges, because they need to develop trust relationships among buyers and suppliers before a critical mass of users can be reached.

## 2.1. Removing adoption barriers

E-marketplace adoption still faces an inordinate number of challenges (see Table 1). Three main categories of adoption barriers are often mentioned: technology, organization and collaboration (based on Farhoomand et al., 2000; Kumar and Crook, 1999). Technological barriers include, for instance, network security, system integration, and the integration of internal systems to Electronic Data Interchange (Truman, 2000). The conversion of a database can easily consume three to four years of a company’s time, with the additional challenge of data transmission and network bandwidth. As security technology is still at an embryonic stage (Knights et al., 2001), companies are also concerned about the safety of electronic transactions.

The organizational barriers include, for example, perceived ease of use, perceived benefits, lack of training, resistance to change, management commitment, and intention to use in order to predict the possibility of IT rejection (e.g. Iacovou et al., 1995; Premkumar and Ramamurthy, 1995). Kendall et al. (2001), in an e-commerce survey of Singapore, also note the importance of users’ perceived receptivity of technological features. Furthermore, failed adoption often resulted from the integration of inter-firm business processes, because most companies have unique processes for managing their supply chains that may not be fully compatible with the processes of their partner companies (Lee and Clark, 1997; Riggins and Mukhopadhyay, 1994).

As Kumar and Crook (1999) summarize, inter-firm collaboration barriers include, for example, economic incentives, strategic alignment, inter-firm conflicts (for example, due to unbalanced power relationships), and social issues such as trust (see also inter-firm collaboration issue in Ref. Yoo et al., 2001). The issue of trust has received widespread attention. For example, Hart and Saunders (1997) associate failed adoption with a lack of ‘competence trust’ (which is concerned with a partner’s ability to perform according to expectations). Allen et al. (2000) analyze TransLease (in the automobile insurance sector) and show how distrust arose between repair agents and lease firms as a result of the lack of intentional trust (which refers to a partner’s intention not to defect from its collaborative party).

Another strand of research focuses on the structural constraints in each geographical area. Farhoomand et al. (2000) explain the infrastructure problem in terms of inactive governmental attitudes, economic concerns, and underdeveloped regulatory infrastructure in Hong Kong and Finland. Markus and Soh (2002) add that most Asian countries still do not have viable infrastructures to support e-marketplace services. These barriers include financial infrastructure, legal and regulatory infrastructure (e.g. taxation), national policy concerned with promoting Internet usage, logistics infrastructure, telecommunications infrastructure, local business practice (e.g. payment), language and education, and industry concentration.

More recent studies have also attempted to identify adoption barriers in cross-cultural settings. For instance, Ranganathan et al. (2001) investigate the context of the USA, Singapore, and Norway, stressing the importance of organization, collaboration and infrastructure barriers. Zhu et al. (2002) examine eight European countries and again stress the significance of technological, organizational and environmental barriers. The crosscountry study of Kraemer et al. (2002) further validates the vital role of infrastructure in e-commerce adoption (covering the USA, France, Denmark, Germany, Japan, Singapore, Taiwan, China, Brazil and Mexico). Similarly, Tan and Wu (2002) conclude that the infrastructure problem is a major barrier to e-marketplace diffusion in China.

Although these studies of adoption barriers have begun to recognize the pivotal role of competence trust (i.e. trust in technically viable systems) and institution-based trust (e.g. trust in reliable intermediary and government supports), the identified variables are often insufficiently explained. The online trust literature fills this theoretical gap.

## 2.2. Chasing the shadow of fear

Zucker (1986: 54) pointedly explains that trust is ‘a set of expectation shared by all those involved in an exchange,’ a precondition which supports effective economic actions. While the success of e-marketplaces is not predicated upon bonds of trust, e-marketplace failure is often the result of distrust. Distrust has serious drawbacks which consume a great deal of energy, make economic actions difficult, and may impede mutual collaboration. In the condition of distrust, one actor has no reason for positive confidence expectation of the partner, resulting in wariness, watchfulness and fear (Lewicki et al., 1998: 448).

Although researchers offer multiple interpretations of distrust (see Table 2), a common thread is that in a situation of distrust one is suspicious because one is confronting social complexities, risks, and dangers and in fear of negative consequences. Especially, Sitkin and Roth (1993) distinguish between reliability-related and value-oriented trust. They find that distrust emerges from two sources. First, reliability-related distrust occurs when people’s expectations about task-specific reliability is reduced. For example, employees may consider the company unreliable when there are no formal policies to deal with complex issues (such as HIV/AIDS illness). On the other hand, value-oriented distrust arises when the compatibility of employees’ beliefs with the organization’s cultural values appeared to be in conflict. For example, in response to HIV/AIDS, the company may be inclined to seek administrative resolutions (e.g. by instituting formal heath check procedures). But this may contradict employees’ expectations, which may be more valueladen and perceive those stigmatized colleagues as cultural outsider (as their fears are associated with plagues of the past).

What can be done in the shadow of suspicion and fear? The online trust literature identifies three control mechanisms to restore trust: institution-, reputation- and technology-based trust (Grabner-Kra¨uter and Kaluscha, 2003). To establish institutional trust (at the inter-organizational level), the intermediary may offer structural assurance and situational normality (e.g. McKnight et al., 2002; Pavlou, 2002). The intermediary needs to let adopters believe that appropriate guarantees, regulations, legal resource, and other infrastructure services (e.g. a payment gateway) are in place to build structural assurance. Additionally, the intermediary needs to convince adopters that the online environment is under proper control, and exchanges among trading partners are likely to be safe (i.e. there is situational normality).

To build trust via a reputation mechanism, the intermediary may apply the principle of ‘initial trust’; that is, users obtain whatever information they have initially to make a trust inference about e-commerce (McKnight et al., 2002). For example, the intermediary may build competence trust via endorsement by respected adopters, or may promote its honesty and benevolence (intentional trust) through seals from agencies (e.g. Trust-e) that attest to their security/privacy policies (e.g. Shankar et al., 2002; Stewart, 2003). The idea is to enhance adopter perception of the hosting intermediary so that they will perceive it as competent and responsible (Bakos and Dellarocas, 2002; Gefen, 2000). Another example is eBay’s Feedback Forum (an online feedback mechanism for auctioneers), which promotes reputation-based trust and leads to higher auction price (Ba and Pavlou, 2002).

With similar purposes, another method of building trust is to use technological means to promote online assurance (Ratnasingham and Pavlou, 2002; Van der Heijden et al., 2003). Currently, a plethora of security technologies is existed, from identification (e.g. passwords-something you know) to identity (e.g. eyeballs and fingerprints scanningsomething you have), to overcome adopters’ fear of fraudulent practices (Knights et al., 2001).

Although the online trust strand offers viable technical remedies, it is limited in two major respects. First, the institution, reputation and technological means of trust building deal with only reliability-related distrust. They do not fully address distrust resulting from value incongruence, as Sitkin and Roth (1993) and Sitkin and Stickel (1996) caution. Secondly, the influence of societal culture is insufficiently considered in the online trust literature. Although trust and distrust are aspects of social relations in virtually all societies, they are enacted differently in different cultural contexts (Yamagishi et al., 1998). What constitutes trust, including its behavioral requirements and pre-conditions, is not the same in Japan or China as it is in Germany or the United States (Fukuyama, 1995).

## 2.3. Persisting cultural beliefs

Value-oriented distrust is intricately linked to culture at the organizational as well as the societal level, and can precipitate fear and rejection of technology. For example, Walsham and Barrett (1999), at the organizational level, analyze the incongruent professional culture between the implementers and adopters (underwriters and brokers) of the London Insurance Market (see also Barrett, 1999). Adopters believed that the moral basis of the Market, founded on honesty and trust (i.e. the Utmost Good Faith clause), may be undermined with the advent of electronic trading. This resulted in the adopters’ fears of the loss of autonomy, fears of insecure future employment, and fear of drastic changes in trading conditions. As the system is incongruent with the Market’s cultural and relational norms, it is thus difficult for trust to be sustained in traditional underwriting practices.

At the societal level, Zucker (1986) notes that trust-production mechanisms differ substantially across cultures. She observes two distinctive types of trust-production mechanism: institution-based (e.g. banking and government), and reputation/relationshipbased. In societies with established institution-based trust (e.g. the USA), it is more realistic to have impersonal exchanges, which assume rational, self-interested behavior affected minimally by social relations. However, in other societies (e.g. Italy, Japan, and China), where the cultural norm of commerce is based more on reputations and relationships, it is more likely that economic actions are embedded in and constrained by structures of social relations (Greif, 1994; Kumar et al., 1998; Montgomery, 1998). It is more realistic for these societies to produce trust through social mechanisms (reputation/ relationship-based) rather than institutional mechanisms.

The sanction for dishonest merchants is different in these two contrasting modes of trust production. In the first case (in ideal terms), merchants’ economic exchanges are assured by institutional arrangements such as government agencies, legal services, insurance, and financial institutions. For example, the dishonest merchants would be brought to court for certain legal punishments; the injured merchants would also be compensated by insurance agencies. Therefore, exchanges based on market terms may become the preferred choice. On the other hand, in societies where viable institutional arrangements are absent, reputation and relationship mechanisms may be a more useful approach to provide sanctions and produce trust. For example, Greif (1989, 1993) analysis of Maghribi traders is a case in point. Maghribi traders were Jewish merchants operating in the western basin of the Mediterranean in the eleventh century. To save costs and avoid the risks of sea journeys, the traders employed agents to trade on their behalf. As it was difficult to bring dishonest agents to court, Maghribi traders thus developed forms of community punishment to sanction ostracized agents until they compensated the injured. As a result, agents were more worried about ‘the interpretation of their actions by other members than about the outcome of their actions’ (Greif, 1993: 544). Thus, trust is produced through this reputation mechanism by making future employment conditional on past conduct, a concept known as repeated game.

Kumar et al. (1998) offer another pointed example via a failed e-marketplace adoption in Prato (Italy) which was designed for textile trading community. They find that the merchants of Prato preferred relationship-based mechanisms of trust production, as most firms were spawned either by relatives or employees and these merchants were often schoolmates or members of the same church or trade associations. This social structure encouraged mutual identification and developed a web of trust relationships. For example, pancheggiare (bench-sitting) was used as a social practice of communication. This was related to the custom of lo struscio, where every evening people slowly ‘stroll around’ the town square for informal socialization. This allows all sorts of information to be exchanged, and also serves as an important medium for building relationships.

In this relationship-based culture, one’s reputation is of prime importance to Pratesian merchants. An example is the non-use of an e-auction function that was designed to sell redundant inventories. With this function, the merchants could offer their overstocked inventories to other firms. However, for the merchants of Prato, admitting the existence of redundant inventories would have meant a demonstration of an inaccurate market forecast and thus a public confession of one’s business incompetence. As a result, this service was never utilized. Similarly, the merchants value social relationships even at the expense of modifying their economic goals in order to avoid harm to the relationship. In the case of distrust, the web of relationships provides not only an external sanction (I have to follow the rule because others are watching me) but also a self-imposed sanction (I follow the norm because of who I am in the society). As the merchants of Prato have internalized the social relationship culturally, ‘in the violation of self-consistence, individuals would no longer recognize themselves’ (Montgomery, 1998).

It is common knowledge that Chinese business communities are founded mainly on personal relationship (Guanxi). It is thus a good point of departure to understand this social mechanism of trust production among Chinese merchants. Guanxi consists of ‘connections that are defined by reciprocity and mutual obligation supported by a sense of goodwill and personal affection’ (Chen, 2001: 46). Although in other societies it is not uncommon to depend on ‘relationships’ to provide the members of particular groups with the measure of trust necessary to underpin business transactions (such as Japanese wa, Korean inhwa, and the English old boy network), Chinese Guanxi has its specific emphasis on family ties, favor (reciprocity) and face (dignity). For the Chinese, Guanxi is a web of trustful relationships, with oneself in the center, radiated from there is the family (Hwang, 2000). A Guanxi network generally consists of three circles of relationship: family members (inner circle), friends (middle circle) and strangers (outer circle), according to Farh et al. (1998); Tsui and Farh (1997). This web of relationships is usually developed along person-to-person, rather than businessto-business, lines (Luo, 2000).

It is important to understand the role of Guanxi networks in the shaping of Chinese merchant culture. According to Lufrano’s (1997) study of mid-eighteenth century merchant culture in the Qing dynasty, at least until late imperial China merchants were considered to occupy the lowest status in social ranking, after scholars, farmers and laborers (in that descending order). In an environment where little institutional supports were available and trustworthy brokers were difficult to find, Chinese merchants began to appropriate Confucian values for two purposes. First, it offered the merchants a way of self-cultivation and allowed them to present themselves as responsible gentlemen ( jun-zi). This provided a ‘realistic and coherent set of guidelines for living in a changing and unsettling world and strengthened their self-esteem’ (Lufrano, 1997: 177), thereby raising their social status to that beneath the scholars, who followed orthodox Confucian principles. Secondly, it helped to distinguish jun-zi (gentlemen), who were doing what was morally right, from xiao-ren (the rabble, petty men), who were only interested in benefiting themselves. It also served as a categorization-based distrust strategy (Brewer, 1996): status as a gentleman separated the mid-level merchants from the rabble.

An ideal Confucian portrait of a gentleman included the characteristics of benevolence, righteousness, sincerity, filial piety, diligence, loyalty, and above all trustworthiness (Hofstede and Bond, 1988; Lufrano, 1997). For example, two well-known Confucian quotations (Chen, 1988: 17–18, translated) are as follows:

Be honest and sincere in words; be gentle and respectful in deeds; you can be successful even in the barbarian countries.

Those who cannot keep their promises are unworthy of trusting anything at all; without trust, it is like driving a carriage without four wheels.

We can therefore appreciate why the existing e-marketplace, which assumes strong institutional arrangements and supports impersonal transactions, is culturally incongruent to the Pratesian and Chinese merchants. The resistance by the merchants is thus only to be expected. From this viewpoint, adopting an e-marketplace also means transferring institution-based trust to a Pratesian/Chinese society that relies on relationship-based trust. This is extremely difficult for two reasons. First, the incorporation of effective institutional arrangements often requires coordinated governmental efforts over time. Secondly, this transfer also means transforming the cultural beliefs of Pratesian merchants. Greif (1994: 915) notes that cultural beliefs are ‘the ideas and thoughts common to several individuals that govern interaction-between theses people, and between them, their gods, and other groups’. Generally, cultural beliefs become commonly known through the socialization process by which culture is unified, maintained and communicated. Cultural beliefs resist change, as they have been transmitted and maintained over generations (Zucker, 1977).

However, this insight is largely under-explored in the existing studies. This has motivated the present research to explore the challenge of e-marketplace adoption through the lens of value-oriented distrust and cultural persistence. To echo Kumar et al. (1998), this study will focus on the issue of e-marketplace adoption facing Chinese merchants.

## 3. Interpretative research methods

Interpretative methods are useful for understanding human thoughts and actions in social contexts. This research follows the principles of interpretative methods outlined by Klein and Myers (1999) as a basis for theoretical development. These principles are summarized in Table 3 and incorporated into the research design reported below.

## 3.1. Underpinning theory

This study acknowledges the socially constructed nature of human understanding and the importance of the interdependent meaning integrating the parts and the whole. It seeks to explore merchants’ practice in e-marketplace adoption in a particular societal context. This assumes that the adopters construct the expectation of technology use influenced by socio-cultural dynamics. The focus is on analyzing the shared meanings of adopters (the parts) with reference to the socio-cultural context in which they are situated (the whole).

The analysis extends the work of Kumar et al. (1998) and analyzes how a trust-production mechanism is culturally constructed in a particular societal context (the Chinese trading community). This perspective helps the researcher to explore the nature of Guanxi- based (rooted in personal relations) business exchanges and their role in trust production among the Chinese merchants (see, e.g. Hwang, 1987; 2000; Lufrano, 1997; Luo, 2000). This yields a fresh understanding of how distrust and cultural persistence may result in fear, leading to e-marketplace resistance.

## 3.2. Research site

SMEOnline, an independent net market maker, was selected for this investigation. The findings reflect the adopters’ expectations of, as well as their early exposure to, this e-marketplace. The case of SMEOnline was selected because it has a complete range of intermediary services, offering a rich context in which to investigate the complexity of distrust in an e-marketplace. Furthermore, although the e-marketplace achieved initial acceptance in its preliminary stage, resistance, colored by the adopters’ value-laden perceptions, later arose. This offers valuable evidence for examining the adoption difficulties with regard to culture-related issues at the societal level.

Field access was gained through local contacts. The researcher participated in a customer survey in order to identify areas of improvement for SMEOnline. Initially, the interviews sought to include a comparative analysis of acceptance and non-acceptance. The researcher soon realized that it was not possible to identify enough committed adopters in this e-marketplace, and decided to focus on non-adopters (including those who later aborted the e-marketplace).

## 3.3. Data collection

Field data were collected through unstructured interviews and on-site observation. Other examined materials included company archives and project documents (such as promotional brochures, training materials, and annual reports). Each interview was about one hour in length, during which field notes were taken. More than half of the company’s employees (15) were interviewed in order to understand the design and operation of this e-marketplace. These staff were functional managers and consultants directly involved in building SMEOnline. They offered valuable perspective on how this electronic exchange was incorporated, operated and promoted. Additionally, a total of 41 non-adopters (all senior executives in their companies) were selected for personal interviews in order to understand their experience with SMEOnline. The fieldwork was divided into two phases. In the first phase, 23 non-adopters were interviewed via telephone (with the help of two research assistants). The gathered data were mainly about problems related to reliabilityrelated distrust. In the second phase, another 18 non-adopters were interviewed and were asked about issues more closely related to socio-cultural concerns associated with value-oriented distrust.

The researcher also participated in four key project meetings and several other key events (including one trade exhibition and two seminars) in order to observe the interactions between the adopters and the intermediary. During the field study (from October 1999 to January 2001), access to the e-marketplace was also available through the Internet, thereby allowing observation of the adopters’ activities in the e-marketplace. This kind of multiple-source data collection seeks to provide triangulation in order to enhance data integrity. For example, although the company stated that the adoption at the later stage had about 200 active buyers/sellers, scrutiny of each company showed that only 16 adopters were active. In this case, the study employed the latter data for analysis.

## 3.4. Data analysis

The initial focus of analysis was to explore the technology fears stemming from the incongruent cultural frames between the intermediary and the adopters (e.g. Barrett, 1999; Orlikowski and Gash, 1994). However, during the course of this study, the researcher consistently encountered a common response from the adopters: that an e-marketplace characterized by the Chinese business culture is required. During intensive interactions with the Chinese merchants, the researcher’s understanding of the situation improved and he came to question his previous course of investigation based on the organizational level. Gradually, the Chinese merchants helped the researcher to understand that their resistance to the e-marketplace was rooted more in their socio-cultural practices of business, which have to be examined at the societal level.

Data were grouped according to the reliability-related and value-oriented fears using inductive content analysis. As a result, stories associated with these issues were used to generate interpretations of distrust in the e-marketplace. Gradually, this study’s critique emerges from the adopters’ complaints about the technological features which ignore the Chinese conventions of commerce. Initially, the analysis was more concerned with the conflicting viewpoints of the intermediary and the prospective adopters. The researcher came to understand that it is more important to investigate the adopters’ multiple interpretations of technology fear in terms of both reliability-related and value-oriented distrust. This leads to an analysis of institution-based and relationship/reputation-based trust in the societal context.

## 4. Research findings-the sum of all fears

## 4.1. Case background

DecisionSys is a Singapore-based consulting firm specializing in IT-related services in the East Asia region (disguised names are used in this study). In 1999, the company recognized the intensive competition resulting from e-commerce and decided to launch an e-marketplace, called SMEOnline, based on the net market maker model. The aim was to provide SME (from Chinese trading communities) with online exchanges through an integrated technological platform. The Managing Director explained:

There is no reason why SME will not get on board with this e-marketplace. It is astonishing to know that most [Chinese] SME are using the traditional payment systems, for example, the letter of credit, which takes a huge amount of time and trouble to process…and the cost for buyers and sellers is high.

The company incorporated many value-added services to offer fulfillment infrastructures for SMEOnline. To broaden infrastructure services, it also established alliances with three strategic partners: TransLog (a regional logistic provider), ExcelMail (an automated mailing company) and DataSolutions (a firm specializing in data-mining and business intelligence). The business idea was that once a critical mass of adopters was achieved, the transaction volume in the e-marketplace would increase accordingly. In this case, the adopters could be charged for a fraction of the transaction and subscription fee. To adopt the e-marketplace, buyers and sellers would also need to install information systems. Therefore, another source of revenue would be from professional services and software licensing fees.

DecisionSys employed an adoption strategy called ‘B2C2B’ (i.e. from B2C to B2B e-commerce). The first step was to offer a low-cost B2C (Business-to-Customer) e-commerce service by building online stores over the Internet at minimal cost. The B2C solution involved designing a website, building membership management, and setting up web-based e-mail systems. Once DecisionSys reached a critical mass of B2C customers, the second step was to turn the online stores into the e-marketplace (SMEOnline), where SME could identify trading opportunities and conduct transactions over the Internet.

At the beginning of its launch (March 2000), SMEOnline built about 60 B2C e-commerce websites for firms from various industries such as food distribution, furniture, chemicals, construction materials, automobiles and electronics. In order to attract more adopters, DecisionSys sought support from trade associations and government subsidies, and instituted a special promotion scheme. Within five months, triggered by adopter curiosity and government incentive schemes, adoption quickly scaled up to 800 (based in Singapore; about 60% of them had businesses operating in China and 40% in the Asia Pacific, Hong Kong and Taiwan). However, although the total number of adopters reached as high as 1500 SME, by the end of 2000 most of the buyers and sellers had begun to abort the e-marketplace. In early 2001, the company attempted to offer enhanced technological functions and more favorable adoption schemes, but the total number of retained adopters was less than 20. The senior team was perplexed by this result and decided to investigate why the e-marketplace had not been well received (see Table 4 for a summary).

## 4.2. Reliability-related distrust/fears

The challenge of SMEOnline adoption is analyzed first through the feedback of adopters in response to the issue of task-specific, technology-related reliability. First, there is the fear of technological complexity. Most adopters perceived the Internet as an untrustworthy technology; they considered the e-marketplace to be associated with online crime due to failures of Internet security. Another worry was that adopters had to consider many technological standards associated with the e-marketplace (e.g. for the exchange of trading documents). Each buyer had to employ different technological standards because of the software used. For example, the e-marketplace designed by Commerce One (one of the leading vendors) might have incompatible technological standards to those of SMEOnline. The sophisticated task of system integration scared these adopters away.

Secondly, the adopters distrusted the novel technology due to their ignorance of e-marketplaces. Their fears were increased by the lack of familiarity with the new technology. For example, two executives were worried that such systems might result in their business getting out of control:

My understanding of the e-marketplace is to have the Internet handle my business transactions. I don’t like the idea of having the machine taken over my business and tell me what I should do for my clients.

Doing business on the Internet is to give away products for free. That’ll make me go out of business in no time.

Thirdly, there was a fear of uncertainty about job security. As the e-marketplace would automate the process of business exchange, potentially this could lead to job cuts. A senior manager (in charge of purchasing) expressed a typical worry:

I like the idea of doing business exchange over the Internet. But I worry that my role in this company will quickly become unnecessary [with the introduction of such systems]. I have been working here for over ten years and it is my turn for the senior executive position soon. I would not like the idea of losing promotion.

Fourthly, adopters were fearful of potential exploitation by unfamiliar trading partners. The owner of a small chemical supplier expressed his distrust of Internet transactions because of the uncertainty of economic outcomes:

I know all the local companies; I know all the suppliers. I’ve been in the business for over 20 years. I know my business partners very well. We know which companies are bad paymasters and so we don’t want to sell them anything. We know which companies have good links to our competitors so that we don’t want to deal with them. And we know which companies pay us good commission so that we need to provide good services to them. It is very much a trust-based trading relationship. You would be too naı¨ve to think that the e-marketplace can replace all the knowledge about your trading partners.

These fears can be understood with regard to the adopters’ distrust in the technological features of the e-marketplace, the competence of the intermediary (e.g. in managing Internet security), and the anticipation of injurious conducts from untrustworthy merchants (e.g. the repudiation issue—buyers do not acknowledge the receipt of goods and refuse to pay). In response to the stalled adoption, DecisionSys attempted to enhance the telecommunication infrastructure to ensure the reliability and security of the emarketplace. The Chief Technology Officer explained:

For Internet business, the electronic infrastructure is the foundation of all services. You need to begin by building a solid base. Otherwise, when you scale up your business, the whole business will falter like the Leaning Tower of Pisa [in Italy]. What we can do is to invest in more data centers, security software, and a payment gateway mechanism in order to ensure that the security issue is dealt with effectively.

Furthermore, the use of proven technologies (such as PKI—Public Key Infrastructure, digital signature, and trust mark) aimed to boost the confidence of the adopters. For instance, the trust mark can pre-screen creditable buyers and sellers before they register with SMEOnline, and digital signature can offer a time stamp to avoid repudiation disputes. Over four months (from September 2000), the company also worked hard to offer better user training and attractive promotional schemes. However, adopters did not respond positively to these efforts. Therefore, in mid-2001, DecisionSys further enhanced the technological function of SMEOnline targeted at trade associations.

A concern often raised by the adopters was that the e-marketplace is grounded in Anglo-American business practice and thus was not applicable to Chinese trading communities. The Chinese entrepreneurs frequently mentioned the need for an emarketplace with a ‘Chinese face’, although they could not articulate precisely what this meant. This left an unanswered question that the analysis of reliability-related distrust alone could not adequately address.

## 4.3. Value-laden distrust/fears

The unanswered question requires us to analyze further the adoption difficulties in relation to socio-cultural dynamics and value-oriented distrust, as explained earlier. We need to appreciate that information systems are socio-culturally constructed products and are not always congruent with a society’s culture. Since the cultural beliefs of a society cannot easily be changed, information systems may not be adopted smoothly into an organization situated in an incongruent societal context. Therefore, an inside look into the Chinese merchant culture and its trust-production mechanism may offer an initial explanation of SMEOnline’s adoption difficulties.

The Guanxi network as a trust-production mechanism: Over time, Confucian values had come to determine the success or failure of a merchant’s business endeavor. Chinese merchants use the gentlemanly criteria to consider who may be included in (or excluded from) their Guanxi networks as a basis for business expansion. For example, in this study, several merchants repeatedly mentioned: ‘who you know determines what you can achieve but what you are determines who you will know’; ‘be a gentleman, then you should become a successful businessman’. According to them, it is best for Chinese merchants to nurture their Guanxi networks based on personal referral trust (i.e. ‘trust me because I am referred by the trusted personal ties’).

In many ways, modern Chinese merchants also use their Guanxi networks as a social mechanism for assessing business risks. The portrait of a Confucian gentleman is often used implicitly to assess the credentials of their business partners. For example, in one interview, Mr Wu, a senior executive of a livestock company, explained that at one business meeting he tried to find common ground with a potential partner (a stranger to him in this situation). As a conventional practice, he hosted a dinner and tacitly asked a series of questions, such as: Is he related to any of my family members? Is he related to any friends I know (e.g. former classmates)? He collected information and made contacts with his networks to check on this person: his past credentials in business, his personality, integrity and trustworthiness. Specifically, through his Guanxi network he investigated the partner’s background through his uncle (who lived in the same city as this potential partner), his former classmates and professors (who knew this person from his university days), and other traders (who had done business with this person before). For Wu, doing business is not only about developing a partnership with a company but also about building a relationship with a person. With such information, Wu decided not to develop this relationship further, based on two assessments: first, Wu’s former classmates’ impression of this person was not entirely positive; secondly, Wu did not ‘feel’ sincerity from this person during dinner (and Wu thus inferred that the person was unlikely to be trustworthy).

It is understandable why Chinese merchants strive to sustain such personal networks through in group favoritism, loyalty and mutual obligations. Usually, people in a particular Guanxi network will enjoy more favoritism than those outside the network. This, on the other hand, also implies that merchants have to obey the implicit governing rules of the network: to fulfill one’s obligation, to return renqing (reciprocity and favor) at the right timing, and to honor one’s word so that people who trust you will not lose face (Hwang, 1987). In such social bonding, explicit agreements about a business transaction become less relevant. As one merchant explained: “if one is not a gentle-tradesman, he will cheat you anyway”. Thus, in the Guanxi network, ‘my word is my bond’ becomes the norm in business dealing. From this viewpoint, the e-marketplace was perceived as a disruption to this trust relationship. As the CEO of a garment manufacturer noted:

Our suppliers would consider the e-marketplace as nothing more than another way to beat down the price. This is perceived as a potential betrayal of the relationships we have built over a long time.

Moreover, SMEOnline was seen to de-personalize such relationships, as one CEO of a food company further explained:

Our Guanxi is built upon mutual trust that each party will act for the other’s benefit in spite of the absence of a legal contract. I don’t have to tell my partner what should be done or what should be avoided. I ‘know’ that whatever he does will honor our relationship. B2B [the e-marketplace] will de-personalize such a process and potentially harm such a close relationship.

The price of distrust: Modern Chinese merchants still seem to value moral virtue over technical knowledge in business. The experienced merchants, having self-cultivated themselves as gentlemen, can establish respectful relationships with others so that they can avoid the traps set by xiao-ren. In the Guanxi network, there are two discernable ways of applying sanctions to dishonest merchants. First, community punishment (similar to Maghribi traders’ sanction strategy) will be used to exclude dishonest merchants from one’s trust networks, which in turn interact closely with others’ trust networks. According to this logic, the present expected utility value of an honest agent is larger than what the agent can obtain by cheating (Greif, 1993: 530). Secondly, the dishonest merchants face discrimination as xiao-ren in their words and deeds, and from others’ assessment. Such a bad reputation is considered not only as counterproductive to future business but also a public humiliation and loss of dignity (face).

Fears of incongruent value: The e-marketplace systems are built upon the economic assumption of impersonal trust, with a large number of price-taking anonymous buyers and sellers supplied with perfect information. In such a marketplace, according to Granovetter (1985: 486), exchanges can function without any prolonged social contact between parties, and social relations thus become frictional matters. The various traders that contract together need not enter into continuing relationships. If traders encounter distrust conditions, they can simply move on to many other traders who are willing to conduct business on market terms. Dishonest practices are to be averted because institutional arrangements make them too costly.

Given the absence of strong institutional supports, Chinese merchants conduct business through the reputation/relationship-based mechanism of trust production. The Guanxi-based network and the emphasis on Confucian self-cultivation are means to build Chinese merchants’ social capital and ultimately their successful business. This is in stark contrast to the impersonal e-marketplace. The president of one electronics company expressed his distrust:

I don’t think the idea of the e-marketplace will get any further in Chinese business communities. We value the Guanxi network above conducting formal business. It is important for us to maintain our reputation in the network; and it is based on the trust relationship within this Guanxi network that has been established over time. It’s a word-of-honor business. I don’t believe that the e-marketplace-with a group of online buyers and suppliers-can replace this kind of trust.

Therefore, when the adopters say that they prefer to do business based on Guanxi, this claim can be backed up by previous research on the value of social networks and codes-ofhonor in conducting business (Greif, 1989, 1993). As the cost of cultivating Guanxi networks of trust is high, the Chinese traders are fearful of jeopardizing the existing Guanxi networks by opting for the e-marketplace.

Understanding the role of Guanxi networks thus helps us understand how economic actions may be affected by social relations and why the potential adopters in this case were worried about replacing Guanxi-based trust with the impersonal trust sustained by the e-marketplace. As one adopter (a consumer goods retailer) explained:

How do you build trust in situations where no track record exists? The existing trust solutions of an e-marketplace are technology-based. They don’t build trust; they at most offer ways to prevent machine down time and discourage cyber crime.

With this expectation, the potential adopters perceived that mutual trust built upon reciprocal favoritism could not be replaced easily by the e-marketplace. One senior executive of a medium-sized telecommunications equipment trader explained his skepticism:

The Guanxi network always involves favoritism and obligation. That does not mean we don’t care about quality and price… We know that our trusted partner will try their best to deliver products according to the specification, with a reasonable price… We know that our personal reputation depends on each deal we make. Of course, we would like to expand our networks and grow our businesses [through the e-marketplace]. But the question is: will the e-marketplace help us to build trust with unknown partners? I don’t think so. Trust is built more through a developmental process than a technological solution.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Theoretical implications

This study examines the resistance to e-marketplace adoption through an interpretation of technology fears by analyzing both reliability-related and value-oriented distrust (cf. Baba, 1999; Walsham and Barrett, 1999). On this basis, it discusses the problem of value incongruence in relation to the role of merchant culture.

The distrustful paths to adoption difficulties: Sitkin and Roth (1993) identify the issues of reliability-related and value-oriented distrust (see also Sitkin and Stickel, 1996). They remark that the use of legalistic remedies would be relatively effective only with respect to reliability-related problems. Expanding on Sitkin and Roth’s (1993) idea, this study focuses on the role of reliability-related and value-oriented distrust/fear in the adoption of an e-marketplace. Based on the empirical analysis, it is possible to propose four paths of technology adoption, as shown in Fig. 1.

Following Path 1, the adopters perceive the risks associated with, for example, fraudulent practices, unsafe technologies, and unfamiliar merchants in the e-marketplace. Thus, the adopters perceive a reduction of reliability (in terms of competence and intentional trust). This increases the level of reliability-related distrust among adopters towards both the intermediary and the e-marketplace. Consequently, reliability-related fear is increased, and e-marketplace is rejected. This finding is consistent with the studies of adoption barriers.

Following Path 2, to restore reliability-related trust, the intermediary may introduce the institutional, reputational and technological mechanisms. The possible result is that adopters perceive the positive enhancement of competence and intentional trust, and reliability-related distrust/fear is reduced. This ultimately might increase the prospects for e-marketplace acceptance, which is consistent with the online trust literature.

Following Path 3, however, there is another tacit dimension that is often neglected by the intermediary: the merchants’ cultural assumption about commerce. What is embedded in the e-marketplace is a mode of trust production supported by institution-based mechanisms. This contradicts the relationship-based trust produced by social mechanisms (e.g. a code-of-honor as a jun-zi) among Chinese merchants. Although the adopters may be unable to articulate their fears, they can rightly sense that there is an incongruence of values. The adopters’ value-oriented distrust and fear will thus be increased, leading to rejection of the e-marketplace eventually.

Following Path 4, if the intermediary were to implement technical remedies (such as building trust via the institutional, reputational and technological mechanisms) in order to overcome the adopters’ distrust, they would most likely encounter indifferent responses. Worse, the technical remedies would possibly increase value-oriented distrust, leading to entrenching fears and persisting resistance to technology (e.g. adopters might be suspicious and think that there must be some cover-ups behind all the expensive technologies).

The analysis of distrust contributes to the existing online literature in two ways. First, the current literature emphasizes the restoration of trust by enhancing ‘the guardian of trust’ (Shapiro, 1987) through, for example, the institution-, reputation- and technologybased mechanisms. This perspective aims to resolve the problem of reliability-related distrust (see Path 2 in Fig. 1) but largely neglects the problems associated with valueoriented distrust. Building upon Sitkin and Roth (1993), the present study stresses the importance of resolving the distrust issues in e-marketplace adoption by employing both perspectives.

Cultural persistence: The findings extend the study of Kumar et al. (1998) in two main ways. First, Kumar et al. examine ‘trust’ as the third rationality (in contrast to technical-economic and socio-political rationality) to explain the failed adoption of an e-marketplace; the present study highlights ‘distrust’ and its link through ‘fear’ to technology adoption. Secondly, Kumar et al. (1998) elaborate the social dynamics of trust (in Prato) in relation to mutual identification and lo struscio; the present study extends this analysis to the Chinese context and offers an initial explanation of the trust-production mechanism through Guanxi, renqing (favor and reciprocity), and jun-zi vs. xiao-ren (gentleman vs. rabble).

Previous studies of e-marketplace adoption have focused on the analysis of adoption barriers and online trust building. Insufficient attention has been paid to understand intersocietal adoption, which means adapting e-marketplaces to cultural contexts that are different from the source culture. The findings explain that the adoption of an e-marketplace involves not only implementing technology but also transferring a different mode of trust-production mechanism. This is equivalent to asking the merchants to transform their socio-cultural practice of trading. As societal culture often persists over generations (Greif, 1994; Zucker, 1977), transforming culture and its embedded trustproduction may not be easy.

The Chinese market system tends to work through the implicit Guanxi dynamics. Although the use of Guanxi consumes much time and energy, it offers greater capacity for generating a frame of action for business exchange, and provides a cushion against unpredictable risks for Chinese traders. In this condition, distrust is thus not always a prerogative term; it functions to identify situations in which merchants need to protect themselves, and in this sense it is an alternative social mechanism to signal danger and to reduce uncertainty by promoting avoidance of risk (Barber, 1983). In this market system, trust is more effectively produced by reputation/relationship-based mechanisms so that ‘standards of expected behavior’ are generated to discourage dishonest merchants (Granovetter, 1985: 498). This is qualitatively different from the situation within the Western market system, for the latter continues to depend on institutional arrangements rather than on long-term social relationships (Boisot and Child, 1996).<sup>1</sup> Table 5 presents a comparison of the two different market systems and the implications for technology adoption.

Overall, this study contributes to the literature of e-marketplace adoption by highlighting the value of distrust/fear-based analysis and the significance of cultural persistence at the societal level. When the adoption of an e-marketplace is contemplated in non-Anglo-American contexts, it is not sufficient to remove adoption barriers and restore online trust through technical remedies; we also need to analyze the possible valueoriented distrust/fear and understand how such distrust/fear might stem from the sociocultural mechanism of trust production in a particular societal context.

## 5.2. Practical implications

The analysis of technology fears bring to the surface three practical concerns: the appropriation of technology, the temporal nature of the fear phenomenon, and the possible limit of value-oriented distrust/fear. These three concerns will be discussed in turn.

The appropriation of e-marketplaces: For companies seeking to expand e-marketplaces to China, Korea, Japan, India and other Asian countries, the distrust/fear analysis offers useful guidelines to minimize resistance to e-marketplace adoption. In markets that are sustained by institution-based trust, information systems are likely to be based on transparency, real-time product information updating, and impersonal transactions. However, in a market system sustained by relationship/reputation-based trust, in addition to economic and information exchanges, business transactions are likely to include the maintenance of social relationships as well (Kumar et al., 1998).

Therefore, for Chinese merchants, the implementers of e-marketplaces should consider developing information systems that take into account the role of existing Guanxi relationships in developing economic activities and producing trust. For instance, such systems could provide personal Guanxi-based checklists, circulate information about cheaters, and connect e-marketplaces to mobile phones and videoconferencing systems in order to support rich communications. Thinking along these lines may provide useful hints for appropriating e-marketplaces suitable for Chinese merchants.

The temporal nature of the fear phenomenon: One might question whether Chinese merchants’ fear might only be a temporary phenomenon. This is to argue that Chinese merchants are simply older and have not experienced technology enough, unlike young teenagers in Asia, who are at the forefront of the world’s technology adoption. In other words, even if the trading practice of Chinese merchants is different from that of the US, perhaps with enough experience with technology, they will use e-marketplace anyway. Thus, one might ask: would the distrust-related issues still be applicable to Chinese merchants?

This question defies easy answer. Based on the findings, we may address this question from at least three related angles. The first issue is: will familiarity breed online trust (e.g. Gefen, 2000)? It assumes that once people are familiar with computers, their unfounded fears will be dissipated and successful adoption will follow. It is possible that the new generation of young technically informed managers would more readily accept e-marketplaces. However, we must recognize that familiarity only overcomes reliabilityrelated distrust with sufficient training and with the trust-building mechanisms in place (institutional, reputational and technological trust). Chinese merchants may still resist e-marketplaces if the issue of value incongruence is not fully addressed. We need to appreciate that their fundamental fear is associated with unfamiliar trading partners, the hosting institutions, and, most importantly, how trust is produced if there is an absence of effective institutional arrangements.

Another issue is: will the cultural resistance to e-marketplaces be temporary? In view of China’s opening policy, will the culture-oriented trust problem in commerce still be valid? The answer may be a mix of yes and no. Zucker (1977) observes that cultural persistence requires three main steps: transmission (heuristics are passing from one generation to another), maintenance (and these heuristics become socially constructed facts, rules, and norms embedded in individual behavior), and resistance to change. Similarly, Greif (1994: 916) observes that a society’s cultural beliefs lead to ‘a distinct trajectory of organizational development’. As these various cultural beliefs become a part of the institutional framework of social groups, they determine ‘the costs and benefits of various actions’ (Greif, 1994: 925). As cultural beliefs persistently influence the social pattern of economic relations in a particular society, culture-laden value is thus difficult (but not impossible) to transform. Hence, overcoming value-oriented distrust/fear is not at all easy.

A third possible issue is: will the distrust issue disappear as culture evolves? For example, will a collectivist society’s culture beliefs be transformed into individualist beliefs? Possibly, as Zucker (1986) has shown, from the mid-1800s to the early 1900s, high rates of immigration and internal migration, plus unstable business enterprises, led to the disruption of reputation/relationship-based trust in the US. As the US developed into industrialized society, institution-based trust was activated due to demographic changes (there were more immigrants, and it became more difficult to establish relationship-based networks). American society thus had established bureaucratic organizations, professional credentialing, a service economy (with the emergence of intermediaries such as banks, insurance companies, and government), regulation and legislation. Consequently, institutions began to offer a more efficient trust-production mechanism than the reputation/relationship-based trust.

Here we witness a fundamental shift in culture and economic patterns in US society, according to Zucker (1986). Before industrialization, US society was more of a collectivist culture in which economic interactions occurred mostly within a specific group, and contracts were enforced through informal contracts. After the industrialization period, US society was transformed into an individualistic culture in which most interactions were across social groups, specialized organizations enforced contracts, and there was a considerable mobility across groups. This cultural shift affected economic performance, the development of new institutions, and in turn, the culture itself. In this case, the change of culture shaped the mechanism through which trust was produced.

Hence, it is possible that in the future Chinese societies may experience a similar cultural shift like their American counterpart. But this will have to be based at least on two pre-conditions: (1) a fundamental shift in demography: in this circumstance, Chinese societies may have to undergo a social transformation in their ethnic composition (e.g. the possible social implication of the one-child policy in China); (2) these Chinese societies (e.g. China, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Singapore) need to establish the ‘reliable’ institutions (e.g. the emergence of intermediaries such as banks, insurance companies, and government) in order to overcome Chinese merchants’ ‘innate distrust and fear’ towards these institutions over centuries (Chen, 2001: 46). Thus, it is predictable that the analysis of value-oriented distrust/fear and its cultural issues will become evermore relevant as more e-marketplace-related technologies are transferred to Asia.

The possible limit of value-oriented distrust: This study stresses the importance of examining culturally constructed value-oriented distrust/fear. However, one might question whether this is always the case. For example, why is Singapore TradeNet (which is a nationwide platform for electronic trade documentation) a success story while SMEOnline is not?

The success of TradeNet (see Applegate et al. 1993, 1995; Konsynski and King, 1990; Teo et al., 1997), at first glimpse, seems to offer a counter argument for socio-cultural analysis. However, we need to note that TradeNet is a G2B (Government-to-Business) rather than a B2B (Business-to-Business) system. It is under the supervision of TDB (the Trade Development Board), which implies compulsory adoption for traders (mainly for companies operating in Singapore) and other government agencies. In a sense, it is more an issue of power relationships and trust (see, for example, Gallivan and Depledge, 2003). Moreover, the participating traders are pre-screened by the TDB, and thus it is more likely that the trusted network will be established via the consortia model (government is the hosting intermediary, in this case). There is no need for a social mechanism to produce trust, and the project’s success is more predictable. Without these preconditions, other intermediaries may continue to encounter adoption difficulties if they consider insufficiently the implications of distrust and cultural persistence (e.g. i-metal.com in Hempel and Kwong, 2001). However, we must also appreciate that, even with a government-led control mechanism, the adoption of an e-marketplace may still face insurmountable challenges if value-oriented distrust is not recognized, as the Hong Kong TradeLink has shown (Ng et al., 1998; Surmon and Huff, 1995).

## 5.3. Limitations and future research directions

The present analysis needs to be enhanced in respect of three areas of possible weakness. First, the selection of a particular Chinese community may affect the empirical observations. We need to acknowledge that the Chinese business community includes traders from overseas—from Hong Kong, Taiwan and other areas in Asia. The overseas Chinese traders may also provide different empirical findings on how Guanxi is used within different institutional contexts. Future research should take these issues into account.

Secondly, the selection of a specific industry may also be crucial. This study covers a range of industries so that it is difficult to identify the specific problems relating to a specific industry. Different industries, such as computers, petroleum and machinery, may present a different set of challenge in addition to the socio-cultural issues discussed in this study. Such industry-specific issues are also important in determining the success of e-marketplace adoption.

Thirdly, more empirical examples are needed to highlight how Chinese traders employ Guanxi as trust-production mechanisms in conducting, sustaining and maintaining business exchanges. This is important because such empirical data can help technology designers to contemplate how an e-marketplace can support relationship-based trust characterized by in group favoritism, reciprocity and face-saving where there is a lack of supportive institutional arrangements.

## 6. Conclusion

While most e-marketplace intermediaries still blame the Asian economic downturn and devote more resources to promoting the benefits of efficiency and cost savings, a word of caution is necessary:

One topic that is of interest to us is the complexities of B2B [e-marketplace] across multiple borders in terms of regulatory, trade and cultural issues and barriers. We find that a US-centric approach to B2B [e-marketplace] does not adequately cover this from both a solution and an implementation approach. (David Dowling, Partner and Director of e-Commerce, Deloitte Consulting, personal communication, 8 February 2001)

Overall, the present study attempts to address this caution by analyzing technology adoption in diverse cultures. It illustrates that the removal of adoption barriers and the deployment of technology-based trust may bring about only minimal improvements in e-marketplace adoption, if issues of distrust and cultural persistence are not fully considered. The implementer needs to recognize that adopting an e-marketplace means asking merchants to accept a set of socio-cultural beliefs and economic assumptions embedded in the technology. On this note, to overcome resistance to e-marketplaces, we must deal with both reliability-related distrust as well as value-oriented distrust (constructed socio-culturally in a particular societal context). Only by tackling the two sources of distrust can the implementer finally allay adopters’ fear towards emarketplaces.
