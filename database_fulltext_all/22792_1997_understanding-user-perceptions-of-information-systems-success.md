# Understanding user perceptions of information systems success

Grafton Whyte, Andy Bytheway\*, Chris Edwards

Information Systems Research Centre, Cranfield School of Management, Cranfield, Bedford MK18 3DZ, UK

## Abstract

There is constant pressure on information systems management to improve service to users, often at the same time that their budgets are being reduced. The question of service management is important, and should be explicitly addressed by IT management.

It follows that we must understand the attributes of information systems that users perceive to be important, because it is these attributes that underpin their perceptions of information systems success. Previous research work dealing with the perceptions of users has led to a number of useful models, but these models are not necessarily appropriate in all situations. Further, we must try to understand how specific attributes relate to the perceived success of the information system and this is not always possible with previous models.

By reference to the service management literature and by using the repertory grid technique, this paper develops a set of service attributes which give a good indication of user perceptions of information system success. The paper examines their relationship with the nature of the organisation, the level and involvement of users, and the kind of system to which they relate. It is found that there is no single overall set of attributes that relate to user perceptions of success, but it is possible to find subsets that do. The measurement and analysis of these attributes are helpful in setting management policies and guidelines for the improvement of perceptions of information systems success. q 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: User perceptions; User satisfaction; Information systems service management

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. The issue

During the 1980s typical organisational expenditure on information systems (IS) was between 2% and 4% of revenue, resulting in a multi-million pound industry which expanded at an ever-increasing rate.<sup>1</sup> Despite this huge expenditure, information systems have often failed to support businesses effectively, and in particular to satisfy user needs. Research from this period shows that more than half of organisational information systems were perceived as unsuccessful by their users (Eosys, 1986; Butler Cox, 1986; Lyytinen, 1988; Galloway and Whyte, 1989). As a result, and because of the more difficult circumstances for business in the 1990s, senior management have become critically concerned with the return on IS investments.

The IS management response has been to reduce costs whilst striving to maintain or even improve service. There have been wide-scale redundancies and organisations are looking for opportunities to outsource: some have gone as far as outsourcing the whole IS function.<sup>2</sup> In either case – where staff levels are reduced or where the IS function is outsourced – the level and quality of IS provided to the business comes under pressure and becomes a critical issue that has to be managed. Further, ever-demanding management require higher levels of service, again providing a need to focus upon the provision of IS to the business.

This paper attempts to understand what elements users of IS perceive as contributing to the success of those systems.<sup>3</sup> The term ‘user’ here indicates anyone actually using an information system, owning one, or having financial responsibility for one.

## 1.2. Process, product and service

Here we postulate that the provision of information systems is a service with characteristics in common with many other services. This statement is barely contentious and many information system managers would argue that they are already providing service. In practice, however, they appear to spend most of their time monitoring the product (the information system) and the process (development, implementation and operation). Perhaps it is this bias towards products and processes that prevents an understanding of user dissatisfaction with systems.

There is no easy definition of service. The concept of service has many aspects, some of which are easily understood (for example, the time to deliver new systems and enhancements, the time to respond to questions, and the time that the system itself takes to complete its tasks) and some which are not easily measured (such as the way in which technical people communicate with the business, the style of their documentation, and the aesthetic qualities of the systems that they deliver – all of which are more qualitative than quantitative). This paper argues that much IS research has tended to take a technical viewpoint and to deal with the more easily measured aspects of service, as seen by the supplier. In consequence, the qualitative aspects of systems development, implementation and operation as seen by users may not be fully understood, and this may be one of the causes of the failure to deliver information systems that are perceived by users to be successful.

These relationships are embodied in the following hypothesis, which was taken as the single, central foundation for the work: for users of information systems there is a set of service attributes, the perceived performance of which relate strongly and positively to perceptions of successful information systems.

Out of this hypothesis, three key questions were addressed by the research:

• Is there a means to identify service attributes?

• Can a complete set of service attributes be identified that adequately characterise information systems, and are they measurable?

• Are measurements of the achievement of these service attributes good indicators of users’ perceptions of information system success?

## 1.4. Structure of this paper

The paper surveys the information systems literature and the service management literature in order to identify ideas in service management that may be applicable to the research question: understanding user perceptions of information systems success. It describes the way in which a set of service attributes was developed and validated by means of a pilot study. It then describes the application of these attributes to a larger number of cases, in order to measure the extent to which they correlate with success as seen by users and to learn how they vary within and across organisations. Finally, the paper shows how an understanding of service attributes can be used to examine current and possible emerging issues in IS management.

## 1.5. Definitions

The following definitions were adopted for the purposes of this work:

• an information system is a computerised set of organised procedures that, when executed, provides information to support processes, decision making and control in the organisation (Lucas, 1990);

• a successful information system is one which achieves the expectations of its users;

• a user is an individual who uses IS or has managerial responsibility for their use, ownership or financing;

• the expectations of users are expressed by means of service attributes, where

• a service attribute is a measurable characteristic of an information system.

Note that success was not defined. It was left to the users to interpret success in their circumstances and against their own expectations, which may have been concerned with cost savings,customerservice improvement, competitive advantage,or maybe othermatters.

## 2. Recent research

Perhaps it is to be expected that much previous information systems research takes a technological or supply-side viewpoint, dealing with the detail of the information systems product and the process that produces it. Studies into the determinants of success and failure have focused upon the detail of the system development process (requirements analysis, technical design, code development, testing and handover), and the more observ able, tangible characteristics of systems products (such as data volumes and the extent of systems usage).

## 2.1. Process and product approaches in previous work

A review of the information systems literature suggests that there are significant difficulties with the management of information system projects, closely related to the product and process viewpoints: over-optimistic estimates that subsequently lead to the system being delivered late (Keen, 1987; Galloway and Whyte, 1989; Brooks, 1975); projects running over budget, again in most instances because of underestimation of the work involved (Keen, 1987; Rademacher, 1989); ill-defined project objectives, mostly arising from uncertainty regarding the business needs to be satisfied (Keen, 1987; Lyytinen, 1988, Rademacher, 1989); lack of user commitment to the project and system (Keen, 1987; Tait and Vessey, 1988) and poor communications (Whyte, 1987; Illes, 1990) – typically between users and the development staff; the technical limitations of a system (Lyytinen, 1988, Galloway and Whyte, 1989), including systems which are unfriendly and inflexible and the use of inexperienced staff to develop systems (Illes, 1990; Keen, 1987; Rademacher, 1989).

However, other problems are evident. Work by Jones and Kydd (1988) and Galloway and Whyte (1989) suggests that these difficulties with project management may actually be symptoms of underlying problems, relating to uncertainty (the lack of information, for example information about the project, its context and its purpose), equivocality (the absence of clarity, or excessive ambiguity in the project) and internal inconsistency (between the key elements of an information system project – for example the users, developers’ skills, tools and technologies employed).

Miller (1989) along with a number of other authors (for example, Melone, 1990 and Srinivasan, 1985), suggests the idea of systems usage as a surrogate for systems effectiveness, but points out that it is beset by a number of complicating issues such as: whether use of the system is mandatory or discretionary, the influence of viable information alternatives, the effect that user experience has on usage, and the extent to which obtained information is actually used.

Other work looks at approaches to improving the contribution of information systems. For example, Kydd (1989) looking at the process of systems development argues that there are four broad but significantly overlapping approaches to improvement, and these are supported and illustrated by many other authors.

For example, technical approaches may seek engineering solutions by doing the work more methodically (further illustrated by: Brooks, 1975; Hughes, 1986; Kuzman, 1989; Morreale, 1985; Kaniper, 1986; Chandler and Holzer, 1986; Simon and Davenport, 1987; Hughes, 1986; Kuzman, 1989; Viskovich, 1988; Casher, 1984; Huling, 1987; Yaffe, 1988). Others adopt a behavioural approach which looks for social issues such as the role of champions and the extent and nature of user involvement (further illustrated by Doll, 1985; Necco, 1989; Pinto and Slevin, 1987; Carroll, 1982; Tait and Vessey, 1988; Jones and Kydd, 1988). The organisational approach focuses on the impact of new systems on the organisation and the reaction of the people affected (further illustrated by Ginzberg, 1980; Rademacher, 1989). Finally, the interactive approach deals with combinations of the above, highlighting the need for adequate communications between the parties involved (further illustrated by Kydd, 1989; Skyrme and Earl, 1990).

## 2.2. The growth of user satisfaction approaches

The first steps towards a service perspective on information systems are evident in the seminal work of Bailey and Pearson (1983), and the frequently-quoted work of Ives et al. (1983). Bailey and Pearson developed a 39-item questionnaire for measuring perceived user satisfaction with IS. Ives et al. built on this work: they improved the reliability and reduced the size of the instrument to 33 items. They also produced a short-form of the instrument with only 13 items, and a 4-item general scale for measuring user satisfaction. Factor analysis identified three things as being closely related to successful IS as perceived by users:

• the quality of the information product being supplied;

• the quality of systems personnel and services;

• the business knowledge and involvement of systems personnel in the business.

The notion of user information satisfaction (UIS) was thereby established and this spawned a number of other studies.

Doll and Torkzadeh, 1988 & 1991 produced a 12-item instrument for measuring enduser satisfaction. The instrument identified and measured five components of end-user satisfaction: the content of systems; accuracy of systems; format of reports; ease of use of systems; the timeliness of systems.

Focusing on information centres, Rivard and Huff (1988) found that the following are good predictors of information centre success: degree of user independence from the systems department; satisfaction with the set-up of the information centre; user friendliness; user attitude; satisfaction with the degree of support received from the information centre.

Magal (1991) in a similar way identified three dimensions of satisfaction: quality of the information centre service; quality of user-developed applications; degree of user self-sufficiency.

The focus of these studies is upon user satisfaction rather than systems products or processes and they extend our understanding of what must be managed, but there are still latent problems.

Melone (1990) suggests that a significant problem with research into user satisfaction is the questionable assumption that user satisfaction is a good surrogate for system success, as satisfied users alone are not indicative of a successful system, nor of business success. It is possible to have systems which users perceive as successful but deliver little to the business. She argues that there is no theoretical basis upon which user information satisfaction translates into behaviour, and she highlights the very subjective nature of the problem.

Another key problem with results from user information satisfaction research is the range and variety of attributes linked to user satisfaction. This variety makes it difficult to find the common ground that could stand as the starting point for a generalised model, although we can observe that there are recurring elements such as user friendliness and the quality of support.

There is an extensive survey of work in this area by DeLone and McLean (1992) which provides 180 references to previously published work – largely from the United States during the 1970s and early 1980s. They derive a model for IS success which relates system quality and information quality to systems use and user satisfaction, and these in turn to the impact on the individual and then the organisation. However, they do not include consideration of the service aspects of information systems provision.

## 2.3. Service operations management research

More recently research into the characteristics of information system success has sought to borrow from the service operations field and has stressed the similarity between IS departments and other service operations (Whyte, 1987; Galloway and Whyte, 1989; Kyu Kim, 1990; Russell and Muskett, 1993; Watson et al., 1993).

Upon examination it becomes clear that the main features of service provision first identified by Sasser et al. (1978) are applicable to IS (see also Normann, 1984; Gronroos, 1983; Gronroos, 1988; Voss et al., 1985). Consider the examples in Table 1 taken from a fast food service outlet and IS in business.

In the service management literature we can find examples of the generic attributes that describe the quality of a service, and instruments with which we can assess them. Definitive work by Parasuraman et al. (1988) and Zeithaml et al. (1988, 1990) in the context of general consumer services identifies certain key service attributes:

• Empathy: caring, individualised attention the company provides its customers.

• Responsiveness: willingness to help customers and provide prompt service.

• Reliability: ability to perform the promised service dependably and accurately.

• Assurance: knowledge and courtesy of employees and their ability to convey trust and confidence.

• Tangibles: appearance of physical facilities, equipment, personnel and communication materials and competence.

They argue that the effective management of these service attributes will lead customers to perceive the service operation as being of superior quality. The minimum goal for any service manager is to achieve a balance between customer expectations and customer perceptions of actual service, as expressed through these service attributes.

Zeithaml et al. developed an instrument for measuring service quality. The instrument consists of two sets of questions, one gauging customer expectations of service and the other perceptions of actual service. A service gap occurs when perceptions differ from expectations. Fig. 2 illustrates aspects of the service model, and shows this service gap as ‘Gap 5’.

Zeithaml et al. argue that this gap between expected and perceived service derives from four other gaps in the service, as shown in the figure. They propose a number of strategies for closing these gaps when they occur. The method for ascertaining which of these gaps is causing problems is by administering a series of questions which probe particular themes within each gap.

Others find shortcomings in this research. Haywood-Farmer and Stuart (1988) argue that the model addresses a host of peripheral aspects of a service but ignores core aspects such as what is being delivered and the extent to which it effectively addresses the needs of the customer. They suggest that service quality dimensions should be extended to include these core aspects. Zeithaml et al. themselves point out that the service quality model is a generic one which has to be extended when used to look at particular industries.

The work by Zeithaml et al. has been widely recognised as a significant step forward in our understanding of service and the factors that influence service quality. The model is now being adopted increasingly by IS researchers as an alternative theoretical approach to the user information satisfaction approach (Kyu Kim, 1990; Watson et al., 1993; Remenyi and Money, 1994).

• early work tended to focus on the process and the product, rather than the service aspects of information systems in business;

## 3. The approach to this research work

This research examines the idea that there are service attributes of information systems which will, when understood and managed effectively, enable information systems departments and their product to be perceived by their users as successful. The research work reported here set out to find a more complete set of attributes than has previously been available in the IS literature, using the concepts developed in service management as a starting point and employing a novel elicitation technique. Rather than reduce and simplify existing service attributes, the work sets out to establish a new and demonstrably complete set; further, it seeks to find out which attributes correlate with IS success in what circumstances, and to what extent.

The work does not attempt to investigate the correlation between perceived IS success and business success – actual or perceived. Whilst it may be possible to speculate about the business success associated with the systems that were the subject of study that was not the intention of the research and it is excluded from this discussion.

## 3.1. The research questions as a starting point

The key questions addressed by the research are presented above. In the light of the review of recent research, and in order to develop a method of working, we discuss ways in which to deal with them.

• Is there a means to identify service attributes? In previous work the service attributes adopted seem to be variable and are not always obviously related to information system success. This reduces our confidence in their relevance and utility. A more rigorous means to identify a more complete set of attributes is needed. Such a means is presented here.

• Can a complete set of service attributes be identified that adequately characterise information systems? We need to find a mechanism whereby service attributes can be reliably measured, so that their relationship with success can be reliably assessed and so that improvements in overall service performance can be traced. Such a mechanism is presented here.

• Are measurements of the achievement of these service attributes good indicators of information systems success? We need to understand the strength of the relationship between individual service attributes and perceptions of success if we are to prioritise our remedial efforts. This element is included in the research.

What similarities or patterns of service attributes and perceived success can be found in information systems in different types of organisations? There are wide variations in the kinds of organisation which use IS: large and small, private and public sector, centralised and decentralised, young and old, to name but a few. A range of different organisations needs to be studied to provide understanding. The case studies undertaken provide the beginning of the required variety.

How are the service attributes perceived by users with different backgrounds and different positions within the organisation? Users at different hierarchical levels within the organisation will have different views about information systems success, and maybe the service attributes that contribute to success. Also, the extent of user involvement in the development of the system may be a factor in the judgement about success. This element is included in the study.

Do different kinds of information system have different service attributes? Different types of information system could affect users’ perceptions of success, especially where the anticipated benefits vary. Success could come from cost savings, improved quality and customer service levels, or from strategic advantage. A variety of systems was included in the studies to achieve such understanding.

The scope of this research therefore embraces much of the model for information systems success developed by DeLone and McLean (1992), although it does not attempt to categorise or limit the drivers of success into the two types that they propose in their model: system quality and information quality. These two concepts are closely associated with the product viewpoint (the quality of the system that is delivered to the user) and the process viewpoint (the quality of the information that is produced by the information system’s processes). Instead, the research reported here develops an unconstrained list of attributes using an elicitation technique which is new to IS research, in order to provide a more broadly-based understanding of the drivers of success.

At the other end of the DeLone and McLean model is the impact of an information system on the individual and on the organisation – the link to real success or failure at the level of the business that decides to make the IS investment. As has been pointed out by Melone (1990), the relationship between individual perceptions of success and information system effectiveness in the business is complex; amongst other things she recommends the use of theoretical models from psychology in future research. In order to avoid these complexities this research work is confined to the users’ perceptions of success. Indeed, the motivation for the work reported here was not to analyse the relationship between user satisfaction and information systems effectiveness in the business, but to identify the elements whereby which users make their personal judgements about success.

## 3.2. The pilot study

A pilot study was undertaken (Whyte and Bytheway, 1996) using the repertory grid technique (Kelly, 1955), which has been found to be effective in eliciting perceptions in management research (Fransella and Bannister, 1977; Smith, 1986; Vyakarnam, 1989). This pilot study established an initial set of service attributes for use in a survey instrument by means of four steps (illustrated in Fig. 3).

## 3.2.1. Step 1: interviews (repertory grid)

The repertory grid technique allows the elicitation of perceptions whilst minimising possible researcher interference or bias. The following elicitation procedure was created for extracting elements and constructs:

1. The respondent was introduced to the research and the interview process.

2. The respondent was asked to identify information systems or related products with which he or she was familiar, and those that they considered the best or worst. To this list was added a notional, ‘ideal’ system or product.

3. These systems were presented back to the respondents three at a time (Kelly’s triadic method) and the interviewee was requested to specify how two of the products differed from the third. In this way the respondent’s constructs were elicited.

## 3.2.2. Step 2: analysis

The data gathered from the interviews allowed the relationship between the constructs and user perceptions of IS success to be analysed. The expert review involved four experienced academic staff from the IS discipline and two independent IS experts; although this review did not take the form of a full Delphic study it did lead to a number of modifications and ultimately to agreement amongst the experts. Finally, a selection of the interviewees (seven of the original 10 – the remaining three were unavailable) were asked to verify attribute definitions relative to the source statements from which they were derived. As a result, further minor modifications were made.

## 3.3. Development of item statements

The pilot study also involved the development of ‘item statements’ which were used in the main study as a means of assessing user perceptions of service attributes (see item 6 in the description of the interviews within the main study, following). Comments made by interviewees were noted and consolidated into 105 item statements for use as ‘agree/ disagree’ questions in the main survey. Two examples illustrate the nature and origin of these item statements:

• From two Reuters users: ‘The level of technical ability displayed by systems personnel always appears very high’ (relating positively to competence).

• From one SEGAS users and five Reuters users: ‘The system is very easy to use (relating positively to friendliness).

## 3.4. Results of the pilot study

Using the repertory grid technique the pilot study identified a set of possible service attributes, with a minimum of bias and based upon the perceptions of information systems users. Each attribute was tested for statistical significance and correlation with success, and as a result of these tests some were rejected. Those surviving the tests were aggregated according to their semantic content to produce a list for further testing.

In this way, the pilot study addressed the first research question: there is a means to identify a set of service attributes and it has been demonstrated. The pilot study also provided a set of item statements to be used in assessing user perceptions of these service attributes.

These results allowed the research to proceed to the main study, which exercised the attributes and item statements in a wider survey providing a larger number of samples.

## 3.5. The main study

The service attributes developed in the pilot study were incorporated into an interviewbased questionnaire, which was then used in nine organisations. A total of 137 users<sup>5</sup> from these nine organisations commented on a total of 118 information systems. Some users commented on more than one system, leading to a total of 283 instances of a user commenting on one or another system. A variety of organisations was chosen including a charity and a public-sector organisation, as well as utilities, manufacturing and service organisations. The objective was to choose different organisations and to work with individuals at different levels, in order to permit this variety to be examined for significant variations in the results. This approach also permits a first examination of the possible effects of different organisational cultures, although there was no attempt in this work to formally assess the cultural aspects of the chosen organisations.

1. The respondent was introduced to the research and the interview process.

4. The respondent’s perception of success of each system was rated on a Likert-type scale of 1 to 7.

5. Interviewees were asked to study the list of service attributes and to mark those that related to the system, then rank the seven most important, and finally to weight each of these out of a score of 10.

## 3.6. Reliability and definition of the attributes

Controls were applied during the main data collection stage to ensure that the survey instrument was working satisfactorily and that the attributes were adequately defined. As explained above, the attribute control was separated into two components: process control and operational control. Further, early in the main data collection it became clear that accessibility is an additional and important attribute of service, so it was added. ## 4. Results and discussion

Three stages of analysis led to results about the attributes and the way that they can be used to explore user perceptions of IS success.

The cross-correlation analysis reveals the extent to which attributes vary with each other: this is important as a first indication of whether the 22 attributes used could be further reduced because they are – for some reason or another – apparently measuring the same thing. The extent to which they really are measuring the same thing could also be examined.

Understanding the relationship between the attributes and users’ perceptions of information system success is the primary purpose of this research. This stage in the analysis investigates the extent to which the attributes might help us to learn about actual user perceptions.

The third stage in the analysis takes a closer look at the data according to variations in the type of system, the kind of user, and the different organisations involved in the main study.

## 4.1. Cross-correlation analysis

Table 5 shows how the attributes related to each other, and it is found that there is some overlap between them.<sup>6</sup> 31% (72 out of 231) of the possible combinations have significance levels of 95% or higher. Six of these have correlations of 0.75 or greater, and by that criterion have been selected for specific comments here. The six below are presented to illustrate the extent of the cross-correlation between attributes, and the way in which real-world arguments can rationalise them.

## • Direction – Process control (0.82)

IS which are a part of a clear strategic direction tend to have greater managerial support and are therefore likely to be better managed as projects (process control). This supports the argument that there needs to be full business and management involvement in the execution of projects, and that projects need to be directed to the intended business benefits. Inappropriate project management styles will obscure direction and put the purpose of the investment at risk.

## • Responsiveness – Reliability (0.78)

If IS staff are perceived as being very responsive then the perception of the system’s reliability is likely to be high. By taking a positive approach to dealing with user queries, it is possible that the actual quality of the system (and therefore its reliability) is enhanced. Where technical staff do not respond to queries users will disassociate themselves from the system, and poor perceptions of reliability will be the consequence.

## • Reliability – Accuracy (0.77)

IS that are accurate and IS staff who provide accurate information are both likely to be perceived as reliable. This is a very natural result, and exactly what one would expect in the circumstances.

## • Specification – Accuracy (0.76)

Systems that have been well specified are more likely to be accurate. This reinforces a very traditional viewpoint that specifications must be done properly, and that user requirements analysis is critical to the systems success.

## • Business alignment – Friendliness (0.75)

Business alignment is concerned with the fit of a system to the business objectives and processes. It is interesting that it relates closely with friendliness, which is a much lower-level concept concerned with ease of use and learning. It is likely that this correlation is picking up a ‘feel-good’ relationship, where systems perceived as having a good business fit are also likely to be perceived as friendly. Equally, it helps us to understand that friendliness is a higher-level consideration for many users than we might have supposed.

## • Friendliness – Accessibility (0.75)

Systems in which data and processes are perceived to be easy to access are also likely to be perceived as friendly. However, systems perceived as friendly may have functions which users find difficult for other reasons. This correlation further reinforces the commonly held view that user-friendliness is an important criterion.

## 4.2. Attributes related to systems success

Fig. 4 shows the way in which the attributes are related to systems success, taking all instances together (the global result). All 22 attributes are included; 17 have a correlation of better than 0.5 and five have a correlation between 0.18 and 0.5.

It is interesting to note that the most important service attribute is quality of specification, the traditional viewpoint long held by many professional information systems people. Close behind are process control and effectiveness, which are likely to be a reflection of the need for confidence in the processes of systems development and delivery, and for real benefits following implementation. The five attributes which score lowest include documentation, operational control, marketing, flexibility and necessity.

## 4.3. Variations in the significance of attributes

The global result provides us with an indication of potentially important service attributes which we could choose to manage more carefully to improve user perceptions of IS success. However, this could be ineffective in particular circumstances because we do not yet know whether there are variations from one situation to the next. Where previous work has fre quently ignored these questions, the research reported here analysed the data in different ways, in order to examine these concerns. In each of the Figs. 5 through 9 the global result is overlaid to provide a reference point, and to permit a discussion of key differences.

We must expect that there will be some variation according to:

## 4.4. Type of system

Using the portfolio model first developed by McFarlan (1984) and extended by Ward (1987) it is possible to categorise information systems into four different types:<sup>7</sup>

• High potential systems which are largely experimental and have only possible future benefits.

• Strategic systems which are important to future success and which are the basis for current competition.

• Key operational systems which provide efficiency and which are critical to current success.

• Support systems which are not critical to current operations but which are valuable for other reasons, for example because they provide local benefits to an office or work group.

• Strategic systems obviously have distinctive characteristics. Friendliness and reporting are also emphasised.

Key operational systems are the traditional applications, such as order processing, inventory management and distribution (although where there are innovative aspects which are important to strategic differentiation they would be classified as strategic). It is not surprising that they fall close to the global result because they are the majority category and weight the global results considerably.

• Support systems are less critical and may often be achieved by the use of packages, or outsourced services. Reliability and competence come through as less important to success, possibly because the application is well known and requires no special knowledge to implement it; also, the lack of criticality reduces the need for reliability. However, specification, process control, effectiveness and accessibility are more important than in the global case although not dramatically so.

• For new systems (less than 2 years old) there is a need to attend to effectiveness, understanding, upkeep, integration, user involvement and documentation.

This differs from old systems (more than 5 years old), where friendliness, accuracy, competence and operational control become significant.

• For systems in the 2–5-year range friendliness, upkeep, integration and responsiveness cease to be so significant but other attributes stay close to the global result.

This is all as we might expect, but how many information system departments actively manage their approach to a variety of systems in different ways, and successfully focus on the critical attributes?

## 4.6. User seniority level

Five levels of seniority were defined including director, senior manager, middle manager, supervisor and operational staff. The results were analysed for differences in correlation with success across these different levels (see Fig. 7).

• Directors place particular importance on reliability, understanding, upkeep, user involvement and necessity.

• Senior managers are also concerned with reliability, and additionally with business alignment.

![](/api/attachments/EWN5NC6Z/fulltext/images/7462d1695be0bd0ab7c6a1ffc05a460333c2a16460a0677d9e38e83ea9317521.jpg)  
Fig. 7. Correlation with system success: seniority level. <sup>O</sup> director; <sup>B</sup> senior manager; <sup>X</sup> middle manager; 3 supervisor; þ staff.

• Middle managers are concerned with flexibility, documentation, responsiveness, direction and specification. We can observe the concerns becoming more pragmatic as we come down the seniority levels.

• Supervisors have a strong perception of the competence of IS staff, the need for user involvement, and the need for the system to be marketed well. Understanding and accuracy are also important. This is a rich perception of the critical attributes of an information system that is obviously based in the need to see the job being done, and being done well.

• At the operational level staff have their own special concerns, for example specification, business alignment, training and necessity. We learn that operational staff do have strong feelings about the qualities of the systems that they use. This is a strong message which might not be the expected one, and which IS people need to be aware of at all stages.

## 4.7. User involvement in development

The question of user involvement in systems development also throws up some interesting insights, especially when we take account of different roles in the organisation – this time in the simpler sense that some users had a management perspective and some users hold a working level perception (see Fig. 8).

![](/api/attachments/EWN5NC6Z/fulltext/images/75eded0d4f20d79bf70f1d20f9c7ef94d57f354ba15db808552995dc1b8a7210.jpg)  
Fig. 8. Correlation with system success: involvement in development. <sup>O</sup> as manager; <sup>B</sup> as operator; <sup>X</sup> not involved.

• Those with a management role involved in systems development are very concerned about the quality of reporting and user involvement (in the more general sense). They also want to see proper provision for training and flexibility.

• Those with a working-level perspective completely reverse some of this strong management concern, being less concerned with reporting but more concerned with friendliness. Elsewhere, they obviously want to be sold the benefits of the system through effective marketing and want to see integration and responsiveness. They also want training.

• Those who were not involved in systems development are less concerned about most attributes, especially user involvement (hardly surprising!). Other variations do not seem significant.

## 4.8. Organisation

The analysis by organisation is very interesting because it shows wide variations (see Fig. 9) and some of the attributes which seemed unimportant at the global level come through as very important, for some organisations.

![](/api/attachments/EWN5NC6Z/fulltext/images/925f98c3fdf0943514c4f7f6b137f512ccbd70fc763b7d4a02efea0cc43cf7e5.jpg)  
Fig. 9. Correlation with systems success: variation by organisation. All organisations are shown as plain circles (see text).

The global service attributes may cover some of the critical ground in an organisation, but it is apparent that there is a unique set of attributes which define an organisation’s perception of successful IS, and that the degree of correlation with success is markedly different. Most organisations have more than seven attributes which correlate strongly with success.

• IT managers need to run a survey at the organisational level if they really want to understand how their user’s perceive the service attributes that relate to successful information systems.

We can also illustrate the differences between the organisations by commenting on three of the organisations.

Reuters is the premier world-wide supplier of news and electronic information. Billions of pounds in many currencies are traded weekly across the world, based on the information Reuters supply. Fortunes are won and lost in moments. The competition to sell services into such markets is, to put it mildly, fierce.

It is therefore not surprising to see that Reuters users demand the same standards from their internal IS: the three service attributes that are most critical are marketing, effectiveness and reliability.

Kelloggs is the premier producer and supplier of cereal products and has a clear vision of how it wants to compete. The religious background of its founders has made Kelloggs a very people-oriented organisation, with a co-operative style of working.

Users perceive that the IS department should have a clear strategy (direction) for developing information systems and that systems should support the business (business alignment). The unnecessary development of systems (necessity) should be avoided because this wastes money and hurts profitability. The co-operative style of working is reflected in the need for user involvement and friendliness.

Anglian Water was privatised, during the late 1980s, along with other UK water authorities. The organisation appears to be trying to shake off its rather sleepy public sector utility image and adopt a more ‘lean and mean’ profile. However, being a regulatory body, they cannot make decisions purely on commercial bases and this leads to some tensions.

Their service attributes for successful information systems seems to reflect this dual existence. The monitoring and treatment of water means that systems have to be reliable, accurate and effective. In cases of emergency such as in water contamination, telemetry systems need to be very accessible. Their new emphasis upon making a profit is reflected in the need for business alignment.

## 5. Concluding comments

## 5.1. What has been achieved by this work?

Problems with information systems are still prevalent and previous research has only partially addressed the possible reasons. Previous work has either taken a supply-led viewpoint, dealing mostly with the development process or the technical attributes of the delivered system, or it has taken an incomplete view of the non-technical issues. By using the repertory grid to identify a more complete set of user-defined attributes, and by rigorously analysing the relationship between those attributes and the perceived success of information systems, this work has identified a large set of service attributes and examined how they might apply in different situations.

## 5.2. The research questions revisited

In order to draw together the results and conclude, it is appropriate to recall the questions originally presented.

## • Is there a means to identify service attributes?

Within the pilot study the repertory grid technique led to a set of service attributes (and service item statements) which was, with only a few exceptions, statistically reliable and valid. In the subsequent main phase of the work, only minor changes were necessary.

## • Can a complete set of service attributes be identified that adequately characterise information systems?

A set of 22 service attributes has been identified, all of which relate to user perceptions of successful information systems.<sup>8</sup> Seventeen of these are strongly related, having correlation coefficients of at least 0.5 at significance levels of p 0.0001, and these are likely to define success in the general case:

Accessibility Accuracy Business Alignment Competence Direction Effectiveness Friendliness Integration Process Control Reliability Reporting Responsiveness Specification Training Understanding Upkeep User Involvement

The remaining five have correlation coefficients between 0.18 and 0.5, and although they passed the initial significance tests they are less likely to define success:

Documentation Flexibility Marketing Necessity Operational control

• Are measurements of the achievement of these service attributes good indicators of information systems success?

These service attributes are likely to define perceptions of success for many users of IS. If information system management ensures that their service performs well against these service attributes, then there is a likelihood that the majority of users will perceive information systems to be successful.

However, to manage information systems even better, the actual attributes in any specific situation should be identified and will depend upon the organisation, the nature of the system, and the relationship of the users to that system.

• What similarities or patterns of service attributes and perceived success can be found in IS in different organisations?

At the organisational level, statistical tests show that the combination of service attributes related to successful information systems is significantly different in detail from the global view. Therefore, although there are 17 strongly correlated service attributes at the global level there are going to be particular instances where the organisational needs are significantly different.

Systems and software service providers need to be aware of these differences. It is not true that a singular approach to service management will necessarily lead to success, and in each case it is important to understand the particular needs of an organisation. Although this work focused on the views of individuals and elicited evidence at that level, it has shown that there is an aggregate view at the organisational level that can be identified and that will – if it is recognised by IS providers – lead to a greatly enhanced probability of perceived success.

Since the conclusion of the research work this analysis method has been successfully applied in further cases, at the organisational level, to help identify the means to improving the perceptions of IS success. It is possible to identify a service profile for an organisation which indicates clearly how important are the different attributes, and how well an organisation is doing in each. Periodic re-assessment then shows how much improvement has been achieved as a result of remedial actions, or to what extent the situation has deteriorated.

• How are the service attributes perceived by different kinds of user within the organisation?

Different users – having different kinds of involvement in the development and use of an information system – can have completely opposing views of what is important to success. The attributes seen as important by different hierarchical levels in the organisation are also different.

It might be argued that this is to be expected, but this work has provided a means to assess what is seen as important and not important for each group at little cost. Typically, the variability of user views is considerable and it is important to understand the differences and to develop an aggregated or even a consensus viewpoint, so that expectations at different hierarchical levels and amongst different groups can be dealt with more effectively. This research has provided the means to achieve this.

## • Do different kinds of information system have different attributes?

Both the nature and age of an information system are important in identifying the attributes which affect perceptions of success.

Non-critical support systems (which are good candidates for outsourcing) are likely to be judged quite differently to critical key operational and strategic systems (where it is important to maintain control over the quality and direction of the system development). Although there were fewer instances available in this study, strategic systems seem to have particular attributes which must be managed effectively. The age of a system affects the critical attributes in a more predictable way, but it is shown that there are important differences which should not be ignored by the party responsible for system upkeep and maintenance.

## 5.3. Summary

Perhaps the most important overall result of this work is that there is no single solution to the question of what must be managed in order to achieve user perceptions of information systems success. There are important differences deriving from organisational, user and system variations which can modify the view as to which attributes are important. The work has demonstrated that it is possible to analyse these differences and leads to the possibility that appropriate policies and priorities can be adopted to deal with them.

At a time when organisations are looking to outsource elements of IT there is a need to put into place relevant measures of service attributes of the outsourced elements. Where outsourcing is not an issue, IS management can now choose to measure how well it is doing in the eyes of the users, and how their efforts correlate with perceptions of information systems success. This is an improvement over the ‘cookbook’ approach which offers a standard set of measures (often developed by the IS department) which it is assumed correlates with information systems success.

## 5.4. Further work

As expected in exploratory research such as this, there is further work that could be done. A summary of some ideas is provided here.

• The question of the correlation of user perceptions of information systems success with business success remains to be addressed. Further work could seek to establish what relationships there are between these attributes of IS and attributes of the ‘host business and its success.

The analysis of different categories of system suggested that key operational and support systems did not differ significantly from the global result as the number of such systems dominate the results. A different classification (or a sub-classification) might expose differences which are important. The number of strategic systems was low, and it would be extremely interesting to research carefully in this category, with a larger number of cases.

This research has made no attempt to analyse how international considerations or organisational culture might affect the perceptions of success, although it has provided minor evidence that culture might be an issue; no doubt international variations will also be interesting to study. There is a large body of literature on international differences and organisational culture which would provide a framework for this kind of analysis when incorporated into the method of working presented here.

● This work has not attempted to identify causal relationships between attributes, perceptions of success, and other aspects of user behaviour. Others have argued (Melone,

1990) that if we are to link perceptions to behaviour then we must incorporate current psychological theories. Extensions in this direction would be useful.
