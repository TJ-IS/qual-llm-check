# From efficiency to flexibility: the strategic use of information in the airline industry

Luís Monteiro $^{a}$ , Stuart Macdonald $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, UK

$^{b}$ Management School, University of Sheffield, Sheffield S1 4DT, UK

Paper received July 1995; revised paper accepted for publication by Professor R.D. Galliers, April 1996

## Abstract

This paper explores the development of competition in the US airline industry from an information perspective. It analyses the uses made of information by industry participants from the regulated era to the present, and finds growing strategic use of information. Information is an enabler, allowing things to be done, but information can also be a resource in itself. The US airline industry has progressed from the obvious use of information for organisational efficiency, to an increasingly strategic use of information for organisational flexibility. The industry provides an excellent example of the route that other information-intensive industries can be expected to follow.

Keywords: Information; Strategy; Airline industry; Information system

## 1. Introduction

The airline business is a politically charged activity, target of much regulatory attention, and a difficulty subject of economic theory. Its information intensity, competitive conduct and peculiar rationality have caused many deviations from academic and regulatory prediction and theory. It has undergone enormous transformation, especially in the United States, where tight regulation was dramatically loosened by the 1978 Deregulation Act. This legislation was the fruit of academic and administrative consensus that the deregulated airline industry would become close to perfectly competitive. According to prevailing economic theory, conditions were conducive to such an outcome: airline capital and resources were mobile, airports were public utilities, and there was no evidence to suggest potential for economies of scale and scope, or for barriers to entry and exit. Theory insisted that potential entry by new firms would serve to discipline the competitive conduct of the small number of market participants, and so price and service would remain competitive.

But deregulation produced surprises, many of them springing from the growing strategic use of information by some airlines. The deregulation of the US airline industry should have brought increased competition. Instead, it allowed companies to make increasingly strategic use of information (for example, in computer reservation systems, yield management systems, and frequent flyer programmes), which meant that use of information often determined competitive advantage. The development of the US airline industry since deregulation illustrates this transition from the use of information for efficiency to the use of information for the flexibility that strategy demands.

## 2. Efficiency or flexibility?

Organisational efficiency requires the capability to use information to carry out routine activities. It can be argued that the need to exploit this capability is the very purpose of organisation (Arrow, 1974). But organisations cannot simply become more and more streamlined in what they already do; they must change, particularly in response to changes in their environment. Formulating and implementing organisational change appropriate to a dynamic environment is strategy (Whittington, 1993). The organisation that would be flexible, that would change, cannot easily do so with only the information it already contains. This must be supplemented with other information from its environment, often information very different from that which the organisation has been accustomed to using for its own efficiency. Mixing the two is a strategic challenge. So, too, is acquiring the necessary external information for the organisation's own internal information systems are ill-suited to searching and screening in a world which is not structured to meet the organisation's efficiency requirements.

Organisations find information much, much easier to use for efficiency than for flexibility (Macdonald, 1995). For the former, the organisation needs to deal only with its own information and with that which comes from other organisations with which it has formal agreements. Information systems rather than information transactions are required (Egelhoff, 1993). The information is thoroughly familiar to the organisation and generally neatly codified. The channels through which it flows replicate the hierarchical structure of the organisation and pose no threat to structure and control (Pettigrew, 1972, 1987). This is the sort of information with which information technology deals most readily. It facilitates the type of information transfer that is often associated with the large organisation, with mass production and with routine work organisation.

External information presents problems for the organisation: it is hard to find, to acquire, to mix with information already in the organisation, and to use. Market transactions to acquire such information being difficult — it is hard to express demand for the supply of what, by definition, is not known — a common organisational response is to seek ownership of the information, just as the organisation would seek ownership and control of any other valued resource (Forsgren, 1989). Hence the attraction of collaborative schemes, joint ventures, mergers and take-overs to acquire external information. While internalising the external may avoid market failure in information transactions, it does not avoid organisational failure. This can be all too evident in organisations which insist that new information meet the demands of their internal information systems, and which use information as much for re-enforcing structure and control as for knowledge (Lamberton, 1984, 1993; Macdonald, 1992).

Organisational efficiency can be a major obstacle to organisational flexibility — the ability to respond and to change — in that the introduction of new information, strange information from the external environment, may disrupt what has been painstakingly perfected. Because the nature of information makes market transactions in information difficult, and the nature of organisation insists on formal transactions, the informal, even serendipitous, acquisition of external information becomes important. The development of the US airline industry since deregulation illustrates how some airline companies managed to acquire information for flexibility, and how they gradually, almost incidentally, learned to use this information strategically and to enormous effect.

## 3. The regulatory environment

## 3.1. The regulated era

A major characteristic of the regulated era in the US was the very limited scope it offered management to influence the performance of airlines. The Civil Aeronautics Act of 1938 established the Civil Aeronautics Board (CAB) as the governing body to regulate the airline industry in the United States. The CAB was given the authority to set prices, control routes, and to “extend anti-trust immunity to carrier business practices and mergers” (Caves et al., 1985). This last created almost insuperable barriers to entry to the airline industry: between 1938 and deregulation in 1978, “the CAB had not allowed entrance of a single new trunk airline” (Rakowski, 1990).

As the industry matured and the regulatory pattern evolved, increasingly standardised CAB policies reduced managerial prerogatives to the point where passenger service was the only competitive variable available for manipulation (Meyer and Oster, 1981). Fruhan (1972) has sought to determine why some airlines were more successful than others, and what less successful firms could do to improve their performance. He discovered a limited ability of management to affect changes in the regulated environment: “managers have a very narrow choice of directly controlled parameters to which an effective competitive strategy might be tied. Indeed . . . CAB decisions in the areas where it exercised direct control were more important to an individual carrier’s overall profitability than the decision of that carrier’s own management team” (Fruhan, 1972). Carriers had virtually no control over the two main influences on their profitability: fares and routes. Amongst nine variables often used in airline profitability studies, Fruhan developed three broad categories: route structure, fare structures and equipment policy. It was in this third category that “boom and bust cycles of airline profitability were causally linked to the competitive environment”. His regression model, using data from 1955 through 1966, could find no clear relationship between relative airline profitability and management-controlled variables. This led Fruhan to conclude that in matters of profitability the CAB represented each carrier’s first string management team; the second string team (the ones with shareholder responsibility) were allowed to play only when the outcome from the game was essentially decided. Kahan (1988) found that while passenger service included amenities such as low seating density and better food and liquor, the service features with most industry impact were scheduling and equipment. This limited range of strategic options available to airlines in the regulated environment may explain their survival despite what Caves calls “grossly ineffective managements” (Caves, 1988).

It is possible to speculate, as Levine (1987) has done, that this heavily regulated environment denied airline management many of the strategic options normally available in other industries, and drew attention to possible alternatives. “In effect, the CAB operated a controlled cartel, not only for the production of airline output, for which deregulation demonstrated that substitute production could be easily arranged — just as academic analysts had predicted — but also for the production of information about an exceptionally complex consumer service” (Levine, 1987). Perhaps, but it was not until after deregulation that the production and distribution of this information was to become the source of much revenue and much market power.

## 3.2. US airline deregulation

The Airline Deregulation Act of 1978, and the two years of regulatory reform that preceded it, introduced carrier managers to a much larger array of competitive threats and opportunities. No longer were airline managers to be gentlemanly second string players enjoying a leisurely game on a levelled playing field. They were forced to rationalise existing operations and to become strategists.

While exogenous factors, such as the fuel shock of 1979 (which reduced economically feasible capacity), and the air traffic controllers' strike of 1981 (which limited the capacity at major airports), had significant influence, it was the airlines' new found freedom over pricing and route structure decisions that began to shape their strategies. However, airlines did not enter the deregulated era well adapted to a world of free competition. "Four decades of CAB control had not only shaped entry, price, supply and demand, but had penetrated to the operational core of the regulated firms. Route structure and fleet composition, the essential plant and operating method of the airline business — work rules and crew assignments, terminal and gate investments, organisation of maintenance, and all critical marketing activities — were shaped to fit the routes and the fleet" (Vietor, 1989). Many airlines had (and still have) labour costs higher than those of new entrants. Virtually all had route structures that were artefacts of regulation and unrelated to customer demand in the new liberalised markets. Virtually all had been driven by regulation to pursue pricing policies different from those demanded by the marketplace, and very different from those which had been predicted for unregulated markets (Levine, 1965). Contractual commitments, unproductive and expensive labour arrangements, purchase debts, and long term leases of unsuitable equipment inflated the costs of incumbent airlines well above those of new entrants.

Deregulation represented a threat to incumbent airlines and an opportunity to potential new entrants. “If committed costs, information lags, transaction costs, firm-specific investments, and sheer human inertia play a role in the behaviour of real world airlines, holdover firms should have been at a disadvantage in comparison with the new entrants” (Levine, 1987). The irony was that, despite a larger number of competitive variables available to strategists, the new freedom also meant that strategic options were limited. Defensive strategies and cost cutting were required and the only realistic way incumbents could survive was by exploiting their incumbency status and their larger number of manageable variables to re-create barriers to entry and to develop the economies of scale and scope previously ignored by academics and regulators.

Airlines have learnt to compensate for above-market cost by successfully pursuing revenue-earning strategies that generate rents through sheer market power. They have also learnt to use their incumbency and large size to impose costs on rivals (especially new entrants), thus making the production cost differences less significant by changing the nature of competition. As Bleeke (1990) notes, “it is crucial not to underestimate the power of large competitors over time to make big better again. In the deregulated airline industry, the use of hub control, computerized yield management systems, and frequent flyer programmes have been powerful tools for competitors to regain clout and pricing power. The top eight airlines now control 92% of revenue passenger miles compared with 80% before deregulation”. This transformed nature of competition was concomitant with the greater information intensity of the industry. Deregulation of the airline industry in 1978 led to “heightened competition … [which] made the airline industry very information-intensive, and the conduit for much of that information was the reservation systems” (Copland and McKenney, 1988).

According to Levine (1987, p. 423), the principal outcomes of deregulation were:

(1) a wave of mergers and consolidations;

(2) a higher than expected degree of vertical integration in the industry, especially among commuter airlines;

(3) the dominance of hub and spoke systems;

(4) the surprisingly complicated fare structure which has become characteristic of deregulated markets;

(5) the importance of frequent flyer programmes;

(6) the significance of travel agents and the proliferation of incentives to them;

(7) the role of the computer reservation system (CRS);

(8) the emphasis of firm strategy in gaining or keeping control of airport slots and gates;

(9) the apparent persistence, despite physically easy entry and exit, of predation; and

(10) as a consequence of all these factors, the high casualty rate among new entrants.

Information was the principal instrument preventing airline markets from being perfectly competitive or even contestable, and therefore in causing many significant deregulation surprises. Levine (1987) observes that “these unanticipated effects of deregulation seem to stem from the economics of information and from related economies of scope and scale, and from production indivisibilities”. He argues that airline deregulation has not unfolded as expected, in part because the uneven control of information in such an information-intensive industry erected barriers to entry. The emergence of information-based competition is central to the development of the strategic management of airlines in that it marks the point at which airline managers started to regard information as a strategic tool rather than as a means for achieving operational efficiency. This metamorphosis took place during the development of computer reservation systems and their installation in travel agents.

## 4. The development of computer reservation systems

American Airlines envisaged an automated reservation system as early as 1958. It set out to establish a real-time data processing system which would enable the airline to access the flight details of any passenger at any of the company's locations. The company's motives were purely operational, with increased efficiency as the goal. Under the existing system, agents could sell seats only after they had confirmed availability for each individual flight. This was posted on the office noticeboard. When a reservation was made, the travel agent notified the airline's central reservation office and then filled in a passenger name record (PNR), which was then sent to the reservation office. The central office monitored seat availability; when this dropped below a certain level, it would send out a note to all the agents to stop selling seats. This, too, was posted on the agent's noticeboard. The procedure was slow, cumbersome and wasteful of labour. Discrepancies between the number of seats booked and the PNRs were frequent, resulting in the under-booking or over-booking of flights, with consequent loss of revenue and customer satisfaction.

It took IBM and American Airlines six years to develop the first version of the computer reservation system — SABRE (Semi-Automated Research Environment). The system came on stream in 1964. TWA and United Airlines were quick to follow in developing their own systems. At this period, all these were universally regarded as labour and time saving systems for handling large and growing amounts of reservations data. There were a number of attempts to develop a single industry-wide system to avoid unnecessary duplication, but all proved unsuccessful. Feldman (1987) suggests that one of the reasons for the failure was “fear of the government’s suspicions that a joint system would be anti-competitive”. In January 1976, United announced that it would be offering its own reservation system (APPOLO) to travel agents within six months, and installed four terminals in different locations, mainly to establish whether any service enhancements to the system were required. American attributed rather more strategic importance to the reservation system, hoping that significant revenues would be generated by the leasing of systems which increased the productivity of travel agencies. The company installed more than 130 terminals that year.

United's advance warning of its installation plans gave American some advantage.

Experience from the initial locations made it apparent to Americans that although travel agents were leasing less hardware than expected, thus reducing the revenue expected from subscriber fees, retail automation was generating revenue rather than merely retaining it. The original estimation had been that the first 200 installations would contribute \$3.1 million annually in incremental passenger revenue. The return on investment had been projected to be 6 percent without incremental revenue, and 67 percent with incremental revenue included. Even before the initial installations had been completed, the estimate for incremental passenger revenue was revised to \$20.1 million, resulting in a return on investment exceeding 500 percent. American's 1982 budget for retail automation called for spending at an annual rate of \$20 million, justifying the expenditure with a projected return on investment in excess of 500 percent and the recognition that the company "had been able to increase its influence over the flow of passengers through the air transportation network in a manner most beneficial to American" (US District Court AA072613).

What began as necessary competitive counter to a precipitous action on the part of a major competitor has now evolved into a project of significant financial magnitude to American Airlines. Further, it is occurring at a time when we are threatened with major regulatory changes which potentially could lead to a situation in which the marketing information provided and even a limited control over the distribution mechanism could prove invaluable. (US District Court AA080717)

The airlines in the best position to observe and comprehend the heightened role of information were American and United. These carriers came to recognise that reservation systems were not merely operational components, but a means for influencing and ultimately controlling the deregulated market. The use of information technology for automation had been extended to provide strategic benefits for the first movers. Those who had developed and implemented computer reservation systems discovered that these could generate incremental revenue. One of the first lessons learnt was that the way information was presented to travel agents could influence their choice of carrier.

Analysis of the development of computer reservation systems as an innovation reveals that automation — efficiency — was the justification for the initial R&D investment. When the system became operational, it continued to be seen as automation and was justified in terms of the increased efficiency and productivity it would bring to travel agencies. The airlines would profit from leasing to travel agents the necessary hardware and software. In fact, the airlines' investment yielded substantial and totally unexpected benefits. Only after these benefits started to emerge did the airlines begin to appreciate the strategic potential of what they had developed. Only then did they begin to consider further strategic applications for computer reservation systems, and to justify further investment in terms of not only increased rents, but also the strengthening of market power. Increased efficiency was no longer the universal justification for investment. The new perception of information saw beyond automation and glimpsed the strategic potential of using information for market advantage in less obvious ways.

Given the uncertainty faced by all airlines after 1978, it was not at all obvious that adopting a strategy of retail automation was the key to industry dominance. Only the first movers were positioned to understand the power of a retail system, making rapid imitation unlikely. By the time regulators and other airlines appreciated the competitive significance of retail automation, it was too late to challenge the dominance of the first movers (Grindley, 1995). American and United were so successful in devising ways to use their systems to exploit the new environment that their rivals were often forced to seek legislative protection.

## 5. Early strategic uses of information

By 1978, American Airlines was faring none too well in its battle with United for market share. This was thought to be because United had a better route structure. So, American Airlines devised the co-host system whereby other airlines were given preferential treatment in the display of flights on the SABRE system upon payment of a fee. Five airlines which had route structures that complemented that of American Airlines joined the system. Thus, American's network of routes was, in effect, increased and United had to fight back with a similar system. The screens of the two systems now showed the host airline's flight first, then the co-host's flights, then those of the airlines not thought to be a direct competitive threat, and finally the remainder.

Research has emphasised the crucial role played by screen position. It has been shown that while, for a busy route, there can be dozens of screens full of information, as many as 50 percent of ticket sales are made from the first line on the first page, and between 70 and 90 percent of all sales are selected from the flights listed on the first screen (Gialloreto, 1988). This information display bias was to prove extremely profitable. More important still from a strategic and financial point of view, carriers could use the system to penetrate the home territory of co-hosts. Research has also detected a 'halo-effect', which is "the systematic tendency [of travel agents] to book a disproportionately large share of revenues on the vendor airline" (US Department of Transportation, 1988). If an airline has the majority of travel agents in a city linked to its reservation system, then the local agents will be likely to book passengers on that carrier, even if this means taking a connecting flight. There were many selective campaigns targeting strategic geographic locations. The competition for the revenue and market share benefits of the 'halo effect' was intensive; though the ethics were debatable, the effort was often well rewarded. By 1980, SABRE was to return a net profit of \$300.2 million on total revenues of \$339.3 million (US District Court AA035416). In 1985, American's profit from SABRE was thought to be \$143 million on revenue of \$336 million. Such profit levels led American's chief executive officer, Robert Crandall, to admit that if forced to chose between SABRE and the air transport business, he would have to keep the information business (SABRE) and sell the airline (Lawless, 1989) (see Appendix).

The sort of benefits airlines received from the reservation systems drove them to insist on exclusivity of use. For example, contracts with travel agents were explicit that 95 percent of reservations had to be on the airline's system, which made subscribing to another system impractical. By 1987, 95 percent of travel agents were automated, which meant that gains could be achieved only by displacing rival systems. Some airlines went to great lengths to do just this; for example, United offered one agent in Northwest's territory the following incentives to switch from SABRE to APPOLO: \$500 000 in cash, a 10 percent 'override' commission (on top of the regular commission) for sales on United, and free use of APPOLO, including telephone line charges, for five years (Feldman, 1987). Texas Air tried to lower the costs of agents switching to its System One by defending them in lawsuits resulting from breach of the exclusivity clauses in contracts. The expected revenue increases from the switching travel agencies were "seen to more than compensate for even vast sums in damages" (Feldman, 1987). Indeed, some leaps in market share were huge. For example, in the 1983–86 period, System One (Texas Air) jumped from 16 to 45 percent in Miami; APPOLO (United) in Orlando went from 10 to 35 percent; and PARS (TWA) in St Louis moved from 59 to 77 percent (US Department of Transportation, 1988).

American and United were gaining revenue with market power achieved through information. So successful were they in using information for their market advantage that the degree of deviation from perfect contestability prompted their competitors to seek legal protection and the government to intervene.

## 6. The antitrust issues

Numerous carriers and travel agents filed petitions with the Civil Aviation Board because of problems associated with computer reservation system dominance. These problems fell into several categories: access to the CRS, bias in the CRS displays, monopolistic and discriminatory pricing of booking fees, and exclusive arrangements with travel agents that limited entry and competition with other vendors. The net effects of these practices were the gains to the CRS vendors labelled ‘incremental revenues’. The government’s extensive investigation into the allegations identified a number of practices that CRS vendors adopted to earn these incremental revenues. The most important of these were bias in CRS displays (and the underlying algorithms that created the displays), and discriminatory access and pricing (Guerin-Calvert, 1989).

American and United were found to have developed algorithms that ordered the display of information in ways that favoured them or their co-hosts. The displays were ordered by using carrier-specific factors to rank flights, rather than factors which were in the best interest of passengers, such as the shortest journey time or the most convenient departure time. These systems were not only generally biased, but also specifically predatory. For example, American Airlines was found to have systematically biased its displays against New York Air by adding 40 minutes delay to all New York Air flights. Furthermore, American failed to show some of Continental's lowest fares in its listings.

The Department of Transportation also found that the CRS vendors had been charging different carriers different prices for bookings. Prices were not related to the costs of the services purchased, but to the extent of competitive threat represented by carriers. This was discriminatory pricing to raise the costs of rivals, especially those of new entrants. Booking fees ranged from about \$0.30 to well over \$3 per segment, and the threat of CRS vendors charging \$3 for bookings, when the average profit per segment was only \$2.50, seems to have deterred a number of potential new entrants. The Civil Aviation Board also found that some carriers had promised United that they would not compete aggressively in some of United's markets.

Other anti-competitive practices were alleged, though there was much less evidence for these. CRS vendors were said to delay the loading into the system of fare and schedule data of rivals so that the vendor could respond more rapidly to changes in market conditions than its rivals. It was also claimed that the CRS vendor could use the system to gain immediate access to information on all carriers' prices and bookings in any market, and so obtain anti-competitive market intelligence.

Although the regulation that emerged from these investigations generally eliminated the more blatant forms of anti-competitive use of computer reservation systems, it was not totally satisfactory. Vendors were not prevented from making charges, though they were required to charge all airlines the same. Standard charges came to be between \$1.75 and \$1.85 per booking. At these levels, CRS ownership continued to be a financial gold mine (Shaw, 1990).

## 7. Present main strategic uses of information

Retail automation used information for efficiency. In the next phase, information was used to earn rents from market power gained from the interdependency relationships created by retail automation, exclusivity contracts, and incentives to travel agents. Market power from information is still a very significant factor in today's airline competition (overriding commissions, geographic dominance, more sophisticated information bias, etc.), but there has been yet another change in the nature of information use. The importance of information for market segmentation and for product differentiation is illustrated by the use of frequent flyer programmes, revenue management systems, and market intelligence.

## 7.1. Frequent flyer programmes

The development of frequent flyer programmes (FFPs) is a fine example of an effective marketing strategy aimed at differentiating the airline product to achieve a degree of customer loyalty, targeting the high yield regular business traveller. American Airlines used its computer reservation system to identify its second largest group of customers after the travel agents, the business travellers. To appeal to this group, American introduced the 'AAdvantage' programme in 1982. Because of the complexity of the information handling required, the programme could not have been introduced without the existing comprehensive reservation system.

The idea behind the frequent flyer programme was to promote brand loyalty by rewarding passengers for continuing to fly American, thus eroding the commodity nature of the service. The rewards were attached to the number of miles flown by passengers and took the form of upgrades or even free flights. The FFP's effect on the demand/revenue ratio is that it creates a principal:agent problem. It frequently rewards a business traveller (the agent) on behalf of the employer (the principal). The reward is most commonly used by the agent for his own leisure travel. The FFP can function as a genuine discount only when the entity accruing the benefits is the one paying for the flights. However, this is not normally the case. Indeed, frequent travellers have strong incentives to pay higher specification fares, to travel more than is strictly necessary, and to take longer routes or more trip segments to reach their destinations. This gives them extra bonus miles, but costs their employers dear, which benefits the airlines greatly.

In 1990, 3 percent of US passengers accounted for 27 percent of all trips and 40 percent of airline revenues (Humphreys, 1991). Observers estimate that the cost of starting such programmes as American Airlines' AAdvantage were between \$2 and \$12 million in 1984, and that they resulted in an increase in an individual carrier's business of between 20 and 35 percent (Business Week, 1984). Frequent Flyer (1986) estimates that ticket prices are between 10 and 15 percent higher than they would be without frequent flyer programmes. Layer and Reid (1988) consider that frequent flyer programmes "may be costing American Business as much as \$7 billion a year in added travel costs". Frequent flyer programmes have become widespread and an essential feature of business travel. In 1991, membership of the main American programmes was reported to be: American 14.2 million; United 13.3 million; Delta 10.1 million; Continental 9.1 million; Northwest 8.6 million; USAir 7.8 million (Humphreys, 1991). The degree of consolidation of programmes has been high since the wider the range of routes on which bonuses can be gained and used, the greater the appeal to frequent flyers (see Appendix).

Another market advantage of frequent flyer programmes is the opportunity they afford the carrier to compile information on customers. Frequent flyer programmes give carriers such information as the customer's name, address, employer, number of flights flown and destinations, preferences for meals, seating, and a host of other details. In the past, airlines had information on the total travel of their passengers, but little, if any, about the individual customer. As this membership database accumulates, the airlines will be able to take advantage of the information it provides for market research, specialised promotions, product differentiation — and no doubt a range of other purposes. Here information is being used not for efficiency, but for flexibility and its use is clearly strategic.

## 7.2. Revenue management systems

Revenue management systems (RMS) have been developed by incumbent airlines to counter the lower fares offered by new entrants. The idea is to offer a sufficient number of discount fares on each flight so that the low fare entrant can be beaten by the higher frequency of the incumbent's flights. Revenue management capabilities enable an incumbent to respond quickly and inexpensively to a low fare entrant, depriving passengers of a price incentive to fly with the new entrant. By meeting or beating the entrant's lowest fares, the incumbent can attract price sensitive discretionary passengers. This way, not only does the incumbent achieve higher revenues, since the marginal cost of carrying an extra passenger approaches zero, but considerable, sometimes fatal, damages are inflicted on the new entrant. Donald Burr (New York Times, 1988), the founder of People Express, the low cost, low fare airline he built into the fifth largest carrier in the United States, attributed its demise to this capability of his rivals.

The objective is to maximise revenues on each individual flight by setting a fare structure which divides the aircraft into many different units and sells them to different segments of the market. Robert Crandall (Fortune, 1984), chief executive officer of American Airlines, joked about the system as enabling airlines to offer “the adjustable rate air fare — tell us what you can afford and we’ll sell you a ticket”. Here airlines are fiercely independent of each other, wielding closely guarded computers that run sophisticated programmes. Basically an RMS works like this: the computer makes frequent checks, sometimes several times a day; on how forthcoming flights are filling up. Each flight may have more than a dozen fares allocated to it, and the computer constantly juggles their availability. If bookings are coming in quickly, it will automatically restrict the number of low fares on offer. If, on the other hand, it predicts there will be some empty seats, more bargains are created. The computer uses historical databases for every flight to check on past flight patterns. Its operators will instruct it to remember forthcoming events which may stimulate travel to a particular city (such as conferences, football matches, etc.). They also spy on the fares of rivals. The calculations become even more complicated when connecting flights are involved. The computer may perhaps deny a seat for a short haul flight, not because the flight is full, but because it is holding back some seats in a calculated gamble that they can be sold to late business travellers wanting to connect to a long haul flight. American Airlines is said to have the smartest revenue management system in the business (Economist, 1993).

The joint product pricing that supports a fare system is very information intensive. It requires market segmentation and computerised inventory controls. Operating these controls requires a substantial information history and real-time monitoring capabilities. These information capabilities provide economies of scale and scope. Revenue management systems have become an essential feature of competition in the airline industry. Small additions in yields can quickly add up to millions in the airline industry and revenue management systems have been very successful in earning airlines extra revenue. The International Air Transport Association (IATA) has calculated that if the average ticket on every international flight had been sold for just \$14 more, the world's airlines would have broken even during the past three years instead of losing a combined \$11.5 billion (Economist, 1993).

## 7.3. Overriding commissions

Travel agents provide information so that passengers may manage their travel, and earn commissions from airlines for distributing their product. Typical commissions are between 8 and 10 percent. Many airlines pay some travel agents ‘overriding commissions’, which are commissions over and above the normal commission. These commissions are given as incentives for travel agents to bias the information they provide passengers so as to influence their choice of carrier. A recent survey in the United States indicated that 24 percent of travel agents usually suggest a specific carrier in order to qualify for an overriding commission, and an additional 27 percent sometimes make such a recommendation. Of course, passengers often have their own preferences as to which carrier they use, and this restricts the agent’s ability to gain overrides. Nevertheless, a study by the US Department of Transportation (1990) observed that “some industry participants believe that agencies can choose the carrier for half of their leisure customers and one-fourth of their business travellers”.

This situation creates yet another principal: agent problem, this time between the travel agent (the agent) and the passenger (the principal). Airline marketing programmes are designed to reward the agent for directing business to the airline at the expense of the principal paying for the tickets. Passengers expect unbiased information on what carrier service is most suitable for their travelling need. On the other hand, travel agents have strong incentives to provide biased information which favours certain carriers. This may go some way towards explaining the increase in commission payments to travel agents by airlines.

Commission payments have risen sharply in recent years. They have done so both in absolute terms, and as a proportion of airlines' operating expenses. Today, commission payments constitute the third most important input cost for airlines after those of labour and fuel ... there is every possible reason to believe that the situation in the US is typical of many other markets. (Shaw, 1990)

Airlines with ‘presence’ — a large volume and variety of flights from any one location — can create more effective override programmes than their competitors. Airlines that combine both market presence and precise market information about their competitors’ activities (provided by having the bookings of competitors made on their own computer reservation systems) can employ incentive programmes very effectively indeed. The travel agency business is becoming increasingly concentrated: “On the travel side, we are seeing the progressive disappearance of the smaller travel agent, with more traffic concentrated in the hands of a few giant multiple agencies. In the UK, a quarter of the market is now under the control of Thomas Cook and Hogg Robinson, the UK’s two largest travel retailers” (Shaw, 1990). Hence, if certain airlines control agencies holding large market shares, this can prove very advantageous indeed.

Since overriding commissions are hidden from the consumer, and most frequently from competitor airlines, some airlines may receive manipulated market signals because the airline owning the computer reservation systems installed in the travel agent can monitor its competitor's fares and set its overriding commission accordingly. This will result in a market signal which suggests that demand is less sensitive to price and such attributes as departure time than it is in reality. Customers will also lose by not being informed about fares and services which they might have preferred.

## 8. Conclusion

Three distinct forms of information use can be discerned from the development of airline competition:

(1) internal automation to improve efficiency and productivity;

(2) retail automation in the extension of information capabilities and their marketing to travel agents (also efficiency-driven, but producing unexpected incremental revenue and market power through the control of information); and

(3) the strategic use of information to segment markets, differentiate services and maximise yields.

King et al. (1989) emphasise the difference between aspects of competitive advantage based on information and those based on information technology. They point out that it is useful to distinguish between the advantages of information technology and the strategic uses of information itself. The strategic use of information in the US airline industry arose from two main forms of information power: (1) information capability (the ability to store, process and transmit vast amounts of information accurately); and (2) information as a resource providing both the intelligence behind strategy (details of frequent flyers, for example) and the means by which strategy might be implemented. It is important to distinguish between these two forms of information power. Information capability normally accrues from the internal capacity of the organisation and its technology. To use information as a resource, the organisation must supplement its internal information with that acquired from external sources (von Hippel, 1988).

The distinction between these two information powers is well illustrated by the airline industry. Information capability is associated with efficiency efforts which may be used internally and/or marketed to other organisations, creating interdependencies which are the source of much incremental revenue and market power. Information as a resource allows an element of flexibility in airline strategy. Neither information power excludes the other. A degree of efficiency, and therefore information capability, is essential to bring an organisation's costs into line with those of competitors. Even the most flexible of airlines has to be reasonably efficient.

However, the use of information for efficiency tends to demand organisational capacities rather different from those required for the use of information for flexibility. These capacities can be in a degree of conflict and there may be something of a trade-off between efficiency and flexibility. According to Nelson and Winter (1973), firms possess certain capabilities and decision rules at any given time. Departing from economic orthodoxy, they distinguish between 'knowing how to do' and 'knowing how to choose'. Knowledge of how to do is stored in routines, which are closely interdependent combinations of human skills, organisational attributes, and technology. Effective maintenance of the interdependencies, and thus the stability of routines, requires a kind of organisational truce. The fragility of the truce, and the individual vested interests that depend on it, make "anything that looks like a new initiative subject to scrutiny, and often de facto foreclosure". Hence, organisations that evolve towards efficiency, and use information largely to that end, may resist any change that reduces the efficiency they have achieved. They may find difficulty acquiring and using information for flexibility.

American Airlines was fortunate to have been introduced to the potential of information for organisational flexibility by the contingencies inherent in its first mover efforts to develop the reservation system. The substantial unexpected revenue gains from retail automation provided the impetus to break the efficiency ‘truce’ and persuade the company that it had much to gain from using information as a resource to inject flexibility into its competitive efforts. Today, American Airlines has achieved much success from the strategic flexibility its use of information allows. Other airlines have little choice but to try to follow suit. More important, though, is the degree to which some companies in other industries will be able to seize the competitive advantage which the strategic use of information can permit (Drucker, 1988; Hopper, 1990; Badaracco, 1991; Davis and Botkin, 1994). It is more than a little interesting that American Airlines is beginning to sell not its information systems or its information, but its information skills in other industries altogether.
