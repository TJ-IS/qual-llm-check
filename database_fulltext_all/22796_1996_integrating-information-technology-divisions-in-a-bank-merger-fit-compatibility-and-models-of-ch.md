# Integrating information technology divisions in a bank merger Fit, compatibility and models of change

Kim D. Johnston\*, Philip W. Yetton

Fujitsu Centre for Managing Information Technology in Organisations, Australian Graduate School of Management, University of New South Wales, Sydney, Australia 2052

Paper received 9 September 1995; revised paper accepted for publication by Prof. Marianne Broadbent, 20 March 1996

## Abstract

Effectively integrating the information technology (IT) divisions in mergers of large organisations can be critical to merger success. This paper analyses a case study of IT integration in a merger of two large Australian banks, and argues that an understanding of organisational and strategic fit at the level of the IT organisation can contribute to effective management of IT integration. The paper explores the fit within and between two organisational configurations of IT, and the dynamics of their integration. We discuss the contingencies underlying use of different models of IT integration, and suggest that 'best of breed' prescriptions may be inappropriate when the IT configurations of merging organisations are incompatible. In which case, an 'absorption' strategy can simplify a multi-dimensional misfit and reduce integration complexity.

Keywords: Mergers and acquisitions; Organisational fit; Management of information technology; Ideal types; Integration strategy; Compatibility

## 1. Mergers and IT integration

The general literature on mergers and acquisitions focuses on the financial aspects of the acquisition process, rather than on the subsequent process of integration. Of those researchers who do study post-acquisition organisational integration, few have examined the organisational and strategic context of information technology (IT)

integration. Instead, they focus on issues of culture and communication (e.g. Mirvis and Marks, 1992), different integration strategies (e.g. Haspeslagh and Jemison, 1991), or analyse the general organisational and strategic fit of merger partners (Jemison and Sitkin, 1986). An exception is recent research on strategic planning for IT integration (Main and Short, 1989; Buck-Lew et al., 1992b; Meriali and McKiernan, 1993).

Where IT in mergers is discussed, it is typically to be found in industry and professional journals, where it focuses on technical aspects of integration and usually treats IT in isolation from its organisational context (e.g. Violano, 1990; Rubin, 1992; Day, 1994). In this literature, integration problems in mergers are usually addressed as technical information systems incompatibilities (e.g. Rosenberg, 1987; Johnson, 1989; Kubilus, 1991). There has been no research that integrates technical, organisational and strategic issues at the level of the IT organisation. Here we attempt to do this by developing a framework to analyse the integration of two IT divisions in a bank merger. Following this analysis, we discuss the normative implications for managing IT integration, and examine how issues of fit and compatibility constrain the choice of a merger strategy and model of integration.

## 2. Configurations, fit and ideal types

To facilitate this integrated analysis, we adapt the configurational view of fit from the organisation theory literature, and apply it to an analysis of the IT function. While rarely used in information systems research (Iivari, 1992), the configurational approach to fit is widely considered to be most appropriate for analysing complex organisations (see Van de Ven and Drazin, 1985; Miller, 1987; Meyer et al., 1993). In this context, fit is “a feasible set of equally effective, internally consistent patterns of organisation context and structure” (Van de Ven and Drazin, 1985, p. 335).

This approach is operationalised by identifying a set of ideal type configurations of contextual and organisational elements that maximise fit. Ideal types are “complex constructs that can be used to represent holistic configurations” of multiple organisational dimensions (Doty and Glick, 1994, p. 233). Each ideal type is a theoretical abstraction that represents a unique and effective combination of internally consistent organisational elements. $^{1}$ For example, Mintzberg (1979a) described five ideal type forms of organisation, each with a characteristic configuration of organisational elements and contextual contingencies — the simple structure, the machine bureaucracy, the divisional form, the professional bureaucracy, and the adhocracy.

Because an ideal type maximises the level of fit for any organisation which uses a similar organising logic to integrate its interdependent elements, the fit of such an organisation can be measured in terms of how far it departs from that ideal type. Fit can thus be assessed by analysing the respective distance of an organisational configuration from its closest ideal type. Organisations which are close to an ideal type are described as having achieved high fit, and are hypothesised, and have been found, to be more effective (Van de Ven and Drazin, 1985; Doty et al., 1993; Galunic and Eisenhardt, 1994). $^{2}$ High fit and high performance could therefore by characteristics of two similar organisational configurations which are both variants on the same ideal type, or of two different configurations each of which are variants on different ideal types.

It then follows that we can define two organisational configurations in a merger as compatible if they are variants of the same ideal type. In that case, they have similar organising logics for the elements within both gestalts. In contrast, two configurations are incompatible when their organising logics are different because they are exemplars of different ideal types. Here we show that the compatibility of IT configurations influences the effectiveness of different models of change for IT integration.

## 3. Analytical framework

We examine these issues by incorporating Mintzberg's (1979a) organisational typology into the elements of the MIT'90s schema (Scott Morton, 1991), creating a framework to analyse the fit of two IT configurations in a merger. In doing so, we broaden the usual technical focus of the term 'IT configuration' to define it as the organisational gestalt of strategy, structure, management processes, technology and individual roles/skills which constitute the IT function in an organisation. $^{3}$ For example taking just one of the organisational elements shown in the MIT'90s schema in Fig. 1, namely strategy, an IT configuration corresponding to Mintzberg's machine bureaucracy would typically have a cost-focused technology-driven IT strategy, whereas an IT configuration corresponding to Mintzberg's divisional form would typically have a business-driven, value-added IT strategy. The selection of typology and elaboration of two ideal type forms of IT configuration in terms of this framework are discussed in the methodology section of the paper.

Typically, both the MIT'90s schema (Scott Morton, 1991) and Mintzberg's (1979a) ideal types are applied at the organisational level of analysis rather than at the level of a function within the organisation — in this case, the IT function. To place this particular study in context, we therefore distinguish between three levels of analysis of IT mergers. The first is the organisational level of analysis. Discussion of organisational fit and IT strategic alignment typically occurs at this level (Henderson and Venkatraman, 1992; McGee and Prusak, 1993). The focus of this paper is on the second level of analysis — the IT organisations. Specifically, we are concerned with the fit within and between two IT organisations in a merger. The third, technical level of analysis, addresses only one component — the IT systems. Consistent with our focus on the integration of two IT functions, we only briefly discuss the other two levels.

Focusing on this second level of analysis in a merger case study, we compare two banks' IT configurations and assess their relative fit and compatibility. We find that each bank's IT configuration was close to a different ideal type — one resembled Mintzberg's machine bureaucracy, the other was closer to his divisional form. Attempting to merge these incompatible IT organisations according to a 'best of breed' model created a complex misfit. Subsequent implementation of an 'absorption' model, whereby one configuration was dismantled and absorbed into the other, simplified the change process and re-established IT-organisational fit, enabling successful completion of IT integration.

In our analysis of the case, we explain the theoretical arguments for selecting such an absorption model, which are especially strong when the two IT configurations in the merger are incompatible, and why the adoption of a 'best of breed' model under these conditions would be complex, risky, and expensive. Then, moving from the level of IT configurations to the organisational level of analysis, we discuss the organisational fit of the merged IT configuration. Finally, we discuss effective combinations of IT merger strategy and models of change in IT integration, and explain how strategic choice can be constrained by the incompatibility of configurations.

## 4. Methodology

The opportunity for a research case study of IT integration in a bank merger arose when the researchers were invited by an Australian bank, which had recently acquired a regional competitor, to study their management of the integration project. $^{4}$ Given the paucity of organisational research in this area of IT management, an exploratory research approach was chosen, with the intention of grounding theory development in the detailed case data (Strauss and Corbin, 1990), and within the context of the researchers' interests in managerially relevant organisational research. Below we discuss the choice of method, provide details of data collection, and describe the assumptions underpinning the analytical framework and selection of typology.

## 5. Case study research

Case study analysis is particularly appropriate for this type of theory building research (Yetton et al., 1995). With its emphasis on understanding the context and process of change, this form of field research is useful for studying organisational dynamics (George and McKeown, 1985; Pettigrew, 1990; Galunic and Eisenhardt, 1994). Case studies permit rich description, through capturing multiple data sources and perspectives, which is especially relevant given the formative state of the field of strategic IT management (Benbasat et al., 1987; Yin, 1994). It is not surprising then, that such research frequently reveals emergent patterns and trends which inform theory, and thus facilitate theory development (Eisenhardt, 1989; Mintzberg, 1979b).

While the theory development in this paper is grounded in and emerges from the case evidence, its perspective remains relatively positivist, as distinct from ethnographic or interpretivist (Eisenhardt, 1989). The latter typically focuses on describing and interpreting the meaning of behaviour within particular contexts. In contrast, our research uses case studies to inform the development of theory which can then be tested and potentially generalised across organisations facing similar contingencies (see for example Sauer and Yetton, 1994; Yetton et al., 1994, 1995).

## 6. Data

Because of the exploratory, theory-building focus of this study, the primary mode of data collection consisted of 21 detailed unstructured interviews, which were conducted with managers from both banks at two different time periods. The initial phase of interviewing took place during April and May 1992, when eight senior managers, six from the national bank and two from the regional bank, were interviewed regarding the merger and specifically the systems integration project. Subsequently, towards the end of the integration project during February–April 1994, a further 13 interviews took place, three of which were with former regional bank IT managers (of whom two had already left the bank). Four key managers were interviewed during both phases of the field research — the head of information systems (IS) in each bank, and the two senior national bank managers leading the IT integration project. In all, a total of 17 managers were interviewed, including three senior business managers from the national's retail banking division.

All interviews were conducted in private with individual managers and all except three interviews were attended by two interviewers. The duration of each interview was between one and two hours. All interviews during the second phase, except one, were tape recorded and transcribed for analysis. Bank planning documents regarding the integration project were also examined, as were annual reports of the two banks, and media coverage of the acquisition. These interviews and supporting documents formed the basis for the case description and analysis which follows. A strong and consistent picture of IT merger dynamics emerged from these interviews with managers from both banks during both time periods, with many interviewees' comments independently reinforcing the comments of others. A key feature of these dynamics was the incompatibility of the two IT organisations.

## 7. Fit and compatibility: assumptions and typology

Underlying the analysis of fit and compatibility in this paper are three key assumptions. First, that fit among the organisational elements of a configuration is an important determinant of effectiveness; second, that there is a limited set of effective ideal type configurations; and third, that fit can be assessed in terms of an organisation's closeness to one of these ideal types. These assumptions underlie our proposition that if two IT configurations are close to different ideal types, then they are likely to be incompatible, thus creating problems for their integration in a merger.

Three fit typologies that could be utilised for such an ideal type analysis are Burns and Stalker's (1961) mechanistic and organic types, Miles and Snow's (1978) strategic typology, or Mintzberg's (1979a) organisational typology. We selected Mintzberg's typology because it provides a more richly detailed and sophisticated treatment of ideal type organisational forms. He describes five organisational types according to their primary mode of coordination, the key part of the organisation, its design parameters and contingency factors. We use these headings in Table 1 to summarise the features of these five types; the simple structure, machine bureaucracy, divisional form, professional bureaucracy, and adhocracy.

From the organising logic of two of these ideal types — the machine bureaucracy and the divisional form — we derive a matching IT configuration. For reasons of parsimony, we do not detail here the equivalent IT configuration for the other three of Mintzberg's ideal types. The simple form refers to small, young and entrepreneurial firms; the adhocracy applies to fluid, dynamic and highly innovative organisations; and the professional bureaucracy pertains to specialist organisations such as universities and hospitals. As such, they are unlikely to be the relevant ideal type for either of the two IT configurations in the bank merger analysed here.

The machine bureaucracy IT organisation is oriented towards efficiency, integration and cost control, with the centralised structure and tight management controls geared towards achieving this end. This is reinforced by a technological emphasis on standards and a dominant platform/architecture. Such technological bureaucracies often ‘grown their own’ expertise, socialising IT staff through internal recruitment, training and promotion, and rewarding loyalty and length of service. This tends to reproduce a conservative and risk-averse management style. These IT configurations are potentially suited to business environments that are relatively stable, and to organisations seeking to control IT costs. This typically large, formalised IT organisation is often functionally insulated from the business.

The other relevant ideal type is the divisional form. This organisation is focused on servicing the needs of the organisation's business units. Supported by decentralised IT management structures and flexible, project-based management processes, this ideal type focuses on how it can add value to the business. Because it is highly responsive to multiple business divisions, this approach can lead to a relatively high IT cost structure, high staffing levels, and a proliferation of systems and platforms. Its professional staff are usually externally trained and mobile, and motivated by a reward system based on performance-driven pay and promotion. Such configurations are typically associated with diversified, market-focused business environments. This configuration often features a corporate IT unit coordinating the business divisions' competing demands for IT service, as well as specialist IT staff located within divisions. ## 8. The bank merger

## 8.1. Background

In January, 1991, a national bank, the Commonwealth Bank of Australia (CBA), formally acquired the assets of an Australian regional bank, the State Bank of Victoria (SBV), for \$A1.6 billion. $^{5}$ The national bank was one of Australia's largest, with 1400 branches across Australia, 40000 staff, and assets of \$A67 billion, and at that time was fully owned by the Australian Federal Government. The regional bank was the largest in the State of Victoria, with 527 branches in that state, 2 million customer records, 12000 staff (including 1000 IT systems staff), and assets of \$A24 billion, and was owned by the Victorian State Government (see Table 3). It was Australia's largest bank merger, and significant on a global scale.

The regional bank was predominantly a successful retail bank, but the losses by its merchant banking arm had created significant financial problems, forcing the bank's sale. For its part, the national bank had targeted the regional bank in strategic planning documents as early as 1987 because of potential synergies. The national bank was under-represented in Victoria, where its market share was less than half that of the regional bank, which was market leader in that state.

From its due diligence exercise on the regional bank during 1990, the national bank had identified integrating the computer systems and IT operations of the two banks as a major source of potential value from the merger. Capturing that value, however, was not a straightforward process. A number of factors contributed to the complexity of the integration process.

## 8.2. Different IT configurations

One of the major factors contributing to this complexity was the significant contrast between the IT operations of the two banks. These were geographically separated, with the national bank's IT operations based in Sydney, New South Wales, while the regional bank's IT operations were located 1000 km away in Melbourne, Victoria. This geographic distance, as well as the traditional cultural and economic rivalry between these two cities, provided fuel for the ‘them and us’ conflicts which developed during the integration project.

In this context, there were four significant differences between the two banks' IT functions. First, they had quite different IT strategies, the regional being business-driven, oriented to meeting the demands of the business units, while the national bank IT department was traditionally quite separate from the business and more technology-focused. Prior to the takeover, the IT department of the national bank had been responsible for all IT projects affecting the branches and retail business. $^{6}$ "Business would throw a request over the wall to applications who would beaver away for a few months and then throw the result back over the wall to business." In the regional bank, however, there was "a long and strong history of joint development" between users and IT. "When [the regional bank's] business arms said 'we need this', the IS area would knock it up fairly quickly." These differences reflected contrasting priorities. The national bank's IT department was efficiency-focused, being very "advanced at measuring their costs and [understanding] what the IT dollar was giving them." The regional bank IT department, on the other hand, was less concerned with costs, and "was allowed to grow because of unconstrained demand for service" by the business units.

Second, the IT systems of the banks were built around different, and incompatible, technology platforms. The regional bank operated primarily ICL based systems, while the national bank was committed to an IBM platform. Furthermore, the regional bank had systems running on a several other platforms (Honeywell, prime, IBM), with complex interdependencies among them (comments such as “a vendor’s delight”, and “spaghetti cabling” were typical of those made by national bank IT managers about the regional systems). The national bank, on the other hand, emphasised compatibility within one platform. In addition, the national bank’s communications network was based on IBM’s SNA standards, while the regional’s communications network was based on X.25 standards. The two banks also used quite different systems testing methodologies. In the national bank, these were focused on technical excellence, while in the regional bank, a more sophisticated customer-focused testing methodology was used.

Third, project management structures and styles were very different. The decentralised, team-based management in the regional IT organisation contrasted with the more hierarchical and formal IT management processes in the national bank. The national bank was “old and staid…we fit in with standards and architectures”, and “certainly never empower people like [the regional bank] did”. The regional IT project managers, on the other hand “were making decisions that [national IT managers] would have to go to God for”. One national bank manager commented that “the [regional bank IT] project managers managed their projects right onto the computer floor — they held onto their project like a bull terrier with a rag … We didn’t do it like that here”. Empowering the IT project managers, however, had encouraged the proliferation of systems and costs in the regional bank, and was in such contrast with the national bank’s more formalised methods and hierarchical structures that during the merger, according to one national bank manager, “our guys just threw their hands up in horror — ‘that is not how you build systems!’”.

Fourth and finally, contrasting IT staffing practices existed within the two banks. The regional bank had focused in recent years on external recruitment of young, educated IT professionals — in the mid-80s, “there was a conscious decision to recruit outside people…to try to change the culture”, and appointed many female IT managers. In contrast, the national bank had a policy of internal recruitment and training — “I don’t think [the national bank] made a conscious decision to recruit outside at all”, and there were few women in IT management positions. Furthermore, promotion and pay were performance-driven in the regional, while the national bank had a more bureaucratic, position-based reward system.

## 8.3. Approaches to integration

In beginning to address these differences, the first major implementation step was to build a (temporary) technical bridge between the two banks' systems so that a customer of either bank going into any branch of the newly merged bank could access their accounts. A critical issue was to retain customers of the former regional bank. This meant proceeding carefully rather than immediately undertaking a radical rationalisation program. So the initial emphasis was on keeping the pre-existing delivery shells operational until full-scale branch systems conversion and 'rebadging' could proceed. The technical bridge provided the necessary connection between the two platforms for capturing and routing transactions and integrating the two general ledgers, while work on systems integration continued in the background.

Systems integration was one of nine interdependent ‘building blocks’ in the overall bank integration project managed by Retail Banking. This systems building block was divided into a number of sub-projects with specific goals and timetables. Initially 12, and later 20, detailed project milestones were identified, and a Project Office was established to coordinate and manage the complex interdependencies among them. An IT project manager for each of the milestones reported to the head of the Project Office.

In planning to integrate the IT operations, the national bank initially pursued a ‘best of breed’ approach. This aimed to identify best practice in each area of the two banks’ IT departments, which could then usefully be adopted as the basis for building an new integrated IT structure. This ‘best of breed’ approach to integration required a lengthy process of meetings between each bank’s specialist IT areas in an attempt to identify, analyse and then adopt what was considered to be ‘best’ from each area. The differences between the two IT configurations made this difficult. Fuelled by these differences as well as by the inevitable emotional and political atmosphere of the takeover, disagreements between the national and regional bank’s IT managers over systems strategy grew. ‘Us’ versus ‘them’ attitudes and behaviours developed, with effort channelled into demonstrating that one bank’s systems and procedures were superior to those of the other, and thus should be retained and/or extended to the whole bank. While expected in attempts to obtain ‘best of breed’, this conflict was not resolved and appeared to become an end in itself rather than a means to an end, leading to delays in planning and implementation. The resulting lack of clarity over the action plan for integration prolonged and increased the uncertainty experienced by the regional bank’s IT staff.

Several senior staffing decisions also contributed to this delay and uncertainty. For example, when the national bank's Retail Chief General Manager retired just after the merger, the top retail position in the national bank was ‘temporarily’ filled for five months. Furthermore, when the national bank's IT General Manager also retired just after the merger, the top IT position in the national bank was ‘temporarily’ filled for 10 months by the senior IT manager from the former regional bank. This move was consistent with a best of breed integration strategy, but in practice slowed the rationalisation of regional IT staff and systems. Subsequently, after an international search, a banker — a senior business manager from the national bank's Retail Banking Division — rather than an IT professional, was appointed to head the merged IT department.

## 8.4. An 'absorption' approach to integration

Increasingly, the national bank faced pressures to complete integration. Firstly, their competitors were making moves on their new market which could not be matched while the national's products were 'frozen' during the integration process. Secondly, The Reserve Bank of Australia, $^{7}$ having ruled that the national bank could not run the regional as a separate ‘brand’ as it was fully owned by the national, was pressuring to have to it rebadged. Finally, it was very expensive for the bank to continue running dual structures and systems, and pressures to reap the considerable rationalisation benefits were mounting. Given the significant incompatibilities between the two IT configurations, the decision was made to convert the products and accounts on the regional bank’s systems over to the national bank’s platform.

The ‘best of breed’ approach was thus replaced by an ‘absorption’ approach, converting the regional bank’s IT operations into the national bank’s existing IT architecture and systems. This involved concentrating all activity in Sydney, and switching from the regional bank’s predominantly ICL based systems in Melbourne to the national bank’s IBM systems in Sydney. Current products from both banks would then be superseded by a ‘preferred product set’, creating a single suite of products for the integrated bank, which were adapted from the existing national bank product set. By the completion of the integration process, all remaining branches of the former regional bank became national bank branches, with the single product line run on the national bank’s IT systems.

At the time of the merger, the two banks had 1200 information systems staff in Melbourne (1000 were regional bank staff), where the regional bank was based. By April 1994, when integration was completed, there was a combined total of only 140 IS staff remaining in Melbourne. All branches, products, accounts, and customer records had been converted to run on the national bank's systems.

The large scale organisational change briefly described here was complex and difficult to manage. For the IT area, it meant a rationalisation and simplification of systems and locations to eliminate the dual platforms, and major integration projects to bring the former regional bank's branches and customers into the national bank's platform. Fully completed on schedule in April, 1994, the IT rationalisation and integration contributed significant value to the merged bank.

## 9. Analysis

To obtain the benefits from this merger, two very different IT configurations had to be integrated. While this is usually treated as a technical problem, we view it as a problem of organisational and strategic fit. Initially, the merger created an organisational misfit between the two incompatible IT configurations, which had to be resolved. Below we describe that resolution. We explain the emergent organisational fit of the combined banks' IT configuration in terms of incompatibility between ideal types, and analyse how this influenced the dynamics of IT integration.

Initially, the incompatibility of the two banks' technology sets received most attention in the combined IT group. Resolving those technical incompatibilities, however, would not have solved the integration problem, because of the underlying incompatibility between IT strategy, structure, management processes, and roles/ skills in each organisation. In fact, regardless of technology differences, the incompatibility of the two IT organisational ‘cultures’ (which emerge from the particular evolution of organisational components within each configuration) was itself sufficient to cause problems with integration. $^{8}$ Each IT configuration had evolved along a different dynamic path involving the development of organisational resources and learning specific to that path, one being technology-centred and efficiency-driven, the other being business-centred and seeking to add value. This leads us to focus on the configurations as a whole, in addition to comparisons between pairs of individual elements.

Such a configurational view emphasises the interdependence and consistency among the components within a particular gestalt. For the banks, each gestalt reflected a different organising logic, and thus a different form of fit. This incompatibility between the two configurations is explained by their closeness to different ideal types. $^{9}$

This becomes clear when we compare the two banks' IT configurations summarised in Table 4 with the ideal types presented in Table 2. Inspection of both tables shows that each of the bank IT configurations had greater similarities to a different ideal type than to each other. The national bank's IT configuration was a variation on the 'IT machine bureaucracy', whereas the regional bank's IT configuration was a variation on the 'divisional' form. This misfit occurring at the configurational level is summarised and presented for visual clarity in Fig. 2. The two IT configurations, while internally congruent and compatible within their own organisations, were incompatible with each other. $^{10}$

## 9.2. Models of change in IT integration

This incompatibility between the two IT configurations helps explain the dynamics of the IT integration process. Strategic planning for IT integration after the takeover envisaged a two step process. First, a technical bridge was to be built between the banks, enabling the separate IT configurations to be maintained, which was a temporary form of 'co-existence'. Second, a new configuration based on a 'best of breed' model of change was to be developed. Later, however, that model was abandoned and an 'absorption' model of change, whereby one configuration absorbed and replaced the other, was adopted. Table 5 summarises these changes during the merger.

The goal of the ‘best of breed’ model was to jointly develop a new configuration, based on an extensive analysis of the two configurations to combine the best from each. This assumed, however, that individual parts of each configuration could be ‘mixed and matched’ to obtain a new best practice configuration. For this to be effective, the two configurations need to be variants of the same ideal type. Then the underlying organising logics of the two configurations would be similar. In which case, a step by step integration dynamic which dealt with one pair of organisational components at a time would be feasible, because the interdependencies with other components would have similar characteristics. With incompatible configurations, however, the competition between every component of the two configurations (strategy, structure, management processes, technology, and roles/skills), as shown in Fig. 2, makes this integration very difficult. A best of breed model of change then becomes a high cost option in terms of time and resources, and has a high risk of failure, because it would involve building a new configuration, rather than developing and improving an existing one. This cost and risk explains why the best of breed IT approach was abandoned.

Faced with two incompatible configurations, and the failure of the best of breed model, what were the options for integration? One was to adopt the model of 'co-existence'. This keeps the organisations separate and establishes minimal links between them. Essentially, this would have limited integration to the technical bridge built between the banks to integrate the general ledger and capture inter-bank transactions. Although co-existence was a necessary initial step, it was not considered to be an ongoing option because of the strategic goal of obtaining economies of scope and scale from rationalisation of the two IT operations.

Instead, the ‘absorption’ model was adopted. The regional bank’s configuration was dismantled, with some parts converted into the national bank’s configuration, and others simply abolished. This simplified the integration problem by eliminating configurational incompatibility, with its multi-dimensional misfit, and reduced the integration problem to one of dealing with a single factor, the IT systems. The simplified project then focused on managing the transfer of customer and product data to national bank systems. In this way, the complex configurational integration task implied by the best of breed model was simplified and transformed into a less complex systems integration task.

## 9.3. Organisational-level fit of IT divisions

The incompatibility between the IT configurations explains the shift from a best of breed to an absorption model of change, but it does not explain why the national bank's configuration absorbed the regional bank's configuration, rather than the reverse. While not the primary focus of this research, the case study suggests the following analysis.

The regional IT configuration was seen by many as advanced and ‘progressive’, as well as more business-focused, which was increasingly the goal of national bank management. It was the national bank IT configuration, however, that was retained. This choice could be interpreted as a function of the power of the national bank’s IT organisation. A political view would explain the absorption of the regional IT configuration as a function of the relative power of the larger acquiring organisation’s IT sub-unit (Linder, 1989). An alternative explanation, however, is that the dominance of the national bank’s IT configuration was a function of the established organisational fit between the acquiring organisation and its IT sub-unit; a form of fit which supported the rationalisation goals of the merger.

To explore this alternative explanation, we begin by assuming that there was an existing fit between the national bank and its IT sub-unit. There is some evidence for this. The national bank was functionally organised and was itself a variant on the machine bureaucracy ideal type. A reverse absorption by the regional bank's IT configuration would therefore have resulted in a misfit between this configuration and its new (national bank) parent organisation. While the regional IT configuration had many characteristics that were attractive to the national bank, the absorption of the national's IT configuration by the regional IT unit in a 'reverse takeover' would have created the need for multiple complex changes in the (national bank) parent organisation to re-establish alignment and fit between IT and the organisation.

If an absorption integration model transformed a complex incompatibility at the level of IT configurations into a more simple systems conversion problem, then a 'reverse takeover' would have transformed it back into a complex incompatibility at the organisational level. Building on the already established alignment between the national bank and its IT configuration, then, the latter's dominance was likely to create significantly lower complexity and cost than a 'reverse takeover' by the regional IT unit. We suggest that a management concern to avoid this increased complexity and misfit helps explain why the national bank's IT configuration was dominant.

## 10. Discussion

In the case study and its analysis, we identified three models of change in IT integration — best of breed, absorption and co-existence; and the importance of the compatibility/incompatibility between IT configurations. Below, we briefly discuss the three IT merger strategies which underly these different models of change, and the contingencies for their successful implementation. We then explore how compatibility between IT configurations can affect these integration dynamics. This has important normative implications for the effectiveness of IT integration in mergers.

## 10.1. Merger strategies and models of change

Many strategic IT management researchers and practitioners adopt the dominant assumptions from the field of strategy concerning the primary role of strategic positioning in organisational change (for example, Porter and Miller, 1985; Scott Morton, 1991; McGee and Prusak, 1993). In this classic view, the firm's choice of strategy determines the appropriate organisation design with which the strategy will be effectively implemented — thus structure follows strategy (Chandler, 1962). An analogous argument can be applied to the analysis of IT mergers. For example, the best of breed model of change would be chosen because it best implements a value-added IT merger strategy. Below, we briefly outline the relationship between three IT merger strategies and models of change, and then discuss how ‘structural’ compatibility constrains strategic choice.

First, when the strategic intent is to maintain the independence of the two organisations, the 'co-existence' model is particularly appropriate. This often occurs in acquisitions of unrelated or geographically distinct businesses. Maintaining separate IT configurations in this scenario is likely to be low risk and minimises integration complexity. Here, the two pre-merger forms of IT fit are sustained, interdependence is minimised, and 'integration' consists only of establishing limited interfaces between the systems. This avoids the organisational complexities associated with attempting to combine the two configurations (Buck-Lew et al., 1992a, b). While it is low risk, this co-existence option generally produces a higher IT cost structure, as rationalisation gains from economies of scale and reduction of duplication are not obtained. Furthermore, keeping the configurations separate means that synergies and learning that may stem from combined operations are more difficult to achieve.

Second, when an organisation's strategic intent is predominantly to achieve benefits from IT rationalisation and cost reduction, ‘absorption’ of one IT configuration by another would be both low cost and low risk. Here, one dominant form of IT fit would be sustained through the integration and consolidation process, reducing complexity, risk and time to completion. The major problems associated with integrating two incompatible configurations are thus avoided. One risk associated with this strategy, however, concerns management of the downsizing process (Violano, 1990; Kubilus, 1991). Timing of downsizing initiatives becomes important where the IT configuration to be absorbed needs to be initially maintained to provide full service until the systems and customer conversions onto the dominant configuration are completed. $^{11}$ Another potential risk occurs when scaling up existing systems to cover increased transaction volumes.

Finally, if the strategic intent is to add value through capitalising on merger synergies or complementarities, then a ‘best of breed’ model of change could be appropriate. The major contingency here is whether the two IT divisions are compatible. Where this is the case, the initial gestalt already exists and synergies can be realised by incremental adjustments, capitalising on possibilities for learning around the individual elements in the gastalt. However, where the configurations are incompatible, as was the case for the national and regional banks, there would be high costs associated with the long period of misfit and re-alignment. From the above discussion, it is clear that the issue of which strategy-change model combination to apply in an IT merger is not purely a function of strategic choice. The limitations of the strategic positioning perspective, in terms of both its descriptive validity and its normative efficacy, have been discussed elsewhere (see for example, Mintzberg, 1991, 1994; Weick, 1993; Yetton et al., 1994, 1995; Ciborra, 1995). Reversing the usual normative view of strategic change, we discuss below how the fit of the IT organisation may influence the success, and therefore the selection, of an integration strategy. Specifically, we explore how compatibility of IT configurations can powerfully constrain strategic positioning for IT integration. This compatibility will influence the dynamics associated with, and the success of, the cost strategy-absorption model and the value added strategy-best of breed model. We discuss these fit dynamics below.

## 10.2. Compatibility and fit

The dynamics of fit in Table 6 are a two stage process: the first stage being to establish an organisational gestalt with high fit among its interdependent elements; the second being, once this gestalt is established, to reinforce the virtuous circle of fit through a process of organisational learning and simplification (Miles and Snow, 1984; Yetton et al., 1995). In managing IT mergers, which of these stages is problematic, and therefore central, varies depending upon the compatibility of the two configurations.

In the case of compatible configurations, the IT organisations would be variants on the same ideal type, and thus the merged IT organisations would still be in internal fit. In which case, the focus would be on the second fit dynamic. Only relatively minor adjustments would be necessary to obtain improvements in performance, because of the congruence between the two configurations' organisational elements, and the congruence of the organising logic reflected in their interdependence. Synergies between the two merged organisations could be realised as the expected benefits from a value-added strategy implemented with a best of breed model of change.

In contrast, the incompatible configurations, the IT organisations would be variants on different ideal types. The merged IT organisation would be in strategic misfit. In which case, the focus would be on the first step of establishing a common and congruent gestalt. This would involve extensive rationalisation to eliminate incompatibility. This rationalisation could be achieved as the expected benefit from a cost reduction strategy implemented with an absorption model of change.

Fig. 3 presents these two combinations as the main diagonal elements of a matrix (Cell B and Cell C). We speculate that the off-diagonal elements are unstable in the sense that those combinations cannot be implemented successfully without changing one of the characteristics. Fig. 3 also indicates the likely direction of such a change.

With incompatible IT configurations, implementing a value-added merger strategy using a best of breed model of change would be difficult (Cell A). Attempting to adopt individual components from each competing configuration, and then mixing them together in a new one, fails because of the misfit both within the interdependent components of the two merged gestalts, and between their underlying organising logics. We speculate that under these conditions, effective resolution of IT misfit in a merger is likely to occur through adoption of an 'absorption' model of change (as in the bank case analysed here). By resolving the configurational misfit, this simplifies the merger problem to that of a single factor — technical conversion of the IT systems. In contrast, in Cell D, with compatible configurations, synergies could be realised relatively easily. In this case, there would be an incentive to pursue the benefits of an emergent value-added strategy and adopt a best of breed model of change.

In summary, the above analysis and discussion highlights situations when the fit within and between merging IT configurations, and the organisational fit of the merged IT configuration, are more important than the strategic intent of the IT merger in shaping outcomes. A key driver of these integration dynamics is the short term need to reduce the complexity and risk created by incompatibility. We have argued elsewhere that this drive to simplify underlies many IT change dynamics (Yetton et al., 1995). The adoption of an absorption model of integration in the bank case study is explained here as a way of efficiently eliminating the complexity associated with the organisational misfit of two incompatible IT configurations.

## 11. Conclusions

The analysis in this paper has important implications for both descriptive and normative models of merger strategy and IT integration. Descriptively it identifies conditions under which an absorption model of integration is likely to be implemented. Normatively, it suggests specific contingencies under which absorption or best of breed may be the most effective form of integration. We distinguish between three levels of analysis of IT mergers — organisational, IT configurations, and IT systems. Focusing on the second level of analysis, we then show that diagnosing the fit of organisational configurations of IT within and between the parties to mergers or acquisitions, can facilitate early adoption of the appropriate form of IT integration.

Analysis of the case study demonstrates the importance of a configurational analysis of the IT function in merged organisation. While practitioners have described the difficulties with technical systems integration in mergers of organisations with incompatible information systems (Johnson, 1989; Violano, 1990), problems of integration can be the consequence of a more complex organisational misfit between the merging IT configurations. A primary concern with technical systems integration issues can be the outcome of a particular integration dynamic which resolves the organisational complexities associated with a prior misfit of IT organisations. In this context, the practitioner focus on systems integration is more easily understood. However, an understanding of the organisational and management issues underlying these dynamics can prevent or resolve significant problems with integration.

In concluding, we note that normative alignment models emphasise top down strategic planning (e.g. Main and Short, 1989; McGee and Prusak, 1993), and typically focus on desired end states (such as a ‘best of breed’ IT configuration). In doing so, they can neglect the important role of the prevailing configuration’s form of fit in determining the dynamics of strategic change. In this way, we suggest that, at least in the short term, strategic choice concerning IT integration is powerfully constrained by configurational compatibility. In a merger, the fit within and between the two parties’ existing IT organisations can play an important role in determining outcomes. It explains why, in this case study, absorption emerged as the form of integration, replacing the initial strategic intention to build a ‘best of breed’ IT configuration. The effectiveness of strategic planning prescriptions can be enhanced by early diagnosis of this fit. Improving our understanding of the contingencies which influence successful integration and fit of IT configurations in mergers can thus improve our normative alignment models and guide management action.
