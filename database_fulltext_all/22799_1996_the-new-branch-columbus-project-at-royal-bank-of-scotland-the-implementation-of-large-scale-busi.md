# The New Branch Columbus project at Royal Bank of Scotland: the implementation of large-scale business process re-engineering

Wendy L. Currie $^{a}$ , Leslie Willcocks $^{b,*}$

$^{a}$ University of Sheffield, Sheffield, UK

$^{b}$ Templeton College, University of Oxford, Kennington, Oxford OX1 5NY, UK

Received August 1995; revised paper accepted for publication by Professor R.D. Galliers, July 1996

## Abstract

The paper examines the findings from a longitudinal case study on the implementation of a large scale business process re-engineering (BPR) project at the Royal Bank of Scotland. The New Branch Columbus project was conceived to radically restructure the traditional branch banking system. It represented a major organizational, managerial and technical challenge. The research found important barriers impeding BPR at the bank. Many originated with the development and implementation of critical information technologies underpinning the project. The structural separation of the business units and IT division engendered disagreements amongst senior managers and IT staff about the key strategic and operational aims of the Columbus project. Project managers were faced with a control problem in managing permanent and contract technical staff working with the latest client server technology. Senior business and IT managers tended to under-estimate the technical difficulties of interfacing PC based client server technology with the mainframe system. Faced with these challenges, efforts to implement large-scale BPR became diluted in practice.

Keywords: Business process re-engineering; Project management; Managing change; Barriers to BPR; Organizational politics

## 1. Introduction

Business process re-engineering (BPR) is described by its supporters as a means of facilitating significant, even fundamental, change in the way an organization operates (Hammer and Champy, 1993a,b; Champy, 1995; Morris and Brandon, 1993). A key element is a focus on business processes, defined by Davenport (1993a) as a structured, measured sets of activities designed to produce a specified output for a particular customer or market. The BPR activity described in the literature varies in the scale and type of change contemplated (Heygate, 1993; Morris and Brandon, 1993). Yet central to BPR practice, according to most sources, is a holistic approach to strategy, structure, process, people and technology (Johansson et al., 1993; Galliers, 1995). This is shown in Table 1 which outlines how Hammer and Champy (1993a, b) conceptualize the transformation of the traditional organization to one which has been re-engineered.

Hammer and Champy (1993a) suggest that the transformation indicated in Table 1 is best achieved through a radical re-engineering process that is relatively quick, i.e. certainly less than two years. 'Radical' here means rethinking and redesigning the organization around a few core processes. This is indicated by Hammer's definition of re-engineering as 'the fundamental rethinking and redesign of business processes to bring about dramatic improvements in performance' (Hammer and Stanton, 1995). There are, of course, less fundamentalist views of the nature and role of re-engineering. For example, Davenport (1993b) questions whether transformation is always that necessary, and suggests that in most cases a more incremental, lower risk re-engineering approach might be more appropriate: 'the revisionist alternative is to allow re-engineering and quality to exist in tandem, applying the radical approach only where it is absolutely necessary, and being happy with ten percent improvements elsewhere'.

A number of recent papers have been critical of the theory and practice of BPR as portrayed in the mainstream literature (for examples only see Coombes and Hull, 1995; Grint and Willcocks, 1995; Willmott, 1995). These have questioned whether re-engineering is simply the latest management fad or technical ‘quick fix’, as opposed to a revolutionary philosophy for organizational transformation, and have pointed to contradictions in both re-engineering messages and practice. At the same time the very size of the phenomenon would suggest that re-engineering is symptomatic of deep-seated problems and lack of competitiveness in Western industry, to which it can be an important, though probably incomplete, set of practical responses (Francis and Southern, 1995; Willcocks and Currie, 1996). However, the critical literature has produced, so far, all too few academically derived longitudinal case studies that detail the actual experiences of organizations in developing and implementing large-scale BPR. Whilst the present authors accept the majority of these criticisms of BPR (see Willcocks et al., 1997) it is outside the scope of this paper to address these in detail. Instead, we will concern ourselves with the empirical literature on the implementation of large scale re-engineering for purposes of comparison with our own case study research.

The most prolific writings on re-engineering theory and practice have emerged from North America and Britain. Recent case study and survey research from these geographical areas have found dramatic improvements in some companies, but documents these examples as few and far between (Bartram, 1992; Hall et al., 1993; Harvey, 1995; Moad, 1993). This empirical literature suggests a mismatch between the rhetoric and promise of re-engineering and what is being actually implemented. It also suggests that wide variations exist on how BPR is interpreted and implemented both within and across national borders. A 100 organization UK survey in 1995, for example, reinforces this picture (Willcocks, 1995, 1996). Here it is shown that high risk, radical BPR approaches of the kind suggested by Hammer and Champy (1993a) were generally not undertaken by the respondent organizations. One indication of this was the low size of investment in BPR, with 43 per cent of medium and large organizations each incurring BPR related expenditure of under £1 million. Many of the processes being re-engineered seemed to be existing ones with moderate improvements being sought rather than wide-ranging changes brought about by a radical rethink of how the organization should be reconfigured, managed and controlled.

Radical BPR is portrayed widely as achieving sizeable job losses (Kavanagh, 1994) together with major changes to human resources and work organization (Willmott, 1995). Yet the survey research by Willcocks (1995, 1996) showed that for all completed BPR projects and all types of processes, staff redundancies averaged less than 5 per cent (against an expected 15 per cent) of total BPR costs. Generally, whatever the process being re-engineered, organizations did not seem to be aiming high when they looked for improvements from BPR. There may well be a cause and effect here, with organizations ‘aiming low and hitting low’, because the actual improvements being achieved were also relatively low. This is significant in the light of the claims of those who support radical re-engineering (Hammer and Champy 1993a, b) since it demonstrates that a less ambitious toe in the water approach was being undertaken by the majority of organizations in the UK sample.

The best performance on actual improvements was found to be with core processes (central to business functioning and relating directly to external customers). Support processes were defined to respondents as those supporting the operation of core processes, including for example information systems and administrative services. Management processes were defined as those processes entailed in directing and running the organization, for example strategic planning and the cycle of corporate reporting. Cross-boundary processes were those extending beyond organizational boundaries and linking with, for example, suppliers and customers (see Earl and Khan, 1994). Re-engineering of these three types of process produced consistently lower improvements. Very few organizations were achieving what could be called 'breakthrough' results. Thus of the organizations which had completed BPR projects, if a relatively conservative benchmark of significance of 20 per cent profitability gain, 20 per cent revenue gain and 10 per cent decrease in costs of doing business is used, only 18 per cent of organizations had actually achieved these significant financial benefits from BPR on all three performance indicators. Organizations were achieving, and in most cases aiming for, incremental improvements rather than radical change. This suggests that radical, discontinuous change was not clearly underscored in BPR practice. In the light of this research, a wide gap appears to exist between the strategic claims or vision of radical BPR espoused by Hammer and Champy (1993a, b), Hammer and Stanton (1995) and Champy (1995) and its implementation (Davenport and Stoddard, 1994). At the implementation stage, other writers suggest that radical BPR strategies became emergent, ad hoc and incremental improvements (Davenport, 1993b; Craig and Yetton, 1994; Grint and Willcocks, 1995; Willcocks and Smith, 1995). While this would seem to reflect the difficulties inherent in introducing large-scale change programmes such as re-engineering it would also seem to be characteristic of other large-scale IT projects in general (Sauer, 1993; Willcocks and Griffiths, 1994; Currie and Seddon, 1995).

Some important points emerging from the literature are endorsed by the case study research presented in this paper. One is the extent to which BPR efforts fail to link closely with business strategy and with effective organizational processes for strategy formulation. This is a feature also of a number of texts which are, ironically, largely concerned with securing the business strategy-BPR linkage (Belmonte and Murray, 1993; Johansson et al., 1993; Morris and Brandon, 1993). Disconnections between sectoral context, what business strategy requires, and the 'one best way' posited in much of the prescriptive BPR literature can lead to BPR providing organizational solutions that go beyond that which contingency theorists would predict as optimal (Francis and Southern, 1995). This has led some to posit a need to go beyond BPR and focus on business systems engineering (Watson, 1994; Galliers, 1995).

Another point concerns the multi-disciplinary holism at the heart of BPR study and practice. This demand for multi-disciplinarity creates a number of challenges for practice. For example, at the present time there would seem to be few robust methodologies and tools available to facilitate large scale BPR. Whilst some writers demonstrate the existence of many approaches, methodologies, techniques, and tools for BPR, the task of bringing together the offerings from such diverse fields has so far proved elusive. What emerges from this picture is an immaturity and a lack of integration on the methodological front (Earl and Khan, 1994; Klein, 1994). This engenders problems for project managers charged with the responsibility for translating BPR strategy into practical reality, and also for the technical staff required to develop IT-enabled business solutions. Against this background we suggest that a lack of coherent methodologies for BPR is likely to increase the possibility for fragmentation and confusion at the implementation stage, and this is demonstrated in our case study of BPR in the financial services sector.

In this paper, we attempt to take the analysis of BPR experiences further by addressing a rich set of findings from recent case study research. After detailing the research approach adopted, the paper discusses the 'new bank' vision at the Royal Bank of Scotland and the genesis of the Columbus project. The issues emerging during a two-and-a-half year implementation period are then discussed. In the final section we provide further analysis of these issues and conclude that whilst some process improvements were apparent, wide ranging process innovations as a result of the Columbus project were proving problematic.

## 2. Research approach

## 2.1. A case study on Royal Bank of Scotland

A longitudinal research study was carried out at the Royal Bank of Scotland beginning in March 1993 through to October 1995 to track the progress of the Columbus project — a major BPR change project at the Branch Banking Division (BBD) of the bank. The strengths and limitations of, and the reasons for pursuing case research on information systems are well established in the literature (see Benbasat et al., 1987; Lee, 1989; Yin, 1989). There is also now a considerable supportive literature on the adoption of qualitative and interpretive methods, while a number of researchers have successfully justified and applied a single case study research strategy for examining phenomena in the information systems field (see for example Markus, 1983; Van Maanen, 1979; Smith, 1990; Walsham and Waema, 1994; Walsham, 1995). The present study is informed by this literature, and part of the analysis was also facilitated by applying a sixfold framework used successfully elsewhere for the analysis of organizational change and information systems risk (Pettigrew and Whipp, 1991; Willcocks and Griffiths, 1994). The framework posits six interplaying categories: history, external and internal contexts, content and process of change, and outcomes (see Discussion below).

The present research adopted an interpretive and qualitative longitudinal single case research strategy. The objective was to explore the critical development and implementation issues and outcomes emerging during a self-styled radical re-engineering project in order to compare these experiences of BPR against the claims of proponents. In more detail, this objective was operationalized in a number of ways. Firstly, the case study was designed to elicit data from bank personnel on how the re-engineering of key business processes was set up and, in a respondent's words, 'rolled out'. The strategic vision for the Columbus project is embodied in the following statements taken from published documents:

Our goal of establishing a New Bank is coming closer. We are well on target to becoming the UK's best bank by 1997... Change is never easy. It requires foresight, determination and dedication. To be effective, a change programme has to have clear goals and achievable timescales ... The New Bank is no longer a distant goal. It is currently reality with all the necessary building blocks in place to deliver a radically different organization.

Secondly, the research foci considered the development and implementation issues emerging as critical during the Columbus project. We relate these issues to the strategic vision outlined by senior managers in various documents published by the bank. For unlike many less ambitious re-engineering projects discussed in the academic and trade literature — producing small scale process improvements, as opposed to large scale process innovations — the Columbus project was an example of large-scale or radical re-engineering since it was more than simply a technical quick-fix for a selected number of business processes. Thus by 1995 senior management had produced a statement stressing that:

Already, significant improvements have been introduced which have resulted in much better business results. In 1992, our aim was to achieve £200 million profit improvement within five years. We are half way there already.

A third element of the research was to develop a longitudinal case study on large-scale BPR to compare the experience of BPR against the claims of proponents (Hammer and Champy, 1993a, b; Champy, 1995; Davenport, 1993a, b). In particular, we were interested to observe whether re-engineering had transformed human relations to the extent that executive, managerial and employee roles had undergone significant changes. One part of this initially was examining the applicability of the notion of ‘empowerment’ in re-engineering as presented by senior management in the case (see below). The concept of empowerment was used by senior managers to describe a situation where people learned to use their initiative for taking decisions to re-design business processes around critical customer segments. By doing so, the bank would become divisionalized resulting in the phasing out of generalist careers in banking. The emphasis would be placed on developing specialist knowledge areas around the three new customer segments: retail, commercial and corporate.

During the preliminary stages of the case study research it became apparent that a primary set of issues were developing around the relationship between managerial and technical roles within the context of re-engineering within the financial services sector. These, therefore shifted to the centre ground for our analytical focus.

## 2.2. Interviewing

A wide selection of bank personnel were interviewed ranging from senior and middle managers from the business units and Technology Division, project managers controlling various elements of the Columbus project, analyst/programmers (permanent and contract) and other associated personnel (database administrators, testing/support personnel and contractors from supplier organizations such as IBM, Arthur Anderson and McKinsey). Interviews were conducted with 18 people (eight managers and 10 IT staff) and lasted between one and two hours in length. A variety of semi-structured questionnaires were used, each targeted at a specific managerial or technical interest group. Within the prior structuring of concerns and questioning detailed above the research proceeded inductively to build the case study from interview material, documentation, organizational records, and a review of physical evidence in the form of systems and their operation in various organizational contexts. This allowed us to develop our own analysis as an overlay on the several accounts produced by the organizational respondents at the bank. We then sought to compare and evaluate the lessons and difficulties that emerged from our analysis with those to be found in the wider literature on BPR development and implementation.

It is recognized that longitudinal research on a wide ranging subject such as large scale BPR is likely to produce complex issues and problems, many of which are relevant to managing other large scale IT projects in the financial services sector. As such, we are unable to describe and analyse all of them in detail in this paper. We therefore focus only on what we interpret as the key issues emerging from the case study. By drawing together the case study research with existing literature on BPR, the paper rejects one of re-engineering's central claims: that people can engage in a process of 'collective forgetting' (Grint and Willcocks, 1995). Whilst this is not to suggest that BPR is unlikely to succeed in all cases, it does however argue for considerable caution in the face of many of the claims espoused by re-engineering advocates about what BPR can achieve and how it can be conducted.

The following section of the paper begins with an overview of the strategic vision and implementation of the Columbus project undertaken at the Royal Bank of Scotland. The discussion is divided into two sections. The first considers the strategic aims and objectives of the Columbus project and, the second, its implementation. The paper concludes with a discussion on how this experience of large-scale BPR compares with existing studies in the area.

## 3. BPR and the 'New Bank' vision: the Columbus project

## 3.1. Context and strategy

As a consequence of 'Big Bang' in 1986, the stock market crash in 1987, and the UK financial services act which effectively deregulated the British financial services industry, the Royal Bank was facing intense competition in the late 1980s and early 1990s, particularly from other banks, the building societies, and insurance companies. Coupled with this, the deep economic recession, mounting bad debts (£1.1 billion bad debt provisions were made between 1991 and 1992 — Financial Times, 1995), lack of underlying profit and costs of 63 per cent of income all provided the impetus to embark on the Columbus project in 1992 (Oram and Wellins, 1995).

Senior managers at the bank conceded that lifetime customer loyalty could no longer be guaranteed. Indeed, customers were becoming more in tune with the range of financial services offered by competitors, and were becoming fickle in their choice of financial institutions. Much of this contextual detail is supported by independent sectoral studies (Child and Loveridge, 1990; Fincham and Scarbrough, 1995; Scarbrough, 1996). As an exercise in ‘radical BPR’, the Columbus project was launched with the support of a Change Management Group (CMG). According to a corporate report published by the bank, Columbus was intended to transform the bank into ‘the best retail bank in the UK by 1997’, and this would be achieved by implementing major changes to structures, products, services, job titles and roles, training policies, technology and marketing/sales. A summary of the key strategic objectives of the Columbus project, as detailed by senior managers in 1992, are outlined below:

Essentially the Columbus project team was set up to identify how these changes could be achieved. According to senior managers, it was crucial that line management were the focus for building the New Bank. Comments made by senior managers in the business units and Technology Division during 1992 and 1993 stressed that, in spite of millions of pounds invested in new technology, few tangible benefits had arisen in the business units. Yet Columbus was intended to be more than simply a large scale IT project. Rather, it was described as major a re-engineering project conceptualized and planned by senior executives with the aim of revolutionizing the entire bank. Six key interrelated areas were identified as comprising the Columbus project (see Table 2). These will now be discussed in detail.

## 3.2. A new philosophy for work organization

Perhaps the most important challenge of Columbus involved changing peoples' attitudes to encourage them to embrace new philosophies of work organization. People were expected to be 'empowered' in the new banking environment. Banks were renowned for being highly bureaucratic and regimented and senior managers were aware that some customers were intimidated by this image. It was pointed out that the old emphasis on geographical splits was no longer appropriate and this had given way to a different structure that established three distinct but interrelated businesses — Retail (for personal and business customers) Commercial and Corporate. To facilitate this structural change, new managerial roles that clearly distinguished between sales and service responsibilities were required. Corporate customers were believed to require a different service level from personal/domestic customers. Several interviewees at the bank were sceptical about these changes, particularly where they viewed ‘empowerment’ as detrimental to their own status, career and promotion prospects. Indeed, the Columbus project was purposely designed to phase out the traditional entry route into a generalist career in banking, and replace this with a greater emphasis upon specialization and in-depth knowledge of a specific customer segment and associated product/service groups. This is examined in more detail below.

## 3.3. New technology

In common with the major proponents of re-engineering, new technology was perceived by senior management at the bank as a critical enabling factor for translating BPR vision into practice. With over £100 million invested in new technology the bank was hoping to achieve a seamless service for its customers. Irrespective of the type of services required, technology would integrate services across the network. One senior manager cautioned that:

Technology is a key component of our change programme but we have not fallen into the trap of seeing technology as an end in itself. Rather it is being used as a means of improving business performance and of producing that essential seamless service.

The bank was aware of the high profile large scale IT disasters such as the Taurus fiasco which beset the International London Stock Exchange in 1993 (Currie, 1994; Willcocks and Griffiths, 1994) and, to this end, attempted to impose more stringent checks and balances for evaluating IT performance. As one senior IT manager pointed out,

IT should not be perceived as a quick fix panacea for BPR or any other large scale project.

## 3.4. The outlet

The Columbus project was designed to improve service levels throughout the entire banking outlets. IT-enabled re-engineering was planned to achieve consistent levels of services where entire transactions would be conducted in front of the customer. Senior managers hoped to reduce the risk of fraudulent transactions by improving customer identification methods. The New Branch PC-based system would have three main components: customer service, cashier/teller and account opening. Typically, technology in the past was perceived by senior managers as 'not always delivering appropriate technical solutions to a particular business problem'. As such, 'technology developments would no longer be planned in an ivory tower' (representative quotes). Communication between project sponsors (those who commission IT systems), project managers, analyst/programmers (those who manage and design IT systems) and users from the business units would need to improve.

Improvements from IT-enabled re-engineering were sought in three areas: (1) proposed technology developments would be user friendly, (2) staff would feel confident about using the new system, (3) customers would equally wish to use the technology. Facilitated by current technical developments, changes to the outlets were planned to enable staff in branches to become more directly involved in serving their customers. The new branch design was being tested in five different prototypes and was designed to utilize space to:

create more flexible surroundings for staff to work in and a welcoming environment for customers to conduct business... Gone are the forbidding exteriors, regimented queuing zones and glass screens. Instead, a more welcoming layout invites customers to feel comfortable. (internal document)

According to one senior manager, BPR should not simply be perceived in terms of how it changes bureaucratic or technical systems of the banking business. It should also be about changing customer perceptions of the bank. Customers should not feel intimidated when they enter a bank, nor should they be faced with long queues. An important activity was to monitor customer perceptions about the new look of the bank. Thus the physical appearance of the new branches was another element of BPR and though it was accepted that some customers would not like the new set-up and withdraw their custom, others would be won.

## 3.5. The network

Major changes to the network were further elements of the Columbus project. The traditional set up where each branch was a mini-bank in its own right was gradually to be phased out. Instead, each branch would have access to specialist centres and knowledge without having to house all the traditional back office functions. New specialist centres would be set up such as a Mortgage Centre serving the whole network, and UK-wide Mortgage Shops. Some 18 Corporate Centres housing specially selected relationship managers were planned where these individuals would develop close links with corporate customers. In addition, 15 Service Centres strategically located throughout the UK would deal with traditional back office functions. At the time of interview, centralized cheque returns would initially be handled by the Service Centres, but ultimately outsourced to EDS (a major outsourcing supplier). Part of the rationale for these changes was to facilitate a fundamental change in that customers would be of the bank and not of a branch. These changes were described by one senior manager as 'a total re-engineering of current practices', where many services would move from a manual system 'with all its inevitable inconsistencies' to a computer-based system.

## 3.6. Credit processes

Credit processes were further being re-engineered primarily to achieve efficiency gains and consistency in credit policies and lending decisions. Technology was essential for this goal to speed up processes and automate administration. A document obtained from the bank stressed that:

Our credit process will comprise an effective team-lending approach between relationship manager, analyst and sanctioner.

## 3.7. Human resources and teamworking

The final element of the Columbus project concerned major changes in human resource policies and practices. This was recognized by senior managers as the most challenging of all six elements of the Columbus project, yet arguably one which was given least detailed attention by the bank. A document obtained from the bank repeated a popular slogan expressed by many organizations. Thus:

None of our planned improvements are possible with the support of our staff. It is only through our people that we can hope to reach our goal of becoming the best bank in the UK by 1997.

Theoretically these changes would encourage staff to take charge of their own careers. As one respondent described it, promotion would no longer be based upon keeping your nose clean, but on achievement and ability. An internal document observed that:

No industry or sector is immune from market forces. Banking is no exception. The current competitive situation calls for different skills and much more flexible working practices. In a world where technological improvements can be copied, it is the people element which is often the only true differentiator ... We believe that it is through our staff that true change will occur.

The above six elements of the Columbus project were wide ranging and politically contentious. To enact such changes demanded the attention of numerous bank personnel from the business units and Technology Division. To this end, several multi-disciplinary teams were formed to translate the New Bank vision into a practical reality.

## 4. Implementing the 'New Bank' vision

Initial interviews with senior managers from the business units and Technology Division confirmed that IT-enabled re-engineering was a fundamental element of the Columbus project. But disappointing results from previous large scale IT projects as a result of poor communication between the business units and Technology Division, among other reasons, encouraged a cautious approach from senior managers. In their opinion, IT-enabled re-engineering was not another technical ‘quick-fix’ for performance improvement in the various business units. Sentiments of this nature reinforce Hammer and Champy’s (1993a, b) claim that as many as 70 per cent of radical re-engineering projects fail, many of which incurred high expenditure on IT and associated management consultancy. According to the Head of the Technology Division, there had previously been little correlation between level of IT investment and business-related project success. Currently with over £100 million invested in new technology, Columbus was perceived not simply as a large-scale IT project, but a large scale BPR project to revolutionize and restructure business processes.

In common with other large scale IT projects, BPR was likely to reinforce ongoing communication problems experienced between the business units and Technology Division. In the past, the business units complained that computer systems had unilaterally been imposed upon them from the Technology Division with little regard for their relevance within a business context. Technical staff, on the other hand, expressed concern that business users changed their minds frequently about what it was they required (e.g. what a computer system was intended to do; and how, and timescale in which it would be developed). Some past large scale IT projects had spun out of control. The consequences were escalating costs, a failure to meet delivery dates, loss of management control, low morale among users and the technical team and the emergence of a 'blame culture'. Failed IT projects were viewed by the business units as the responsibility of the Technology Division. According to the respondents, IT managers were accused of not listening to their customers, poor project management, not following procedures and methodologies, and failing to understand the business situation. Typical comments from Technology Division respondents were that business customers did not know what they required as a technical solution for a specific business problem, were technically 'illiterate', were 'lunched to death' by large computer suppliers (and therefore purchased hardware and software that was not appropriate for their requirements), and constantly imposed system changes even though the functional and technical specifications for the proposed computer system had been authorized by senior level management. The Columbus project was intended to deal with the problems engendered by the separation of the business units and Technology Division. However, with Columbus, all staff were expected to become involved in re-engineering the business. Senior managers from the business units were therefore keen to align IT with the Columbus strategic vision by focusing upon key implementation issues.

## 4.1. Aligning business strategy with technology re-engineering

Tracking the development of the Columbus project over a two-and-a-half year period suggested that aligning business strategy with technology through re-engineering remained as difficult as ever. What emerged from the interviews with senior managers from the business units and IT managers and technical staff from the Technology Division was that a wide gulf between the two groups persisted. The Technology Division was perceived by the business units as a support function, and not therefore strategic nor critical to the core business. In fact, some senior managers were keen to outsource a significant proportion of the technology facility together. This reflects the growing trend for major organizations to seek IT outsourcing as an alternative to operating an in-house IT division (Currie, 1996).

Notwithstanding the possibility of outsourcing, the effectiveness of the Columbus project in business terms was highly dependent upon the design, development and implementation of computer systems, supplied in-house by the Technology Division and externally by software suppliers and consultancies. By late 1995 the bank had shed about 2000 jobs, with a further 1500 planned job losses over the next two years. Against a background of large scale redundancies, the role of technology was becoming even more political. Many of the job losses were in middle management where old skills were perceived to be redundant in the New Bank environment. Within the Technology Division, the bank hired as many as 40 per cent IT contractors for some projects. Contracts were between three months and one year, though many were extended beyond a year if the skills were felt to be critical to the project. Two reasons were given for the hiring of IT contractors. The first was concerned with efficiency and cost minimization. IT contractors were hired to meet the peaks and troughs of computer projects at the bank. As stated elsewhere, some senior managers viewed IT as a support function, and to this effect, it was not critical to the core activities of banking. As such, hiring IT contractors reflected the desire of some senior managers to pursue an IT outsourcing policy. The second reason was to hire scarce or expert technical skills which were not available in-house. Many IT contractors possessed scarce up-to-date PC-based skills which were essential to the technical requirements underpinning the Columbus project. Senior managers believed that 'technology transfer' would occur when IT contractors and others in a management or technical consultancy role would pass on their skills to permanent members of staff at the bank. This was described as 'skills scraping' by senior managers. However, many IT contractors were critical of this position for two reasons. First, they did not see their role as essentially one of training. On the contrary, they worked to strict project deadlines which left little time to inculcate others with their technical know-how. Second, they saw themselves as 'portfolio workers' (Currie, 1995). In this capacity, they perceived their technical knowledge and expertise as a commodity, and not something to be transferred to permanent staff.

## 4.2. Developing project management skills

As recently as 1994, the bank had hired a firm of management consultants to develop and administer core competency tests to its senior and middle managers. Some 44 managerial jobs were shed as a result of this testing process. Interviews carried out with managers and technical staff suggested that, against a backdrop of large scale job losses, the bank was suffering from serious skills shortages in project management and technical (client/server) skills. These skills shortages were believed to be an impediment to the re-engineering efforts of the bank. Yet perceptions about the nature of managerial and technical skills required for the New Bank varied within and across managerial and technical grades.

At the project management level, managers with traditional skills acquired in previous decades were becoming threatened by the new PC-based technical environment. In some cases, project managers with a mainframe background were not considered to possess the right skills for managing client/server technology. Many of these criticisms were voiced by IT contractors who, in some cases, formed a political alliance with senior executives wishing to find legitimate reasons to shed managerial positions in the business units and Technology Division.

Interviews with IT staff (permanent and contract) suggested that new project management skills were needed. These respondents placed the emphasis on self-managing teams and autonomous working practices rather than management skills reflecting what was described as a ‘command and control’ approach. However the bank was considered to be an old-fashioned establishment which continued to be structured according to hierarchical and functional demarcation boundaries. Criticisms about project managers by technical staff (particularly analyst/programmers) suggested they had not kept pace with the latest technological changes. But some technical staff went further by arguing that many project managers were unable to manage highly skilled technical staff since they had abandoned their technical skills by becoming managers. Many technical staff rejected the view that technical knowledge was not essential for managing large scale IT-enabled re-engineering projects. This view was more apparent among contract IT staff who were themselves managed by permanent project managers of the bank. The rigid structured methodologies used by project managers were also considered to be obsolete by many IT contractors and even by some of the project managers themselves. According to one IT contractor (a senior analyst/programmer on a six month contract), in a not untypical comment:

My project manager usually asks me to set my own deadlines. He doesn't really understand what it is I do, even though he is responsible for my performance. This works because we have a good working relationship. It's all about trust and respect, not charts, checklists and performance indicators.

In other examples, the opposite was the case where interpersonal relationships between project managers and technical staff had broken down. Interviews suggested that top-down, rigid project management methods and techniques were inadequate for large scale computer projects where IT staff were working with the latest client/server technology. As one IT contractor commented:

Project management is too concerned with rule-based, linear approaches to measuring performance. Just because you measure something doesn't mean you can manage it. The best management practice involves an understanding of what it is you can't measure. It is very difficult to manage the work of technical people when you don't understand the technology.

Equally, it is important to report the perceptions of many project managers critical of the demands placed upon them to manage large scale IT projects in a constantly changing business environment. In the 1990s the bank had undergone significant organizational, managerial and technical changes. The business had been restructured and part of the Columbus project was to see through further fundamental changes in how banking services were offered to customers. This had placed significant demands on managers, many of whom remained nervous about the recent round of staff redundancies. Project managers were the interface between the business units and Technology Division and had to ensure that appropriate technical solutions were delivered to business customers. But this was proving increasingly difficult in an environment of constant flux. The situation for project managers was worsening since senior executives were attempting to impose stringent performance targets on project managers. In turn, their own performance would be judged according to the outcome of the IT projects they managed. Pressures on project managers were growing and were not ameliorated by the severe skills shortages in specialist technical areas.

## 4.3. Developing technical skills

Paradoxically, the technical skills shortages of the bank were not helped by the increasing tendency to hire external IT contractors on short term (six months to one year) contracts. From a financial perspective, this approach was viewed by senior executives as the most appropriate given the inevitable peaks and troughs of IT work, not to mention the financial constraints on the technical choices of the business units. Whereas many permanent IT staff were seen to have traditional mainframe skills, IT contractors were brought in to work on new client/server and other applications. Yet the problems of interfacing programming code written in Fourth Generation Languages (4 GLs) with the mainframe (COBOL code) proved technically challenging and politically contentious, particularly where people interpreted the development of client/server to be detrimental to their own career interests.

Interviews with the technical team working on the New Branch computer system at the bank raised many issues which were more than just technical quibbles. For example, this project was intended to develop a customer based system to replace the existing account based one used by the branches. This meant that staff would be able to call up all the customer accounts on one screen to carry out funds transfers and monitor transactions, among other things. In turn, processing time would be greatly reduced. This would involve the development of many new programmes written in SQLWindows to interface with the legacy (mainframe) system.

Many of the technical staff working on this project were IT contractors, brought in for only six months at a time. During their contract period, they encountered numerous obstacles in the development process. An important constraint was that the mainframe was essentially account based and could not easily be changed. Thus each customer enquiry was handled by a separate program pertaining to a particular customer account and the complexity of attempting to call up numerous customer information only added to the intense traffic on the network.

As a result of these problems, the technical team were unable to meet the original business objective of developing a full customer based system within the time and budgetary constraints of the project. This meant that senior managers from the business and project managers working within the Technology Division were faced with the dilemma of either (1) developing a less ambitious account based system, or (2) pursuing the expensive option of developing a full customer based system. In the end managers decided to follow the first route and descope the original technical specification. But this was felt to be detrimental to the original New Branch computer project overall. To this end, senior executives attempted to justify their decision on the grounds of changes in the business situation. IT staff, on the other hand, took a different view. As one IT contractor commented:

Senior managers think they know what the business requires, but they don't understand how it can be achieved technically. They seem to think that technical solutions are just a matter of pressing a few buttons on a keyboard. This happens all the time. They come up with a great idea and then we have to tell them it is not technically feasible!

An important issue emerging from the interviews with project managers and technical staff concerned the division of labour between the two groups and the inherent communication difficulties which resulted. Whereas project managers wished to retain control of computer projects by providing an interface between the business units and Technology Division, their role became increasing difficult as a consequence of their lack of technical expertise (in client server). This problem was not easily reconciled given that project managers had effectively abandoned their technical skills by becoming managers. Yet the technical complexities of transforming the New Bank vision into a reality required the fusing of business expertise with technical know-how. At the time of writing, some technical aspects of the Columbus project were being ‘de-scoped’ as the technical challenges were proving more difficult than was originally anticipated. This represented a low maturity in client/server technology development and use, the problems of which were not simply confined to the Royal Bank of Scotland, but to other commercial users as well.

## 5. Discussion of findings

The case study research on the Columbus project at the Royal Bank of Scotland raised many organizational, managerial, strategic and technical issues which produced a complex picture of a large scale re-engineering project. We now attempt to address some of these issues and discuss their relevance in the light of existing studies.

Notwithstanding the critical literature that has emerged on the theory and practice of re-engineering (Grint and Willcocks, 1995; Willmott, 1995) our case study on the Royal Bank of Scotland raised a number of issues about the difficulties of managing large-scale organizational change which have been highlighted in other studies (Beer et al., 1990; Sauer, 1993; Currie, 1995). The claim amongst proponents of radical re-engineering that an organization can embark on process innovation using a blank sheet of paper (Hammer and Champy, 1993a, b) would seem unrealistic in the context of the organizational, strategic, managerial and technical antecedents in contemporary organizations (Davenport and Stoddart, 1994; Willcocks and Smith, 1995). In practice, at the Royal Bank of Scotland traditional, conservative practices and procedures served to shape and inhibit large scale re-engineering activities to a considerable extent. One key observation was that re-engineering actually threatened the existing status quo at the bank as traditional career paths and working practices underwent major change. This was producing instability and confusion as managerial staff increasingly began to question their own commitment to the change programme. In this capacity the relationship between continuity and change was important in the re-engineering activity. This has wide resonance in the literature on major change (Currie, 1994; Pettigrew and Whipp, 1991; Walton, 1989).

Increasingly in the burgeoning literature on BPR three issues are raised about creating the context for senior executives to achieve their goals with radical re-engineering projects. These relate to the ability of the senior executive to create what has been called a 'burning platform' equating radical change with business survival; the need for rapid completion of core process re-engineering, preferably within 12 months, otherwise losses of focus and momentum occur coupled with loss of enthusiasm and commitment from top management; and the need for a clear strategy applied to an identifiable strategic business unit with clear objectives and relatively straightforward, easily monitored performance criteria (Heygate, 1993; Hammer and Stanton, 1995; Willcocks, 1995). At the Royal Bank of Scotland the 'New Bank' vision arose from top management analysis of an increasingly difficult competitive position and the need to address changing customer requirements. However, in practice, the bank was already profitable, and change could not be communicated clearly as a survival issue. In practice re-engineering emerged less as a definitive strategy for addressing important external competitive business and labour market pressures in the banking industry, and more as a complex, ad hoc and fragmented response that was constantly being revised over a long time period — in many ways resembling emergent strategy as discussed by Mintzberg (1989). The problematical re-engineering experienced in the bank environment would also seem to have been influenced by the large size and complexity of the organization, a point further developed below.

In line with Willmott (1995), the multi-disciplinary holism at the heart of BPR in the bank context turned out to be little more than a melting pot of conflicting disciplines, coalitions and interests across managerial and technical groups. At the organizational level, the separation of the business units with the Technology Division seemed to hinder re-engineering efforts, particularly where managers and technical staff interpreted strategic and operational change in line with their own political interests. Traditional hierarchical and functional boundaries in an industry which is historically conservative did not assist those wishing to enforce new working practices and attitudinal change on its staff. Senior managers from the business units perceived the Technology Division as separate from core banking activities even though IT-enabled re-engineering was intended to further integrate technologies throughout the organization. These views led some senior managers to a position where they felt that outsourcing IT could achieve greater cost efficiencies. Attitudinal differences between business managers and technical staff on the role of IT in re-engineering were wide and reflected institutional communication barriers between the two groups. This was demonstrated by analysing the nature of project management of computer systems development within the Technology Division. Senior managers' attempts at developing the New Branch project were inhibited by their own lack of understanding of technology and also by how computer projects were managed and co-ordinated.

In the extant literature such issues are commonly cited as barriers to technological change (Earl, 1989; Grindley, 1995; Harvey, 1995).

The case findings can also be related to earlier empirical work on profiling risks in major IT projects (Willcocks and Griffiths, 1994). A central finding there was that risk of failure correlated strongly to a drive into the technology resulting in a loss of focus on the business purpose to be achieved, and neglect of fundamental organizational and behavioural changes fundamental to the delivery of espoused objectives. At the bank senior managers were sensitive to the need to avoid technology-driven projects, and introduced countervailing measures, but risks were increased by the size, complexity and time-length of projects and the ‘newness’ of the technologies to the organization (Feeny et al., 1996; Willcocks and Griffiths, 1994).

In other work risk of failure in major IT-enabled projects has also been related to factors relating to history, external and internal contexts, content and process of change (see Willcocks and Margetts, 1994). On history, the record of previous disappointment provided some platform for instigating new ways of working on IT projects at the bank. However, the buying in of technical expertise to mitigate risk because of complex large scale technical issues also created tensions and difficulties. On external context, environmental pressures for change seemed less obvious to many of the workforce at the bank, and this created tensions and problems during re-engineering activity. On internal context, radical re-engineering, by definition, meant wholesale change in structure, strategy, reward systems, skills, culture, management styles, types of teamwork. This inevitably implied the generation of major cultural and political issues. The processes adopted to handle these seemed less than convincing to many respondents because it appeared to embody mixed messages — on the one hand full staff involvement and ‘empowerment’, on the other large job reductions (3500 by 1997) and management pushing through change. Some 20 grades were being cut to only five broad bands each with different pay scales. This would first be introduced in the bank’s Glasgow offices then be extended nation-wide to 10 000 staff. However the content of change provided the greatest risk factors in the Columbus project. The main risk factors identified in major change projects by Willcocks and Griffiths (1994) — namely size, complexity, number of business units involved, technical uncertainty, and definitional uncertainty (lack of clarity on business requirements) — these were all highly prevalent in the Columbus project.

At Royal Bank, re-engineered core processes were to be heavily dependent on IT to deliver the anticipated large-scale improvements in financial performance. The technical difficulties inherent in Columbus have already been indicated, not least the problems of interfacing legacy mainframes with client server technologies. However the bank project is also illustrative of the difficulties experienced by a complex organization with low technology maturity. Maturity refers to where a organization is dealing with a radically new/unstable technology, an existing technology in a radically new application, and/or where in-house staff lack the relevant IT experience. All these factors prevailed in some degree in the Columbus project. Following Feeny et al. (1996), this made necessary a 'user focused' as opposed to a 'specialist-led' approach to systems development. In fact this was attempted but it became clear from respondents that project managers selected for their dual business and technical skills and background became out of touch with the technology. A shortage of project management and technical skills and the newness of the technologies also contributed to delays and compromises in delivering, for example, the full customer-based account system. The general sense was one of lack of understanding of the technical issues at senior management levels together with their under-estimation of the complexities and difficulties inherent in exploiting the new technologies chosen.

Following on from this, a particularly interesting set of issues around the division of labour and the management of expertise in new technology projects appear to come together in the Royal Bank of Scotland/Columbus case. On expertise, Scarbrough's (1996) contention that 'the nearer to the expert work process, the more that coordination and control depends on expertise rather than managerial authority' (p. 41) can be clearly read into the disaffection with project and other managers felt by those defining themselves as possessing the relevant technical expertise. A related debate suggests the importance of a spread of knowledge — organization specific knowledge (that is, how to get things done in the organization), business knowledge and technical knowledge and experience — in different degrees across at least four roles in IS-related projects. For Earl (1989) these were impresarios ('the few executive managers in the IS function who can propel the organization into strategic considerations of IT') leaders (IT-orientated business executives), hybrids (link managers with a blend of all three knowledge bases and the ability to get things done), and technicians. Beyond such a division of labour and the acknowledged shortage of 'hybrids' in the IS field, other research has pointed to more detailed bridging mechanisms being required in new technology projects. As just one example, Skyrme (1996) describes 17 mechanisms, divided into position (e.g. co-location, IT director on COE team), composition (e.g. cross-functional steering teams), processes (e.g. joint business planning), processes and people (e.g. job rotation). While some of these elements can be found in the case, judged against these standards, it becomes a little more clear why the Columbus project experienced the scale of difficulties it did on the technical front, unlike for example in the large client/server based project reported by Lacity et al. (1997).

These issues were exacerbated by early reliance on existing project methodologies that emerged as not always appropriate. In practice systems development relied as much on trial and error as formal methodology. Project managers who provided an interface between the business and Technology Division faced a difficult challenge as few methodologies and techniques existed which reflected the complex nature of managing large scale re-engineering. Whilst formal-rational project management and software development methodologies have been heavily criticized for not addressing the 'soft' issues of managing projects and people (Currie and Seddon, 1995) our case study also showed that these methods and techniques were outdated for the present organizational and technical environment. Client server technologies apparently demand a new project management style which emphasizes a more autonomous and iterative approach to software development rather than one based upon a formal, autocratic command and control management style (Nickson, 1995).

The new technical environment also had considerable political and human resource implications and impacts. New technologies could be equated with large reductions in job numbers. For existing technical staff if it did not threaten jobs, it certainly placed doubts about the relevance of their traditional IT skills. The large influx of technical staff and contractors signalled new working practices and types of contract, and that banking was no longer a job for life. The technological environment symbolized the 'new organization' in a positive sense i.e. progress and investment and so could be tied in with politics as the management of meaning (Willcocks et al., 1997). But simultaneously it symbolized a threat to existing work practices, and the redistribution of power and resources. This symbolic ambivalence had considerable political ramifications for the Columbus project.

Finally, it is worth highlighting the complex political tensions raised by managers and IT staff working on the Columbus project. Issues of this natures are similarly highlighted in a recent survey which found that five common barriers to BPR programmes were: middle and line management resistance; lack of commitment/non-enthusiastic support from the top; the prevailing culture and political structure; and lower level employee fear and resistance to change (Willcocks, 1995). In the Royal Bank of Scotland, senior management chose to lever changes through lower levels of management, project management and widespread use of contractors, rather than adopt a top-down approach suggested by Hammer and Champy (1993a, b) and others. However, this inevitably dissipated focus. Moreover, the 'New Bank' vision, in its human resource implications, could be perceived as threatening interests and change at all levels. Not surprisingly we found considerable evidence of emergent protectionist strategies amongst the bank's workforces.

The findings suggest that all the areas need to be examined to produce a rich and complex picture of re-engineering since one area alone (for example the choice of technology) will not significantly influence the outcomes of major organizational change.

It is postulated that re-engineering projects are more likely to succeed if all four areas are addressed by the organization. Whilst this is not to suggest a rational-linear approach for re-engineering (Buchanan, 1991), it is to recognize that large-scale BPR is highly complex and not determined or influenced by one area alone. For example, past (Hammer, 1990) and more recent literature has found that lack of senior management commitment to re-engineering is usually a major factor underpinning poor results (Hammer and Stanton, 1995; Willcocks, 1995). But this finding needs to be addressed within a wider organizational context which considers the role of senior managers in re-engineering projects.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper has attempted to contribute to the understanding of radical re-engineering by detailing a major IT-enabled re-engineering project as it unfolded. Our research uncovered a wide gulf emerging between the vision and reality of re-engineering at the bank. While implementation of the New Bank vision was producing many of the changes desired by senior management, the experience of transforming the vision of re-engineering into reality was both ad hoc and unpredictable. It was apparent that no structured methodology or 'one best way' of introducing re-engineering could be pinpointed. Indeed, no cohesive or comprehensive methodologies or techniques were available for BPR. Nor were they likely to become available, especially as the success of BPR was as much a political achievement as an organizational or technical achievement. Traditional project management methodologies were outdated. Yet senior management was faced with many uncertainties in defining the nature and type of new skills and competencies needed for a re-engineered 'New Branch' environment. The social and political processes of re-engineering were also underplayed by senior bank personnel, and this appeared to manifest itself in poor communication between the business units and Technology Division.

The case study on the Royal Bank of Scotland clearly raises a number of complex questions and provides some answers about the degree and circumstances whereby re-engineering can produce the wide-ranging organizational, managerial and technical changes espoused by those who advocate radical, revolutionary change. Whereas the bank had eliminated the traditional multi-grade structure through re-engineering, the technical challenges were apparently becoming increasingly difficult. This factor alone inhibited the goal of process innovation at the bank. As organizational transformation through new technology proved difficult to implement, elements of re-engineering became 'de-scoped' to accommodate these problems, a phenomenon which explains to some degree 'why change programmes don't produce change' (Beer et al., 1990). At the same time the bank continued to increase profits in the 1992–95 period, partly through large-scale reductions in job numbers. In conclusion, the research uncovered a wide range of practical difficulties, many of which are likely to be experienced by other organizations attempting large-scale IT-enabled re-engineering. By early 1996, however, the wider question facing the bank remained whether the New Bank vision would be delivered on in time to match the intense and growing competition from the banks, insurance companies, building societies and other new entrants into the financial services marketplace.
