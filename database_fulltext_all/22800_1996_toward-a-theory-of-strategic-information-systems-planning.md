# Toward a theory of strategic information systems planning

Albert L. Lederer $^{a}$ , Hannu Salmela $^{b}$

$^{a}$ College of Business and Economics, University of Kentucky, Lexington, KY 40506-0034, USA $^{b}$ Turku School of Economics and Business Administration, Department of Administration/Information Systems Science, Rehtorinpellonkatu 3, 20500 Turku, Finland

Revised paper accepted for publication May 1996 by Professor Jon Turner

## Abstract

Strategic information systems planning is the process of identifying a portfolio of computer-based applications that will assist an organization in executing its business plans and realizing its business goals. Carrying it out is a critical challenge for many information systems and business executives. Despite its importance to them, the absence of a theory of strategic information systems planning impedes research in the area. An input-process-output model provides the initial basis for such a theory. Constructs in the final version of a theory are: (1) the external environment, (2) the internal environment, (3) planning resources, (4) the planning process, (5) the information plan, (6) the implementation of the information plan, and (7) the alignment of the information plan with the organization's business plan. The constructs exhibit causal relationships among each other. Hypotheses illustrate the relationships. The theory has value for both researchers and practitioners.

Keywords: Strategic information systems planning; Input-process-output model; Information systems management

## 1. Introduction

Both corporate general managers and information systems executives have viewed improved strategic information systems planning as a key issue (Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1987; Niederman et al., 1991; Champy, 1993; Galliers, 1993). On the one hand, strategic information systems planning can contribute to their organizations in many ways. For example, it can identify the most desirable information systems applications in which to invest (Henderson and Sifonis, 1988), help an organization use its information systems to carry out its existing business strategies (Hartog and Herbert, 1986), help it define new business strategies, technology policies, and architectures (Earl, 1993; Porter, 1985), and help it align its information systems strategy with its business strategy (King, 1988). On the other hand, the failure to carry strategic information systems plans out can cause lost opportunities, duplicated efforts, incompatible systems, and wasted resources (Raghunathan and King, 1988).

Strategic information systems planning is also a critical issue because it is often unsuccessful. Many applications recommended by strategic information systems planning studies are often disregarded while many other applications are implemented (Early, 1990; Lederer and Sethi, 1988).

Because strategic information systems planning has importance to management, can influence an organization favorably, and is difficult to accomplish, it is a legitimate target for research. Indeed, many researchers have studied it. However, the study of strategic information systems planning is hampered by the absence of a theory that describes it. The objective of this paper is to provide such a theory.

## 2. What is strategic information system planning?

Strategic information systems planning has been defined as ‘the process of identifying a portfolio of computer-based applications that will assist an organization in executing its business plans and realizing its business goals’ (Lederer and Sethi, 1988, p. 446). In many organizations, it also includes the specification of databases and systems to support those applications. It might embrace the selection of rather prosaic applications, from an existing list of possibilities, that would best fill the organization’s current and future needs (Carlson, 1979; Kerner, 1979). It might also entail the discovery of new applications with the potential to create an advantage over competitors (Parsons, 1983; Ives and Learmonth, 1984; McFarlan, 1984; Porter, 1985; Porter and Millar, 1985; Clemons, 1986; Boynton and Zmud, 1987).

To perform strategic information systems planning, an organization would typically carry out a major, intensive study. It would follow one of several, similar well-defined and documented methodologies (IBM Corporation, 1975; Carlson, 1979; Kerner, 1979; Rockart, 1979; Vacca, 1984; Andersen Consulting, 1989; Holland Systems Corp., 1989; Martin and Leben, 1989) to guide it or it could customize its own. It would form committees of users and information systems specialists. It would often use the methodology's vendor for training and guidance. During the multi-step study, it would define a portfolio of applications, their priorities, databases, data elements, and a network of computers and communications equipment to support them. The study would also provide a schedule for their development and installation. During the ensuing implementation of the plan, the organization would typically update it. Such a planning approach may, however, be too expensive and time consuming; by the time the plan has been developed, the organization's concerns and priorities may have changed enough to obsolete the plan (Lederer and Mendelow, 1993).

Hence, although many organizations conduct such a formal planning process, others do not. Instead, they practice a more informal approach with continuous adaptation to the availability of resources in the presence of environmental threats and opportunities (Vitale et al., 1986). Planning is based on a dynamic administrative process to ensure continuous alignment between business and IT management (Venkatraman et al., 1993). Planning evolves as the result of decisions based on the use of particular methods and to fit current needs (Earl, 1993). In extreme cases, managers may merely ‘muddle through’ (Lindblom, 1959) to find satisfactory solutions to exigent problems (Cyert and March, 1963; Cohen et al., 1972; March and Olsen, 1979). With such piecemeal planning, shifting priorities may impede the development of large-scale systems (Lederer and Mendelow, 1993).

## 3. Need for a theory of strategic information systems planning

Although the importance of strategic information systems planning to corporate general managers and information systems executives may have provided the initial impetus to its study, a theory is also essential for the success of this research. In general, theories fulfill several purposes (Dubin, 1978). They focus observations by giving order to human experiences. They sort observations into classes of objects or events to help the observer give this order. They also help the observer understand and predict. Thus '[t]he "Need" for theories lies in the human behavior of wanting to impose order on unordered experiences' (Dubin, 1978, p. 6).

More specifically, a theory is needed to enable researchers to study strategic information systems planning and present their findings in an organized, comprehensive, parsimonious, and meaningful manner. One study needs to follow another study and thus build a cumulative collection of findings which identify the antecedents—namely, the environmental conditions and managerial behaviors—that have presaged successful strategic information systems planning.

Such a collection of findings will make it easier to describe what researchers know and what they need to discover. An organized description of what researchers know will facilitate the practical guidance that organizations need. Organizations may be able to control some of the antecedents and thus improve their strategic information systems outcomes. Such control would not reduce planning options but rather enable organizations to make a better selection of them.

A theory is also necessary to prevent researchers from being exposed to criticism that their work lacks scientific rigor. Theory is the basis of scientific endeavor and its absence raises doubts about the adherence to commonly accepted research practice. In fact, journals have used the lack of a theoretical basis as a reason for declining to publish research about strategic information systems planning.

Thus after almost two decades of research, many excellent and highly relevant individual studies have drawn conclusions about strategic information systems planning. Still, the overall practical contribution is more a collection of different, and sometimes even conflicting, methods and prescriptions for planning rather than an organized understanding of it. Hence, the goal of the theory described herein is to present an organized means of understanding the antecedents to successful strategic information systems planning in order to stimulate research and assist practice.

## 4. Overview of a theory $^{1}$

Strategic information systems planning has been described as a system comprised of inputs, processing, and outputs (King, 1988). In that context, various inputs such as objectives, resources, and information influence a process of specific, predetermined, planning activities. The process results in an information plan whose major component is a set of recommendations for new information systems.

Elements both within the organization's internal environment and beyond its control influence the planning process (Lederer and Mendelow, 1990). Hence we hypostatize both external and internal environments as constructs that affect the planning process.

However, organizations often fail to develop the systems in the information plan (Lederer and Sethi, 1988). Thus implementation of the plan is an important construct.

Finally, the objective of the implementation of the information systems plan is to effect the success of the organization (Porter, 1985; Hartog and Herbert, 1986; Henderson and Sifonis, 1988; Earl, 1993). This is presumably done by aligning the results of the strategic information systems planning process—the implemented information systems—with the business needs of the organization (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978a; King, 1988). Thus alignment is an important construct in the theory. As a measure of successful strategic information systems planning, it is so important that it is the final dependent variable. In effect, the theory predicts alignment.

Hence our theory of strategic information systems planning consists of seven constructs: (1) the external environment, (2) the internal environment, (3) planning resources, (4) the planning process, (5) the information plan, (6) the implementation of the information plan, and (7) the alignment of the information plan with the organization's business plan. Fig. 1 shows them in boxes and their relationships with arrows. Labels on the arrows designate the initials of the associated constructs for use in the discussion of the theory.

The relationships (i.e. the arrows) represent the hypotheses of the theory. Past research has not tested them explicitly and intentionally but instead has provided evidence to suggest them. They are discussed below and are written to be specific enough to guide and stimulate research yet still be general enough to facilitate variation in their interpretation and operationalization. $^{2}$

## 5. The effect of the external environment on the planning process

Several researchers have articulated the need to consider the external environment as an influence on strategic information systems planning. In doing so, they have identified a number of elements within that environment that can affect the planning process (as indicated by arrow EE -> PP in Fig. 1). $^{3}$

For example, changes in supplier trends, customer preferences, emerging technology, government legislation, and competitors' actions may influence strategic information systems planning by making it more difficult (Lederer and Mendelow,

1990; Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1991). The economic stability of the industry and country can also complicate it because information systems development time frames are shorter than other organizational time frames (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978b; Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1991). These elements are external to an organization and are typically difficult to control.

Likewise, the information intensity of the business sector may affect strategic information systems planning (Earl, 1993). For example, firms in service industries are expected to have more resources invested in strategic information systems planning, better planning, and greater expectations about planning outcomes than do firms in manufacturing industries (Premkumar and King, 1994a). Planners whose industry is more dependent on information must consider a greater number of diverse influences on planning and this makes the identification of major planning issues more difficult (Sambamurthy et al., 1993). Planners with more external stakeholders face greater complexity and more planning constraints (Sambamurthy et al., 1994).

Strategic information systems planning in multinational companies is similarly more complex (Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1993). Different geographic regions and cultures impose different constraints on organizations; one study found more informal strategic information systems planning in a region with less planning experience (Galliers, 1987).

These elements are influential primarily because they create environmental uncertainty (Earl, 1993). During prolonged plan implementation—that is, during systems development, the external environment (suppliers, customers, technology, etc.) may change and alter the organization's needs. The inability of planners to anticipate such change during this time complicates the planning process and vitiates plans. In effect, the more turbulent the external environment is, the more difficult strategic information systems planning is (Pyburn, 1983; Vitale et al., 1986; Lederer and Mendelow, 1990).

Thus in summary, the stability and predictability of industry, government, and economics appear to promote strategic information systems planning (Byrd et al., 1995; Sambamurthy et al., 1993; Pyburn, 1983; Vitale et al., 1986; Premkumar and King, 1994b; Lederer and Mendelow, 1990). These findings suggest the following hypothesis:

H1: A more stable external environment produces a more effective and efficient planning process.

## 6. The effect of the internal environment on the planning process

Several researchers have articulated the need to consider how the internal environment is a determinant of the strategic information systems planning process (arrow IE -> PP). For example, the organization's corporate culture can facilitate its strategic information systems planning by being congruent with it (Applegate et al., 1996; Guimares and KcKeen, 1989). The organization's planning and control style, perhaps in part a function of corporate culture, similarly influences strategic information systems planning (Earl, 1993).

Likewise, organization size, organization structure (mechanistic vs. organic) and management style (entrepreneurial vs. conservative) may influence strategic information systems planning (Doukidis et al., 1994). For example, large size increases the need for formal strategic information planning (Premkumar and King, 1994b). An organization's decision making approach (group or individual decision making) also affects strategic information systems planning (Guimares and McKeen, 1989). More conservative cultures may be more risk averse in their information planning; larger organizations may lack the flexibility to create more changeable strategic information plans.

In some organizations, information systems play a more important role than in others in terms of the significance of existing systems and future systems development (Applegate et al., 1996; Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1990; Premkumar and King, 1994a). (While such a role could be a function of the organization's external environment—namely its industry, the current discussion considers it as a function of its internal environment—its management, culture, history, etc.) More resources are used for strategic information systems planning in organizations where information systems play a more significant role (Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1990).

Some organizations are more sophisticated in their business planning than others and this is reflected by more sophisticated strategic information systems planning (Lederer and Sethi, 1988). The maturity of the organization's information systems management experience may similarly affect strategic information systems planning (Earl, 1993). Moreover, as the organization becomes technologically more mature, strategic information systems planning also evolves (Nolan, 1973; Gibson and Nolan, 1974; Lucas and Sutton, 1977; Benbasat et al., 1984; King and Kraemer, 1984; Sullivan, 1985; Galliers and Sutherland, 1991). Technological maturity has resulted in the evolution of strategic information planning from intuitive-based planning to formal planning (McFarlan et al., 1983).

This evolution has, perhaps, led to the emergence of data planning as an important focus for strategic information systems planning (Goodhue et al., 1988, 1992). Information planners thus consider an organization's current database as well as its current hardware and software architecture during a strategic information systems planning study (Lederer and Gardiner, 1992). In addition, the organization's management practices—such as the extent to which IT acquisition is centralized or decentralized—influence the manner in which resources are made available to users and thus affect the planning process (Guimares and McKeen, 1989).

In fact, the growing diversity in IT resources, uses, and acquisition channels has made the task of strategic information systems planning more complex (Boynton and Zmud, 1987). With the dispersal of information systems resources and management responsibilities throughout the internal environment, strategic information systems planning based on a premise of centralized control may prove ineffective. At the same time, end-user preferences evolve. End-users become more sophisticated and strategic information systems planning responds to their preferences (Lederer and Mendelow, 1990; Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1990).

Information planning goals and objectives are also elements of the internal environment. Such goals include gaining competitive advantage (Galliers, 1987), identifying new and higher payback applications (Galliers, 1988), identifying strategic applications (Sullivan, 1985), increasing top management commitment (Earl, 1993), improving communications with users (McLean and Soden, 1977), forecasting IT resource requirements (McLean and Soden, 1977), allocating IT resources (Bowman et al., 1983), developing an information architecture (Goodhue et al., 1988, 1992), and increasing the visibility of information systems (McLean and Soden, 1977).

The level of existing consensus about the goals and objectives of strategic information systems planning also influence planning effectiveness (Sambamurthy et al., 1994). IT planning groups characterized by low levels of initial consensus over the goals, priorities and preferences relative to organizational use of IT face increased pressures to reach agreement. Thus, they require planning processes that support bargaining, negotiation, and integrative problem solving (Ruohonen, 1991; Sambamurthy et al., 1994).

Although these findings—dealing with culture, style, size, structure, sophistication, and goals—may appear diverse, they are generally tied to a common theme, namely that greater predictability and simplicity in the internal environment appear to promote strategic information systems planning. These findings thus suggest the following hypothesis:

H2: A simpler internal environment produces a more effective and efficient planning process.

## 7. The effect of planning resources on the planning process

A major resource required for strategic information systems planning is the time and effort of managers and other professionals who participate in the planning process (Lederer and Gardiner, 1992). Their presence can have a favorable effect on strategic information systems planning (PR->PP). Top management participation has been associated with more successful strategic information systems planning (McLean and Soden, 1977; Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1988; Premkumar and King, 1994b; Reponen, 1994). Likewise, the participation of user management (Reponen, 1994) and information systems managers and professionals (Lederer and Sethi, 1992; Reponen, 1994) can greatly facilitate the planning process. Consultants are another human resource used in strategic information systems planning (Holland Systems Corp., 1989).

The participation of these people is necessary for successful planning but not sufficient. Their skills and experiences are also important to the success of strategic information systems planning (Vitale et al., 1986; Sambamurthy et al., 1993; Lederer and Gardiner, 1992).

Another important resource is software. Software can help manage the planning process (Lederer and Mahaney, forthcoming).

## 8. The effect of the planning process on the plan

A typical strategic information systems planning process follows a series of well-defined steps. Nevertheless, this process may differ substantially from one organization to another. Some organizations might choose to select or omit some particular steps or the tasks within them. Other organizations might choose to emphasize certain steps or tasks by applying resources to them more diligently. Some organizations might choose to complete their planning study more rapidly than others. These choices ultimately determine the information plan (PP -> IP).

Other formal strategic information systems planning methodologies are fairly similar to this one and thus offer planners a number of steps and tasks from which to tailor their own planning process.

Typical activities comprising the tasks are meetings, interviews, and the analysis of documents. In addition, organizations may use team exercises, lectures, and reports from experts (Earl, 1990; Galliers et al., 1994; Reponen, 1994). As a result of these activities, strategic information systems planners prepare extensive text as well as a variety of tables, diagrams, and charts. In fact, many observers have offered a number of prescriptions about how to carry out these tasks and thus perform a strategic information systems planning study more effectively (Bowman et al., 1983; Boynton and Zmud, 1987; Brancheau et al., 1989; Conrath et al., 1992; Errico et al., 1993; Galliers, 1987; Goodhue et al., 1992; Gupta and Raghunathan, 1989; Hoffer et al., 1989; Karimi, 1988; Lederer and Sethi, forthcoming). It is thus not surprising that a planning process of higher quality produces a plan of higher quality (Premkumar and King, 1991). At the same time, insufficient attention to the appropriate issues during planning result in a plan that proposes technical solutions to the wrong business problems (Byrd et al., 1995).

Some observers have contrasted comprehensive planning with incremental planning (Fredrickson, 1984; Fredrickson and Mitchell, 1984; Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978a; Sambamurthy et al., 1994; McLean and Soden, 1977; Pyburn, 1983; Guimares and McKeen, 1989; Earl, 1993). In general, these studies have suggested that a more comprehensive planning approach produces a more useful plan.

Hence, the following hypothesis is put forth:

H4: A more comprehensive planning process produces a more useful information plan.

## 9. The effect of the information plan on implementation

The output of the strategic information systems planning process is an information plan. The contents of the plan vary depending on the processes carried out during planning (PP -> IP). A typical plan includes technical, competitive, business, and organizational information about current information systems and the recommended strategic information systems strategy (Lederer and Gardiner, 1992). It includes the analysis explaining the rationale for its recommendations. None of the sections of a plan is unimportant but sections focusing on change management (i.e. how to avoid resistance) have become increasingly important (Andersen Consulting, 1989).

The information plan itself affects the implementation of its recommendations (IP -> PI). Organizations often fail to implement the plan and they consider this a serious problem (Lederer and Sethi, 1988; Earl, 1993; Lederer and Mendelow, 1993). Thus, the implementation of a greater number of projects is a desirable outcome of strategic information systems planning (Raghunathan and King, 1988; Wilson, 1989; Premkumar and King, 1994b). 'Implementation mechanisms' such as resource plans for project implementation, and systems for monitoring the plan implementation, as well as top management and user participation in this monitoring improve the chances of implementation (Premkumar and King, 1994b, p. 90). The extent of plan implementation is indicated by the existence of clear project goals, the degree of formal evaluation of progress, and the sufficiency of resources allocated to meeting the plan's goals (Raghunathan and King, 1988, p. 87).

Researchers have emphasized the importance of the relevance of proposed projects in the plan to organization goals (King, 1988; Bergeron et al., 1991) and that the presence of this relevance increases the likelihood that the organization will implement more of the recommendations of the plan. Likewise, the sections of the plan are important (Wilson, 1989; Flynn and Goleniewska, 1993) and their presence or absence can influence plan implementation. The clarity and analysis of presentation of the plan can also improve the chances of adopting a greater number of its recommendations (McFarlan, 1971). These findings thus suggest the following hypothesis:

H5: A more useful information plan produces greater plan implementation.

## 10. The effect of plan implementation on alignment

The purpose of information systems—and thus the purpose of strategic information systems planning for them—is to affect the organization favorably (Powell, 1993). One indication of such a favorable effect is that implemented projects fit organization's objectives (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978a; King, 1988; Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1991, 1994; Venkatraman, et al., 1993; Zviran, 1990; Chan, 1992).

Such a fit is often referred to as alignment. Alignment is the degree to which the information systems plan reflects the business plan (King, 1978). It can be measured as the number or quality of the recommendations implemented from the strategic information systems plan that also appear in a strategic business plan. Alignment can thus be seen as the degree to which the planning process resulted in strategic information systems.

In a sense, alignment represents the fulfillment of goals. The fulfillment of goals has been used to measure strategic information systems planning effectiveness (Premkumar and King, 1992; Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1994).

Although alignment is the preferred, final dependent variable, other ways to assess strategic information systems planning have been used. For example, the implementation of the information plan is intended to achieve organizational impact in the form of competitive advantage, improved productivity, increased sales, reduced costs, and/or greater profits (Wiseman, 1985). Such impact could definitely be a worthwhile dependent variable. However, the success of the organization is contingent upon more than its information systems and this complicates the assessment of information planning success (Fitzgerald, 1993). Hence such variables may not demonstrate the effects of strategic information systems planning.

Planner satisfaction is often used as a surrogate measure of the success of strategic information systems planning and does to some extent reflect its impact on the organization (McLean and Soden, 1977; Lederer and Sethi, 1988; Flynn and Goleniewska, 1993). However, satisfaction can be biased.

Thus the theory employs alignment as the preferred, final dependent variable while still recognizing that others have merit. It relies on the evidence suggesting that by implementing projects according to comprehensive strategic plans, organizations validate the usefulness of the plan and should thus achieve better alignment. Thus this is the final hypothesis:

H6: Greater plan implementation produces better alignment.

## 11. The theory embellished

This theory presents each hypothesis as portraying a simple, predictable, direct effect of one construct on another. It does so in the belief that it most faithfully represents planning and in the anticipation that researchers gain the most by testing it rather than alternative theories. However, in their efforts to test it, researchers might also benefit by considering the possibility of testing indirect and contingency effects.

Specifically, it may be feasible for researchers to test the relationships among constructs whose effects take place through other constructs. As one example, researchers might test the effect of the external environment on alignment. As another example, they might test the effect of the planning process on alignment. The rationale for such testing would include the greater simplicity of doing so and the possibility of at least some contribution to the support or refutation of the theory.

Likewise, it may be feasible for researchers to incorporate contingency effects in their studies. The fact that a theory may hold in general but not in specific situations (referred to by Dubin as the power paradox) may motivate the study of the conditions under which it is and is not supported.

In strategic information systems planning research, a few contingencies have been proposed to explain such exceptions. For example, some planning practices may be more effective in different environmental contexts (Earl, 1993; Galliers, 1987). Likewise, different planning activities—such as those within comprehensive versus incremental approaches—may be more successful depending on the instability in the organization's external environment (Salmela, 1996).

In fact, a variety of related hypotheses—based on contingency—may eventually flow from the theory.

## 12. Conclusion

The theory of strategic information planning primarily benefits researchers. Its contribution is as a new means to help them study strategic information systems planning and present their findings in an organized, comprehensive, parsimonious, and meaningful manner. One study can follow another and build a cumulative collection of findings about the antecedents that have presaged successful strategic information systems planning.

However, the theory also helps practitioners by giving them a framework for evaluating strategic information systems planning. For example, they can assess the constructs in their own organizations. They can establish their goals and objectives for their planning inputs, planning process, information plan, plan implementation and alignment and then assess the relationships between these constructs in their own organization. Despite the absence of comprehensive, well-organized research on the theory, they can attempt to improve the results of their strategic information systems planning by manipulating the constructs and relationships in the theory.

Strategic information systems planning is thus an important target for research and an important challenge for practitioners. Much is known about it but much more remains to be learned. The theory proposed herein can serve as an impetus to researchers and practitioners to understand and practice it better.
