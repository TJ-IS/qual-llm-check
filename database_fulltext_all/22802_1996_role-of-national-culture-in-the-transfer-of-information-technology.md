# Role of national culture in the transfer of information technology

Barry Shore\*, A.R. Venkatachalam

Department of Decision Sciences, The Whittemore School of Business and Economics, University of New Hampshire, Durham, NH 03824-3593, USA

Revised paper accepted for publication by Professor Don McCubbrey, November 1995

## Abstract

Implementing an information system developed in one national culture for use in another national culture may present its own set of unique problems. This paper explores the role of national culture and other intervening variables including the competitive environment and task congruency in the process of implementing an application across cultures. A general framework is developed and three case studies, one taken from the literature and two undertaken by the authors, are used to validate this framework.

Keywords: IT transfer; Culture; Competitive environment

## 1. Introduction

An increasing number of information systems (IS) applications are implemented across national and cultural boundaries. During this transfer process many of these applications encounter problems which can be attributed to the differences between the national cultures of the headquarters and host organizations (Roche, 1992; Deans and Ricks, 1993). While several studies have focused on the implementation problems associated with the transfer of technology (Babington, 1987; Janczewski, 1992; Verstraete, 1992) the development of a broader and integrative framework, which directly addresses culture's role in the transfer process, has been neglected (Nasif et al., 1991; Palvia, 1993).

There are many factors which influence the transfer process and may need to be included in such a framework. Kedia and Bhagat (1988), studying culture's role, suggest four factors which may constrain this process including: the characteristics of the technology involved, societal culture-based differences, organizational culture differences, and the absorptive capacity of recipient organizations. Alavi and Joachimsthaler (1992), reviewing the literature on implementation, suggest three contextual variables which influence implementation including: task type or task complexity, organizational factors, and external factors such as competitive considerations. Ein-Dor et al. (1993), focusing directly on information systems in a global context, suggest that several variables affect information systems including: the environment, the physical structure of IS, the behavioral components of executives, users and implementors, and procedural methods which include IS processes. Vozakis et al. (1992), studying transfer of information technology across countries, found several factors which influence this process including: market, behavioral and cultural, technical, financial, government, and intangible factors. Drawing from these studies we suggest that when IS applications are transferred across cultures, the transfer process is influenced by three factors: (1) the national cultures of the organizations (headquarters and host); (2) the competitive environment; and (3) the difference between the host organization's old and new methods, which we will call task congruency.

The purpose of this paper is to develop a simple but useful framework which illustrates how these three factors interact during the transfer process. In the following section, a brief justification for choosing these three factors is presented. The three factors are then described and their influence on the information technology (IT) transfer process discussed. Next, a model depicting the interaction among the factors is developed and three cases used to validate the model. Finally, the implications of this study are discussed, and a summary of the research presented.

## 2. Justification of factors

## 2.1. National culture

National culture has been defined in many ways. Hofstede (1980) defines culture as the collective programming of the mind that distinguishes one group from another. Parsons and Shils (1951) define it as shared characteristic of a high-level social system. It can also be defined as the shared values of a particular group of people (Erez and Earley, 1993). According to Lachman (1983) and Triandis (1995), national culture reflects the core values and beliefs of individuals formed during childhood and reinforced throughout life.

Hofstede (1980) contends that national culture is an important issue in management theory and indeed national culture has been identified as an important variable in many global studies. Kedia and Bhagat (1988) suggest that variations in culture at both the national and organizational levels are considered two major factors in the transfer of technology. Janczewski (1992) suggests that cultural differences found in African countries requires that information systems be modified to suit the needs of host organizations in that country. Shore and Venkatachalam (1995) establish the role of culture in systems analysis and design.

Since national culture influences the core values formed early in life, differences among organizational cultures within a country may be limited to the organization's influence over peripheral values (Erez and Earley, 1993). Accordingly, in this study we emphasize national culture's role in the IT transfer process, while acknowledging that organizational culture may play an intervening role in this process.

## 2.2. Competitive environment

There are several linkages between the competitive environment and IT performance. The competitive environment influences the firms' competitive strategy and structure. This, in turn, influences IT strategy and structure, which then shapes IT adoption strategies. Finally, IT adoption decisions affect the performance of the organization. Research in global IS has addressed several of these linkages. Lederer and Mendelow (1990) suggest a link between the competitive environment and the adoption of IT. Zahra et al. (1994) addressed the link between the competitive environment and information technology. Ginsberg and Venkatraman (1992) studied the link between the competitive structure of the firm and IT adoption. Tavakolian (1989) studied the way in which the competitive strategy of the firm influences its information system architecture. Floyd and Wooldridge (1990), in a study of 127 banks, found that the competitive strategies of organizations have a significant effect on IT adoption. While most of these studies do not take a global perspective, Ein-Dor et al. (1993) directly implicate the role of the competitive environment as an important variable in global IS research. In summary, the literature strongly supports the linkage between the competitive environment and IT strategy and adoption. We suggest that this influence extends to the implementation process as well.

## 2.3. Task congruency

Several researchers have established the role of tasks in information systems and the role these tasks play in the transfer of applications across cultures. Gorry and Scott-Morton (1971) identify three application levels in the information hierarchy: operational planning and control, management planning and control information, and strategic planning. They contend that tasks in each level differ by such dimensions as structure, time horizon, frequency with which they are undertaken, and level of management involvement.

Tasks are also implicated in McFarlan's portfolio approach to information systems (McFarlan, 1981). He contends that project risk is related to project size, experience with the technology, and project structure. Both project size and project structure relate directly to the concept of task. Project structure, in his framework, can range from low to high structured projects. The higher the structure, the lower the degree of project risk.

Of the four factors which Kedia and Bhagat (1988) identify as instrumental in the transfer of technology, ‘the characteristics of the technology’ is the most closely related to the concept of task. Indeed, technology embodies tasks, and therefore tasks again emerge, although indirectly, as an important factor. Ein-Dor et al.

(1993) suggest the importance of IT processes in their framework of global IT research. Since IT processes also embody tasks, it is not unreasonable to conclude that they too acknowledge the role of tasks as a global IT issue.

In summary, the literature suggests that the nature of the task is an important variable in the IT and global IT process: tasks change as the applications migrate up the information hierarchy; tasks affect the risk of a project; and tasks are implicated in the transfer of information technology across cultures.

We suggest that a more appropriate representation of task in the IT transfer process may be ‘task congruency’, which we define as the difference between current and proposed methods and procedures. It is one of the attributes used by McFarlan in distinguishing between high and low structure projects. Task congruency, therefore, not only acknowledges the way in which the complexities of the tasks change, but it emphasizes the problems which may surface as an old system is replaced by a new one.

## 3. Description of factors and their influence on IT transfer

## 3.1. National culture

Several models of national culture have been suggested in the literature. These include: Parsons and Shils (1951); Kluckhohn and Strodtbeck (1961); Rokeach (1973); McClelland (1961); Hofstede (1980); and Glenn and Glenn (1981). Hofstede's model (1980) identifies four dimensions by which national cultures differ. From his analysis of 116 000 questionnaires he found they differed by: power distance, uncertainty avoidance, individualism–collectivism, and masculinity–femininity. The data compared beliefs and values of employees from a multinational corporation with operations in 40 countries. His study concludes that these dimensions are valid measures of cultural differences among these countries.

Power distance, according to Hofstede, is the extent to which less powerful members of organizations accept that power is distributed unequally. Uncertainty avoidance is the extent to which a society feels threatened by uncertain situations and avoids these situations by providing career stability, establishing formal rules, and not tolerating deviant ideas. Individualism–collectivism suggests a loosely knit social fabric in which people take care of themselves contrasted with a social fabric in which groups take care of the individual in exchange for his or her loyalty. Masculinity–feminity reflects whether the dominant values are associated with the collection of money and things (masculine) contrasted with values associated with the caring for other and the quality of life (feminine).

Of these four dimensions, power distance (PD) and uncertainty avoidance (UA) are emphasized by Hofstede (1991) in studying organizations within a particular culture. The empirical evidence suggests that a country's PD-UA score best reflects the way decision-making power in organizations is distributed, rules and methods followed, and uncertainty accepted. The influence of PD and UA on the management of organizations is further confirmed by the Aston studies (Pugh and Hickson, 1976). Accordingly, we emphasize the same two dimensions in this study.

Hofstede's model has been widely used in management research. For example, Kedia and Bhagat (1988) used his four dimensions in the development of their conceptual model of technology transfer. Tricker (1988) considered two dimensions of Hofstede, namely, power distance and individualism, to study information resource management in China. Other works using Hofstede's model include: Morris et al. (1994) in their study of corporate entrepreneurship; Kogut and Singh (1988) in their model for selecting entry modes across cultures; Ueno and Sekaran (1992) in their empirical study of budget control practices in the USA and Japan; Shane (1994) in his study of licensing and direct foreign investment; Rodriguez (1991) in a study of leadership styles in different organizations; and Sekaran and Snodgrass (1986) in their study of networks and their configurations. The popularity of Hofstede's model in management research can be attributed to several factors: its large sample size, codification of cultural attributes using numerical indices and emphasis on attitudes in the workplace (Kogut and Singh, 1988; Erez and Earley, 1993). However, Hofstede's model has been criticized in at least two areas: one, the model is based on, and specific to, a single organization (IBM) and hence raises the question of generalizability; and two, the four dimensions used in the study are not sufficient enough to study all aspects of cultural differences. Addressing the first criticism, Erez and Earley (1993) argue that national culture reflects core values and beliefs and dominates organizational influence which can at best affect an individual's peripheral values and beliefs. Using this argument it is reasonable to suggest that Hofstede's findings can be generalized to other organizations in the world. To address the second criticism, Hofstede introduced a fifth dimension, namely, long-term vs. short-term orientation, in his later studies (Hofstede, 1991). Notwithstanding its limitations, we suggest that Hofstede's research can provide a workable context within which to describe the nature of the problems which may occur as the transfer of information technology unfolds. In particular, we suggest that considerable insight into the transfer process can be gained by exploring the way in which power distance and uncertainty avoidance may affect the introduction of a new application. Four situations will be considered including the transfer of applications to cultures with high and low power distance and high and low uncertainty avoidance.

## 3.1.1. High power distance cultures

In high power distance cultures, individuals with status or position in the hierarchy wield considerable power: lines of authority and responsibility are clearly defined. When an application introduces new methods and procedures, these relationships are often challenged. This may occur as a new information system provides end-users with access to previously guarded and inaccessible information; it may occur as new lines of authority are established through new channels of electronic communication such as e-mail; or it may occur as decision-making authority, previously reserved for those with more status, is transferred to end-users with less status (Davenport et al., 1992).

Not only may this change be difficult to accommodate for many of those in authority roles, but it may also be difficult for subordinates. Subordinates, or end-users, within these cultures have been culturally conditioned to accommodate the direction and control of superiors. Applications which impose new work practices and require independent actions may directly conflict with core cultural values. As a result, subordinates may be as culturally unprepared to accept new authority relationships as their superiors.

Within high power distance cultures there may be a need to please those in authority. Consequently, as new applications are introduced, there may be a tendency for management in the host country to report that implementation problems are minor and that the project is under control. But closer examination may suggest a significant conflict between headquarters and the host. Management at the host organization may feel compelled to please headquarters, but at the same time may feel constrained by its own cultural values which may be acting to obstruct, often indirectly and unconsciously, the transfer process.

## 3.1.2. Low power distance cultures

Consider the introduction of a new application to an organization in a low power distance culture. While transfer still requires the approval and support of top management, the difference in low power distance cultures is that end-users can be expected to play a very different role than in high power distance cultures. Low power distance suggests that users may now wield significant influence in the transfer process. They may feel entitled to express their views on the new application, not only to host management but to headquarters as well. Accordingly, users may be instrumental in the systems' acceptance, rejection and critical evaluation.

Low power distance may also suggest that certain methods or procedures, with which the host organization is familiar, may have to be accommodated to win management and user support. Accordingly, it is useful if some dimensions of the application can be tailored to the user environment. The concept of thinking globally but acting locally takes on its own meaning in this context.

## 3.1.3. High uncertainty avoidance cultures

When culture is characterized by high uncertainty avoidance, the introduction of new technology will raise the anxiety level of its host implementors, top managers and end-users. They will show concern over the uncertainty of new methods and procedures associated with the new application and will be particularly resistant to the abandonment of systems with which they are familiar and feel secure. The way in which this anxiety is expressed will vary: criticism of headquarters, complaints about the new system, absenteeism, sabotage of new methods and procedures, and an increase in friction between management and labor in the host organization.

To some extent the anxiety experienced by top management may be reduced if they are made aware that this technology has been successfully used elsewhere. This, for example, may help explain why the Japanese have been characterized in the past as imitators, not innovators and have waited for technology to be tried in other countries before it is adopted or further refined for use in their own organizations (Vogel, 1979; Magaziner and Hout, 1980).

## 3.1.4. Low uncertainty avoidance cultures

In cultures characterized by low uncertainty avoidance there is a somewhat greater willingness to take risks associated with new methods and procedures. While it is never easy to make the transition – there is considerable literature devoted to resistance to change issues (Blake, 1992; Coghlan, 1993) – low uncertainty avoidance cultures are somewhat more tolerable of the uncertainty surrounding change than high uncertainty avoidance cultures. These cultures, for example, may be more willing to try new information technology before it has been proven in other organizations.

## 3.2. Competitive environment

Information systems play a significant role in the development of a firm's competitive strategy (Wiseman, 1988; Neumann, 1994). Porter's competitive forces framework (1980, 1986) is perhaps the most widely used (Sundaram and Black, 1992), although not without criticism (Neumann, 1994). Porter suggests that the forces driving industry competition include: the rivalry among firms, the threat of substitute products, the bargaining power of customers, the threat of new entrants, and the bargaining power of suppliers.

Rivalry among firms is the jockeying for position using such tactics as price competition, advertising battles, product introductions and increased customer service or warranties. All firms are threatened by the substitute products sold by competitors. The more attractive the price-performance of the substitute product the greater is the sense of competition. Buyers affect the competitive nature of the industry by: forcing prices lower, bargaining for higher quality or more services, and playing competitors against each other. Suppliers affect competition within the industry by threatening to raise prices or reducing the quality of purchased goods and services. New entrants to the market can bring new resources and capacity as well as redistribute market share.

Collectively, these forces affect the competitive nature of the marketplace. In this paper we will classify the competitive environment as high or low. While a classification into two categories is somewhat arbitrary, we feel it is a useful construct for the development of a manageable framework of IS transfer. In some industries where competition is high, pressure is exerted through all five forces. For example, in the manufacture of computers: the rivalry among firms is high; the threat of substitute products is high; the threat of potential entrants is high; and the bargaining power of buyers is considerable. In other industries, for example, telecommunications monopolies in Western Europe (Janczewski and Targowski, 1994), the competitive environment can be classified as low because: there is little rivalry among firms; there are few substitute products (wireless); potential entrants (until recently in most countries) have been limited; and buyers have little bargaining power.

How does the competitive environment affect IS transfer? A firm's global business strategy and organizational structure are responses to its competitive environment (Porter, 1986; Bartlett and Ghoshal, 1989). Cheung and Burn (1994) suggest that most organizations can be placed on a continuum between Global Multi National Corporation (MNC) and Multi Domestic MNC, and that information strategy within these organizations reflect its organizational structure. A Global MNC is a highly centralized form of organization with very little, if any, attempt to adjust products, work methods, and procedures to the local country in which its products or services are manufactured or distributed. Decisions are made centrally, while manufacturing and distribution organizations throughout the world are treated equally. The Multi Domestic MNC, on the other hand, is organized to respond to differences in host countries. It often behaves as a national organization within each country.

## 3.2.1. Global MNC

Airline companies are examples of Global MNCs that have developed information systems and worldwide customer databases which support their organization structure. Because they operate in a highly competitive market, and methods and procedures have been carefully developed and integrated, there is little room for these practices to vary by country. Information must be gathered, stored, distributed, and presented in such a way as to ensure the integrity and effectiveness of the global network. There is little room for accommodation to local culture. When a new airline office is opened in any corner of the globe, standard methods and procedures must be transferred to the new host organization. Users in this context are allowed few degrees of freedom.

We therefore conclude that when the international business segment within which the firm operates is highly competitive, economic risk may dominate business strategy and national culture may be placed in the background. In general, the emphasis will be placed on those factors which address economic risk, and it is within this context that task dominates culture.

Accordingly, the transfer process for many Global MNCs, such as airlines, can be expected to focus on technical issues associated with bringing the worldwide database into operation. Furthermore, training is directed at ensuring that a clearly defined set of methods and procedures are followed by the host organization.

## 3.2.2. Multi Domestic MNC

A Multi Domestic MNC, selling its products or services in local markets, responds differently to a competitive environment. Kentucky Fried Chicken has varied its recipe for chicken to accommodate local taste in Asia, while McDonald's has added gelato to its menu in Italy. Information systems, like the products or services they are developed to support, may also vary within limited constraints. For example, transaction procedures might accommodate authority relationships (power distance issues), but database design and management reporting might be standardized to accommodate the need by central headquarters to exert control over global quality and delivery issues. In a Multi Domestic MNC we would therefore expect that highly competitive local environments may require some customization of the information system. We would expect that the flexible nature of the headquarters' organizational strategy would also accommodate a flexible implementation strategy and that this strategy would take into consideration core values which, if left ignored, may interfere with the operation of the information system.

While the examples given for both the Global MNC and Multi Domestic MNC represent organizations in highly competitive markets, the transfer process may differ considerably when the organization is in a market characterized by low competitive pressures. Culture within this scenario may increase in significance to play a major role in the transfer of technology. In one large multinational corporation studied by the authors, the host organizations were encouraged, but not compelled, to adopt common computing systems. As a result, about one-third of the host organizations around the world encountered severe obstacles in the process of adopting these applications; another one-third found it impossible to even begin. Lack of competitive pressures failed to impose a sense of urgency on the transfer process. Core and peripheral values, while challenged through ‘encouragement’, were not dominated by economic risk.

## 3.3. Task congruency

Task congruency may also introduce conflict during transfer and implementation. To keep the framework manageable, we suggest that congruency be classified as high or low. High task congruency occurs, for example, when the old and new systems are similar. Consider an old general ledger accounting system about to be replaced by one using similar methods and procedures but differing only in the introduction of similar but improved technology. Or consider a software package about to be upgraded with a recent revision. In both situations the task congruency factor would be high. But, if the introduction of a new accounting system substantially changes the methods and procedures previously used and introduces more complex technology, then the congruency factor would be low.

How does task congruency affect IT transfer? We suggest an inverse relationship. As congruency decreases, national culture's role can be expected to increase. Consider a manual system replaced by a computer system requiring users to accept not only new technology but also a change in the methods and procedures with which they are familiar and comfortable. These new methods and procedures may introduce conflict during implementation, the source of which may be tied to core and peripheral values. Consequently, in this example of low task congruency, national culture may contribute to the conflict that prevents effective IS transfer.

If the transfer imposes few changes in methods and procedures (high task congruency), then cultural issues are less likely to emerge. For example, the shift from a manual reservation system to an automated one, which follows the same methods and procedures, would not likely force cultural issues since authority relationships and social systems may be unaffected. Nonetheless, even this change may experience ‘resistance to change’ common to all cultures.

## 4. Interaction among factors

It has been suggested that both task congruency and the competitive environment may influence the nature of the conflict which national culture may impose during the transfer process. Table 1, which we call a Cultural Conflict Matrix, suggests the level or magnitude of these conflicts. When both the task congruency and competitive environment is high we suggest that the conflict introduced by the difference between headquarters and host national cultures on the IS transfer process will be low. Consider the transfer of an inventory record keeping system by a Global MNC in a highly competitive business environment. If the old methods and procedures followed by the host organization are similar to the methods and procedures associated with the new system, and competitive conditions demand that this system be followed, then we would expect national culture to be under considerable pressure to yield to the few changes that must be made.

In the next category, task congruency is high and competitive environment is low. We suggest that the conflict introduced by the differences in national cultures will be moderate. For example, consider the transfer of an accounting application for a Multi Domestic MNC. The methods and procedures required by the new system are not substantially different from the ones which the host organization is following. Furthermore, the competitive environment is characterized as low. In this situation it is the lack of competitive pressures that provide the 'slack' for cultural values to be expressed, but the lack of significant changes in methods and procedures minimizes the potential cultural conflict.

When the congruency factor is low and the competitive environment is high, we suggest that the conflict introduced by differences in culture will be high. This situation would prevail, for example, when a Global MNC, operating in a highly competitive market, transfers a manufacturing scheduling system which is very different from the system previously used by the host company. In this scenario, the host organization will be under great pressure to adopt unfamiliar methods and procedures in a competitive environment that demands compliance. Implementation problems can be expected as host cultural values are threatened.

When both congruency and the competitive environment are low, the potential conflict introduced by differences in national culture can be very high. For example, consider an international company opening a sales office. If its product is in high demand and there are few competitors, and if the systems which the company wishes to introduce are very different from the methods and procedures with which end-users are familiar, a very high level of conflict is likely to prevail. In this scenario ‘slack’ is high since competitive pressures are weak. It will be the existence of this slack that makes it possible for problems to surface as management and en-users resist unfamiliar methods and procedures.

## 5. Examples

## 5.1. Accounting application in the former Soviet Union

Verstraete (1992), studying information systems in the former Soviet Union, concluded that the transfer of accounting and manufacturing systems to this region was difficult at best. He found an extreme emphasis on centrality, a result consistent with the findings of Hofstede (1991), in which Russia ranked highest of all countries in power distance. Verstraete suggested that information systems in this cultural context threatened firmly entrenched bureaucracies and set into motion a process which weakened management's control over its subordinates. This is in contrast to the use of computers in the West which often enhances individual autonomy at the expense of centrality.

In addition to management style, several other factors, some of which may be related to high power distance, impeded the effective transfer of IS technology. He discovered the presence of underground economies, official and unofficial systems, avoidance of documentation, and even manipulation of data to support beliefs. For example, Verstraete reports that manufacturers in much of Eastern Europe count all productive output as earned revenues, even though the products may not actually be sold. Indeed, the manipulation of data and even the avoidance of documentation may not be unexpected in high uncertainty avoidance cultures. Russia did rank second highest in uncertainty avoidance (Hofstede, 1991), suggesting a core need to avoid uncertainty and be in control even if that control jeopardized downstream activities.

Verstraete's findings tend to verify the influence of culture predicted by the Cultural Conflict Matrix. First, the competitive environment in the Soviet Union at that time, in the absence of a market economy, can be classified as low. Centralized organizations established 'demand'. There were no market mechanisms to clear demand and supply imbalances, and competitive products or services were virtually nonexistent. Second, the congruency of the application, while not stated explicitly in the study, would be low, since it was made clear that new methods and procedures (the abandonment of unofficial for official systems, as an example) would be imposed. According to Table 1 this suggests that the influence of national culture on IS transfer would be expected to be very high. Not only will core values dominate in such an environment, but it may be difficult to change even peripheral values, those learned later in life and easiest to change. Indeed this is what led Verstraete to conclude that the introduction of IS technology in Eastern Europe would be slow and difficult.

Two additional studies undertaken by the authors also support culture's influence as suggested in the Cultural Conflict Matrix. These studies include Roberts Express Europe, and a medical electronics manufacturer in Western Europe, whose identity needs to be protected.

## 5.2. Physical distribution in Western Europe

Roberts Express Europe (REE), located in the southern region of the Netherlands, was the first company in Europe to enter the overnight emergency shipment market.

This market is different from the overnight market, dominated by such companies as Federal Express. Unlike Federal Express, REE assigns a dedicated vehicle to pick up, transport, and deliver a shipment between any two points in Western Europe. Pick-up and delivery times are guaranteed and the price charged is significantly discounted if REE fails to meet its commitment.

The trucking industry in Europe is characterized by over capacity and low profit margins. To compete in this sector, REE was compelled to develop efficient business and transportation systems, relying heavily on IS technology. Since the organization would initially serve 12 countries in the European Union, information systems were developed to accommodate differences in currencies, languages, laws, customs duties, accounting and tax systems. From the beginning of the development of their business plan it was clear that strategies would have to directly address the intense competition within this region. Accordingly, if we return to the Cultural Conflict Matrix this application falls in the category of high competitive environment.

Placing this application in the task congruency column is more difficult. REE started their European operation from scratch and built a central European headquarters in the southern region of the Netherlands. They then established contracts with established carriers within the European Union countries served. These independent contractors would allocate a specific percentage of their fleet to REE and these vehicles would not be used to serve other customers.

Drivers were already experienced with dispatching procedures as well as the laws and customs which governed their right to serve customers in their region. Under the agreement with REE, they would drive their own trucks and adhere to many of the same methods and procedures with which they were familiar. The major change was not necessarily related to the routine which drivers followed, but to the methods and procedures followed at headquarters in the Netherlands. Indeed there were some new procedures required of the drivers, but they were not considered to be substantial changes. Accordingly, we might conclude that the congruency factor was high.

Returning to the Cultural Conflict Matrix, we find that when the competitive environment and task congruency are both high, culture's influence of IS transfer is expected to be low. Indeed this was the case. Little resistance from drivers was encountered. In a short period of time, drivers were trained and the system was in operation. Perhaps the competitive environment dominated. With unemployment high in many of these countries, the drivers felt fortunate to have jobs, and were quite cooperative in putting the new system into operation. Consequently we can conclude that within this context, technology, through the intermediary factors of a strong competitive environment and a reasonable level of task congruency, dominated culture.

## 5.3. Medical electronics manufacturing in Western Europe

A second study by the authors explored the experience of a Multi Domestic MNC located in the Netherlands. The global organization competes in the moderately competitive environment of medical electronics. Profit margins are high, and the firm captures approximately 50 per cent of the world market for its highly sophisticated medical devices.

Global headquarters, located in the USA, had relied on its European headquarters operation to first adopt a common manufacturing planning and control system, already operational in the USA, and then to transfer it to other manufacturing operations in France and Germany. The introduction of this system to the Dutch facility met some resistance, but became operational in a short period of time. The Dutch plant then began the task of transferring this application to facilities in France and Germany, but these efforts met with stronger resistance. The German operation was not convinced that the new application showed an improvement over the manufacturing control systems which they had already put into place. Those in the French plant were less willing to consider a system to which they did not contribute. The transfer project failed. After many months the data needed by headquarters were still sent from France and Germany to the Netherlands where it was entered into the European database before it could be accessed by US headquarters. After many months there was still little hope that a cooperative solution would be found. The director of MIS in the Dutch plant tried to explain this failure. He expressed concern over the differences in the way in which individuals in other countries responded to new ideas. “These people just do things very differently”, he explained, suggesting that culture could help explain why his job was so difficult.

The Culture Conflict Matrix provides some insight into this problem. The congruency factor in this transfer was clearly low. Methods and procedures used in the USA were different from those used elsewhere. Even the methods and procedures among Western European operations were different. The competitive environment in this situation is not nearly as high as in the Roberts Express case study. Nonetheless, it seems reasonable, especially after discussing the issue with Dutch managers, to classify it as high. With task congruency low and competition high, one would expect the influence of national culture on the transfer of this application to be high. Indeed this is not out of step with what was observed. The operation in the Netherlands eventually adopted many of the changes required by the common system, but it was more difficult for the plants in the other countries to cooperate. This suggests that while culture may not have played as strong an influence as in the Soviet Union, it still contributed to the problems that prevented a successful implementation.

In analyzing this and the previous two cases it may still be compelling to suggest that culture is not really a part of the puzzle. It may be more concrete to point a finger at such behaviors as: resistance to change, low rates of absorption, lack of ownership, unskilled workforce, uncooperative host management, or even inadequate infrastructure. While these factors may indeed contribute to the problem, we suggest that culture too is an important factor. Core beliefs may prove to be significant impediments in IS transfer, especially if these beliefs are challenged in the reengineering of tasks.

## 6. Implications

If national culture is indeed an important piece of the information technology transfer puzzle, then we suggest that direct consideration of cultural values must become part of the transfer process. This may require customization of the IS technology itself or long term training to make the transfer problem less overwhelming. Hofstede (1980) contends that culture is difficult to change and that the pace of change is very slow. Therefore it seems reasonable that any approach in bridging this gap must be undertaken with a culturally sensitive strategy and executed with patience.

But it seems reasonable to suggest that IT transfer strategy also depends upon the organizational strategy and structure of the multinational organization. The ability of a global MNC to accommodate culture may be very different from a Multi Domestic MNC. Multi Domestic MNC's have a greater degree of freedom to adapt and customize the IS technology to the host organization.

Verstraete (1992) suggests that the transfer of IT technology to Eastern Europe requires that technology be adapted to fit the organizations it will support. Accordingly, it seems reasonable to conclude that when organizational structures permit, the influence of the host national culture must be accommodated. While this may be possible for a Multi Domestic MNC, it may be more problematical for a Global MNC. Indeed, the host organization, in this situation, may have to do the adapting. To facilitate this implementation process, Shore and Venkatachalam (1994) suggest a series of staged prototypes that move the end-users on a carefully managed path from the existing application to the new one. This approach, sensitive to the cultural issues which need to be addressed, is called ‘cultural prototyping’ and minimizes both ‘cultural’ and business risk in contrast to conventional prototyping which minimizes business risk alone. Supporting this gradual implementation strategy, Ives and Jarvenpaa (1991) found that some firms engage in a gradual roll-out of their worldwide systems. They give an example of a US firm modifying its systems to meet European subsidiaries first, then adding Far Eastern, and finally South American subsidiaries. This gradual roll-out suggests that cultural differences are part of the transfer puzzle.

In summary, the implication of this study is that, regardless of organizational structure, national culture may be an important factor in the transfer process and needs to become an explicit part of the IT transfer plan.

## 7. Conclusion

While a considerable body of work has been developed around the management of information technology in multinational corporations, a review of the literature suggests that very little work has been done linking national culture with theories of information systems transfer.

Culture's role, however, is dependent on several factors. We suggest that the competitive environment as well as the nature of the task itself affects the impact that cultural values will have over the transfer process. When a new application requires few changes at the host organization and when the competitive environment is very strong, then task may dominate culture; culture's impact will be minimal. But as the competitive environment weakens, and as the changes in the methods and procedures increase, we suggest that the influence of national culture on IT transfer becomes very high. At this extreme, culture dominates task. The Cultural Conflict Matrix is an attempt to estimate the degree of conflict that will emerge when applications are transferred across cultures.

Three case studies, one taken from the literature and two undertaken by the authors, suggest that the cultural conflict matrix has enough explanatory value to warrant further exploration. It suggests that while organizations are likely to attribute transfer problems to a host of factors including resistance to change, organizational culture, uncooperative management, and inexperienced end-users, it may be unreasonable to dismiss the role of culture in explaining why one fails while another succeeds.

The framework presented here must be considered as a preliminary attempt to build a transnational model of information technology transfer. It hopefully has established the fact that it is possible to build a simple yet useful model which generalizes the problems in information technology transfer and allows researchers to go, as Palvia (1993) has suggested, several steps beyond descriptive studies in order to develop further insights into the nature of country issues as well as differences among countries.
