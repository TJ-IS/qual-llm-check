# Reconciling the IT/business relationship: a troubled marriage in need of guidance

John Ward, Joe Peppard

Cranfield School of Management, Cranfield, Bedford, MK43 0AL, UK

Paper submitted July 1994; revised paper accepted for publication by Professor R.D. Galliers, October 1995

## Abstract

Over the past 30 years the relationship between the information technology (IT) organisation and the rest of the business has been a troubled one which few organisations have managed to satisfactorily resolve. This ‘gap’ has been explained by the cultural differences existing between the two. Yet despite the criticality of IT to the business little attempt has been made to explore this culture gap further and much of the writings and research on the subject are dispersed, progressing little beyond the centralisation/decentralisation debate and offer little by way of guidance. Much of the literature is concerned with issues of control of resources rather than managing the relationship. Some organisations have chosen to rid themselves of the problem through outsourcing but recent debate has raised the question of the long term consequences of short term decisions which are based on a historical problem. Yet often the ‘trouble’ has no foundation beyond a level of perceptions. This paper attempts to understand and interpret the problems in the relationship, to describe the gap, to understand the reasons why it exists, and to offer some advice. A framework which can be used as a basis for further constructive research to improve our understanding is developed and discussed.

Keywords: IT organisation; Culture; Business value; Relationship management

## 1. Introduction

Since it was first used in organisations the focus of information technology (IT) has shifted from being a technical concern to being recognised as a critical business issue. From the early 1980s information management through the use of IT has been seen as being crucial to competitiveness and taking on a strategic significance for organisations (Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Earl, 1987; Porter and Miller, 1985; Rockart and Scott Morton, 1984) and offering new competitive advantages (Ives and Learmonth, 1984; Malone et al., 1987; Johnston and Vitale, 1988; McFarlan, 1984). Yet despite the undoubted criticality of IT to the business, the IT organisation, $^{1}$ as the provider of IT resources, often does not have a harmonious relationship with the rest of the business.

Perhaps a reason for this problem is that IT in general and IT professionals in particular were often late coming into an organisation. The business already had its own value system and behaviour and in many instances the emergence of the IT organisation could be seen as being imposed on the rest of the business; a ‘forced’ rather than ‘mutually desired’ marriage. In many organisations the current IT organisation has grown out of a specialised group of professionals dedicated to ensuring that the ‘computer’ was able to function. $^{2}$ The professionals staffing the IT function are often more attracted to work with the technology than to contribute to the success of the business. In short, they see their future careers in the context of IT and do not give much consideration to the organisation in which they work, perhaps one reason for the traditionally high turnover of IT staff. If they do, it is likely to be in relation to the ability of the organisation to keep up with the latest technologies. There is little personal advantage to be gained in working for an organisation which is not state-of-the-art in the technology it uses. It may not be challenging to work in a ‘low-tech shop’ and IT skills can become quickly outdated, thus diminishing the ‘marketability’ of the IT professional. Indeed, perhaps the analogy can be made with that of actuaries who have are often seen as having more loyalty to the profession than to the institution where they work.

Of course, the attitudes of the rest of the business have not helped. Many managers are technologically inept or averse (McLeod and Jones, 1986), feel incapable of understanding – let alone managing – IT, $^{3}$ and in a great many instances this is by choice: they run the business while the IT organisation provides them with IT solutions. $^{4}$ The reputation which the IT organisation has attained has not helped matters. Many IT projects often come in over budget, over time and when eventually developed very often fail to satisfy user requirements. $^{5}$ IT projects regularly focus on delivery of the technology using special ‘methodologies’ with little attempt to manage the delivery of benefits. A recent benefits management survey of UK companies highlighted that only 10% of companies actively managed the delivery of benefits from IT projects (Ward et al., 1995). Perhaps blame could be laid at how investments in IT are appraised. $^{6}$ Even the traditional IS/IT planning process focuses on how the organisation can use technology and not how the business could best benefit from IT.

A picture can be painted of two different worlds. One, the world of business and the never ending quest for competitive excellence. The other, the world of technology where there is similar striving for excellence, albeit technical excellence. $^{7}$ Each treats the other with suspicion or indifference perhaps due to the lack of mutual understanding. A recent IT survey reported that 79% of managers surveyed see IT as a support function (Price Waterhouse, 1993). Many IT organisations may indeed be happy with this situation: it gives them the licence to experiment with the latest technologies.

Culture is often used as a variable to explain the troubled relationship between the IT organisation and the rest of the business (Grindley, 1991a). IT directors clearly perceive a problem – a recent survey reporting that 47% of them stated their main problem was the culture gap existing between the IT and business professionals; and its importance – 56% believe the culture gap is inhibiting their companies from gaining competitive edge from IT (Grindley, 1991b). This survey concluded that ‘the culture gap is a very deep rooted problem, of a much more fundamental nature than the simple knowledge gap experienced when dealing with other specialists’.

The concept of culture has been receiving increasing attention in the management literature since the early 1980s, for example in the area of strategy and managing and understanding change, particularly strategic change (Johnson, 1992; Pettigrew, 1985). Few would argue that IT/business relationship is not suffering increased pressures due to changes in the competitive environments and the potential offered by IT. Yet detailed analysis of the cultural IT/business gap has yet to progress beyond describing its symptoms and consequences and most discussion is diverted to addressing centralisation and decentralisation issues or discussing the merits or otherwise of outsourcing versus insourcing.

In the IT world today there is much discussion about the issues associated with legacy systems. $^{8}$ Organisations also have a cultural legacy. This suggests that not only do they have to manage the future but organisations also have to deal with the consequences of the past relationship. Like any relationship which is in trouble we need to understand the cause of the trouble, understand the various perspectives and perceptions and then decide how best to proceed. Given that there is a gap, understanding its nature should help in identifying how it might be bridged. Must one party change or do both have to make some concessions? Many organisations have already decided to deal with the ‘problem’ by completely outsourcing the IT organisation. But the wisdom of treating IT services as a commodity service best managed by a large supplier has been questioned (Lacity and Hirschheim, 1993; Hewett, 1994; I/S Analyzer, 1993; Bettis et al., 1992).

In this paper we first briefly review the concept of culture and assess the cultural aspects of the evolving role of the IT organisation from a number of different perspectives. The changing role of IS/IT in the business also has implications both for the organisation and its culture and these are examined. A number of different perspectives are presented which can help in interpreting the cultural gap. We then present a framework which we have found useful in gaining mutual understanding and determining the action required in an attempt to bridge the 'gap'. Finally, we suggest a model which could form a useful starting point for further research to explore the IT/business relationship more fruitfully in the future.

## 2. Culture

Culture has become a key concept in the language of today's manager. It is a term which is used as the excuse for many occurrences in organisational life. Yet if culture is a major influence on the behaviour of the organisation, there is a need for managers to manage the ‘cultural’ context of the organisation in order to achieve required objectives.

Despite the widespread use of the term in today's management literature, the word culture in fact originates from social anthropology. $^{9}$ Studies during the late 19th and early 20th century of so called primitive societies highlighted ways of life that were not only different from the more industrial and technically advanced parts of the USA and Europe but were very often different among themselves. The culture of these societies was deemed to explain the difference, and in a holistic sense, the qualities of any specific grouping of people that are passed from one generation to the next.

The notion of culture was popularised in the general management literature by Anthony Athos's (1981) The Art of Japanese Management, William Ouchi's (1981) Theory Z and Peters and Waterman's (1982) In Search of Excellence. In particular, Peters and Waterman's work focused on culture (or shared values) as a variable, which could be manipulated just like structure or systems or style. Hofstede (1991) used four dimensions of culture to analyse different values and to explain different attitudes to work issues in organisations in 50 countries: power structures, collectivism versus individualism, femininity versus masculinity, and uncertainty avoidance.

Organisational culture can be defined as the shared values and beliefs which take the form of rules of behaviour in a work group or organisation. At an elementary level, corporate culture can be viewed as ‘the particular way things are done’ in organisations. Culture will be influenced by many variables such as the ‘baggage’ people bring with them from their educational and social background or traditions and myths about the management style of the organisation, reporting structures, etc. It is shaped by numerous pragmatic actions over time based on what does and does not work. These beliefs and assumptions lie within a cultural web which in some sense represents the organisation’s social energy and personality and the assumptions and beliefs which it holds (Johnson, 1992). Culture helps to concentrate individual energy in particular directions since the culture bottom line is expected behaviour of organisation members. Indeed, Smircich (1983) proposes the view that organisations are cultures.

Kotter and Heskett (1992) propose that it is useful to think of culture at two levels which differ in terms of their visibility and their resistance to change (see Fig. 1). At the deeper and less visible level, culture refers to values that are shared by people in a group and that tend to persist over time even when group membership changes. At this level culture can be difficult to change, due largely to the fact that people do not recognise the values which they hold. At the more visible level, culture represents the behaviour pattern and style of an organisation that new employees are automatically encouraged to follow by their fellow employees. At this level, while difficult to change, it is less difficult than the deep-seated values.

## 2.1. Does culture have an impact?

A critical question relates to an implicit assumption made at the outset of this section and that is that culture does have an impact. The evidence would suggest strongly that this is the case (Davis, 1984; Pettigrew, 1985; Hofstede, 1991). Kilman et al. (186) distinguish three interrelated aspects of impact: direction, pervasiveness, and strength.

The direction of impact is the course that culture is causing the organisation to follow. The pervasiveness of impact is the degree to which the culture is widespread, or shared, among the members of the organisation. The strength of impact is the level of pressure that a culture exerts on members in the organisation, regardless of the direction.

Kotter and Heskett's (1992) recent studies which examine whether or not a relationship exists between corporate culture and long-term economic performance indicate four trends:

\- Corporate culture can have a significant impact on a firm's long-term economic performance.

\- Corporate culture will probably be an even more important factor in determining the success or failure of firms in the next decade.

\- Corporate cultures that inhibit strong long-term financial performance are not rare; they develop easily, even in firms that are full of reasonable and intelligent people.

\- Although difficult to change, corporate cultures can be made more performance enhancing.

If one can draw any conclusion it is that culture does have a powerful impact over the behaviour of an organisation. When Digital Equipment Corporation moved its XSEL expert order-entry and configuration system from the USA to Europe, it encountered considerable difficulties because of cultural differences. What was correct for sales people to do in the USA was done by administrators in Europe (Mumford, 1991). The calling off of the recent proposed merger between The Leeds Permanent and National & Provincial Building Societies was said to be due to irreconcilable differences in culture between the two and their ‘distinct approaches’ to business. $^{10}$ The hostile bid by Paramount for Time Inc. was blocked by arguments relating to the dramatic consequences the take-over would have for the culture of Time, which would have serious repercussions for the customers, shareholders and society. $^{11}$ In a 1993 study of business process reengineering, Delphi Consulting reported that two-thirds of the respondents cited cultural resistance as the major challenge to succeeding at business process reengineering (BPR) (Radosevich, 1993).

## 2.2. Sub-cultures

The view of organisations as a unified set of values makes problematic the recognition of different subcultures within organisations. Much of the literature on organisational culture has been criticised by Young (1989) who notes that writings suggest either a view of organisations as a single culture which ignores the existence of subgroups with different interests, or a collection of sectional groups who basically strive for their own gain. Subcultures are groups of people whose culture differs from others or the organisation as a whole. This will be expressed in their holding of different expectations, values and goals. These values may be about seemingly superficial things such as preferred style of dress in that subculture, or their preference for being allowed to take their own decisions rather than being given strict guidelines. However, in an organisational context, these cultural differences may signify the potential for conflict and can pose obstacles to communication. Over long periods of time subcultures may generate behaviours or actions which are dysfunctional to the dominant corporate culture.

A critical question is whether IT professionals are different from others in the organisation and, if so, whether this difference might in some sense have cultural underpinnings. Couger and Zawacki (1980) espouse the position that there are indeed two ‘types’, who behave differently. This position has been challenged by Ferratt and Short (1988) who, based on a field survey, asserted that IT and non-IT employees are not different and consequently should not be managed differently. However, their methodology has limitations for broad generalisations to be made, not least the use of ‘work-unit environment’ as a surrogate measure for identifying differences.

Although there is little academic work specifically addressing the issue of information systems (IS) subcultures (Walsham, 1993b, p. 37), the IT organisation is often described as having its own culture, which can be quite distinct from the rest of the business resulting in quite different codes of behaviour. The IT profession has developed a language of its own which is dominated by acronyms which only contributes to and reinforces the development of an IT subculture.

There may be an implicit assumption that the thinking and problem solving styles of the IT professionals and users of their systems are similar. Very often, IT professionals fail to see the political nature of organisations and usually assume that all problems have a technical solution. IT professionals are trained to be highly disciplined, and this is reinforced by traditional systems development methodologies.

They tend to have a low tolerance for ambiguity and often shy away from dealing with emotions. According to Rochester and Douglas (1990) they have a strong task orientation and prefer neat, tidy, lasting solutions to problems. This can have major implications if there is a high-level of resistance to a new IT application. While communication between users and the IT professionals must be two way, this must be reconciled with the project management dimensions: IT projects have milestones and targets must be met if system implementation is to be kept on schedule.

Straub (1991) examined the ‘mindset difference’ between those who are professionally trained in computing and those who are not. In a philosophical analysis he asserted that the reference discipline of those trained in IT resulted in them viewing the world in particular ways which differed from those not trained in such a way. In a somewhat similar vein, Orlikowski and Gash (1994) assert that organisational actors hold different frames of reference and that these guide their development and use of technology.

Therefore, at a sub-group level, culture may lead to behaviour which is dysfunctional to the organisation. Given that the IT organisations and the rest of the business can have different cultures, it is worthwhile examining the evolution of the IT/business relationship from a cultural perspective.

## 3. Cultural aspects of the evolving role of the IT organisation

There has been much analysis of the changing role of IT in organisations over the last 30 years, resulting in a number of ‘evolution’ models. Most authors agree that there have been probably three distinct ‘eras’ in this brief and rapid evolution. These can be summarised as the Data Processing. Management Information Systems and Strategic Information Systems eras (Ward et al., 1990; Galliers and Somogyi, 1987). These descriptions emphasise the changing nature of the role of IT in terms of its use, its business contribution and the resulting management issues. However few studies have specifically dealt with the changing issues at the IT/business boundary in the organisation. Much of the apparently relevant literature deals with the issue of control of IT, i.e. the issue of centralisation or decentralisation of IT resources in the organisation (McFarlan et al., 1983; McFarlan and McKenny, 1983). Whilst clearly the organisational positioning of the IT people will affect their relationship with the business colleagues they ‘serve’, the arguments tend to assume that who manages the resources determines the nature of the relationship. Again, little analysis has been done and it can be observed that even when IT resources have been decentralised, there is often an uneasy and uncomfortable relationship between the IT specialists and business colleagues and managers. As mentioned earlier, it is interesting that the Price Waterhouse IT Review 1993/94 raises both issues of centralisation versus decentralisation of IT resources (and it describes the roughly equally balanced arguments for both) and the culture gap (which is associated with the increasing problems arising from legacy systems). It would appear that another cycle of argument about the merits of centralisation versus decentralisation may well obscure any reasonable debate of how to improve that IT/business relationship, wherever IT resources are located.

The few writers who have addressed the cultural aspects of the role of the IT organisation and people in relation to the rest of the organisation, offer some valuable insight. Zmud (1984) considered the changing type of activity performed by IT staff, arguing that during the 1980s it changed from a ‘manufacturing’ based activity set to a ‘distribution’ set. He proposed a different type of IT organisation structure to reflect the need to deliver services rather than develop and deliver products. Others including La Belle and Nyce (1987), Keen (1988) and a summary article in EDP Analyzer (1986) discussed both the merits of different organisational structures and the changing nature of skills required by IT specialists. But again little is said about the interrelationship between IT and the business.

Hirschheim et al. (1988) are more specific and begin to address the relationship issues, albeit by describing the need for IT departments to respond effectively to changing demands from the ‘business’. They describe three stages of the evolving relationship:

1. Delivery – when IT services are mainly internal and their focus is on improving their ability to deliver and support the IT based systems. This does not imply delivering what the business needs!

2. Reorientation - when IT attempts to develop good relationships with the main business functions (easier with some than others) and provides a variety of services to meet demands from the functions, albeit without regard to the business importance of those demands.

3. Reorganisation – when IT tries to develop an integral relationship with business management to ensure that the supply of services matches overall business priorities and responsibilities are shared and balanced between IT specialists and business managers.

Most mature organisations are probably struggling in the third of these states, but often encumbered by a cultural legacy, as well as systems legacy, inherited from the first two.

Another more detailed assessment of the evolving relationship is described by Galliers and Sutherland (1991) who synthesised Earl's (1989) planning model with Nolan's (1984) six stage model and the well known '7S' model for analysing the interacting management attributes, some of which clearly have a cultural dimension (style, skills, staff and shared values). Their conclusions, tested in a number of organisations are essentially that:

1. each of the attributes needs to change as the organisation becomes more dependent on its IT systems and more mature in its planning of them;

2. if any of the attributes is unsatisfactorily addressed in an early stage of the evolution, then the organisation will be less able (even unable) to achieve success in the later, more demanding, stages;

3. positive attributes developed in the early stages should not be discarded later since the organisation will have a legacy of products from the earlier stages to support;

4. for an organisation to succeed, without major hiatus of disruption of IT supply, it should address all of the 7S elements coherently at each stage before moving forward.

Technological developments coupled with increasing disappointments and frustration with IT delivery capability saw the advent of end-user computing during the late 1970s. Individual departments, and latterly individual users, assumed responsibility for their own application development. The proliferation of personal computers saw for the first time responsibility for IT development move away from the IT organisation. The issue of control of resources, referred to earlier, became a central theme, although it was usually shrouded in the attendant problems of incompatibility of hardware and software and issues of data integrity.

In the context of the rapidly evolving use of IT in the 1980s and the changing nature of the IT applications many organisations turned to outside suppliers to deliver the newer often more complex systems. This was in part due to the skills required, which perhaps did not exist in the internal IT function, and was often seen as necessary since the in house IT resources were fully occupied maintaining the old systems and had no time to work on the often ‘urgent’ new systems. Whether or not the arguments were really valid, or the business managers preferred to work with other parties than the in-house IT function, this caused further problems between in-house IT specialists and the business. IT specialists could not fail to notice that the most interesting and often business significant work was now being done by outsiders. Although while some IT staff probably did not mind – being happy to work within their old skill set – many resented this change and became further alienated from the business.

Up to this point, in the mid-1980s, organisations may have employed additional external resources to supplement their own staff in varying ways, but few got rid of their own staff as a result. Only in the late 1980s did ‘outsourcing’ of IT resources become a seriously considered alternative, but it has been rapidly taken up by many organisations who are unhappy, for any one of a range of reasons, with their in house IT people particularly if IT is viewed as a commodity (Lacity et al., 1995). It appears that whilst it is rarely overtly stated, the original causes which make outsourcing eventually a preferred option, have been built up in the poor relationship between IT and the business. It could be observed that many IT departments were ‘culturally outsourced’, often due to their own actions, long before an issue triggers consideration of physical outsourcing. It is then an easy and relatively painless step for management to announce the divorce! Interestingly, a recent outsourcing survey highlighted the importance of cultural fit in deciding on an outsourcing vendor as opposed to evaluating tenders based on a straight lowest cost basis (Fitzgerald and Mitchell, 1994).

Today, most businesses have a far richer choice of ‘sourcing’ options for IT products and services than 10 years ago. This implies a further set of changes in the role of any in-house IT function and also more complex decisions for business managers. Of course a crucial question is where responsibility for IT should lie (Griffiths, 1994). Some organisations accommodate the problem by declaring the IT function a ‘profit centre’ and demand that it earns a living from external and internal customers. This is effectively outsourcing by another means, making the relationship an ‘arm’s length’, customer-supplier business relationship; perhaps it should be called ‘shake it all about’ sourcing! Yet research shows that they are often sub-optimal in terms of satisfying the host organisation’s needs (Feeney et al., 1987). Dearden (1987) argues that making IT a profit centre is the first step in an inevitable journey to oblivion.

Venkatraman and Loh (1993) consider this changing nature of the IT function whereby it moves from managing a technical or product portfolio to a ‘relationship portfolio’. They suggest that the IT function should focus on managing its relationship with its ‘customers’ (in the business) and its suppliers (in the IT industry). It needs to clearly define its ‘locus of competence’ or value adding capability between the two and also to fulfil a new role in enabling the development of interorganisational systems between the firm and its trading partners. They contrast this new role with the skills and values of a traditional IT organisation but also argue that simplistic outsourcing leaves a competence gap inside the organisation.

In a similar vein Moad (1994) argues that the changing nature of the technology infrastructure, especially ‘mainframe’ to ‘client server’, is causing the creation of the ‘Virtual IS Organisation’. He suggests that the IT resource is becoming dispersed and de facto includes business people and external resources which need to be co-ordinated within and outside the organisation via some as yet unspecified process! In many ways his views are similar to von Simson (1990) who described a more structured version of how to manage a ‘centrally-decentralised IS organisation’. But in both cases the implicit rationale is about control of activity and resources and adapting to a changing IT environment rather than to establishing an effective relationship. Moad does point out that many IT organisations are uncomfortable with this new concept and many of them attempt to regain control of the dispersed components of the virtual organisation through an insistence on managing the new technology infrastructure.

One final viewpoint that is worth considering in this section, and a view that perhaps offers more insight into the culture gap problems than most, is that described by Hedberg. $^{12}$ He presents his three-stage view of how organisations mature with new technology from the point of view of the technologist (see Table 3). Whilst the particulars are interesting in themselves the table perhaps explains in a simple way why organisations have found it exceedingly difficult to fully integrate the IT function with the business. In Phase 1, the technologist is exploring or pioneering, attempting to exploit the technology and the rest of the business is the normally unwilling subject of this haphazard and uncertain exploration – not a satisfactory start to the relationship. Eventually the technologist (and the rest of the business) realises the limits of the technology and care has to be taken to avoid unacceptable effects on the rest of the organisation – the technologist becomes defensive – again not a very equal or mutually responsible relationship. Finally in the third phase the technology can clearly deliver strategic change if managed well, but the technologist is seen as the agent of that change – a source of potential revolution in the organisation. Once more this is a threatening role which is difficult to accommodate comfortably. Hence even if the technologist has the wit to recognise these changing roles and can acquire the skills needed, almost by definition he or she is an intruder or even a threat to the rest of the organisation. The natural reactions of any organism in such circumstances is to (a) pretend it's not there and/or (b) reject it at the earliest opportunity.

This is not really surprising but it is surprising that in $83\%$ of the sample (25/30) this was not recognised before the project failed. There is some correlation with Galliers and Sutherland's (1991) view of the changing nature of the 7Ss, albeit that theirs is a view of historical evolution and the other reflects the 'current' situation in organisations. However, the comparison does reinforce the assessment that most IT organisations' and their staff's attributes normally align more closely with those that seem appropriate in the key operational and support segments.

It should not be surprising therefore that unless the IT organisation culture adapts to the different environment implied by the strategic and high potential types of applications, the more the business functions will take on the responsibility for them and reject IT involvement. It is more likely, after all, that business people will possess the types of attributes needed in the top half of the matrix. Equally the IT function is likely to have a skill-set and mind-set determined by the current portfolio, which will be dominated by a legacy of key operational and support systems. This is reinforced by Grindley's suggestion of contrasting differences in approach to decision-making about IT matters between IT specialists and business people – ‘polarised concepts’ (Grindley, 1991b).

Viewed from this standpoint, slightly different if complementary conclusions can be drawn, as compared with the previous section.

1. It is simplistically attractive to argue that 'the business comes first and IT people must understand that', implying that IT should adopt values etc. which are determined solely by the rest of the business. But this would deny the fact that in some aspects at least, the IT skill and mind sets have to satisfy the need for stability and efficiency in key operational and support systems. Here the traditional IT values need perhaps to be adopted more wholeheartedly by the business if the optimum contribution is to be achieved from IT in the organisation. Hence whilst there is a need for the IT people to change significantly, as below, there is likely to be a need for change of attitude in the business to accommodate the inevitable consequences of extensive business use of IT. Reconciliation is needed of both the perception and reality of the relationship.

2. In the newer ‘future critical’ areas for the use of IT in the business the values of business development, innovation and consequent risk-taking and change have to be shared successfully within the relationship. Here the IT community cannot argue that others must become reconciled to IT’s view. It is the business managers who will, inevitably, decide what IT is to be used for and the IT specialists must adopt ways of working which enable the business to succeed. In the process they must also provide the knowledge to enable the business managers to make informed judgements about IT feasibility – not tell the business managers what will and will not work, or how things must be done. In the strategic and high potential segment the ‘IT process’ is of secondary importance to the business development and change process. All this implies that IT specialists adopt a different set of values, or else in many instances the IT department will be rejected as a partner in favour of outsiders, who can appear to adjust their value set, like chameleons, to any set of surroundings. Once rejected from the strategic quadrant of the matrix the IT function will probably have to fight a rearguard action across the whole matrix, justifying its value added everywhere.

The long-term consequences of the IT function not being able to understand the realities and perceptions applying to these new areas will be that the organisation will become reliant on outside suppliers for success in its pursuit of its business strategy. Therefore whilst it is incumbent on the IT people to change, it is the responsibility of organisational management to create the climate for that change to occur – or the business as a whole will probably suffer in the long term.

Whilst these conclusions are reasonably obvious in many ways, achieving reconciliation in this increasingly pressured environment, requires new ways of thinking on both sides of the relationship and ways of thinking which are not immediately perverted by the troubles of the past.

## 5. Routes towards gaining mutual understanding of the situation

Cultures and issues surrounding culture are difficult to define and understand. Dealing with fuzzy, intangible things like ‘mindsets’ is a complex task, yet the Peters and Waterman (1982) work referred to earlier was clear in its suggestion that culture is a variable which could be manipulated. The message from their work is that ‘excellent’ organisations exhibit a particular type of culture which any company aspiring to excellence should seek to emulate.

While this might be a logical implication, of critical concern is the extent to which management can influence members values which are necessary for the survival of the organisation and how influential are traditions and beliefs about how things have been done in the past in influencing current action. Sounding a word of caution, Fitzgerald (1988) has argued that those who urge direct management of corporate culture largely fail to appreciate that the deep-seated values, beliefs, and assumptions underlying that culture can rarely if ever be engaged by such an approach. He proposes that ‘we can’t talk intelligently about changing cultures until we understand how to change underlying values.’ Despite this reservation we suggest that it is useful to explore the cultural context of the IT/business relationship from a number of different viewpoints. $^{13}$

## 5.1. Perceptions

The perspective of culture as shared meaning has been suggested (Smircich, 1983). This view is linked to symbolic anthropology (Geertz, 1973) and when applied to organisational analysis, culture is conceived as a pattern of symbolic discourse needing interpreting, reading or deciphering. Organisation theorists have emphasised that environments are not given realities, but created through processes of attention and interpretation (Pfeffer and Salancik, 1978).

Perception is a critical variable in examining culture because people's behaviour is based on their perception of what reality is and not reality itself. Perceptions will also play a leading role in how individuals react to and enact changes in culture. For example, adopting this basic premise in examining resistance to change is to take the perspective of the individual in an attempt to understand change, a view shared in disciplines such as anthropology and ethnography. Human behaviour is guided by the constructs and meaning which individuals use to interpret their surroundings. $^{14}$ Thus this hermeneutic approach involves the mutual understanding of different peoples ‘frame of reference’ which guide their meaning. For example, while business strategy is developed in response to what senior management perceive the competitive environment to be, research shows that even managers from the same firm can perceive their environment quite differently and will therefore seek to implement different strategies to meet competitive challenges.

## 5.2. Roles

A key task in examining the IT/business relationship is to examine the roles performed by the IT organisation. At this basic level, the Price Waterhouse (1993) study, referred to earlier, showed that there is a rift between the IT organisation and the business concerning what exactly is the role and function of the IT organisation. Both have different interpretations of what these roles entail or should entail. For example, providing infrastructure services scores most mentions among IT executives and the rest of the company, yet there is no agreement as to what these services are.

IT directors are unsure amongst themselves whether the role of the IT organisation is primarily to be engaged in a process that brings about business change, or to be the implementor of the consequences of change or merely to facilitate the activities of others. This dilemma of lack of agreed role clearly impacts the IT/business relationship and causes confusion and uncertainty. In a recent paper Earl (1992) called for the business to be put back into IT. The phenomenon of BPR is perhaps further recognition of the need to rethink how IT is applied in organisational settings (Hammer, 1990; Peppard and Rowland, 1995).

One of the roles of the IT organisation is the development of information systems. Many view information systems development (ISD) as a technical process. Most development methodologies promote the distinction between IT and the rest of the business by delineating the roles and responsibilities and using ‘deliverables’ or process decision points as the vehicle for reconciliation and agreement. This view has been challenged by a number of scholars who present systems development as a form of social action. Hirschheim et al. (1991) hypothesise that ISD can be more effectively understood by applying seven basic building blocks of social action theories: knowledge, power, subjective meaning, human interests, concensus, conflict, and resistance. They suggest that it is the interplay of these seven elements which make up the fundamental basis of systems development.

## 5.3. Metaphors

One of the problems we face in both studying and working in organisations is their complexity. There are just too many factors and their relationships are too complex or ambiguous for us to understand in their entirety. To understand and make sense of the world we need to simplify it and draw a conceptual line around what we are interested in and, within that line, what we wish to examine. To do this we consciously and subconsciously use metaphors and analogies to help focus our thoughts and understanding. This use of metaphor implies ‘a way of thinking and a way of seeing that pervade how we understand our world generally’ (Morgan, 1986) and can provide interesting ways of ‘reading’ organisations as part of an interpretative epistemology (Walsham, 1993). Boland (1989) argues that metaphors are not just colourful ways of expressing ourselves but underlie the ways in which we can think and act, and thus we ‘cannot not use metaphors’, a view also subscribed to by Lakoff and Johnson (1980).

Drawing insights from a variety of disciplines including organisation behaviour, sociology, cybernetics, management theory, and political science, Morgan (1986) considers a wide range of different approaches to the study of organisations. He contends that in talking about culture we are really talking about the process of reality construction that allows people to see and understand particular events, actions and utterances in distinctive ways. This has echoes of Weick's (1969) concept of ‘enactment’ which describes the subjective way in which we draw meaning from previously lived experiences and thus determine our attitudes to ongoing activity. We suggest that one reason for the perceived culture gap is that the IT organisation and the business subscribe to different metaphors. Each has a different Weltanschauung. Perhaps the IT organisation sees the rest of the organisation like a machine to be manipulated in a similar way to how they view the other machine which they work with, i.e. the computer. Expected outcomes are seen to be predictable. The business, on the other hand, subscribes to a different metaphor, perhaps more to do with flux, and therefore expect the IT department to be as adaptable and accommodating as possible. $^{15}$

## 6. A framework for understanding

Until both sides recognise that there is a problem the gap is likely to remain. When one speaks about culture, much of the discussion is invariably at a level of abstraction which makes it difficult to articulate any response. Examining perceptions, roles and metaphors is a helpful beginning to understanding this gap and can be brought together using the ‘cultural web’. This framework, developed by Johnson (1992) $^{16}$ , has proved valuable in understanding the cultural aspects of business performance. Devised to assess the inter-related components of a whole organisation’s cultural attributes in the context of its business environment, it is also a powerful tool to enable the business and the IT organisation articulate and understand the cultural gap that exists between them.

The web is based on the premise that while individuals may hold different sets of beliefs, there is at some level a core set of values, beliefs and assumptions commonly held throughout the organisation which Johnson refers to as the paradigm. The paradigm governs and influences an organisation's view of itself and its environment. We suggest that the business and IT organisation often subscribe to two different paradigms. Through their respective paradigms each creates a relatively homogeneous approach to the interpretation of the complexity that the organisation faces. Since it evolves through time and is reinforced by history, it provides a repertoire of actions and responses to the interpretations of signals, which are experienced by members and seen by them as demonstrably relevant.

The paradigm is surrounded by a web of ‘cultural artefacts’, composed of three ‘hard’ and three ‘soft’ components through which the organisation promulgates, deliberately or unintentionally, its core beliefs. Power and organisational structures and the control systems reflect how the organisation should work and can be defined and described in rational, objective terms. The other three – symbols, stories and myths, rituals and routines – are less rational and reflect what people inside and outside the organisation actually see and often remember most about the way the organisation conducts itself. This web is illustrated in Fig. 3 and below we briefly describe each of these artefacts.

Stories and myths. In every organisation there are stories, some true, others either variations of the truth or simply myths. Examples are the big IT failures, the products that flopped, the legendary leaders and mavericks. In particular, new employees hear stories about those who broke the cultural norms and the consequences of their actions. Most have evolved the years and have become part of the organisation's folklore.

Symbols. Every organisation has its symbols, although they are often so much ingrained in day-to-day life that they may not be recognised. $^{17}$ The dress code, job titles, executive parking spaces, the MD's Rolls Royce are all symbols. At one particular insurance company there were five different categories of restaurants and as one progressed up the management hierarchy the quality of both food and dining room surroundings improved considerably. Symbols also include company specific language which reinforces entrenched attitudes, like addressing directors as 'sir'. Indeed, it has been argued that much of the information in organisation is symbol (Feldman and March, 1981).

Rituals and routines. Rituals are those aspects of organisational life which hold a special significance and may include the monthly board meeting, the annual company barbecue, or the need for partners in some consultancies to sign off anything that goes to a client. In many instances they serve no purpose other than being a part of a ritual which may at one time have had relevance. Robey and Markus (1984) argue that elements of the system design process can be interpreted as rituals which enable actors to remain overtly rational while negotiating to achieve private interests.

Control systems. Organisations have particular control systems to monitor and encourage performance. Pay and reward systems, budgetary control systems and the management hierarchy are all examples of such systems and serve to highlight what is valued by the organisation. The historical relationship between IT and the finance department has produced almost a double set of controls for IT and financial management.

Organisational structures. Organisations do have formal structures which do have an impact on behaviour. Functions, departments, geographically based business units, product-based business units, flat management hierarchies, large bureaucratic hierarchies, are all examples of how the structure of an organisation can impact the paradigm. The position of the IT organisation in the organisational structure and the structure of the IT organisation itself are often designed to manage IT not support the business.

Power structures. Power is concerned with where influence resides, particularly where it can reduce uncertainty. Because the power structures tend to reinforce the paradigm they are often targets for change. This is particularly difficult given that those who may be required to change often hold the power. $^{18}$

## 6.1. Changing culture

In attempting to change their culture many organisations manipulate the 'hard' elements of the web, i.e. the power structures, the control systems and the organisational structures, neglecting to address the more intangible elements. Indeed, by treating the problem as a centralisation or decentralisation issue or in considering the outsourcing route, this is exactly that scenario. All elements of the web must be examined and acted on if cultural change is to take place and this change translated into tangible actions and results.

For example, Morgan (1986) argues that managers can influence the evolution of culture by being aware of the symbolic consequences of their actions and by attempting to foster desired values. In one particular engineering company the IT department was viewed as a support service to the business and was looked upon as the 'mover of boxes'. When a new IT director was appointed he immediately outsourced this 'movement of boxes' to give a clear message to the rest of the organisation that the IT department was involved in more value-added work. This symbolic gesture sent a powerful message to the rest of the organisation and had the immediate effect of changing the perception which the business held of the department.

## 6.2. Cultural audits

The web can be used as a powerful tool in order to conduct an audit of the culture of an organisation. It can highlight both the hard and softer influences on the corporate culture. An example of using the technique to describe the views a business had of its IT department, obtained from a ‘customer survey’ is given in Fig. 4. This is derived from adapting the questions Johnson suggests to elicit the contents of the web (Johnson and Scholes, 1993).

Clearly the business did not think highly of its IT department. In fact the management of the IT department had been working hard restructuring the IT organisation, improving the control of activities and getting line managers involved in decision making about IT. However, the symbols, stories and rituals as perceived from the 'customers' had changed little over time – the management was unaware of these and the impact that had on its ability to work effectively in the business. This is partly to be expected given that IT managers are generally logical, rational thinkers and they therefore focus on the more rational elements of the web. Even when these are made more effective, the other components will colour both internal and external perceptions of the function, based on past performance. Unless conscious efforts are made to change these elements of the web to make them consistent with the intended paradigm the organisation will not be more successful. This takes time and in the process it is necessary to ‘unmake’ history, eradicate old perceptions as well as create the required future. Traditionally IT management have not focused their attention on these softer aspects of their relationship with the business, resulting in the IT department’s contribution being less valuable and less valued than it could and should have been.

Understanding perceptions and the reasons they are held is often the first step in reconciliation of the views and determining changes needed to improve the working relationship. The ‘web’ has proved a valuable technique in facilitating the articulation of those views and structuring them for review. In many cases it shows that in terms of the Galliers and Sutherland (1991) and Hirschheim et al. (1988) evolutionary models, business and IT views of the situation are significantly often ‘out of phase’ in terms of intention or delivered reality and/or perceptions of both.

## 7. Some conclusions and areas for further research

The relationship in internal IT organisation has with other business functions is under stress due to a number of forces:

1. the rate of technological change, which has not relented for 30 or more years and demands that organisations keep up to date with technology developments. This tends to reinforce the argument for a separate group responsible for IT.

2. the ever extending and changing role of IT in organisations. Using Sullivan's (1985) terms, the degrees of ‘infusion’ (business dependence) and ‘diffusion’ (dispersion of technology and related decision making) are producing a ‘complex’ internal environment. This results in a variety of roles for IT specialists and requires a range of services to deliver and support the systems and users activities, which can cause confusion, even conflict in making choices and decisions.

3. the increasingly demanding business environment is causing more rapid business and organisational change than ever before. This in turn requires a more dynamic and flexible approach from IT specialists, to deliver more adaptable solutions, more quickly. In many cases the existing systems legacy, which determines the existing IT mind and skill sets, also require major change to support the results of business process reengineering and organisational restructuring. Or worse still, radical business change cannot be effected due to the constraints of existing systems and technology. As a result IT can be seen as an inhibitor of change, at the same time as it is required to become an enabler of future change.

4. the range of supply options for IT has increased dramatically, perhaps fuelled by recession and the resulting drive to reduce all costs including IT. Outsourcing is not new, packaged software has been in common use for many years, but the range of services available today can enable large parts of IT's traditional function to be provided by outside suppliers. This is seen as a threat by the internal IT specialists and often as a ‘simple’ solution, not just to cost problems, by organisational management. But it is interesting to note, as mentioned earlier, that whilst costs reduction and/or improved skills may be the primary drivers for outsourcing ‘cultural fit’ is often a key criterion in selecting an outsourcing partner (Fitzgerald and Michell, 1994).

Essentially forces 1 and 3 are supply driven pressures whereas 2 and 4 are demand driven. Forces 3 and 4 have become more intense recently and are increasing the stress on what is already often a strained relationship between the IT organisation and the business. Forces 1 and 2 have existed to a large degree throughout the evolution of IT in organisations, during which the 'culture gap' has developed. The 'culture gap' itself is a simplistic, if convenient notion describing a variety of complex issues which are influenced by external pressures as described above. The argument in this paper is that there are ways of describing the gap such that at least the problems can be diagnosed and then acted upon. Understanding different perceptions and then adjusting both how IT and business people behave is one step towards achieving the necessary congruence of shared values. This search for the means of reconciliation is suggested as more pertinent to real world issues than further debate on the merits of centralisation and decentralisation of resource control – a debate which itself perhaps increases the width of the gap?

In order to move forward constructively, in relation to the issues of today we perhaps need to look at a variety of types of relationship between IT professionals and business management. Fig. 5 suggests a way of describing a set of relationship options based on different shared values. An organisation might need to be operating in all four dimensions at any one time which implies a complex set of cultural issues to be understood. Briefly the suggested relationship types are:

1. Financial – where the emphasis is on the price of procurement of IT, enabling users to select solutions based on short term financial criteria. The corollary is that an internal IT department will be judged on financial criteria – i.e. as a ‘profit centre’. If it cannot compete successfully it will eventually have no business – if it is successful its ‘profit’ will contribute to the organisation’s finances.

2. Contractual – the emphasis is on satisfying a set of business service needs in the most cost effective way. This is appropriate when IT is a ‘cost of doing business’ – a cost which is accepted but is to be minimised without detriment to meeting contracted requirements. This is described as ‘outsourcing’ even if the internal IT department provides the services currently. Selection of in-house or outhouse suppliers will be reviewed periodically to select the best contractors for the set of service requirements, the ‘contracts’ being framed in terms of service level agreements.

In both these cases IT is not seen as a core competence of the business but something which is procured in the optimum way to satisfy business requirements as they arise and evolve. Recent strategic management research suggests that ‘competence based strategies’ are becoming important in achieving sustainable competitive success (Hamel and Prahalad, 1994; Stalk et al., 1992). In many cases business competencies will derive from or depend upon competence in IT (Treacy and Wiersma, 1993) and therefore the other two types of relationship need to be considered.

3. Organisational – where the implication is that the value of IT is embedded in unique ways into the chosen areas of business competence, or as the means of delivering the integration of other competencies. Continuing alignment between the deployment of IT and the development of those competencies will be essential for business success. In this case the 'value added' of IT is not just in the delivery of effective technology solutions, but in the IT organisation's in-depth understanding of how the applications and their integration deliver the required business strategy. This knowledge will arise from actual experience of the systems in use and would be difficult to sustain via a contractual form of relationship – it demands continuous dialogue and requires an understanding of implicit assumptions and values as well as explicit statements of requirements.

4. Intimate – whereas the ‘insourcing’ approach described above is intended to make IT an integral support for other business competencies, in many organisations IT will itself be essential business competence. ‘Partnership’ implies that aspects of the business strategy will derive directly from an ability to innovate and create new business options from the application of the technology. Here IT is as much an input to strategy development as a means of implementation. The IT specialists involved will be partners in the derivation of strategy – a process that can only be achieved through mutual trust, based on congruence of values. In such cases, significant changes to the business and organisation are likely to be a consequence of IT instigated innovation. These are unlikely to be contemplated, let alone commissioned and even less likely achieved successfully without mutual confidence and trust.

This relationship based view to some extent synthesises the views of other researchers and observers. Each type of relationship implies different roles for IT specialists, can be considered in terms of different metaphors and requires different shared values. Each would be expected to result in different realities and perceptions throughout the cultural web. Further research into each of these different types of relationship might prove valuable. Existing research has tended to treat the IT function as a more or less homogeneous, evolving entity or has addressed only some of the factors involved e.g. control of resources. There would seem to be connections to be made between the nature of the applications portfolio and the types of relationships required to determine and deliver it. Such examination might explain why some organisations are more successful than others in aligning IT investment with business and organisational strategic development. Many organisation's have appointed 'IT directors' or 'Chief Information Officers (CIO)' and recent research suggests that competence and cultural fit of the individual are significant determinants of perceived success or otherwise in the appointment (Earl and Feeney, 1994). Research does not however reveal whether IT as a whole contributes more to the business following such appointments. More research would be worthwhile.

Perhaps a more basic question relates to where responsibility for IT should lie. Over the years the pendulum has tended to swing between centralisation and decentralised and distributed approaches. Initiatives, such as the hybrid manager, attempted to pull this pendulum in one direction or another. We have suggested that it is not just a matter of repositioning the IT organisation but requires a more fundamental evaluation of the broader issues of organisational IS/IT competencies.

This paper has used the metaphor of a ‘troubled marriage’ to describe frequently observed relationship problems between IT and the business. We have suggested some guidance on how to understand the causes and implications of what is generally described as the ‘culture gap’. It can be concluded perhaps that many IT organisations with poor relationships are ‘culturally outsourced’ long before some event triggers the divorce and real outsourcing. Perhaps with better understanding of the causes of the culture gaps plus new views of the types of relationship required, reconciliation rather than divorce might be feasible. This is important if divorce leads to the removal of a key future competence, simply because the organisation failed to understand and address culture gap issues.
