# Toward more effective management of information technology benefits

Jaak Jurison

Information and Communications Systems, Fordham University, Graduate School of Business Administration, 113 West 60th Street, New York, NY 10023, USA

Received October 1994; revised December 1995; second revision March 1996; accepted for publication by Professor Jon Turner, April 1996

## Abstract

A critical issue facing senior executives is how to improve the return on their information technology (IT) investments. The results of numerous studies are inconclusive as they have failed to show conclusively that IT investments produce expected payoffs. The purpose of this paper is to offer an explanation for the perceived lack of IT payoff and show how managers can improve the return on their IT investments. We argue that IT can provide significant benefits, but in many cases these benefits are not captured by the firm that made the investment. Instead, a large portion of the benefits are reaped by a variety of participants or stakeholders. The implications of this for managers is that they need to view IT payoff as a portfolio of benefits across many stakeholders. They must also understand and manage the distribution of the benefits within the portfolio to assure that benefits are transferred to the bottom line. The paper offers a framework for analyzing the nature and distribution of IT benefits among various stakeholder groups and concludes with a set of guidelines for measuring and managing these benefits.

Keywords: Information technology investment; Business value of information technology; Management of information technology; Strategic information technology; Stakeholder groups

## 1. Introduction

A growing number of senior executives are questioning the value of information technology (IT) investments. Questions like these are typical:

\- Are we getting our money's worth from our IT investments?

\- Are we spending too much or too little on IT?

\- What IT projects are the best uses of our resources?

\- How can we improve the return on our IT investments?

These concerns are quite legitimate and well-founded. IT costs are continuing to increase at a rapid rate and investments in computers and telecommunications amount to approximately half of the annual capital expenditures in most large companies. At the same time, competitive pressures are increasing demands for improved performance. Attempts to prove a linkage between IT investments and business performance have produced mixed results. While some studies have shown positive impact (Harris and Katz, 1991; Brynjolfson and Hitt, 1993), comprehensive literature reviews by Brynjolfson (1993) and Wilson (1993) indicate that a large number of studies have found little or even negative correlation between IT investment and business performance. Given these inconclusive findings, business managers are experiencing increasing difficulties in justifying new IT investments. Although recent studies offer much promise for clarifying the confusion about IT value (e.g. Hitt and Brynjolfson, 1994), it is clear that both practicing managers as well as researchers need to have a better understanding of IT value and the process of creating it.

Why is there so much confusion about IT value? Why has it been so difficult to prove IT impact on business performance? Among many explanations, the most commonly cited reasons are:

\- inappropriate measures;

\- inappropriate unit of analysis;

\- failure to account for time lag.

The first problem lies in the use of a single metric for defining IT value. Because of the variety of ways business is deploying IT, a traditional single global metric, such as productivity or return on assets, is not adequate for assessing the full value of IT. Nowadays IT investments are made for a variety of reasons, including improved quality, increased variety of products or services, and better responsiveness to customer needs. Brynjolfson (1993), in his review of empirical literature on IT value, concludes that we may have to rethink the way we are measuring the value of IT because “traditional measures of the relationship between inputs and outputs fail to account for nontraditional sources of value”.

The second difficulty stems from the fact that the majority of IT value studies use the industry sector or the whole economy as the unit of analysis. Consequently they suffer from aggregation effects. By aggregating data over a large number of different businesses the critical mediating effect of management is not captured in these studies. IT benefits depend to a large degree not on the size of the investment, but on management effectiveness in converting the investment into business results. Organizations differ vastly in their conversion effectiveness. While some firms enjoy phenomenal success from IT spending, others do not (Weill, 1990; Markus and Soh, 1993). By aggregating many diverse results, the data from successful firms are offset by data from firms with poor or negative returns.

The third factor that complicates IT value studies is the time lag between IT investment and the benefits stream. Evidence from empirical studies shows that the time lag between investment and benefits varies depending on the type of the investment and organizational absorption rates (Weill, 1990; Strassmann, 1990). This time lag can range from less than one year for the least difficult to more than 10 years for the most difficult applications. For managers, investment costs are immediate and highly visible, while benefits appear only as future expectations of unclear value. This creates particular difficulties in determining the value of IT infrastructure investments which make future applications possible. The benefits which accrue from infrastructure investments are usually attributed to the applications, while the costs are charged to the infrastructure development. Failure to recognize the presence of the time lag can lead to severely distorted investment decisions and underinvestment in IT infrastructure projects.

Another possible reason why traditional IT value studies have produced inconsistent results is their overly narrow perspective. The perspective adopted in past studies has tended to be one of top management — a search for bottom line results. Researchers, being limited to a relatively small number of factors in order to maintain statistical power, limit their studies to the outcomes they consider most significant. Consequently, the majority of studies to date have omitted many important classes of benefits, such as the benefits that accrue to various stakeholders of a firm: customers, suppliers, employees, and others. These benefits can be in many different forms: e.g. lower price, time savings, increased variety of products and services, or improved quality. Stakeholder benefits have become more significant in recent years because IT is no longer confined to an isolated area, but is permeating the whole value chain in modern business. This is particularly true for strategic information systems that extend beyond the boundaries of a single firm. Given this multidimensional nature of IT benefits, we must stop thinking about IT payoff in terms of a single financial metric but start treating it as a portfolio of benefits that are distributed across several stakeholder groups. The key to getting the best return on IT investment lies in active management of this portfolio and particularly the distribution of benefits within the portfolio.

Thinking of IT payoff in terms of a portfolio of benefits is important to both managers and researchers for several reasons. First, any IT value analysis without an assessment of all relevant stakeholder benefits is incomplete and is likely to understate the full extent of the benefits. A consequence of this omission is underinvestment in IT. The omission can also mask important positive relationships and result in poor decisions and misleading generalizations.

Second, business managers need information not only for measuring and evaluating IT benefits, but more importantly, they need guidance on how to manage the investments so that benefits can be captured in the bottom line. Managers must be able to evaluate the full portfolio of benefits and actively manage it to achieve the desired results. Given the large IT base built up over the past 10 years in the service industry without adequate proof of payoff, there is now a critical need to manage it for better business results.

The objective of this paper is twofold:

\- to present a framework for analyzing the nature and distribution of IT benefits across various stakeholder groups; and

\- to develop a set of guidelines for effective management intervention.

## 2. Distribution of IT benefits

There is growing evidence that a significant portion of IT benefits does not go to the bottom line, but accrues to a variety of participants or stakeholders of the investment (Banker et al., 1993; Hitt and Brynjolfson, 1994). By definition, stakeholders are all those parties who affect or are affected by a corporation's actions, behavior, and policies. Stockholders, employees, customers, suppliers, competitors, regulators, unions, and general public are all relevant stakeholders who have the ability to influence organizational performance and whose goals need to be considered in a systematic approach to management. They are also likely to be affected by IT investment decisions by shouldering some costs and receiving some benefits. The nature of the benefits and their distribution across the stakeholder groups can vary over a wide range depending on the characteristics and purpose of the system and how the benefits are managed.

Many new systems are no longer introduced for the purpose of improving operational efficiency, but for creating competitive advantage or strategic opportunities in the future. Benefits from systems in this category include improved quality, increased variety of products and services, better and faster customer service, and faster response to changes in the market. Notowidigdo (1984) notes that strategic systems can be considered either internal, having direct benefit to the company, or external, having direct benefit to customers. Investments in the latter category are justified because they are expected to benefit the company indirectly. Although this distinction is useful for categorizing IT benefits, many systems can provide benefits internally as well as externally.

An excellent example of such a system is McKesson's electronic order entry and distribution system Economost, which provided benefits for both McKesson and its customers, the independent pharmacies (Clemons and Row, 1988). The customers benefited from reduced transaction costs, reduced wholesale prices, reduced inventory holding costs, and various value-added management services. McKesson's benefits included significant cost reductions in order entry, sales, and warehouse operations, leading to improved market share and profitability. It also allowed McKesson to diversify into other related distribution businesses. The introduction of Economost led to a major improvement in the whole drug distribution industry which benefited mostly the large national and regional wholesalers who were able to exploit the technology. But the most noteworthy result was that many of the benefits from Economost were passed on to the independent pharmacies in the form of lower prices allowing them to compete against the large chains.

There is evidence that other IT investments have also produced more benefits for the customers than for the businesses which made the investments. Economists refer to these social benefits that flow to the downstream sector of the value chain as consumer surplus. Bresnahan (1986) found that IT investments by commercial banks created benefits which were not captured by the banks but went mostly to their customers in the long run. Similar spillover benefits can occur in other service industries like finance and insurance. A recent study by Hitt and Brynjolfson (1994), based on data from 367 large firms, concludes that IT has led to higher productivity, but these gains benefited mostly consumers with no measurable impact on business performance. Although these spillover benefits can be large, they have been ignored in virtually all evaluations of IT payoffs.

IT investments, especially in interorganizational systems which span organizational boundaries, can produce disproportionate benefits for some participants. Clemons and Weber (1993) describe how the benefits from London Stock Exchange's investment in electronic trading technology resulted in significant gains for the exchange and investors but left the traders, who paid for the system, without any benefits at all.

Benefits distribution is also an issue with IT applications within a firm. IT benefits do not accrue uniformly to internal stakeholders such as users, workgroups, departments, functional areas, or business units. In addition, the contribution of each stakeholder group to profits is uneven. It is not unusual for some highly effective area to be cross-subsidizing others which are either less effective in adopting IT or are converting benefits into organizational slack.

IT infrastructure investments present the most difficult challenges for defining benefits and identifying beneficiaries. Because of the enabling and shared nature of IT infrastructure, its benefits are more diffuse and difficult to measure than the benefits of more traditional systems. The real value of IT infrastructure comes from its flexibility: the ability to provide options for leveraging future IT applications. This means that IT infrastructure benefits may take many years to materialize, making it difficult to define, predict, and evaluate their value. For example, the benefits of a sophisticated communications network that links a firm's worldwide locations aimed to position the firm for anticipated future business opportunities are not known at the time of the investment and will not be known until the applications for exploiting the business opportunities are in place.

The above discussion illustrates the complexity and difficulty of defining and measuring the value of IT investments. By limiting the analysis to a single enterprise or business unit as in the majority of past studies, many benefits are likely to be overlooked. Therefore it is important to take a holistic view by including all types of spillover benefits. A more appropriate evaluation strategy is to measure the benefits to all participants at the points where value is created. By including all relevant stakeholder or beneficiary groups in the analysis, one of the major shortcomings of IT value studies can be avoided. It is important for management to know where the benefits are going and be able to manage the mix of benefits so as to optimize the total return on the investment. The next sections expand on the concept of stakeholder-based analysis in more detail and develop a framework for practical use of the concept.

The firm, by investing in IT, incurs a cost and creates value internally and to both stakeholder groups. The value to customers may be in a better product, better service, or lower cost. If the value to customers is higher than the price charged by the firm (net value = value created - cost), they will buy the product or services and the firm will recover a part of its investment through increased sales and profits.

The amount of benefits that can be recovered by the firm is largely influenced by the firm's competitive environment. Although significant benefits may accrue to customers, competition may prevent the recovery through increased sales and profits. For example, when competitors are able to match the new product or service, most of the benefits are permanently captured by the customers. Therefore it is often advantageous to be the first to introduce a new and innovative system so as to be in a position to recover investment costs before competitors can introduce similar or superior capability. Unless a firm is able to create technological or other types of barriers to entry, competitive pressures will eventually work in the customers' favor.

In some cases IT costs can be recovered by transferring some internal costs to customers. It is often done in service industries by making customers pay in terms of personal labor and waiting time. For example, many bank customers perform the functions of bank tellers at automatic teller machines. These customers benefit from increased convenience, but at the cost of doing some of the work themselves, allowing the bank to benefit from reduced labor costs.

As shown in Fig. 2, a firm can also create value to its employees by investing in IT. The added value may be in the form of more effective work processes, allowing the workers to accomplish their work in less time or with less rework. But the firm must eventually recover some of the value in order to earn sufficient return on the investment. This can be accomplished through cost displacement (performing the same amount of work with fewer employees) or cost avoidance (performing more work with the same number of employees). No matter what form the recovery takes, it is a cost to employees that reduces their net value from the investment.

It is important to note that for a firm to receive the expected payoff from its investment, the system must first create sufficient benefits that can be recovered in the bottom line. Because of stakeholders' propensity for maximizing their own benefits, the firm will not be able to reclaim its share of benefits without an effective benefits management process. In many firms the customer side is managed by marketing and the employee side is managed by operations management without much coordination between the two functions, making the recovery process suboptimal. The key to successful management of benefits lies in understanding where value is created and establishing a recovery mechanisms that will produce an appropriate balance of benefits among all beneficiaries. This process should lead to a win-win situation for all stakeholders and produce a positive net value for the firm. Appropriate measures of benefits are essential for successful management of this process.

## 4. Measures of stakeholder benefits

The determination of appropriate performance measures for stakeholder benefit presents a major challenge. Traditional measures have not been very effective in capturing the various components of IT benefits.

Existing performance measures based on conventional management accounting measures (e.g. return on investment (ROI), return on assets (ROA), revenue growth rate, and indirect labor) can capture only the objectives of a limited number of stakeholders. For measuring benefits at a business unit level they have certain advantages. The primary advantage is that they are standard business measures, well understood and accepted by managers. They are also part of the financial reporting system, making them easy to accumulate and simple to apply.

But the main reason for their strength is also the root of their weakness. Because they are tied to the financial reporting system, they cannot capture those benefits that are outside the accounting system. Because these measures are internal, they cannot recognize many types of spillover effects beyond the boundaries of the firm. Therefore assessments based solely on standard financial measures are likely to understate the full value of IT and lead to poor investment decisions.

For evaluating a full range of stakeholder benefits, a variety of financial and non-financial measures can be adopted, either individually or in combination. The measures must be selected on the basis of their ability to reflect the objectives of the stakeholders of the IT investment.

Due to the limitations of traditional financial measures, a broader value-based measurement approach should be considered. This approach was proposed by Keen (1981) for cost justifying decision support systems and has since gained growing support among researchers and practitioners for a wide range of IT applications (Berger et al., 1988; Parker and Benson, 1988; Kauffman and Weill, 1989). The value based approach consists of identifying relevant benefits and determining what management is willing to pay to obtain these benefits. It focuses on the question: What is it worth to us to make the investment in IT? This value-based analysis can be equally appropriate for stakeholder benefits evaluation. First, we need to identify the benefits of every relevant stakeholder group and then ask: What are the benefits worth to you?

How do we measure stakeholder value? Keen (1991) provides a compelling argument for a set of operational indicators of IT performance, called anchor measures. These operational indicators are designed to reflect IT generated benefits, depending on the type of business and its business objectives. The concept of anchor measures can be similarly extended to relevant stakeholder groups, whether they are internal or external to the organization. The choice of the anchors must depend on management judgement and take into consideration the role of the stakeholders in the overall value chain. For example, a retail bank may find it appropriate to measure the annual cost of serving a customer or cost per transaction if its objective is to improve cost structure. A manufacturing firm evaluating its investment in electronic data interchange may use document cost per shipment or average delivery time in days for its anchors. Similarly, customers and suppliers may use the average cost per transaction or inventory holding costs as anchor measures. Benefits for system users might be measured in increased discretionary time or improved quality of life for employees of the firm. Tracking business performance and stakeholder benefits through these anchor measures can overcome many shortcomings of the traditional financial measures and allow managers to make more informed IT investment decisions.

## 5. Management of IT benefits

IT can create only a potential for value. Whether this potential is realized depends on how effectively the benefits are managed for business results. Without an effective benefits management process, IT benefits will be dissipated among various stakeholder groups leaving the investing organization without satisfactory payoff. In many cases the meager returns on IT investment is caused by management's failure to bring the benefits to the bottom line (Brynjolfson, 1993). It can be argued that in the case of US financial service industries, most of the benefits from IT investments in the past went to the employees. Only recent competitive pressures from global service providers have shaken management from complacency to take the necessary tough measures to reduce the size of the workforce and bring the benefits to the bottom line.

An important challenge for management is to find a fair balance of benefits between the firm and its stakeholders. The issue of gain sharing is of critical importance. Obviously the firm needs to recover the cost of the investment. However, to implement the system effectively, it needs the cooperation of the stakeholders. If employees see only the costs of the new system (e.g. the need to learn new skills, potential loss of jobs) with no apparent benefits to them, they are likely to resist the new system. Leonard-Barton and Kraus (1985) describe implementation problems arising from situations where the benefits from new technology accrued to the organization and not to the individuals who were to use the system. In order to avoid potential implementation problems, they recommend making stakeholder benefits visible and explicit and directing some benefits into individual rewards.

The well-known case of Frito-Lay's introduction of handheld computer technology offers an excellent example of effective stakeholder benefits management (Wishart and Applegate, 1992). Frito-Lay's management recognized that the field sales organization was its key stakeholder group whose support to the project success was essential. The system offered potentially large benefits for the corporation in terms of increased sales and better efficiency. Frito-Lay's sales people were also going to benefit from the system by saving time, eliminating paperwork, and helping sales people do their jobs more professionally. By using field test data to quantify and substantiate the expected individual benefits, management was able to negotiate with corporate sales to reduce cost of sales by 1 per cent, allowing each region to define the details of achieving the objective. By making the benefits explicit to the key stakeholder group and getting a commitment to share those benefits with the corporation, Frito-Lay's management was able to motivate the sales organization and recover a significant part of the benefits in the bottom line.

Equitable gainsharing of benefits among the participants is particularly important for interorganizational systems where the benefits are shared among the providers and users. Clemons and Kleindorfer (1992) have developed a formal bargaining model showing that the potential surplus generated by interorganizational systems will depend to a large degree on the bargaining power of the participants in the system. A fair balance of benefits among the participants is of crucial importance for ensuring participants' support for investment and adoption.

The preceding examples and cases lend growing support to the notion that the process by which a firm manages the benefits creation and distribution is of critical importance and is likely to be a key determinant of its IT conversion effectiveness.

## 6. Management guidelines

The main message of this paper is that a holistic stakeholder-based view is more likely to improve our understanding of IT value than a conventional macroeconomic approach. Coupled with appropriate anchor measures, this approach can be a powerful tool for managing IT benefits. IT can create value in different ways to different beneficiaries. Unless management understands how and where benefits are created and has a strategy for managing these benefits systematically among the various stakeholder groups, it will not be able to maximize the return on the investment in IT.

The plan for effective assessment and management of business value must start with the alignment of business and technology strategies. Through this dialogue between business and information systems leaders priorities about IT investments are developed. We must then ask the following questions regarding each investment:

\- What is the purpose of the IT investment?

\- Who are the stakeholders and potential beneficiaries and what are their objectives?

Once we have answered these basic questions and have a clear understanding of the objectives and stakeholder interests, we need to follow-up our investment decisions with a systematic management process that includes:

\- developing operational anchor measures for assessing the value to all relevant stakeholders;

\- developing a tracking system for costs and benefits;

\- using pilot operations to get initial assessment of the benefits and their distribution;

\- managing both the costs and benefits side of the IT equation, recognizing stakeholders interests and using appropriate incentives.

## 7. Conclusions

The main contribution of this paper is to highlight the distributed nature of IT benefits and propose a stakeholder-based approach to account for the diversity of benefits and the stakeholders to whom these benefits accrue. This value-based stakeholder analysis can be a useful management tool for both justifying IT investments and for managing these investments in order to obtain maximum bottom line results. Researchers should also find this perspective useful in their efforts to investigate the relationship between IT value and its creation.

The assessment of IT business value is complex and will remain a major management challenge for the foreseeable future. As investments in IT continue to grow, management cannot afford to ignore the issue. IT benefits must be identified, measured, and managed in a systematic way if we want to gain true competitive advantage through the application of information technology.
