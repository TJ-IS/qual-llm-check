# Case study

# The role of information technology in retailing: the case of supporting fashion purchasing at a European department store chain

Claudia Loebbecke $^{a}$ , Juliane H. Kronen $^{a}$ , Tawfik Jelassi $^{b}$

$^{a}$ University of Cologne, Wilh.-Bakhaus-Str. 23, 50931 Köln, Germany $^{b}$ THESEUS/INSEAD, Fontainebleau, France

## Abstract

This paper consists of an actual case study that illustrates the increasingly strategic role of information technology (IT) in retailing in the context of a large European chain of department stores. Following changes in retailing and especially in the fashion industry, this chain has developed a strategic information system intended to improve its purchasing and distribution processes. The system, called FIPS (for 'Fashion Information and Planning System'), is integrated with the company's warehouse management and financial planning functions. It consists of two main modules: the 'Fashion Information System' and the 'Purchase Planning and Control'. The case study first reports on the business factors that led to the development of FIPS, the design features of the system, as well as its cost and benefits. It then discusses the organizational impact of the system, mainly in terms of IT-enabled innovative business procedures, changing functions and competencies within the company, and redefining the role of IT in the corporation. Finally, possible future extensions of the system are presented.

Keywords: Competitive use of information technology; Strategic information systems; Retailing; Role of IT in retailing; Business planning and monitoring

## 1. Introduction

The following case study illustrates the strategic impact of information technology (IT) on fashion purchasing at a large European department store chain. It also shows how IT can facilitate much closer integration of the purchasing and distribution functions and highlights the benefits that result from such an integration.

## 1.1. Company background

AAA is one of the largest chains of department stores in Europe, with 65000 employees and over 100 outlets of different sizes and mainly located in downtown areas. It is a typical multicategory retailer characterized by value pricing, wide assortments of goods, and offering both brand and private label merchandise. During the business year 1992/93, AAA achieved a sales growth of 7.1 per cent to almost US\$6 billion, thus exceeding the overall growth in the European department store market. During the same year, it made a profit of US\$85 million. During 1992, AAA increased its total store space by approximately 1 per cent in line with an increase of productivity by almost 2 per cent; stocks in the stores turned 3.7 times (compared with 3.9 times during the previous year) (AAA's Annual Report, 1992).

## 1.2. Changes in the business environment

The market share of European department stores compared to other retail forms has decreased by 1 per cent between 1988 and 1993, and is expected to further decrease by 1995. Too broad assortments, lack of focus, and too many different store types have resulted in lower turnover and profit. Spending on department store goods went down, especially in clothing and footwear. For AAA's turnover classified by the categories of expenditure see Table 1.

Moreover, an important trend in the industry is backward integration, i.e. the merging of retailing with functions originally performed by manufacturers. In the traditional business, the retailer simply buys and sells products; the manufacturer or importer performs the functions of design, branding, production and physical distribution. In many cases, the actual manufacturing is done by a domestic subcontractor or by an overseas factory. Recently however, retailers have increasingly integrated backwards and taken over some of the manufacturers' functions. They put their store label on designer and brand name merchandise instead of, or alongside, the brand label. $^{1}$ The design and inventory commitments are still handled by the manufacturer (see also Szyperski and Klein, 1993).

## 2. Challenges faced by AAA and resulting management changes

In addition to the above changes in retailing and in the fasion industry, a specific challenge for AAA is to improve the purchasing and distribution process of its two main merchandise groups: staple and fashion goods. Staple merchandise includes most hard goods as well as certain textiles (e.g. socks, underwear, certain home textiles such as towels). It constitutes a permanent part of the assortment and is often repurchased without any changes at a certain predefined inventory level. Typically, it is only after long periods of time that a given staple item gets replaced by a different model or taken out of the assortment.

In contrast with staple merchandise, fashion goods are usually purchased only once and are characterized by continuous changes (e.g. in product categories, colors, materials, and cuts). Moreover, since low-priced items are mainly produced in the Far East and must be ordered at least one year in advance, $^{2}$ only part of the season's fashion goods is purchased ahead of time from overseas as to reduce overstocking risk. For example, articles coming from the Far East for the winter season must be ordered in August of the previous year, while fashion purchases in Europe for the winter season are usually placed in January or February. Based on the first sales in the season, trends are recognized and translated into short-term orders placed in Europe. $^{3}$ Therefore, the challenge for AAA is to foresee and recognize these trends in an accurate and timely manner. Furthermore, in order to be as close as possible to the customer, purchasing decisions for fashion ware were made in almost each department store of the AAA chain. This decentralization process resulted in high coordination efforts and lack of synergy with respect to buying bargaining power and transportation.

Another specific feature of fashion goods which further increases the complexity of the business consists of price reductions which take place during the scales season. Caused by these apparent changes of the assortment, apparel can only be planned and controlled according to price categories. Due to the uniqueness of fashion goods, purchases cannot be based on previous figures of certain items. Moreover, in order to be close to the customer, purchases of fashion goods were made in a decentralized way. This approach required a strong coordination and prevented from gaining synergy benefits (in terms of buying power, transportation, and skills) between the stores of the chain. In 1986, AAA started to change its buying organization by drastically reducing the number of purchasing employees and making central buyers responsible for most buying activities. Stores are divided into eight regions; for each department and region there is one regional buyer. The division of rights and responsibilities between central and regional buyers depends mostly on the central buyer and the business environment.

## 3. The 'New Merchandising System'

The newly designed business and logistics concept mentioned above is backed by an integrated system approach, the ‘New Merchandising System’, covering the different phases of the concept. Through the use of the system, AAA aims to:

\- Release department managers from non sales-oriented activities so they can spend more time selling the company goods;

\- Generate processing- and storage capacity for increased business scope;

\- Benefit from additional sales opportunities by offering complete and up-to-date assortments;

\- Improve purchase prices through greater importation;

\- Lower sales price reductions by adjusting purchases to market requirements and by flexibly adapting predefined distribution lists;

\- Handle in a more cost-efficient way the goods stored in the regional warehouses.

From an architectural point of view, the ‘New Merchandising System’ consists of four operational modules and the integrated ‘Fashion Information and Planning System’ (or FIPS) (see Fig. 1). These modules and FIPS cover the whole merchandising process: from receipt of goods to delivering them to the stores. To illustrate the interlocking nature of the four modules within the system, a brief description of each module is now provided.

## 3.1. Module 'goods' core data'

This module provides common core data for staple and fashion goods since many departments purchase and sell both groups of items. In 1991, it was rolled out to all fashion departments and, in 1992, to most staple goods outlets. The main features brought up by this new module are as follows:

\- Supports a larger number of order positions per article group;

\- Provides an easy aggregation of order positions per article;

\- Allows automatic incorporation of core data;

\- Processes in the system only deliverable item sizes;

\- Keeps order positions valid until the last piece is sold;

\- Offers real-time logistics data to control the warehouses; and

\- Handles automatic requests of selected statistics and reports.

## 3.2. Module 'order management'

This module allows to place orders coming from different geographical sources and only differentiated by the goods' core data. $^{4}$ All orders are copied into the merchandising module 'Demand Poll/Distribution', thus significantly reducing redundant data input. Moreover, the goods' core data and information on the incoming goods can be extracted from the 'Demand Poll/Distribution' module and copied into the order management module. Different types of user inquiries are executed automatically by this module; examples include: order- and delivery dates, current inventory orders per week, orders without valid goods' core data, order inconsistencies, and order cancellations.

## 3.3. Module 'demand poll/distribution'

This module supports three main functions: (a) it polls the demand in the stores and transfers it to the 'Order Management' module; (b) it can use temporary goods' core data during early stages of determining the demand and sample preselections; and (c) it supports the distribution of 'pre-sales' to the stores. The distribution of volumes can be managed online according to different criteria, such as 'store-group' or 'pre-defined distribution lists'.

## 3.4. Module 'warehouse data processing'

This module controls the flow of goods in the two main central warehouses as well as in the six large distribution centres, three regional fashion centres, the storage facility for special sales goods or promotions, and in the storage facility for eye-glasses. Its main characteristics include: supporting time-critical procedures, handling high transaction volumes, and integrating several automated operational systems. Moreover, this module supports generating, updating and optimizing transportation routes as well as developing warehouse strategies for various categories of goods.

## 4. FIPS: an application supporting business planning and monitoring

This section describes the FIPS architecture as well as the system's operation and use, its cost and benefits.

## 4.1. FIPS modules

FIPS is designed as the strategic module of the ‘New Merchandising System’; it consists of the module ‘Fashion Information System’ and the module ‘Purchase Planning and Control’.

The ‘Fashion Information System’ is an integral part of the overall information system for staple and fashion goods. It handles information related to sales, inventory, and sales price reductions in the stores. Moreover, it ensures an easy and secure data input, a high degree of data consistency across different hierarchical levels, and the availability of real-time information.

The ‘Purchase Planning and Control’ module is used every six months by AAA’s central buyers to plan, taking into account the latest fashion trends, the volumes of inventory, sales, and demand of different categories of goods. The system also supports warehouse planning (through the warehouse data processing module) as well as AAA’s financial planning.

## 4.2. FIPS operation and use

AAA's 35 central buyers have been using FIPS since 1993 to make decisions regarding purchases of regular assortment items, purchases for special promotions (which constitutes 60 per cent of turnover in AAA's fashion retailing) and sales price reductions. The assumption of a maximum of 100 sales price reductions, 50 'promotion purchases', and 20 'regular assortment purchases' per month is rather conservative. A central buyer takes on average 170 decisions per month which require the analysis of 280 000 data items extracted from FIPS. $^{5}$

The second category of FIPS users consists of AAA's regional buyers, whereas department managers constitute the third user category. Approximately 900 department managers daily focus on a different set of decision categories: sales price reductions, synchronization of incoming goods and sales, and placement of goods. The manager of a department located in an ‘excellent’ store of AAA's chain typically needs 460 000 pieces of information for making approximately 120 decisions (20 sales price reductions, 50 synchronization decisions, and 50 special placements).

Grasping this information is time-consuming; on average, 35 central buyers responsible for fashion goods spend approximately 4200 hours per annum reading and analyzing the reports which they have requested. To perform the same task, the 60 regional buyers need approximately 7200 hours per year, and the 900 department managers require 87300 hours per year. $^{6}$ Altogether, FIPS supports up to 800 data requests per second, accessing approximately 250 different applications.

## 4.3. FIPS cost

Due to delays in the project schedule and to an increase in functional requirements of the system, the development cost of FIPS has significantly risen to almost US\$900 000. However, considering the fact that the estimated cost of the system during its life cycle (1993–2000) will reach US\$65 million, the development cost of FIPS accounts for less than 2 per cent of the total cost over the eight year life span (Fig. 2). $^{7}$

The major part of the cost is user cost; it is due to the nature of the system users. Supporting AAA's three user groups (central buyers, regional buyers and department managers) until the year 2000 accounts for US\$37 million, i.e. for about 60 per cent of the total cost. The more information the system contains, the more users interact with it by requesting reports and feeding data into the system. In order to control usage cost, AAA is considering (a) reducing the use of the system in terms of hours per day or types of reports available, (b) limiting the access for certain decision makers (e.g. department managers), and (c) targeting FIPS towards specific hierarchical levels within AAA and thus providing appropriate levels of data aggregation and decision support. The system provides the basis for passing the responsibility of purchasing fashion goods and the related decision authority to the central and partially to the regional buyers of the chain. Nevertheless, the department managers are arguing that they still need to buy short-term goods up to a certain financial limit in order to cater for some customer demands. Therefore, as long as purchase responsibilities are not fully centralized or decentralized, the 40 department managers in many of the 100 chain stores will continue requesting the same data as the buyers need for purchasing goods.

The operating cost of FIPS until the year 2000 is approximately US\$24 million. It stems from two main sources: the high number of data transactions per day and the corresponding database storage and manipulating costs; and the growing data communication expenses for interactions between the chain headquarters and stores. The comparatively small budget for FIPS' maintenance and user support (US\$1.5 million until the year 2000) comes mainly from the IT-User Support Group.

## 4.4. FIPS benefits

Although tangible results achieved through FIPS have been difficult to measure, the system led to several qualitative benefits. It helped AAA to manage the increasing complexity of the business; for example to cope in staple as well as in fashion ware with the increase in the volume of goods core data which rose from 250 to 10 000 per department or article group – a figure which could not be managed without adequate IT applications. Moreover, the system supported managers at different levels of the organizational structure (e.g. central buyers, regional buyers, department managers) in their decision-making tasks. These tasks typically consisted of deciding on sales price reductions, inventory levels as well as type and range of assortments in the stores. FIPS users stressed the value FIPS has added to their business activities while AAA managers stated the competitive dimension of the system. The latter results from higher turnover due to lower sales price reductions, reduced inventory, and more complete assortments in the stores.

The implementation of the new business and logistics concept and the benefits it has led to would not have been possible without an appropriate IT-based system. FIPS modularity offers the much needed flexibility to adapt AAA's business to rapidly and continuously changing customer needs, and to manage an increasing range of products. Consequently, it helps improve the market position of a large chain of department stores like AAA.

## 5. Organizational impact of FIPS

FIPS has already affected in a significant way AAA's conduct of the business and the importance of IT in the company. More specifically, it has changed the business processes underlying the purchasing and sales functions, the distribution patterns within the company, and the role of the IT-User Support Group (Loebbecke and Kronen, 1994a).

## 5.1. New business process underlying the purchase function

FIPS supports adjusting the pattern of purchasing decisions to new business needs by improving the link between different functional processes within AAA and between AAA and its suppliers. Due to the pressure to provide low-price items and to keep prices stable, the company increasingly places pre-sales abroad, whereas late orders during the seasons are placed mainly in Europe. However, the long time-span between ordering and payment on the one hand, and on the other hand sale of the goods, increases economic and decision risks. Hence, the company uses additional working capital and the risk of a misjudging fashion trends increases. Given the changing planning horizon for buying decisions, AAA needs to make a trade-off between using a longer planning period and having faster fashion cycles.

## 5.2. Strengthening purchase versus sales function

FIPS has led to a shift of purchase functions between different positions in the company. Traditionally, purchases were placed both centrally and decentrally. With FIPS, buying competencies were gradually moved to the headquarters, hence resulting in an increasingly centralized purchasing function. $^{8}$ This centralization can be justified by the following factors:

\- Gaining some economies of scale in ordering goods through the bundling of buying volumes (Clemons and Redii, 1993);

\- Having complete and better controlled assortments in the stores and thus increasing turnover and performance (for a detailed analysis of the IT control function, see Malone et al., 1987);

\- Enhancing purchase planning and automatic allocation of goods to stores which reduces the necessary stock and thus increases AAA's cashflow; and

\- Improving the flow of goods to stores by optimizing the shipping schedule specific to each store.

## 5.3. Changing role of the IT-user support group

For a company of AAA's size, appropriate data gathering in the stores, data processing and storage, and the meaningful output of lists and reports constitute difficult tasks. While the data gathering can be achieved through implementing advanced scanner or chip-card technologies, the other tasks require intelligent data aggregation and cannot be solved by technical means only. The ever-increasing volume of available data has changed the type of skills required from users. A necessary core skill, for example for making purchasing decisions, is knowing how to extract relevant information from the daily produced data. $^{9}$ Therefore, the task for the IT-User Support Group is to train the purchasing staff to use the system efficiently and effectively. Moreover, this group is in charge of collecting, analyzing, and integrating user requests for system changes, adaptations, and additions.

## 5.4. Areas for potential improvement

The integration between FIPS and the other modules of the ‘New Merchandising System’ provides a technical platform for enhancing the linkage between business processes and their IS backbones. For example, price category reports, which include regular goods, sales promotions, and sales price reductions, as well as the level of detail of the gathered information can be further improved. More specifically, due to high-price reductions, sales may seem stronger in low-price categories (which include the originally higher priced goods). Therefore, the central buyer purchases a larger volume of goods in the low-price ranges, ‘ignoring’ the effect of price reductions on profit making.

For many fashion goods, having labels with size and color information gets too expensive. In these cases, even after having scanned the sold items, the central buyer does not know if specific sizes or colors are missing in some stores and, therefore, is unable to adequately replenish the concerned stores. Moreover, in some cases, detailed data are gathered although it does not seem relevant for customers' buying behavior.

Another management issue is deciding on the appropriate number of customized systems to have in the company in order to meet specific business needs, daily routines, and personal preferences. On the one hand, each system should be as specific as possible to satisfy user preferences (e.g. those of central buyers dealing with fashion goods). On the other hand, in order to alleviate the costs of common development and support (e.g. of hardware and maintenance), there is a need to establish an integrated system for the whole company. AAA has started with the development of nine separate systems (for the different categories of goods listed in Table 1), and is currently integrating the system for staple goods into the more advanced one for fashion goods (see also Loebbecke and Kronen, 1994b).

## 6. Future extensions of FIPS and further challenges

Future extensions of FIPS can occur along two tracks: the scale and the scope of the system (Ebers, 1992).

## 6.1. Extension of scale

The scale of the system relates to the frequency and the volume at which transactions are handled. An increase in FIPS' scale can rise from three main sources: additional business following a merger or an acquisition operation; an extension of AAA's geographical scope of business activities; and an increase in the range of products (numbers of articles listed in the system) due to a further integration of AAA's current merchandising systems.

## 6.2. Extension of scope

The dimension of scope relates to the range of functions the system can perform. Three types of extension can be differentiated: increased functionality of the currently performed tasks; backward integration (previously discussed); and forward integration.

User suggestions for an increased functionality include getting system support for budget-based planning and the preparation of promotions, automatic updating of vendor accounts, and assessing the impact of proactive advertising. Moreover, FIPS should be able to track sales and to determine productivity levels per product group. $^{10}$ However, in spite of the availability in the system of large sets of data, FIPS does not allow performing a detailed analysis of profitability per item and vendor. Furthermore, it does not support the transfer of order information to the stores (currently, paper-based documents are monthly sent to department managers to tell them what goods they can expect!).

Forward integration can take place internally as well as beyond AAA's boundaries. In the former case, it consists of including in the system market research information and specialized marketing functions. FIPS would then support extracting selected information for various marketing purposes; the challenge would then be to formulate the 'right' queries to the system's database. In the latter case, forward integration would mainly require the use of telecommunications and multimedia, and the integration of AAA's value chain with that of its business partners (e.g. its suppliers, large customers, and eventually third-parties such as transportation companies). Therefore, developing technical advancements will foster AAA's extensions of scope.

On-line connections with offices abroad have already been established, while those with vendors are still in the planing stage. The introduction of electronic data interchange (EDI) links with vendors will decrease transactions cost by avoiding media disruptions, and will further improve the integration of warehouse logistics, transportation and administration. On the other hand, it will limit AAA's flexibility and require a considerable up-front investment.

## 6.3. Further challenges

Another challenge that FIPS faces stems from the business strategy of the company. AAA has recently acquired a large mail-order company and is currently preparing a merger with another large chain of department stores. Whether and, if so, how FIPS can support these growth strategies, still need to be investigated. However, there is a gap between the desirability to link AAA's FIPS with the appropriate IT systems of the acquired corporation(s) and the technical feasibility of implementing such a link. This gap must be bridged if AAA wants to reap the potential benefits created by its merger and acquisition decisions.

Moreover, having assessed on one hand the strengths and the weaknesses of FIPS, and, on the other hand, the technical, financial and political constraints of the system's further extension, AAA top-management realizes the opportunities that lie ahead to further improve the system. These opportunities, which spell the future of the strategic use of IT at AAA, were summarized by the company Chief Information Officer as follows:

The new technologies (relational data bases, data communication) and the new system will provide important options for future solutions to business challenges: online dialogs and graphic interfaces will make information easier accessible and readable. Menu-driven applications will allow users to determine themselves how their reports should look like. Direct data exchange with vendors will lead to even faster and more differentiated reactions to market requirements. Finally, the customer will be able to participate in the information, to check assortments, specific items and their sales locations, as well as to place specific orders.
