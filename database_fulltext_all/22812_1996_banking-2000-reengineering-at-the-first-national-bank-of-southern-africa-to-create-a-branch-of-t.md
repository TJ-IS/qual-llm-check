# Case study

# Banking 2000? Reengineering at the First National Bank of Southern Africa to create a branch of the future

Dan Remenyi $^{a,*}$ , Bill Cinnamond $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Curtis Farm, Kidmore End, Reading RG4 9AY, UK $^{b}$ Information Systems Audit, KPMG, Carlton, International Trade Centre, P.O. Box 7400, Johannesburg 2000, South Africa

## Abstract

This case study describes how a major bank reorganised, restructured and retrained a branch in order to solve an operational and financial problem. It applied well-trusted information technology in an innovative way to improve client throughput, increase turnover and raise staff productivity. This was undertaken to simultaneously show the way to the rest of the organisation as to how retail banking should be conducted in the 21st century. The branch of the future showed how reengineering could be used to substantially improve a branch's performance while increasing its attractiveness to the bank's clients. This new approach to retail banking is referred to by the authors as Banking 2000? or the branch of the future.

Keywords: Reengineering; Reorganisation; Information technology; Future; Change

## 1. Introduction

This case study describes how a major bank reengineered a branch in order to solve an operational and financial problem and to show the way to the rest of the organisation as to how retail banking should be conducted in the 21st century. This reengineering exercise required the reorganisation of a traditional branch as well as the application of the bank's information technology resources is a novel way. This new approach to retail banking is referred to by the authors as Banking 2000 or the branch of the future. Although this application of information technology provides some insights as to how the technology may be used strategically in the banking sector it also raises a number of questions about the form future banking may take. Thus the title of the paper is, Banking 2000?

The retail banking industry in South Africa is very competitive. There are approximately 20 banks in the country, all of which have commercial banking operations. Only the larger banks offer a full range of modern financial services which include corporate electronic delivery mechanisms. There are four big players which constitute more than 90 per cent of the market.

The four big banking groups are, in alphabetical order, Associated Banks of South Africa (ABSA), First National Bank of Southern Africa (FNB), Nedbank and Standard Bank of South Africa.

ABSA, which is the largest banking group, is the end product of a large number of banking mergers over the years bringing together some medium sized and relatively small banks, some of which had specialised in the Afrikaans speaking market over the years. These mergers were concluded in 1992. A number of members of the ABSA group have been in existence for quite some time, with Allied Building Society dating back to 1880, United Building Society to 1889 and Volkskas was established in 1934.

First National Bank, which is similar in size to the Standard Bank, may be regarded as a comparatively new bank in the sense that it has only operated under this name since 1987. FNB was created as a result of the disinvestment policies of the British bank, Barclays Bank plc. However Barclays Bank DCO had roots in South Africa dating back to 1838 and as such FNB sees itself as the inheritor of this tradition.

Nedbank, which is the smallest of the big four, has its roots in the Nederlandsche Bank en Creditverreening which was established in Amsterdam in 1888 by a Dutch group of financiers. The bank changed its name to the Nederlandsche Bank Creditverreening voor Zuid-Afrika in 1903. The bank remained in Dutch hands until 1969 when it became a public company and was listed on the Johannesburg Stock Exchange. The name of the bank was changed to Nedbank Limited in 1971. As it was South African owned it did not change hands in the 1980s in the same way as Barclays or Standard Banks. Nedbank Limited merged with the South African Permanent Building Society in 1988.

The Standard Bank of South Africa, which is one of the oldest banks in the country dating back to the 1860s, has been through a similar process to FNB, having been originally owned by the Standard Chartered Bank of the United Kingdom.

Although the banking industry in South Africa is not as deregulated as it is in some parts of the world, all four of these banks offer a wide range of financial services to both corporate and personal clients. These services range from money market dealings to home loans, from share issues to forward buying of foreign currencies for clients.

It is also important to note that the South African banking sector has made extensive use of information technology for quite some time. South African banks have been computerised since about 1964. The computerised banking systems in South

Africa are generally regarded as being among the most sophisticated anywhere in the world which is evidenced by the sophisticated and user friendly customer interfaces as well the on-line clearance of negotiable instruments. Many of the banks have real-time update capability for their own customers. Furthermore, FNB was named one of the world's top 100 computer users by ComputerWorld magazine in May 1995. In July 1996 FNB won the Smithsonian prize for the innovative application of biometrics in their information technology.

This case study is about FNB which has approximately 550 branches, 300 agencies and employs approximately 30 000 staff members. FNB's declared strategy is to be a bank for all the people of Southern Africa and to supply a full range of banking services.

## 2. Background

Traditional banking worked well for Barclays Bank DCO and the renamed FNB for over 150 years. However the original principles of banking on which this success was based have been under pressure throughout the world for at least the past 20 years.

Traditional banking was based around a well understood paradigm, which has been described in a somewhat jocular fashion by the bank's general manager of information technology Mr Mike Jarvis, as the 3-6-3 rule. The 3-6-3 rule means that bankers should borrow money at 3 per cent, lend the money at 6 per cent and be on the golf course by 3 o'clock in the afternoon. In reality the bank's ability to make the profit it requires on the margin between what it pays to its depositors and what it earns on its advances has been under severe pressure for years. This is because there is today, and has been for some time, a much smarter investor looking for the maximum return on funds and a sharper borrower seeking the lowest interest rate available. In addition there is increasing competition between the banks and thus finer rates, than was previously the case, are frequently offered. Furthermore the operating cost of the branch network has increased considerably both in terms of staff and premises expenses. In fact in general banks' profits have come under considerable pressure (Liechtfuss and Mattern, 1996).

The result of this is that the cost of lending is no longer as inexpensive for banks as it used to be. One particular example is that 20 years ago many organisations left sizeable amounts in current accounts which did not earn interest from the banks. Today many organisations have treasury functions which ensure that they earn as

## 2.1. Banking in South Africa

Although the interest turn of the bank is still a most important contributor to its profitability, non-interest income, which is earned from services which the banks provide, has become increasingly significant. This may be seen from Table 1 which shows the relative value of these two incoming items to FNB over the past three years.

Table 1 clearly shows a decline in interest generated income while there has been a definite increase in non-interest generated income.

Like their counterparts in many other parts of the world. South African banks are past masters at minimising their risk by filtering out any financially questionable prospective clients. This is, of course, not to say that the South African banks have not had their share of bad debts over the years. $^{2}$ However, with the collapse of apartheid they are looking to extend their services to those they would not have accepted as clients only a few years ago. This is in part a political decision which is referred to as reaching the unbanked, $^{3}$ of whom it is estimated there may be as many as 10 to 15 million such people in South Africa, out of a total population of approximately 43 million. Central to this issue is the bank's ability to process the banking requirements of large numbers of people at low costs and it is believed that this can only be achieved by the application of innovative information technology.

Another change issue which the banks are facing is the fact that they have traditionally operated in a very strict command and control environment in which functional silos or stovepipes ensure all the rules are obeyed by their staff.

Changing circumstances in South Africa, partly due to rapid urbanisation during the past two decades and also due to the crumbling and final collapse of apartheid, have resulted in an increased number of customers and therefore, transaction volumes. Furthermore the world-wide increase in customer demands for a higher level of service and lower banking costs, from which South Africans were not immune, severely impacted FNB's profitability. This had occurred by the start of the 1990s to such an extent that some of the bank's seven regions were no longer earning the required corporate return on the funds invested in them.

Furthermore, the retail banking experience in South Africa had become a fairly dismal affair for the individual bank client. This was primarily due to the very high level of urban crime which has become a regular feature of South African life. Criminal activity included a strong interest in banks and their cash and for some years banks were a prime target for armed raiders. As a result banks installed heavy duty bullet-proof glass in front of their tellers which immediately distanced the teller from the client and removed an important element of intimacy between the bank employees and their customers. In addition many of the banks installed metal detecting double doored cubicles at the front entrances through which all members of the public have to pass before gaining access to the bank's premises. These cubicles are expensive to install and to operate. They are not popular with the bank's clients and their removal is seen as a positive development.

## 3. The catalysts for change and the key change issues

Their were a number of important drivers behind the need for change in FNB. From the above description of banking in South Africa it appears that there is substantial room for improvement in productivity and customer service. The branch of the future or Banking 2000 concept is the product of two initiatives which were set in motion at the start of the 1990s by Mr Willie Willers (General Manager, Central Transvaal region) and Mr Mike Jarvis (General Manager, IT). It is worth noting that neither Willie Willers or Mike Jarvis embarked upon this project because they felt strongly that the branch network needed enhancement. It can be argued that their view contrasts with the views of others who claim than branch banking may in the long run be obsolete (Mendonca and Nakache, 1996).

In 1990, the General Manager of the then Central Transvaal region was promoted, and was replaced by Willie Willers, a younger, progressive man with a Masters of Business Administration degree from the Witwatersrand Graduate School of Business. Willers who had joined the bank as a young man had seen service in many different departments and functions and previous to this appointment had been the assistant general manager of corporate banking. Willers, a dynamic manager who does not believe in incremental change, but in quantum leaps, immediately set about the creation of a novel vision of future banking which he communicated throughout his region. His attitude corresponds well to that of Hammer and Champy (1990; 1993) and Davenport (1993) who are advocates of business process reengineering and thus of radical change. Willers' timeframe for these changes in his region was five years. He began by setting a number of management development initiatives in motion to achieve his bank of the future $^{4}$ vision. The most important of these were:

## 3.1. Leadership focus

One of the urgent problems which Willers faced was an unacceptable level of staff turnover. By 1990 the then Central Transvaal region, which employed 3001 staff, was experiencing a phenomenal staff turnover of up to 1 in 3 in some extreme cases. To

Willers this was a major problem as his region was losing experienced bankers and by and large the staff lost were being replaced with relative newcomers to the industry. Having closely analysed the situation, he came to the conclusion that the branches which were managed by individuals who were achievement oriented and who showed aptitude for leadership, were retaining their staff while the others were losing them. Thus Willers focused on a programme of leadership development for critical individuals in his region. This was attained through a series of workshops to engender the concept of a winning mindset $^{5}$ (Ball and Asbury, 1990), and the need to be achievement oriented, among his branch management.

Willers realised that he needed to create a momentum of continuous change in his region and thus his change programme was designed to develop leadership skills to ensure that management would obtain the full commitment of the staff in their organisations.

## 3.2. Sales focus

As business success is usually achieved through the attainment of more revenue. Willers emphasised the need for a better level of selling skills in the region. This was impressed on the region through a series of workshops and extensive communication to attain a quantum leap in sales, therefore attaining the infrastructure's ‘critical mass’. This initiative supported the objective of increased revenue and profitability.

## 3.3. Urgency

To emphasise the importance of these programmes Willers made sure his initiatives were perceived as being urgent. He personally stressed to his management the need to build this attitude into all aspects of everyone's jobs, he has coined the phrase 'energised' which is followed throughout the region.

## 3.4. Restructuring of the traditional branch

Prior to Willers' arrival as General Manager a typical traditional branch in the Gauteng region had seven or eight hierarchical levels of staff. Willers was aware that this structure often detracted the staff from the client focus which he felt was essential to the function of the branch of the future. As a consequence he introduced a new approach of branch organisation which he refers to as the value chain $^{6}$ model. Branch organisation before and after these changes is shown in Fig. 1.

This change resulted in a flatter structure in the branch only three levels $^{7}$ of staff. It also reduced bureaucratic management posts. This directly supports improvements in productivity, better customer contact and increased profitability. The front line manager in a branch can now have as many as 21 individuals reporting to him or her. This gives the manager a much greater awareness of what is actually happening in the branch. This is in keeping with the trend suggested by Cantrell and Colby (1993). The reorganisation also resulted in more direct reports into Willers personally, at the Regional Head Office. In fact he now has 45 people reporting directly to him. $^{8}$

## 3.5. The retail marketing concept

One of Willers' strongly held views is that banking needs to adopt a retail marketing approach. Thus the bank's branches should be managed in terms of some of the principles of retailing. This is reflected in his desire to see greater customer interface, use of the space available in all the branches, and his notion that the bank's resources should be measured in terms of their ability to generate revenue and profit.

## 3.6. Quantum change

Willers is committed to the notion that little can be achieved by incremental change and thus he placed considerable store on the notion of the quantum leap being the way to ensure business success. He believes that marginal or incremental change frequently leads to temporary change which can be relatively easily reversed when the project has been completed.

## 3.7. The information technology dimension

Initially the branch of the future project had been undertaken by Mike Jarvis, the General Manager of IT in 1991. The objective of this project was to use FNB's enormous investment in an information technology infrastructure, as well as its substantial technological expertise and experience, to gain further competitive advantage. It would achieve this by creating strategic information systems which would deliver a shopping centre wide client server system. This system would provide benefits to both merchants and customers within the shopping complex. This would enable the bank to more completely service existing merchants and thus ensure their continued custom or support as well as attract new business..

Jarvis joined the bank in 1985 and had immediately become champion of the use of information technology, not only to maximise the organisation's efficiency and effectiveness, but also to gain a competitive advantage, primarily by increasing the bank's market share. Under Jarvis's leadership, changes to the controversial Hogan $^{9}$ system, which had given the bank a considerable amount of trouble, were finalised and the system was fully integrated into the bank's business. Jarvis' success is seen by the fact that according to a leading South African stockbroker (McAllister, 1994) FNB uses its information technology in the most progressive manner in the country. Also, as mentioned above, FNB has been named one of the world's top 100 computer users by ComputerWorld magazine in May 1995.

## 3.8. Line management commitment

Initially some useful progress was made by Jarvis with this initiative in securing an interest of top management within FNB. However no formal mandate was established, and the project was not furnished with adequate resources in the form of funds or a dedicated team, headed up at the appropriate level of management. Thus enthusiasm for the idea of the branch of the future as it was expressed by Jarvis soon lost momentum and by late 1991 it had somewhat fizzled out. This initial attempt lost momentum due to the fact that FNB had not appointed a line manager to champion the project. Fortunately Willers, who has line responsibility and who saw the concept as a major vehicle to improve the performance of his region, and a way to assist with the achievement of his longer term vision of banking, assumed ownership of the project in October 1993.

## 4. The visions

Willers' vision of what he could achieve by reengineering his region is best expressed in terms of the following:

to significantly improve the performance of his region while ensuring that the service delivered to both personal and corporate clients was enhanced by reorganising the way a branch functioned.

According to members of the project team, Willers' vision was not always readily accepted throughout the bank. On many occasions Willers' personality was pitted against the bank's management establishment and it was only his tough minded conviction that the branch of the future was the only way forward that ensured the continuation of the project.

Central to Jarvis' vision was to use FNB's established large scale information technology and backbone data and telecommunications network to provide a value added service to customers. At the heart of this vision was the belief that the bank's information technology infrastructure could be leveraged to provide a real payoff in terms of increased productivity and improved customer service. FNB's infrastructure requires enormous investment and is not always perceived as delivering an adequate return. This view supported the notion of using the established investment and expertise to gain a further competitive advantage over the other South African banks.

The combination of these two quite different visions, which bring together information technology and management, may be seen as one of the major contributing factors to the success of the bank of the future project.

## 5. The decision

The decision to commit large resources to this project was not undertaken without considerable thought and analysis. The slow deterioration in performance over the previous years meant that the returns required to satisfy the bank's stakeholders would not be available unless a direct intervention was undertaken to reduce costs and to increase revenue.

Willers had been thorough with his homework and had considered a number of alternatives to achieve these objectives. These included:

## 5.1. To create one or more additional banking outlets

This was the traditional method in which the bank sought increased revenue and profit or attempted to resolve a customer or service overload issue. Willers' region has approximately 69000 square meters of banking premises which represents a very substantial expense. Increasing the branch network would only add to the problem and thus this route to solving the current challenge was relatively easy to discount as a viable option in this case due to the high and steadily escalating cost patterns of the traditional branch network resulting in the relatively low returns.

## 5.2. Upgrade existing premises

Upgrading would involve extending the existing branches, and increasing the number or complement of staff. This option was rejected for the same reasons as the previous one. Although it could possibly solve the service problem, it would impact heavily on the already fragile returns and therefore not produce the increases required in profit and growth.

## 5.3 Implement the branch of the future project

This was eventually chosen as the best option, as it was anticipated that by amending processes and supporting staff with technology, customer service could be radically improved. Although this option was expensive, it was anticipated that with the increased customer throughput, substantial economies of scale could be achieved and this would have the required effect on the profit and the return on investment.

Clearly the first two options would not address the underlying problems of productivity and return on investment faced by FNB. Neither amounted to a new strategic direction which the bank felt it needed in relation to its branch network, and thus option three was considered the only way forward.

## 5.4. The design of the branch of the future

It had been realised for some time that FNB needed a new approach to operating individual branches. During the 1990–1993 period, two of the bank's staff, Dave Burke and Liz Pollard, visited a large number of banks in the United Kingdom, the USA, Switzerland and the Far East, looking for new ideas. This investigation may be seen as a rudimentary benchmarking exercise. Subsequent to this a firm of management consultants were engaged to assist the bank in ascertaining what their clients thought and felt about their branch operation. The consultants arranged five focus groups, each consisting of 20 clients of the bank. These focus groups met for a substantial amount of time over a number of evenings and much of the information supplied by them was fed into the design of the branch of the future.

Underlying the design of the branch of the future is the need to optimise the utilisation of very expensive office and shop space. Traditionally a branch uses 70 per cent of its space for backroom paper processing and 30 per cent of its space for the client interface. As the cost of high street office and shop space has been escalating at 10 to 20 per cent per annum in South Africa over the past decade, these proportions are now regarded as not being optimal. The backroom operations need to be conducted is a smaller area giving more space for the real business of the bank which is serving clients. This is one of the key objectives of the bank of the future project.

## 6. Starting the project

Once Willers had committed himself to the branch of the future option, he formalised a clear mandate, albeit different from the initial one proposed by Jarvis, with the following objectives:

\- create a high touch, high technology value chain environment model;

\- improve customer service;

\- provide a quantum leap improvement in queue management;

\- facilitate excellent financial performance;

\- expedite outstanding volume growth;

\- allow for a reduction in staff numbers;

\- field a physical redesign that is state-of-the art.

Willers divided the project into two phases, phase one would run from January 1994 to March 1995 and would concentrate mainly on direct customer interfaces, as it was agreed this was the area where most impact could be made. The second phase began in October 1995 with the reengineering of branch processes using enabling technologies such as image processing and office automation.

A pilot site had to be chosen. After considerable deliberation it was decided to choose the FNB at Cresta shopping centre in Randburg. Cresta is a well-established shopping centre dating back to 1976. It is located some eight kilometres north west of central Johannesburg. It is in a moderately populated area of middle to high income families. There are more than 100 shops in Cresta, including major grocery chains, fashion stores, discount shops, boutiques, restaurants and fast food outlets drawing many tens of thousands of shoppers each week. Several different banks are represented in this shopping centre.

This branch was selected mainly because the centre was undergoing a major refurbishment and renovation. This was going to force the bank to undergo an expensive refurbishment of the branch in any event, and it was decided to undertake the changes in line with branch of the future design concepts. In addition, it was envisaged (after meetings with the centre management) that Cresta would have:

\- a reasonably diverse customer population;

\- the appropriate physical layout and shop front;

\- a wide range of merchants.

Finally the Cresta branch was regarded as a suitable test site for the ideas of the branch of the future because it was perceived as being a very average branch and therefore if the new system worked there it would probably work elsewhere just as well.

## 7. The branch of the future

The branch of the future will look and feel quite different to the branches in the current bank network and so a number of major alterations were made to the physical layout.

## 7.1. Physical layout

The original Cresta branch was identical in layout to most other branches within the group. As mentioned above it was made up of approximately 30 per cent customer space, and 70 per cent factory type back office space.

The first major change was the removal of cash from the front of the branch. This was achieved by the implementation of a substantial amount of technology including the teller cash dispenser (TCD) and the customer details imaging systems. The TCD resulted in the security glass traditionally found in the bank's branches being removed from in front of the teller's workstation. This allowed the project to change the teller's role from that of handling monetary transactions to that of a multi-skilled service consultant. In the branch of the future, when clients wish to deposit cash and cheques the money is accepted by the service consultant and after being checked it is inserted in a chute which carries it to a vault away from the customer access area. When a client requires cash the service consultant uses the TCD which performs a similar role to an automatic teller machine to extract the correct amount of cash for the client. Thus effectively the service consultant is not in possession of any cash except a few coins which are kept in case a client wants a precise amount of cash.

As a result of this, the teller's counter was lowered and chairs provided, as it was expected that the customer would now spend far greater time with a service consultant. However, as most of the customer's needs were being met by one multi-skilled individual, the customer would not be required to be ferried from one individual bank employee to another who would deal with specific issues. It was therefore expected that overall the customer would spend considerably less time in the branch.

In addition to the above, other changes include:

\- Easily accessible customer interview cubicles, staffed by specialists and supported by sophisticated IT systems to deal, with more intricate customer requirements.

\- A central open information desk or cubicle which acts as a customer enquiries counter. This is not the traditional post which dealt with account queries, but is used to direct customers to the appropriate area of the branch. In addition, this cubicle acts as a promotional and sales office for affiliated products such as airline ticket purchases, cellular telephones, Videobank and Speedpoints sales.

\- A shop-like front window where FNB products are displayed.

Another important aspect of the branch layout was the availability of the information self-service device. This was prominently displayed in the front of the bank so that clients could help themselves to information. This helped reduce staff and space requirements.

Thus on completion of phase one, the physical layout of the branch had changed dramatically, whereby approximately 53 per cent of the branch space is occupied by bank staff and 47 per cent of the space utilised by the client interface functions. Although this is a considerable improvement on the traditional space utilisation position, it is still believed in the bank that there is room for more improvement.

## 8. The information technology

The information technology philosophy underpinning the branch of the future project was to use well established and proven technology in an innovative way to support the major changes required. It was specifically part of FNB information technology strategy with respect to this project that they would not attempt to employ leading or bleeding edge technologies, although some members of the staff felt that the imaging technology employed was actually or very close to leading edge.

It is generally accepted that information technology is the enabler for business process reengineering, and this has played a central role in the bank of the future project. Although many alternatives were investigated with respect to peripheral hardware and support software, the FNB networks and their operating system were assumed to be the basic standard required. This standard was insisted upon because the bank did not wish to deviate from its communications network technology base that was uniform throughout all the branches in South Africa. In addition, an important objective of its technology selection was re-usability and ease of implementation in other areas when the project was successful.

Specifying the hardware and software requirement for the branch of the future was not a trivial task. This was undertaken by Liz Pollard who ensured that standards were complied with and that the new arrangements were consistent with the bank's overall information technology architecture. Liz Pollard was the head of the Organisation and Methods Department and her mandate was to identify and streamline processes throughout FNB. In addition to this hardware and software specification work, Pollard also designed the staff development programmes which were necessary to ensure that the staff at Cresta would be properly trained and correctly oriented towards the new arrangements. This resulted in the transition process for the majority of the staff at Cresta being relatively smooth and thereby allowed the branch to take advantage of the new arrangements with the minimum delay.

The main applications used to create the branch of the future may be considered under three headings. These are: the automation of the client interface process; the automation of the client support services; and the paper factory or back room operation.

## 8.1. The client interface process

This refers to how the bank employee, that is the service consultant, directly works with the client and can be discussed in terms of four key systems which are, branch automation, the application of TCDs, the use of a customer details imaging system, and the cross selling system.

## 8.1.1. Branch automation

The use of large scale processors together with a high speed backbone underpins the technological thrust of the bank of the future. In addition high-powered workstations connected to a client server system constitute the cornerstone of the technology provided to the staff of the branch of the future.

The hardware aspect is made up of a client server system comprising an IBM PS2/486 model 95 fileserver and 22 PS2/486 model 80 workstations.

Fourteen of these workstations are available to the tellers. Each of the teller workstations have a peripheral called a 'toaster' (as it looks like one). The toaster has that ability to:

\- read the magnetic ink character recognition (MICR) details on a cheque;

\- read, and where applicable write to a smartcard or magstripe;

\- record images documents.

The software component runs under OS/2, and provides real time updating to the host via graphical user interface screens. This connectivity through the high speed backbone, coupled with the toaster provides the ability to implement electronic validation. It also facilitates the use of preventative, as opposed to traditional paper based detective controls.

## 8.1.2. The teller cash dispenser (TCD)

The TCD device dispenses cash, and has a separate compartment for the acceptance of deposits. Although the system resides on the branch automation platform, it is not yet fully integrated with branch automation via application program interfaces (APIs). This means that the teller has to key in the amount of the transaction twice.

On the whole, the branch has adapted extremely well to the TCD operation and has implemented some effective operating practices and controls. An example of this is that the teller no longer needs to count cash that is dispensed to the customer. The TCD has reduced teller's differences by a factor of 28 times from March 1994 to March 1995.

## 8.1.3. Customer details imaging system

This system operates on the branch automation OS/2 platform but as yet, it is not fully integrated with branch automation via APIs. It is envisaged that by generating images of a customer's identification and signature for tellers and supervisors when certain transactions or events occur, customer service will be further dramatically improved and client loss will be reduced.

The Cresta branch has 20 000 accounts of which approximately 40 per cent hold supportive documentation relating to additional signing powers etc. It is obvious that where a number of accounts are held by the same customer, an image of their identification document as well as a customer signature card need only be held once.

Even though the request for an image is a conscious manual process, time savings on accessing a signature card have reduced from an average of five minutes for that of a manual search to five seconds on-line.

## 8.1.4. Cross selling system

This application is a rule based system specifically designed to facilitate cross-selling opportunities when certain circumstances occur. For example, if a pensioner deposits R10 000 the system would suggest that the customer should rather open a saving account where he or she would earn better interest, and the bank would benefit by being able to charge the customer for utilisation of an additional account.

The system also has batch rules, whereby the branch manager, or account executive can enter a series of questions. The system will then interrogate the local database and produce a variety of outputs ranging from a simple list, to computer generated letters. It also has a diary system which will ensure that future actions are performed.

## 8.2. Client support services

These systems are designed to help clients obtain information about the bank's services without having to meet with several members of staff to get information about different services.

## 8.2.1. Information self service devices

This is a high resolution colour interactive touch screen, with sound capability. It is simple to operate, as it 'talks' the customer through the system. It provides functionality such as:

\- product knowledge;

• estimates on loans;

• purchasing of shares;

\- requesting a visit from a product specialist;

\- linking existing accounts (for nominated electronic payments).

The implementation of this device, as well as the multi functional teller and the information desk has eliminated the need for enquiries clerks, public relations officers and product executives.

## 8.3. The paper factory and back room operations

The current reengineering initiative focused on how the branch could improve its customer interface. Little attention was placed on the issues relating to how the back room operation or paper factory which lies behind the customer interface would be reengineered. It was intended that the difficult issues $^{10}$ relating to the paper factory would be tackled during phase two of the reengineering operation. However, even the customer oriented systems partially automate the reporting that the bank needs to retain up-to-date information about the arrangements clients have with the bank. These systems already greatly reduced the amount of paper and procedures previously required to maintain this important information. For example, the piloting of an automated F90(a) process (this reports when a customer is over his arrangements with the Bank) has reduced the production of new F90(a)'s from five days to one day. It has reduced the number of actions from 29 steps to 9. This has the potential of massive savings in high cost manpower, as credit managers could be centralised in a 'warehouse' type operation.

## 9. Process identification

Phase one of the project, which ended in March 1995, concentrated on the client interface which is the front line of the branch. In terms of the activities directly associated with the clients 46 branch processes were identified and mapped and a number of key workflows on the front-line were reengineered. These included:

(1) The collection of deposits in the form of both negotiable instruments and cash was radically changed. In the new environment the client consultants perform a cursory examination of the deposit slip with the accompanying funds and commit them to a chute which delivers them to a secure area far from the banking hall.

(2) Client consultants have access to only coins. When a client requires bank notes the consultant obtains these through the use of the specialised ATM.

(3) Validation of signatures was obtained through a process employing optical character readers which reduced the average time of this process from three minutes to 30 seconds.

(4) The opening account procedures efficiency was improved by 64 per cent with a reduction in activities from 71 to 25.

The work involved in the identification of these processes was conducted by internal staff supported by IBM consultants.

However, as mentioned above the branch is still substantially paper-bound and procedure intensive due to group policies that dictate day-to-day operations and which create major inefficiencies due to discontinuity and duplication in the branch functions. This is being addressed with phase two of the project which began in October 1995. The philosophy articulated by Willers which will underpin phase two in High-Touch, High-Tech, Low-Doc which suggests the removal of much of the traditional processes and associated paper work.

## 10. Human resource issues

In the bank's traditional branches, the division of labour creates narrow jobs for staff members that make one-client-banker servicing, in the sense of one bank employee taking care of all the customers needs, impossible. However, with the branch of the future and the multi-skilling of the service consultants, the idea of one-point banking becomes a reality as customers can have all their needs attended to by the one customer consultant. This of course means considerable retraining of the branch staff.

The management function for the value chain model required a team-based management development approach. Re-learning was difficult for the management team and it was very apparent to the project team that the power of the culture change could not be underestimated. Two of the five original managers at the Cresta branch have requested a transfer to other branches.

In general the staff at Cresta who were mostly traditional bankers and thus somewhat resistant to change did not rush to embrace the new arrangements. However as the project proceeded, the benefits of the branch of the future slowly proved themselves to all those who worked with the new systems and today the project is regarded by those individuals who stayed involved with the changes as a great improvement.

## 14. Lessons learned

A number of important lessons were learned by FNB during the process of formulating and implementing the branch of the future project.

The first of these lessons is that this project is more a leadership and change management project than an information technology application project. Information technology was a secondary issue. This is supported by Dixon et al. (1994) when they said:

Contrary to the conclusion drawn from the most famous reengineering success stories, information technology was not the most critical enabler of reengineering efforts we studied.

From the start, Willers vision was not wholly dependent on the application of information technology but encompassed much broader issues. This supported the view expressed by Hackett (1990) when he pointed out,

Adding high-powered automation to an obsolete operating environment dangerously institutionalises inefficient and ineffective operating procedures, making it difficult to streamline and update operation — and to achieve optimal payoff from technology investments.

This focus on management is evidenced by the fact that line management in FNB was the only effective project champion and without Willers' tough minded commitment the project would have floundered in a similar way to Jarvis' first attempt. Willers' initial leadership training for the management in his division was a critical success factor of this project. Furthermore the project champion's ability to convince staff from the highest level to the lowest level of the bank was essential to the success of the project. Part of this lesson is not to expect the staff who are being exposed to the change to automatically embrace it willingly. The new arrangements must be carefully and thoroughly sold to them.

The second learning point was that FNB's enormous investment in its information technology infrastructure was indeed exploitable and this was proved by the fact that many different technologies were relatively easily and rapidly integrated at Cresta to deliver the unique service required for FNB's interpretation of the bank of the future. This suggests that investment in information technology which does not immediately show a return can eventually lead to a substantial competitive advantage. As a direct result of infrastructure investments made in a previous period, Cresta has been able to easily utilise this technological base to create new applications and thereby increase its throughput of clients, its turnover of revenue and its profitability. This was a most satisfactory outcome from the point of view of the senior information technology executives who are frequently hard pressed to demonstrate the benefits of infrastructural investment in information technology.

The third lesson is that it is not possible to skimp on the investment either in the technology or the people. This project involved substantial investment in various types of additional information technology and these investments were critical to the success achieved. Furthermore, extensive education and training was essential in order that the staff saw the value of the new arrangements and thus accept ownership of the system.

Finally from a survey of bank clients using Cresta, conducted by independent consultants, it was clearly established that they are very pleased with FNB's interpretation of the branch of the future. Generally clients feel that the service has improved and that the bank has shown more interest in them.

The first indications are that a satisfactory ROI $^{11}$ is being made, but the general feelings of the staff involved in the project was expressed by one individual who declared that ‘you ain’t seen nothing yet!’. This comment suggests that the bank of the future technology will revolutionise the way that FNB will function in the future. However, it must be realised that the achievements described in this paper were not accomplished easily, and it has been difficult to ascertain the strain placed on the infrastructure to attain the growth discussed here. Only the future will tell if the bank of the future initiative and its consequent growth are sustainable, or if in fact Willers has ‘squeezed the last drop of blood out of the stone’.

## 15. Discussion

There is much debate as to how banking will develop over the coming years (Wuffi and Hunt, 1993; Leichtfuss and Mattern, 1996; Mendonca and Nakache, 1996). It is likely that the changes which have been seen in banking over the past 30 years will continue, perhaps at an increasing rate into the 21st century. It is not possible to predict whether the types of changes which this period has seen will continue on an incrementally or linearly basis. It may also change on a radical or exponential basis. Furthermore there could be a large scale paradigm shift, which will really accelerate the pace of change in the way the banking community conducts its business. The source of these changes will not only be information technology, but also management practices, demographics, globalisation, to mention only a few of the more important drivers. To succeed in this environment a combination of technology and management vision are required. The need for such a wide, multi-disciplinary vision is supported by Davidow and Malone (1992) when they said

[In the Information Economy] there is danger in believing that technological supremacy is enough to revitalise and keep our corporations competitive. The journey to this new industrial paradigm requires more than just engineering. It will challenge us to re-think the role of every office, every laboratory and every factory workstation in the company. This technological innovation alone cannot save us.... In every case, technology will be subordinate to, not a substitute for, a complete understanding of the market and business.

An incremental or linear change model would suggest that as information technology becomes more powerful and less expensive it will progressively be used to improve corporate efficiency and effectiveness, and will enhance client service in order to provide a competitive advantage. The key concepts here will be productivity tools, ease of use, user friendliness and support for the basic banking process which has been in place for hundreds of years. Branch networks will continue to be the main mechanisms for delivering banking services, of course, supported by automatic tellers and other similar devices. Some specialised branches may be developed (Mendonca and Nakache, 1996). This model of future development suggests a staff reduction of perhaps 30 to 40 per cent across the whole banking community. However in South Africa, due to the increasing demand to service the unbanked, and due to pressure not to downsize as a result of the Reconstruction and Development Programme (RDP $^{12}$ ), it is unlikely that there will be any reduction, and certainly no material reduction in staff in the foreseeable future. Changes in management thinking will cause a greater degree of decentralisation as staff empowerment becomes a reality. The demographics of the unbanked will see great pressure being placed on the reduction of transaction costs. Globalisation will mean more competition from abroad, but probably only in the specialised corporate sector.

The radical or exponential change model would suggest that information technology will fundamentally change how banking is performed. Money handling will be devolved away from branches to far more intelligent automatic tellers and banks will have to commit considerable resources to reassuring the public that such procedure are secure (Mendonca and Nakache, 1996). Of course there will still be branch networks but these will be relatively small and specialised. Much of the cash handling will be undertaken by institutions which will collaborate with banks. These will be supermarkets, service stations, railway stations etc. In this model it is possible to envisage the staff requirement shrinking to less than 50 per cent of those currently employed. Management practices will have to be even more radical with much greater emphasis on strategic alliances, partnerships and client service.

A large scale paradigm shift means forgetting much of what is currently believed about banking and starts with the question ‘Why are banks needed?’ Has the need which brought banks into existence about 500 years ago continued or does society face a different set of requirements and challenges?

Thus what is the function of a bank in the 21st century? This type of question could trigger a number of socio-technological paradigm shifts and probably banking developments. Issues such as home banking and branchless banking are points with which this discussion may be started. Although many members of the middle classes live without much use of cash, it is probably true to say that the working classes still make more use of bank notes and coins. However perhaps in the next 10 years this will change with developments such as electronic cash, smart cards, electronic wallets, etc. (Mendonca and Nakache, 1996). If this third model comes to pass then the banks may be able to downsize by as much as 75 per cent of staff. This would result in there being only jobs for 25 per cent of the current staff complement and only a very small number of branches. The socio-economics of such a radical change in banking practices are enormous and clearly beyond the scope of a paper such as this.

In any discussion of the bank of the future these issues and these alternative paradigms need to be considered. The work undertaken by FNB for their branch of the future suggests that their thinking is largely along the lines of the first suggestion, that is the liner model, although there is some activity in other parts of the bank related to working closer with supermarkets as well as home banking. It is probably inevitable that a large institution such as FNB would take this approach which focuses on improving the branch network because of its history and the enormous emotional and financial capital which has been invested in conducting its business in the traditional way.

Put into perspective the work done by FNB at Cresta, is probably only the first step towards coping with the very profound changes which the banks will have to accept over the next 10 to 20 years. Although the reengineering of a branch at Cresta has been a financial success, its greater achievement is probably the fact that it is the first step on a road which will ultimately lead to much greater changes in the banking system as a whole.

## 16. Summary and conclusions

FNB embarked upon a novel approach to solving problems of profitability, space utilisation and customer satisfaction. They did this by focusing on leadership, by reorganising the layout and structure of a branch and by applying creative information technology solutions. The initial indications are that this branch of FNB has achieved a substantial improvement in financial returns, as well as repositioning itself in the market.

It is interesting to note that despite the fact FNB has been regarded as a leader in the use of information technology, both in South Africa (McAllister, 1994) and world-wide (ComputerWorld, 1995), the technology applied to their branch of the future is essentially not leading edge. In fact in keeping with the bank's conservative approach within the financial community only tried and tested information technology was applied.

In terms of the technology available in the mid-1990s, and also bearing in mind the average bank client's need, whether the client be personal or corporate, to have access to coins and notes, this type of project, which firmly retains a bank network, may well be a forerunner of how banks will operate in the future, not only in South Africa, but around the world.

However, considering the longer term the branch of the future which FNB has now established may not be the solution. Clearly FNB still see a bank as a physical place or location rather than a service concept. A type of paradigm shift which allows the physical nature of banking to be seen as secondary, is needed to really grasp the major opportunities of the future. The next step which the banks must face may well involve the abolition of the branch network itself and the creation of electronic networks which substantially, although it is not likely that they will ever exclusively, piggy back other cash dispensers such as supermarkets, service stations, police stations and railway stations. This type of thinking which some new banks have already begun to implement, has not been fully faced by any of the established banks who have more than a small interest in the bricks and mortar in which they have billions of rands invested.

What is clear is that the bank of the medium-term future, never mind the long-term future, will be a very different organisation to manage than the institutions which exist today.
