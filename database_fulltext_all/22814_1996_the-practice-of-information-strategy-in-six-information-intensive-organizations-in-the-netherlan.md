# The practice of information strategy in six information intensive organizations in The Netherlands $^{1}$

M.T. Smits\*, K.G. van der Poel

School of Economics, Tilburg University, P.O. Box 90153, 5000 LE Tilburg, The Netherlands

Revised paper accepted for publication by Professor R. D. Galliers, November 1995

## Abstract

The aim of this research is to understand how and why business organizations in the past few years shaped their information strategies and to generalize these observations. To support the data collection in organizations, a research model was used, dividing information strategy into four aspects: environment, process, content and effects. The experiences of three insurance companies and three document processors are described with the help of the model, resulting in six case descriptions of the information strategy practices in these companies over the past five years. We found changes in the information strategies over the period studied, as well as differences between the organizations. Based on the case studies, we propose a phase model of information strategies: during each phase, a period of one to several years, a company shows a specific approach towards information strategy. All six organisations that we studied showed a change of phase in the past five years. We distinguish five phases: turbulence, orientation, consolidation, exploitation, tension. The phases are characterized by means of the aspects of the information strategy model and the six cases are fitted onto the typology. The typology can be used to determine the current position of an organization with respect to information systems planning, and the possibilities to improve or change it.

Keywords: Information strategy; Information systems strategic planning; Case studies; Insurance companies; Publishing companies

## 1. Introduction

The purpose of this investigation is to learn how information intensive organizations in practice make plans with respect to the demand and supply of information, and how this relates to the planning of information technology (IT). This subject, addressed by the term ‘information strategy’, is an important issue in organizations (Galliers, 1991; Earl, 1993; Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993). In other words, this paper focuses on the practice of information strategy in organizations, that is, how organizations presently deal with the planning and strategy for information resources. Our approach is descriptive rather than normative, and we try to understand and explain how organizations actually experience and shape their information strategies.

Information strategy is a complex phenomenon. In many organizations and in much of the information systems (IS) literature different terminology is used, which is often not explicitly defined. This leads to poor communications, about which several researchers have complained (Fitzgerald, 1993). We define information strategy as: 'a complex of implicit or explicit goals, visions, guidelines and plans with respect to the supply and demand of formal information in an organization, sanctioned by management, intended in the long run to support the objectives of the organization and adjusted to the environment'. Operationally expressed, information strategy is an instrument to manage information services and technology in an organization. A frequently used term, related to information strategy, is strategic information systems planning (SISP) (Lederer and Sethi, 1988; King, 1988; Ward et al., 1990; Galliers, 1991; Earl, 1993). SISP is defined as 'the process of deciding the objectives for organizational computing and identifying potential computer applications which the organization should implement' (Lederer and Sethi, 1988). The two definitions look very similar. A strict comparison shows that the SISP definition tends to focus on explicit objectives and on applications and technology. Our definition concentrates on the use and the importance of information in an organization, starting with the planning of information (in the end influencing IT, as well as influenced by IT). We preferred this definition as a starting point to investigate how contemporary organizations deal with their needs for information and the planning of IT.

After scanning the literature we chose to do in-depth case studies in a small number of organizations, describing the planning process for information strategies as well as the contents of the plans (King, 1988; Lederer and Sethi, 1988; Walsham and Waema, 1994), and interviewing both IS managers as well as general managers (Chan and Huff, 1992). We also decided to examine the information strategy activities in the past five years, to look for possible changes in the approach to information strategy.

In this paper we present our findings about the practice of information strategy in six information intensive organizations in the Netherlands: three insurance companies and three companies involved in document processing. Based on these findings we try to understand how and why these organizations have in the past few years shaped and adapted their information strategies. We expected the information strategy of an organization to be contingent: we look for relationships with environmental factors and attempt to identify patterns. We have found recurring changes in the activities in the organizations with respect to information strategy. In some years the information strategy process and content are focused on selecting opportunities for IT, in other years the organizations focus on implementation strategies, or consolidation strategies. We conclude the paper with the description of a model that explains the changes in information strategies over the years.

## 2. Research method

## 2.1. Research model

Our research is based on an input-process-output model of information strategy. This approach to strategy is previously described by several authors (e.g. King, 1988; Premkumar and King, 1991; Fitzgerald, 1993; Walsham and Waema, 1994). King presented an input-process-output model for information systems planning where the planning process gets input from the environment in the form of ‘resources’ and ‘information input’. The planning process results in ‘output’ (the plan) and ‘outcome’ (the effects). In this model the environment influences all four aspects (input, process, output and outcome). King describes eight theoretical dependencies between the aspects of the model. However, the dependencies in the model are not validated, and instruments to measure the constructs in the model are vaguely described (Fitzgerald, 1993).

Walsham and Waema (1994) describe one extensive case study of the information strategy formation and implementation in a medium-sized UK building society. The organization is studied over a period of several years under the contrasting leadership of two different chief executive officers (CEOs) (one autocratic and one participative leadership style). The authors present a framework for SISP research, based on the theory of strategy and contextualism (Pettigrew, 1985), in which three elements are considered: 'content of strategy', 'context of strategy', and 'process of strategy'. Information strategy content changes with context factors such as various levels in the organization as well as the higher level of the industry as a whole, and changes in national economics and politics (Walsham and Waema, 1994). The 'industry' in which an organization operates has often been recognized as a major determinant of the information strategy (Cash et al., 1983).

The input-process-output models found in the literature show various differences with respect to the items or elements used to describe the input, the process and the output of information strategy. Based on the literature, we developed a model to describe the practice of information strategy (Smits et al., 1993). This is different from the model by King: his model is more prescriptive, specifying aspects and relationships exhaustively, whereas our model is intended to be descriptive. The main purpose of the model was to structure interviews with managers in organizations, and to structure the resulting case descriptions, to enable further analysis and comparison of the cases. According to the model, information strategy has four aspects: environment, process, form and content, and effects. The structure of the model and the relationships between the four aspects are represented in Fig. 1.

The model indicates the effect of the environment on the process of strategy formulation. The process leads to a strategy (a plan) with an explicit or implicit form and content. The effects (the outcome) of strategies are realized by the implementation activities in the organization, that produce the ultimate architectures of people, hardware, software and data. These effects form again a part of the information strategy environment. Each of the four aspects of the model contain a number of items. These items will now be described briefly.

## 2.1.1.Environment

The environment is defined as all those facts and conditions that do not form part of the information strategy process, but that can or should influence it. We divided the environment in ‘internal environment’ and ‘external environment’, both with ‘technological aspects’ and ‘organizational aspects’. Internal environment includes a typology of the organization, the general strategy, the actual IT competencies in the organization and the information strategy awareness (the managers attitudes) in the organization. External environment entails the customers, the products, and the markets of the organization, and the relevant external developments in technology, legislation, and society.

## 2.1.2. Process

The information strategy process relates to the way in which the information strategy is created or changed. An important item is the actual dominant view of the process as either ‘mechanistic’ (the process is taken care of by creating a machinery for it), ‘problem driven’ (the occurrence of problems or opportunities triggers a tailored activity), or as ‘political’ (the responsibility is delegated to an individual) (Schwenk, 1988). Other items in the information strategy process are ‘the methods and tools’ that are used, ‘the participants and their roles’, ‘the linkages with corporate strategy’. Process can also include managerial views with respect to ‘organizational learning’ (Galliers, 1991; Lane, 1992), that is how do previous (IS) planning experiences affect information strategy.

## 2.1.3. Form and content

The form of an information strategy defines some formal characteristics, such as the degree of formality and regularity of the documentation (is it written down or is it implicitly (supposed to be) known? How many pages?) and the time span. The content falls into the following items: ‘objectives or targets for the information function’, ‘architectures’ (applications, technical and organizational), ‘rules’, and ‘plans’. Rules include guidelines and standards (or policies) which set a framework for decisions, such as, for instance, a hurdle rate for investments. Rules also include alliances, concerning make or buy decisions and outsourcing relations. Plans are limited here to priorities and budgets and do not include detailed designs and project plans.

## 2.1.4. Effects

Effects are defined as the intended and unintended effects of the information strategy. Results may be conceived in terms of ‘better information systems’, ‘better information’, or ‘better business results’. Items of interest are ‘what are the results, outcomes, effects of the implementation of (previous) information strategies?’, ‘are these evaluated, and how, by whom?’. A circular relation exists in Fig. 1, because ‘effects’ include the changes in the IT and IS architectures, thereby influencing the ‘internal environment’.

## 2.2. Data gathering

The model formed the basis for the collection of data in interviews and document analysis, and for the description of the practices in six selected organizations: three insurance companies and three organizations in the document processing industry. These industries were chosen, as their primary processes are strongly information oriented (Codington and Wilson, 1994; Byrd et al., 1995). The organizations all had a turnover of over \$100 million to about \$5 billion, and those organizations approached all agreed to cooperate.

Two questionnaires were used for the interviews. The first questionnaire is highly structured (along the four aspects of the model), and contains both open and closed questions, all based on the items described above. It is intended to obtain factual and attitudinal information from people functionally involved with information strategy (typically IS managers and IT users). The second questionnaire contains mostly open-ended questions. It leads from questions about factual decisions taken in the previous years to the discussion of the value and appreciation of information strategy. The second questionnaire is intended to steer interviews with top-executives. These relatively open interviews deal with: (i) the key decisions taken in the previous years concerning information strategy, (ii) the information strategy process and the roles of different parties in the organization, (iii) the outcome of information strategy activities.

Interviews were held with three to five people in each organization, including IT managers, line managers and top-executives. Also relevant documents (policy statements, IS plans, annual reports) were analyzed. The focus was on the current situation, but perspectives on the previous five years were included. Case reports were produced and were fed back to the executives for correction and approval. It typically took 60 to 80 man-hours and an elapsed time of about three months to complete the investigation and description of each case.

## 3. Findings

The findings in the six organisations are based on multiple interviews and document analysis in each organization. The measurements were largely recorded on nominal or ordinal scales, and described in six case reports. The findings are therefore not expressed in numbers, but rather in images or 'gestalts' of organizations and groups or organizations. Presented below are the main observations in the insurance companies, the document processing companies, and a summary of all findings.

## 3.1. The insurance companies

## 3.1.1. Environment

In the Netherlands there are about 10 larger insurance firms, covering the insurances of life, retirement funds and property. The total premium income in the insurance market (excluding health insurance) in the Netherlands is about \$30 billion per year. In the past five years the insurance industry in the Netherlands experienced several important external shocks. There were developments in the (national) social security system and new legislation on life insurance. Changes in the legislation concerning the cooperation between banks and insurance companies and the opening of the European market led to a re-configuration and new alliances. New marketing channels such as ‘direct writing’ were opened and new products such as combined savings and insurance schemes were launched. The industry grew strongly and was in general profitable. An important instance of the influence of information technology on the insurance industry was (in the 1980s) the development of the ‘Assurance Data Network’ (ADN). This value added network links various parties in the insurance industry and has forced traditional competitors into some form of alliance.

We have studied three insurance companies, of which one is a direct writer, the other two use intermediaries as distribution channel. In the three organizations studied, the information strategy awareness is high for all parties: the executive board, the information management and IT departments, as well as functional departments. Attitudes towards IT of general managers and functional managers were generally positive and deviated little from each other. The following typical statement was found in the corporate plan of company C: 'the battle for the distribution channel can be influenced decisively by the use of IT'. Insurance companies have generally invested heavily in information technology and have built up strong internal IT organizations. Two of the three companies studied, recently became part of a financial conglomerate, but this had not (yet) affected their information architectures.

## 3.1.2. Process

Information strategy typically evolves through a problem-driven process, with both top-down and bottom-up inputs from IT managers as well as from general managers. The use of information technology in the organizations is not an activity that is planned or ruled from one specific department or person. Information strategy is influenced by many parties, partly historically and personally based. All three organizations had several formal and informal forums for planning and using information technology and information services. In all cases there was an interdepartmental and multi-skilled 'IS'-steering group or team present at the company level. Formal methods play a minor, supporting role in the information strategy process. Technology scanning, SWOT (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats) analysis tend to be used periodically as building blocks. Competitive analysis is seldom used explicitly. The regularly produced 'Information Plan' serves as a means of communication within the information systems department and between this department and the rest of the organization. The annual corporate planning cycle is a 'staging post' in a continuous information strategy process.

## 3.1.3. Form and content

The format of the information strategy is a written document in all three organizations. The size of the document varies considerably (10 to 100 pages), covering a two-, three- or five-year period. Policies and guidelines on aspects such as investment criteria, risk management, security and technical standards are essential parts of the information strategy, but remain often implicit and are assumed to be known. Linkage between corporate strategies and information strategies is well established, both in the sense of impact of technology as in the sense of alignment to business goals. The strategies of all three organizations are more oriented to information systems and services than to the use of technology or infrastructures. Whereas the emphasis is generally on the (application) architectures and plans, reformulation of objectives of IS and IT occasionally received much attention.

## 3.1.4. Effects

The companies put increasing emphasis on sophisticated methods to determine and control costs and benefits at a micro level of information strategy: the management of systems development and implementation. On the other hand, organizations do not systematically evaluate the effects and consequences of an information strategy at a macro-level, such as answering questions like 'what are the effects of IS/IT applications on the market performance, or the performance of business processes', and 'what are the bottom line financial effects of application X'.

## 3.2. The document processing companies

## 3.2.1. Environment

The document processing industry is not an industry in the traditional sense of the word, but rather a broad group of organizations (both public and private) engaged in similar activities. To this industry belong printers and publishers, but also the keepers of official records such as property deeds, vehicle registrations and patents. The traditional structure and operations of document processors (handling paper) are thoroughly changed by new information recording media (from tapes to CD-ROMs) and new capabilities or retrieving and communicating information. In publishing this has led to new integrated processes and reduced 'time to market'. In general, document processing has been growing strongly and most companies (particularly the large publishers) have been quite profitable. However, concern is mounting that their traditional products and markets are vulnerable to attack by new products such as online data bases and new market entrants such as network operators.

We have studied three ‘document processing organizations’: two printing and publishing companies, and one company that handles patent information, from the gathering and judging of patent requests, up to publication and distribution of information on patents. The three companies differed in the level of previous investments in, and experience with IT. One company had installed a ‘company wide’ document imaging and handling system a few years ago, had experimented workflow management, and was now cutting transaction and processing costs. The other two companies had no or only little experience with document imaging, and were now looking for new IT/IS opportunities. External shocks, such as mergers, take-overs or redirection of the business seem to have a large effect on the information strategy, leading to reorganization of responsibilities and general re-focusing.

## 3.2.2. Process

When the information strategy is re-focused, the political or problem driven approach to information strategy making dominates. In more stable situations, a mechanistic approach is favoured. Top management typically plays a significant role in the information strategy process. Small project teams are used to scan technologies and to develop one or two specific applications. Comprehensive methodologies were previously used, but have largely been abandoned in favour of a more personalized approach, with piecemeal use of supporting methods in the analysis phase.

## 3.2.3. Form and content

The format of an information strategy is sometimes a written document. The strategy often remains implicit and informal. Rules, controls and alliances are important aspects of the information strategy, but remain typically implicit. Organizations that are re-focusing their information strategy pay more attention to goals and objectives than to architectures and alliances. Architectures are more explicitly documented in organizations with a high investment in IT.

## 3.2.4. Effects

Companies put little effort in formal reviews of their IT architectures, or in assessment of user satisfaction. Effects of the information strategy are typically monitored at the project level. Monitoring at the corporate level occurred only in a situation of relative stability and high investment.

## 3.3. Summary of the findings

There are substantial differences between the six companies with respect to information strategy. These differences are also visible in individual companies over time and manifest themselves both in the process and in the content of the information strategy. Organizations show an evolution in their approach to information strategy. External shocks such as mergers or a new legal framework appear to plan an important part in this evolution.

In all companies attention to information strategy at board level was always present, and was at certain times very high. This had to do with personal interests of individuals, but also with a genuine appreciation of the significance of IT to the business. Through this high level of attention, linkage between corporate strategy and information strategy was not a problem, as reported by all respondents. Externally defined methodologies were largely abandoned in favour of own procedures, linked to the corporate planning cycle. The annual information plans are considered to be only staging posts in an essentially (but informally) continuous process.

All companies included objectives, architectures rules and plans in their information strategies, albeit with different degrees of emphasis and elaboration over time. Rules remained generally implicit and alliances received very little attention.

Nearly all companies considered monitoring of the results of the information strategy very difficult and therefore did not even try. However, most kept a close watch at the project level.

## 4. Proposition: a phase model of information strategy

## 4.1. Five organizational phases

In this study we examined the information strategies in six organizations in the current situation (1993) and the situation a few years ago. The findings with respect to the process, content, and effects of information strategies are divers, and vary not only between organizations, but also over time in each individual organization. We have been looking for a model, a construct that we could use to explain this diversity in the findings. When reviewing the case descriptions, a certain development appeared in the way the organizations deal with information strategy over the years investigated. Some organizations are looking for possible applications of IT, without having many formal procedures and organizational structures. Other organizations have already found and decided how to use IT in their situation. Others again are reconsidering their goals and organizational structure. The content and the process of the information strategies vary between the organizations and over time. We propose a model consisting of five phases or stages in which an organization has a specific approach to information strategy. Each approach is described along the items and aspects described under ‘Research model’.

The five organizational phases are:

\- Turbulence A specific event, such as a merger, a new senior manager, external disturbances (legislation, competitors, technological developments), arouses strategic interests in IT. There is uncertainty and experimentation with entirely new approaches.

\- Orientation A new analysis of the internal and external environment has been made. A task force, or a small project group, is successful in applying new thinking to one or two activities. New goals are formulated for. Information strategy is rewritten and new methods of strategy making are tried.

\- Consolidation Senior management decides formally to apply the new uses of IT in the organization. The organizational and managerial structures for the application of new IT are adjusted. Steering groups and project groups with members of many departments come into being. Successful applications and methods are extended and duplicated. Architectures are conceived.

\- Exploitation The organization and its members get used to the new structures, procedures and technologies. They become part of organizational routine. Efficiency measures come into focus: tuning of organizational procedures and adjustment of activities become important. Reductions of costs and budgets become part of the business and information strategy. Information systems have become routine in most areas. Detailed procedures exist for access to information systems and information technology. Responsibilities have been decentralized.

\- Tension In some areas shortcomings and missed opportunities become acute and the existing structures seem to obstruct organizational development. Some stakeholders are actively looking for and experimenting with change. Turbulence is at the horizon.

The phases are closely linked to the external and internal environment, and differ with respect to the information strategy process as well as the information strategy content and effects. Important items are the type of the information strategy process (mechanistic, problem oriented or political), the roles of various management levels, the attention to objectives or architectures, and the interest in the effects of the information strategy. Table 1 is an overview of the approaches to information strategy in each organizational phase. For each phase the information strategy is described in terms of the four aspects of the information strategy (environment, process, content and effects). Each aspect is characterized by several items. For instance, for the aspect 'environment' the item 'IT-competencies' denotes the actual, current competencies of using information technology in the organization. Seeking for new IT occurs in the phase turbulence. During the phases 'orientation' and 'consolidation' the IT-competencies are expanding, in exploitation competencies are stable. Another item of 'environment' is 'IT awareness', indicating the management attitude towards information technology. During the phases consolidation and exploitation, organizations pay more attention to new applications of information technology, compared with the other phases. A dash $(-)$ means that we do not define the item in that phase.

From the findings we cannot conclude that the five phases will always appear in this order for each organization, although, this order seems to be plausible. In our observations the phases seem to appear sequentially, whereby each phase can take several years, or many only last for a short period and may therefore be regarded negligible. We want to stress the hypothetical nature of this model. The model is suitable to explain the differences in information strategies between organizations and through time, as described below. Further research is needed to validate the model.

## 4.2. Mapping the six cases onto the five phases

We may now map the six cases onto the five phases. For each case we have at least a good identification of the 'present' situation (in 1993) with respect to the items and aspects of the information strategy. For all cases we have also gained enough insight in the development of the information strategy of the past few years to allow us to determine the position of the company a few years ago (denoted as 1989). It should be noted that the positioning of a case in the typology is not an exact science. In some situations a case will show characteristics of more than one type, as the organization is making the transition from one phase to the next. The positions are therefore indicated in Fig. 2 as more or less stretched lines, rather than as fixed points.

Fig. 2 shows a spread of the cases over the different types, with a clustering of the document processors (Doc proc) in 1993 at the 'turbulence and consolidation' end of the spectrum and the insurance companies in 1993 at the ‘execution and tension’ end of the spectrum. The positions of 1993 differ from the previous positions, denoted by 1989. A superficial glance at Fig. 2 suggests that the positions of companies A, B, C and Y have moved to the right, while companies X and Z moved to the left. The positions of the companies are explained below.

Insurance A has moved from an ‘execution’ position to a ‘tension’ position. A few years ago, the position with respect to the information strategy was rather stable. The technical, application and organizational architectures were well defined and were being vigorously extended. The planning process was formal and clear and mostly in the hands of IT management. Top management was supportive, but not overly involved. The type is classified as ‘execution’. Soon thereafter, some tension developed in the technical and application architectures, manifested by the introduction of a significant software package. The recent merger with the partner-bank and the realization that basic processes will need to be re-arranged gave rise in 1993 to concern but not yet to great changes. Top management involvement increased substantially and while the strategy process formally proceeded along the same lines as before, real issues were increasingly handled as they arose. In that process formal tools played no role. The situation is not (yet) to be described as ‘turbulent’, but there is certainly substantial tension. The next stage could be either ‘turbulence’ or ‘orientation’ towards a new situation.

Insurance B is in a stable but expanding condition with respect to its use of IT. IT awareness of the users is high and management is supportive, but has delegated a lot of authority. Strategy making is done in a rather personal fashion, but has been routinized to a large extent (mechanistic is, however, an overstatement). The architectures appear particularly clear and stable. Efforts have been initiated to report and control overall quality and performance measures, but these are not yet well established. The merger with another insurance company has not produced the substantial shock which it might have. Company B seems to be on its way to an ‘execution’ type information strategy, unless the current merger (with another (smaller) insurance company) would lead to turbulence or re-orientation.

Insurance C is in a fairly similar stage as company B. It has a long period of steady development and consolidation behind it. The organization and the technical architecture appear very well established, but the same is not true for the (decaying) application architecture. Strategic decision making has been routinized and delegated, to the point of being mechanistic. An overall methodology has been abandoned and tools are hardly used in the strategy process. Top management is certainly aware, but only moderately involved. There is a heightened interest in the business results of IT, but this manifests itself mainly on the project level. Company C therefore seems to straddle the 'execution' type, but also of 'tension' due to stresses in the application architecture, as well as the arrival of a new CIO. The ADN experiences and the potential influences of the 'All Finanz' partnership don't seem to have affected the position of C (yet).

Doc Proc X has moved from a stable ‘consolidation/exploitation’ type of information strategy to a ‘turbulence’ stage due to the recent merger and ensuing reorganization. Until 1992, company X had delegated a great deal of authority to the information manager. The planning process was partly formalized, but largely driven by the information manager. Interest from top management was intermittent, but positive. Work had been done to consolidate the technical and organizational architectures and there were firm development plans. Following the merger a mixture of turbulence and reorientation is evident. The awareness that IT is important and the information strategy needs to be re-defined is very high. Top management plays an active role, but the process is not at all formalised. There is a strong orientation towards objectives, but all plans are still tentative. Architectures are not yet defined. Results are only defined at project level.

Doc Proc Y passed through a crisis and an ‘orientation’ phase in 1991/92. At that time the awareness of (the problems around) IT was very high and top management played a very active role. Decision making was very ‘political’ (in the sense of personal). A new organizational architecture was put in place and extensions to the application architecture were initiated. Since then, the organization is reverting to a more consolidating approach. The process is a mixture of the mechanistic and the political approach. Top management still plays an important but diminishing role. The architectures, notably the application architecture, are elaborate and the plans are firm. The company has embarked on an effort to establish the overall benefit of automation. The company appears to be moving towards the ‘exploitation’ type of information strategy making, but the ultimate responsibility of users and top management for automation has been clearly established.

Doc Proc Z is in a very dynamic position. After many years of consolidation and exploitation, it was decided in 1992 to make IT a major line of services. The need (or the opportunity) to do this were indicated by the recent privatization. Having established the global objective, further strategy making is still proceeding according to the 'turbulent' type: the awareness of the significance of IT and the level of top management involvement are very high indeed. The process is of the political (personal) type and the focus is on the definition of objectives and opportunities. A temporary organization has been established and tentative choices of technology have been made, but plans are decidedly flexible. Costs and benefits are controlled per project, but it is too early to assess the overall effects of the strategy.

## 5. Typologies and stage models in the literature

We reviewed the literature on stage models and on the classification of strategies in general and of information strategies in particular. The following contributions appeared particularly relevant, and are compared with the phase model for information strategy presented above.

## 5.1. Linear stage models

Greiner (1972) presented a description of four phases that typically occur as an organization grows in size and complexity. The model is linear and normative in the sense that it only describes a company developing from small to large and presents the four phases as inevitable. A few years later Nolan gave a similar account of the growth of data processing departments. The model originally comprised four phases (Gibson and Nolan, 1974), but was later extended to six (Nolan, 1979, 1984). In the present study, we made the observation that phases or orientation on new technologies are followed (as well as preceded) by phases of exploiting technologies and adjusting business processes to the technologies. Another difference with our phase model is that Nolan's stages are more based on internal circumstances. We stress the importance of external factors on the information strategy orientation of an organization. Galliers and Sutherland (1991) reviewed the literature on models and views on information systems planning. Based on literature and the experiences in four case studies, they present a revised growth model of information systems planning, being a combination of Nolan's six stages of IT maturity growth, and the McKinsey seven 'Ss' model. The model is useful, in that it takes a holistic view of information systems management issues, and so provides a framework which enables appropriate questions for research and consultancy. Our research was focused on six information intensive organizations, and almost 10 years later. The information strategies that we found, appeared to be non-linear, but rather of a circular nature, possibly beyond Stage 6.

## 5.2. The strategic grid

This analysis was introduced (Cash et al., 1983) to draw attention to the fact that information strategies must be expected to be different in different circumstances. The strategic grid identifies two environmental factors: the strategic significance of existing IT applications and the strategic significance of future IT applications. However, the authors do not work out what the consequences are of the location of an organization in a particular quadrant of the strategic grid, but conclude that 'Good management of IT differs widely in different settings'. The strategic grid therefore gives us, at best, a rough suggestion how information strategies can be classified, but not what the characteristics of the different classes are or how they are likely to develop in time.

## 5.3. A process-oriented classification of information strategies

Based on the investigation of practices of 27 UK-based companies, Earl (1993) has identified five different approaches to information strategy. An approach comprises a mix of ‘procedures, techniques, user-IS interactions, special analyses and random discoveries’. This is about the same as what is called ‘process’ in our model. The five approaches and their typifying slogans are: Business-led (business drives IS), Method-driven (strategy needs method), Administrative (follow the rules), Technological (IS needs blueprints) and Organizational (themes with teams). Earl has positioned the 27 organizations into one of the five categories. In our investigation we have observed (aspects of) business led, administrative, and organizational approaches. However, the approach of the organizations towards information strategy was found to change over time. This aspect of change is important, and represented in our model.

## 5.4. Classifications of corporate strategies

The classification of corporate strategies in ‘defenders, prospectors, analyzers and reactors’, proposed by Miles and Snow (1978) is widely known and used (Das et al., 1991; Segars et al., 1994). The classification proposed by Porter (1980) of cost leadership, differentiation and focus is in many respects similar to that of Miles and Snow. These models are static, in that they do not presume or describe a transition from the one type to the other. Rather, it is assumed to be up to management to choose to migrate to a new strategy, presumably under the pressure of circumstances.

Another classification for corporate strategies is given by Mintzberg (1973). He distinguishes three modes of strategy making: entrepreneurial, adaptive and planning. In Mintzberg's paper, these modes relate to corporate strategy and specifically to how strategy is made, i.e. to the process. The entrepreneurial mode is characterized by: active search for new opportunities, power in the hands of the chief executive, bold decisions, oriented to growth. The environment is assumed to be malleable, a force to be confronted and controlled. Characteristics of the adaptive mode are: there are no clear objectives, but solutions are sought that are good enough. Uncertainties are avoided or reduced. Decisions are taken in small steps and policy-making is a never-ending process. The philosophy is that the organization must understand and adjust to the environment. In the planning mode the emphasis is on rational decision making. The key actor is the analyst who uses scientific techniques to develop formal comprehensive plans. These characterizations obviously relate to those of Miles and Snow, but are more vivid and more process-oriented. In a later publication (Mintzberg, 1987), the strategy process is presented as consisting of two alternating 'modes': for long periods, strategy consists of the elaboration of established concepts (planning mode). However, from time to time upheavals occur. Either through adaptive search, or through bold moves new ground is broken and a new position or pattern is established. So, strategy moves through cycles of innovation and evolution. The occurrence of a new wave of innovation depends partly on personalities, partly on external pressures and partly on chance. This theory is attractive as it avoids both the 'immobility' of the corporate strategy typologies and the 'linearity' of the stages of growth.

Our typology is (tentatively) based on qualitative, descriptive data and research. A very nice example of the formation of a typology of (corporate) strategies based on empirical, quantitative data, is given by Miller and Friesen (1978). First, based on the literature, they defined 30 variables that might explain corporate strategic behaviour. The variables are comparable with the ones that we used to describe the aspects 'environment', 'process', and 'content' and 'business effects'. Second, they collected data on all 30 variables in 80 organizations. Statistical analysis lead to the identification of 10 archetypes of corporate strategy. Third, they identified successful and less successful archetypes, where each archetype is characterized by the values of specific variables. In our opinion, such quantitative research into the relations between 'types of strategies' and 'business results' is difficult to perform with respect to information strategies, and other functional strategies. One reason for this is the very indirect linkage between one functional strategy and business (or corporate) performance.

## 6. Discussion and conclusions

From practice and from the literature it is known that organizations differ in their approach to information strategy. Some organizations are highly involved in systematical planning of demand and supply of information, or the use of information technology. Other organizations seem not to be interested. The implicit or explicit information strategy of an organization can be seen as an expression of the way the organization deals with the complex problem of relating its business objectives with information needs, and technological opportunities. The subject of our research has been how and why organizations have approached this problem during the past five years. The results in this paper are in fact based on in depth case research in six organizations, focused on the situation in 1993, and combined with a retrospective view of about four years. The organizations were in two lines of industry.

In this paper we described five phases in which organizations show different approaches to information strategies. Organizations have limited capacities and resources for dealing with the complex problem called information strategy. Therefore they focus attention to specific aspects of information strategy. During periods of stress they tend to focus on looking for new opportunities (based on actual strength). After that phase choices are made and implemented, taking considerable process time. Another phase begins, in which implemented technologies and business processes are further tuned, waiting for the next crisis.

We developed this phase model because we think it might explain the large differences in approach to information strategy, as seen in practice. Description and explanation of the differences eventually leads to better practice. The phase model can be used to decide when and how an organization can approach the subject of information strategy, in order to effectively use IT. In the phase model the approach to information strategy depends on the phase the organization is in. We distinguish five phases, which differ with respect to the information strategy environment, the process, the form and content, and the effects. For very large organizations (with several business units) this may differ from business unit to business unit.

Further research is needed to answer questions like 'How fast can organizations go through the stages?', and 'Can different parts of an organization have different velocities? Why?'. We can think of different velocities between the front-office and the back-office parts of an organization. Another interesting question is how the velocity can be increased, for instance by improving organizational learning. Organizational learning is based on the development of shared visions on the future, and on evaluating past performance. The typology might be used to structure and to gain insight in processes of information strategy making, and to evaluate the roles of parties and stakeholders.

Practitioners rarely appreciate the different types of information strategy and the circumstances in which these may be appropriate. The typology emphasizes that there are specific approaches to information strategies for different circumstances. This invites some lateral thinking and discussion about which type of information strategy is best suited to the present circumstances of the organization. A second dimension is thereby added to the discussion of information strategy. The dynamic nature of the typology emphasizes the fact that information strategies tend to evolve over time. This encourages managers to think about the evolution of information strategy in their own organization, both in the past and the future. This third dimension in the concept of information strategy may be very fruitful in the dialogue between the various managers who need to shape the information strategy together, by steering the discussion away from what is right or wrong to how the evolution of information strategy can be enabled.

Up to now, the phase model is not validated. For instance, the criteria for describing the information strategy in an organization might need improvements. To validate the model more research is necessary. As a first step in validation, we held a review session in late 1994 with representatives of three of the six organizations. The positioning of the organizations was checked, as well as the quality of the items used for describing information strategy in the phases. The participants acknowledged the typology and the positions of their organizations, and also indicated new developments in their organizations, indicating slight positional changes in the phase model, since our final measurements in early 1993. Further validation of the model is necessary, and can be done by applying it in other organizations within the same or in other lines of industry.
