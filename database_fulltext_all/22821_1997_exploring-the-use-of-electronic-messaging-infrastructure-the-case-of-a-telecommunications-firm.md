# Exploring the use of electronic messaging infrastructure: the case of a telecommunications firm

Michael Bennett\*, Peter Weill

Melbourne Business School, The University of Melbourne, 200 Leicester Street, Carlton, Melbourne, VIC 3053, Australia

Received 1 November 1995; accepted 1 January 1997 Revised paper accepted for publication by Professor Marianne Broadbent

## Abstract

Some companies are now utilising the PC-based messaging infrastructure for purposes integrated within their business processes. This study seeks to understand the organisational and technologica requirements to take advantage of electronic messaging in this relatively new way.

The experience with PC-based electronic messaging within a large information technology user, PhoneCo, is studied. The electronic messaging infrastructure in this firm was originally designed for relatively unstructured person-to-person communication. However, it is now enabling the execution of business processes by using computer applications as senders and receivers of messages.

A model of electronic messaging Value and Sophistication is presented and 12 electronic messaging applications implemented at PhoneCo are examined and analysed using the framework. A series of six propositions are developed from the evidence. For example, electronic messaging’s contribution to the business is dependent on the presence of standard technology infrastructure with sufficient functional sophistication. This requires centralised approval, design, development, deployment and maintenance of standards and guidelines.

A series of recommendations are given for organisations interested in building applications on their electronic messaging system. q 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Electronic messaging; Business processes

## 1. Introduction

Researchers and managers are beginning to understand the iterative relationship between the strategy of a firm, the nature of its business processes, and the flexibility of its information technology (IT) (Bashaein et al., 1994; Coulson-Thomas, 1994; Stoddard and Jarvenpaa, 1993). While IT is an enabler of change it is also a potential constraint, inhibitor or disenabler (Davenport, 1993; Benjamin, 1993; Broadbent and Butler, 1995; Earl, 1994; Earl and Kuan, 1994). One important enabler or limitation is the firms IT infrastructure (Wastell et al., 1994).

As part of their firm-wide IT infrastructure some large corporations have made a predictable, uniform and capable desktop computing infrastructure available to their workforce. Homogeneous, firm-wide electronic messaging is often one of the first services of this desktop infrastructure. Leveraging the existing investment in electronic messaging infrastructure is often a low incremental cost with high potential strategic value.

Present organisational trends are for firms to have flatter structures, with less management hierarchies which used to provide a relay for communication up and down the organisation (Quinn, 1992). In these companies, electronic messaging can provide benefits aiding the communication of information (Reinhardt, 1993; Sproull and Kiesler, 1991; Scott Morton, 1991). The use of these communications applications is often unstructured, particularly when applied to the business processes in areas strategic to the value chain (Porter, 1985) of the business.

Electronic messaging has the potential to become strategic to a firm’s operations. Electronic messaging is an integrating part of a firm’s IT infrastructure. The support of these types of enabling IT infrastructure capabilities underpins the emergence of new organisational forms (Davidow and Malone, 1992), such as global virtual corporations (Miller et al., 1993), and facilitates the development of virtual value chains (Rayport and Sviokla, 1995). IT infrastructure capability such as electronic messaging is particularly important to garner synergy from business units which result in greater value to the firm than just the sum of the individual business unit’s value (Broadbent et al., 1996). These types of infrastructure capabilities are also critical to globally competing firms (IS Analyzer, 1991; Clemons et al., 1989; Neo, 1991) and all forms of electronic commerce (Kalakota and Whinston, 1995).

Some companies are now utilising the PC-based messaging infrastructure, which was developed for unstructured use, for purposes more formally integrated with the business process. They are using electronic messaging to assist business processes, such as taking orders outside business hours. Because of the lack of experience in this type of messaging use this study seeks to understand the strategic opportunities and the organisational and technological requirements to take advantage of electronic messaging in this way.

This paper researches the experience with PC-based electronic messaging within a large IT user, PhoneCo. The electronic messaging infrastructure in this firm was originally designed for person-to-person communication and is now enabling the execution of business processes by using computer applications as senders and receivers of messages.

The research questions addressed are:

1. How can electronic messaging be used to add value?

2. What IT infrastructure and project specific attributes are required to integrate electronic messaging usage into business processes?

The paper develops a model and then describes, examines, classifies and documents the learning from 12 projects where electronic messaging is used as part of business activities at PhoneCo.

Infrastructure and project-specific attributes gathered from interviews and project documentation are examined for each project, and conclusions drawn. Recommendations are provided for the infrastructure builders and business unit project groups within firms wishing to extend the use of electronic messaging connection to processes essential to a firm’s business.

## 2. Using electronic messaging for enhancing business processes

Electronic messaging is part of a firms IT infrastructure (also called the IT platform (Keen, 1991)), which includes a firm’s backbone of networks, mainframe processing, shared databases and expertise. IT infrastructure is the base foundation of IT capability often budgeted for and provided by the information systems function and shared across multiple business units or functional areas (Weill, 1993; Weill et al., 1996).

As part of the firm’s IT infrastructure Zisman (1989) describes electronic messaging as:

the store-and-forward transport of electronic objects, across a heterogeneous environment, among people, among people and applications, and among applications.

Electronic messaging is typical of IT infrastructure as an enabler as it is often put in place before precise business needs are known (Weill, 1993). The existence of electronic messaging is expected to facilitate applications which were not predicted and thus provide flexibility (Kambil et al., 1993).

Electronic messaging exists in most firms with firm-wide infrastructure. In a recent study, of 25 large, multi-business unit firms, 23 different IT infrastructure services were provided on a firm-wide basis and only five of these were provided by all firms (Weill et al., 1995). Electronic messaging was one of the five services provided by all firms.

A model of electronic messaging is proposed adapted from the work of Le Roux (1993) (p. 71), McKay and Brockway (1989), and Broadbent et al. (1996) to describe the components of electronic messaging similar to the OSI model. The layers are numbered from the bottom up and are independent of each other, with communication between the interfaces well defined (see Fig. 1).

![](/api/attachments/2CW46NY9/fulltext/images/61d23b4c1984fc49cd08e1e62f886e5c314e685ab1022f9702f7d7630ab5ff17.jpg)  
Fig. 1. Model of electronic messaging as infrastructure.

The lower three layers constitute the infrastructure which is the base foundation of IT capability (both technical and human), shared throughout the firm in the form of reliable services, and usually managed by the IS group.

Layer 1 of this model is the network hardware platform comprising computers and communications technologies, which are commodities and readily available in the market place. The second layer consists of the transport protocols defined by the network provider which enable applications, such as messaging, to interface with the network hardware platform (Bennett, 1994; Le Roux, 1993).

The third layer is the a set of shared electronic messaging services provided centrally and shared across the firm. Typical electronic messaging services are: message store and forwarding, file attachment, message return receipt, user authentication and encryption of transmitted messages.

Layers 1 and 2 are converted into useful electronic messaging infrastructure services by the human IT infrastructure composed of knowledge, skills and experience. This human infrastructure exploits the IT components in layers 1 and 2 by building the shared messaging infrastructure services in layer 3.

Layer 4 contains the electronic messaging ‘client’. This ‘client’ is the application which uses the electronic messaging as one of the inputs to perform a business function (Laudon and Laudon, 1996, Glossary) via information processing (Davis and Olson, 1983, p. 67). Whilst the infrastructure services in level 3 are shared across the firm, applications vary from client to client.

Until recently, text-based electronic mail was the only application of electronic messaging found. Electronic mail ‘uses computer based processing to provide high speed information exchange service’ (Sproull and Kiesler, 1986, p. 1493). A new use of electronic messaging is emerging in organisations – the automation of business processes. Electronic messaging can be far broader than person-to-person communication and can be applied to the transport of objects from a user to an application, from an application to a user, and even between applications. Applications which rely on electronic messaging for information transport are called mail-enabled (Zisman, 1989).

Applications vary widely, including using electronic messaging to schedule a meeting or summarising transactional data into a management report. For example, a general manager has a sales collation application on his desktop system. The firm’s sales manager owns an order entry application which captures all sales. On every new entry or update to the order entry system an electronic message is automatically triggered to the general manager. When the general manager wishes to view the sales information, an application on his workstation is started which extracts the electronic messaging messages and updates the general manager’s summary screen.

This type of application-to-application communication using the (often existing) electronic messaging has great potential for integrating the diverse computer platforms found within an organisation or between business partners. For the foreseeable future there will be multiple computer platforms in large corporations, and growing use is expected of distributed computer systems. Mail-enabled approaches provide a standard means of communication between otherwise incompatible computer applications. It is possible that the costs of real-time computer systems and networking will be more expensive than electronic messaging for some business applications. For example:

• Customer demands are requiring 24 hours, seven days a week service in some cases, and running mainframe transaction monitors, such as CICS, 24 hours a day is very difficult and expensive.

Electronic messaging is good at ‘persisting’, or getting through despite failures. A realtime application either works or it doesn’t; there is no middle ground as there is with electronic messaging where messages are queued if there is a network slowdown or outage.

• Vaskevitch<sup>1</sup> estimates that 5% of the business processes in a typical organisation require real-time processing, leaving 95 that don’t require such speedy response. Thus the use of electronic messaging to automate a business process is attractive.

Observations of business processes of this type have led to the coining of the term real-enough<sup>2</sup> time, where the time to completion provided by the technology supporting the process is not critical to process success. This is the area where electronic messaging’s asynchronous operation can be used effectively, and we are likely to see a wider variety of applications in layer 4 in Fig. 1.

With real-enough time, mail-enabled applications remove the need to log on to the mainframe host; data can be entered from start to finish into the workstation in a familiar predictable environment without worrying about response times. The user can define a data query, send it off and move on to other tasks while waiting for the result to arrive. Exception reports can be sent automatically to the manager instead of having to log-in to get them. The mainframe host can notify the user when there is a problem with their application and describe the nature of it. Desktop spreadsheets can be fed with new sales and cost figures overnight, so that graphs and analyses are available in the morning.

## 3. Model of electronic messaging Value and Sophistication

In composing a model of electronic messaging use in a firm we considered a number of different theoretical bases. The technology diffusion and adoption literature offers a rich variety of perspectives of how information technologies penetrate organisations (Damsgaard et al., 1994; Klempa, 1994). However, ‘it has become increasingly clear that no single, strongly predictive theory of innovation is likely to emerge … (e.g. product vs process, administrative vs technical, incremental vs radical)’ (Fishman and Kemerer, 1994). We adopted the advice of Fishman and Kemerer to ‘work at higher level of abstraction and identify general classes’ or ‘characteristic patterns related to adoption’. To achieve this goal we adapted Keen’s (Keen, 1991, p. 179) reach and range, highlevel, framework as a vehicle to identify and plot general classes of electronic messaging use.

![](/api/attachments/2CW46NY9/fulltext/images/81fcb3cfdfee33d384d32e95cc8082068d1910469f322d1c99882c6a934f15fa.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Electronic messaging Value and Sophistication model.

The business functionality of the corporate IT infrastructure<sup>3</sup> can be defined in terms of reach and range. Reach refers to the locations an infrastructure is capable of linking. The ideal reach is to be able to connect to anyone, anywhere, just as the telephone system has almost ideal reach across the world. Range refers to the level of functionality which can be directly and automatically shared across systems and services. The ideal reach and range is that any computer-generated transaction, document, message, and even data item is able to be used in any other system, regardless of hardware or software base or location.

The Electronic Messaging Association (1993) (p. v) states that ‘Electronic messaging is a powerful tool that can be used for a wide range of applications. Its value is a function of its reach, and that reach is growing every day.’

We propose an adaptation of Keen’s approach to describe the electronic messaging infrastructure. We suggest the notions of the Value Potential (Value) and Sophistication of electronic messaging which can then be used as a measure of the degree of integration of electronic messaging into business processes (Fig. 2).

• Value is the potential of the application of electronic messaging to create business value through supporting business activities.

Electronic messaging use is typical of ‘complex organisational technologies’ for which Attewell (1992) argues diffusion can be conceptualised as a process driven by lowering knowledge barriers over time. More sophisticated and integrated uses of the technology offering greater value will occur over time. This greater value comes from (1) learning by doing, (2) economies of scale, (3) technological interrelatedness (i.e., the emergence of supporting technical infrastructure), (4) informational increasing returns (i.e., lowering of uncertainty) and (5) network externalities (belonging to a larger network) (Arthur, 1988; Fishman and Kemerer, 1994). We suggest that electronic messaging use is strongly subject to all ‘five sources of increasing returns’.

Through the technologies of computing, communications and software, IT generically offers three economic contributions to value for firms: information, coordination and reducing the costs of production (Earl and Kuan, 1994, p. 26).

We have used these three categories in combination with Fishman and Kemerer’s (adapted from Arthur, 1988) five sources of increasing returns to create a scale for the Value Potential of electronic messaging use. In order to be comprehensive the scale incorporates all five sources of increasing return and all three sources of economic contribution. The scale has five gradations (A to E): ‘Individual Work Flow’ (A) is electronic messaging use for information; ‘Group Tool’ (B) is the use of electronic messaging for coordination; ‘Aid to Business Activities’ (C) is the use of electronic messaging for both coordination and information; and ‘Enables the Business Process’ (D, E) is the use of electronic messaging, embedded in a business process, generally to reduce production costs by substitution of capital for labour. In moving from A to E we have attempted to incorporate the increasing influence of the ‘five sources of increasing return’. Thus in moving from A to E we posit increasing potential value from the use of electronic messaging.

Levels A and B of Value Potential cover the general category of using ‘computer based processing to provide a high speed information exchange service’ (Sproull and Kiesler, 1986, p. 1493). Individual Work Flow refers to an individual manager sending or receiving information. Typical uses could involve the sending of memos, to influence, persuade or sell an idea or to keep someone informed (Markus, 1994). A Group Tool is where electronic messaging is used to facilitate work amongst groups (Sproull and Kiesler, 1991) such as a group authoring project.

An Aid to a Business Activity is where the electronic messaging provides input to the business activity. For example, in performing the business activity of looking for a candidate to fill a vacant position the manager uses a skills database application which is updated automatically by electronic messaging messages which are automatically generated each time a person changes jobs.

Enables the Business Process is where the electronic messaging actually takes part in performing the business process. A business process is defined as ‘a set of logically related tasks performed to achieve a defined business outcome’ (Davenport and Short, 1990). For example, when ordering components for next month’s production the Director of Purchasing for a motor vehicle manufacturer raises a purchase order. Once approved the purchase order is automatically sent via a electronic message to two suppliers who bid on the work providing price and delivery details.

The scale is anchored with low-value, non-business usage, such as arranging social activities, or informal ‘team-building’. These are of value in increasing the utility of the work environment, despite having little relationship with business. However, they do not directly generate business value.

• Sophistication is the degree of exploitation of the electronic messaging services to share information between people, and computer applications.

Sophistication is categorised by source target and object type, ranging from simple text transmission up to electronic data interchange. The Sophistication scale has 5 gradations which increase in sophistication from 1 to 5. Gradations 1–3 are person-to-person communication using electronic messaging and increase in media richness (Daft and Lengel, 1984, 1986). Gradation 1 is the transmission of simple ASCII text via electronic messaging. The media richness is increased at gradation 2 with the transmission of formatted text such as word processor document files. Gradation 3, with the inclusion of data, images and voice within the message, offers increased information capacity (Daft and Lengel, 1984) to clarify ambiguous issues in a timely manner (Markus, 1994).

Gradations 4 and 5 represent increased sophistication in the use of electronic messaging by providing electronic transport for applications (Zisman, 1989). At this level of sophistication in the use of electronic messaging, applications become the initiator and receiver of messages. The result is increased automation and substitution of capital for labour (Thurow, 1987; Attewell and Rule, 1984). Gradation 4 is the use of electronic messaging to send rich information (Daft and Lengel, 1984, p. 196; e.g., text, voice, data, images) between a person and an application. Gradation 5 is the use of electronic messaging to carry rich information from one application to another application automatically.

As the use of electronic messaging moves out the diagonal, with the associated increase in Value and Sophistication, the electronic messaging application becomes more integrated into the business processes. For example, in cell E5 electronic messaging is fully integrated into a business process (e.g., purchasing) by providing automatic electronic connection to customers or suppliers (e.g., electronic data interchange).

Much of the rich tradition of research into the impacts of electronic mail use (e.g., Sproull and Kiesler, 1986, 1991; Markus, 1994; Lee, 1994) has focused on cells B1 and A1 in Fig. 2. One of the objectives of this study is to explore and classify the use of electronic messaging for tasks more deeply woven into the business processes.

## 4. PhoneCo

PhoneCo is a large phone company with revenues in 1994 of over US \$7.5 billion and a very large-scale user of IT, with an annual investment of many hundreds of millions of dollars. Approximately 42% of the IT investment is directly controlled through the central Information Technology Department (ITD) budget and another 38% is indirectly managed by ITD. The remaining 20% is controlled directly by the firm’s business units.

PhoneCo is absolutely reliant on the performance of its information system-based operational support systems and management information systems to run its business. The IT environment includes:

• Four major data centres with 1100 installed MIPS, the majority used for on-line transaction processing and 3400 gigabytes of on-line storage.

• Mainframe environment is Amdahl/Fujitsu running IBM MVS.

• Key transaction processing systems (order processing, billing, trouble management) handle up to 1 000 000 transactions per day.

• 1500 installed Unix minicomputers (1000 are high-performance workstations).

• Networking environment is point-to-point to switched (X.25) and backbone (TCP/IP and IPX).

• 1200 Novell local area networks (LANs).

• 35 000 users connect to hosts via PC workstations (mostly using X.25 gateways from LANs).

• 225 000 electronic messaging messages per week between sites.

PhoneCo’s systems architecture (SA) has focused on a number of key areas where IT will have to provide business benefit and be an enabler of change to compete more effectively (PhoneCo, 1993):

• Automation of service activation to a level where the customers can ‘self-serve PhoneCo products, and configure the products they use on an individual basis.

• Provision of customer service through direct access by customers to front-end service systems.

• Development of new products and introduction of new network technologies, with investment decisions clearly linked to market requirements.

• Increasingly software-driven product and network development, replacing productspecific technology and network hardware.

• Improvement in the quality of information through the capture of related data together, and the management of data on an end-to-end basis along business process lines.

Four key business processes have been identified by PhoneCo that underpin the core business activities: customer support, service activation, service assurance, and charging and billing.

To be relevant to PhoneCo’s core business, computing technology needs to be applied to the enhancing of processes like customer support and service activation. There is a clear opportunity to replace some of the use of telephone and facsimile with electronic messaging, where the business need is not for real-time support. Facsimile is used only for convenience and ability to keep a paper record, not for its speed of transmission in many of the process activities. Mail-enabled applications have been used as an enabling technology for these types of activities.

## 4.1. Standard Operating Environment

Since October 1990, ITD has been promoting the standardisation of the computing infrastructure through the Standard Operating Environment (SOE) guidelines as part of the SA, defined and controlled by the CIO.

The SOEs define the software, hardware, networking and telecommunications products to be used within PhoneCo. By defining and retaining tight control of the SOEs, PhoneCo is aiming to gain maximum value from its IT expenditure by standardisation. The SOE categories cover the general technical environment for a broad base of users across all areas of PhoneCo and enable a significantly larger reach and range.

In October 1990 the Deputy CEO released the ‘Corporate Policy for Computing Workstations’, where the ITD was given the responsibility of setting standards for desktop computing in PhoneCo.:

Workstations for corporate projects will be purchased in accordance with the standards and programmes of ITD. Purchases for other applications require approval of a business case by the appropriate management.

Groups developing new applications must provide software that is consistent with the SOE. ITD must be consulted if it is considered that user requirements cannot be met with the existing architecture and a joint solution agreed upon. Purchase of additional equipment for new applications is to be avoided.<sup>4</sup>

The result for PhoneCo has been the implementation of a richly featured desktop and LAN standard computing infrastructure with more than 35 000 nodes which is pervasive throughout the firm. A major part of this infrastructure is a single standard electronic messaging system which reaches the majority of desktop computer users in PhoneCo.

With implementation beginning in July 1991, by early 1994 PhoneCo had 35 000 people connected to the corporate system on 470 different electronic messaging servers across Australia (Fig. 3).

PhoneCo’s SOE for the desktop defines the hardware configurations, operating system, software packages, training, provisioning and support issues and security matters for desktop computing.

## 4.2. Research design

To address our research questions a case design was chosen to suit the objectives of description and theory building (Benbasat et al., 1987). The objective is to develop a series of propositions for enhancing business processes using electronic messaging infrastructure. Using the natural controls (Lee, 1989) of a single-case environment we can study the application project-specific attributes whilst holding the firm-wide mail infrastructure constant. ‘Analytic generalisation’ was used where the previously developed theory is used as a template with which to compare the different projects in the case study (Yin, 1989, p. 38). If two or more cases are shown to support the same theory, then replication may be claimed.

The project is the unit of analysis. Twelve mail-enabled projects were chosen to be representative of Value and Sophistication at PhoneCo. IT infrastructure and projectspecific attributes gathered from interviews and project documentation are examined for each project, and propositions and conclusions are drawn.

To enable cross-referencing of the information gained, structured questions were asked of each of the 12 project managers of the projects and other relevant project staff during interviews of 1 <sup>1</sup>–2 hours’ duration. The questionnaire in Appendix A was used as a guide and each item was collected for each project.

Individuals from the ITD were also interviewed for 2 hours each, including a representative of the electronic messaging infrastructure group, and an electronic messagingenabled application developer.

The interview data was recorded using note-taking and tape-recording. Copies of project documentation, including scope documents, business cases and post-implementation review results, were examined. A practical demonstration was obtained of all the projects during a field visit. Follow-up questions were asked of each of the project managers by telephone, based on findings from interviews as required. In addition, one end-user of each project, chosen to be representative of the broader user base, was also interviewed by telephone. A total of 85 hours of interviews were conducted.

## 5.1.2. Report creation (Value: group tool; Sophistication: person/person)

Each month, branches within the ITD submit reports up through the organisational hierarchy. This was a clumsy process using paper and many photocopies. Assisted by the company’s single wordprocessor standard, documents are attached to electronic messaging messages and are sent by individuals to their managers at the end of each month. The manager collates the report and submits the combined and edited version. This may happen through up to four layers before the final report is complete.

## 5.1.3. Group scheduler (Value: group tool; Sophistication: person/person)

The group scheduler is an application available to all electronic messaging users that manages the process of arranging meetings between groups of people, and managing shared resources such as conference rooms. By using the corporate electronic messaging directory, users can select attendees and resources for a meeting. Confirmation messages are sent using specially formatted electronic messaging messages, which update the receiver’s schedule automatically once they elect to accept the invitation. If attendees do not use the scheduling application they receive a standard electronic messaging message.

The following two projects are presently applications managed by the business units where they were developed. However, they both have the potential to become infrastructure due to their broader applicability and would then be shared across the firm and managed by ITD.

## 5.1.4. FORUM facilities (Value: individual work flow; Sophistication: person/application)

A FORUM is a network-connected PC running a mail-enabled application. Any user of the corporate electronic messaging system can send a standard message to the mail address of the PC requesting a document in the ‘subject’ field of the message. The document will be forwarded as an attached file in an electronic messaging message. It is also possible for a user with the necessary security level to ‘post’ a document on the FORUM to be available to other users. FORUM provides group messaging and online library facilities to allow easy access to published manuals, and provides forums for discussing products or services to minimise reliance on telephone support hotlines.

## 5.1.5. Moving data (Value: individual work flow; Sophistication: application/application)

DB\_Move is the technology used to move data from databases (and/or stand-alone computer files) from one site to another, using electronic messaging. DB\_Move can be integrated with existing applications by launching it at predetermined intervals to achieve a data update effect. The prime use of DB\_Move is to extract all of the records in a system which have been recently updated and export them for integration into other database systems. It is best suited to the situation where particular sites ‘own’ records and maintain sole update capability (as in the Legal Database project, discussed below).

## 5.2. Electronic messaging applications

## 5.2.1. Internal Communications Ordering (Value: aid to business process; Sophistication: person/person)

PhoneCo currently spends hundreds of millions of dollars annually on internal telecommunications usage, used for running the business in addition to building the commercial products that comprise the network that PhoneCo sells to the Australian public. A constraint to the rate at which rationalisation of controllable costs can occur is imposed by the internal service-requesting process. The Internal Communications Ordering system replaced the system of faxing handwritten copies with an electronic form transmitted using corporate electronic messaging. This assisted in reducing labour input and time delays as well as greatly increasing data validity.

The proposed Stage 2 of the Internal Communications Ordering system includes automating the construction of telecommunications products prior to sale. This will cause the project to enable the firm’s business process (Value D in Fig. 5). As the application in Stage 2 will automatically generate electronic messages to conform on an order’s status to relevant personnel, it will achieve application/person messaging (Sophistication 4).

## 5.2.2. After Hours Orders (Value: aid to business activities; Sophistication: person/ person)

Previously, when a customer called after hours with an issue that could only be resolved during standard business hours, the staff hand-wrote requests and forwarded them to the appropriate area using facsimile. Problems found with this system were that it was resource-intensive, transmission was not assured, and there was no way of knowing in a timely manner that the advice had been received. The After Hours Orders solution removed the fax communications mechanism and replaced it with the electronic messaging infrastructure. It integrates background information from mainframe systems used by the operator, with details of the specific customer inquiry, and automatically routes this information to the correct destination using electronic messaging.

The planned Stage 2 of After Hours Orders will support a re-engineered process across all customer service centres. With the associated increased complexity the previous use of facsimile as backup to the electronic messaging system will be inadequate to the task. The project then will enable the business process (Value D in Fig. 5).

## 5.2.3. Sales Authorisation (Value: aid to business activities; Sophistication: person/ person)

Sales Authorisation is an electronic in-tray and authorisation system enabling a user to issue a request for a group to perform maintenance on customer data. Before the request can be acted upon it must be authorised, and the system provides the mechanism for this to occur, including providing the local supervisor with a copy as management information.

## 5.2.4. Legal Database (Value: group tool; Sophistication: application/application)

The Legal Database project is designed to bring together all of the information on legal liabilities within the corporation. Business units own all of the records they generate, although other sites may need to be able to view the records and report on them. The system is implemented as a set of independent databases sharing common information. Site-specific information, including any new records or changes, is despatched by each of the nine sites to a central site on a weekly basis using the electronic messaging infrastructure. Once at the central site, all of the information is collated and a bundle of global changes is despatched back to each of the remote sites at a regular frequency, using an electronic messaging-enabled application. Legal Database uses the DB\_Move technology.

## 5.2.5. Staff Suggestions (Value: aid to business activities; Sophistication: person/ application)

There had been general dissatisfaction with the PhoneCo staff suggestions scheme and the process was completely re-engineered to better suit the business needs. Electronic messaging was used to assist the new process. Staff suggestion schemes need people across the firm to be involved and the only system used by the majority of staff and management is electronic messaging.

Key aspects of the system are the involvement of the individual suggester’s supervisor to check relevance to the local workplace, and the management information that is accessible by anyone in the firm. The system also sends reminders and allows the assessment panels to share their evaluations. This builds on the person/person functionality with some person/application attributes. By focusing on the individuals in the company who are responsible for quality management it is intended that the system will become part of the overall approach to quality in the company.

## 5.2.6. Sales Information Collation (Value: aid to business activities; Sophistication: application/application)

The process used in one business unit to collate sales management information has been re-engineered to use the electronic messaging system, reducing the reliance on people transferring information manually. The previous process required the account managers to supply a secretarial area with information on sales prospects and likely revenue using text from various sources. The secretarial group entered this information in a simple stand-alone database. A separate group then used this information to generate a monthly report for the general manager to read.

The revised system is based on a database application accessed on the account manager’s workstation, which ensures all relevant information is captured. The account manager owns this information, but on every new entry or update an electronic messaging message is automatically sent with the information to the general manager’s mailbox. When the general manager wishes to view the information, the application on their workstation is started which extracts information from the electronic messaging messages, and updates their database. The information can be viewed using a number of graphs and tables, or standard printouts can be generated. Each account manager only has access to their own area of operation, and the general manager is able to get a consolidated view of the whole business area.

## 5.2.7. Skills Database (Value: aid to business activities; Sophistication: application/ application)

PhoneCo’s central human resources (HR) group required a uniform corporate approach to skills management. They wished to deploy a computer system throughout the organisation that allowed the data to be owned and managed locally by regional HR groups, but allowed central reporting.

The corporate electronic messaging system was chosen as the mechanism for the data distribution between the regional sites running the Skills Management System. The application that allows the process is called Skills Database. It is an application-to-application system with no human users. Skills Database consists of a central management system, or control centre, to control Skills Database; and mail robots (separate PCs) which take instructions and pass messages between the central hub and the remote sites.

## 6. Discussion

There are several patterns that emerge in examining the attributes in Fig. 4.

The larger cost projects tend to have greater site numbers, as would be expected with the increased complexity of implementation management. Relative process improvement ranges from unmeasurable in three cases, due to a new process being implemented; through a subjectively evaluated ‘more effective’; to ‘10 times’ or ‘many times’ in three cases. All the individuals interviewed believed the effort had been worthwhile. In general the projects delivered impressive improvements in performance.

Development elapsed time ranges from days up to 12 months – a short amount of time compared with the typical development undertaken in this company, where months to years would be more normal. It was clear from the interviews that the time for effort expended for the seven non-infrastructure cases was proportional to project cost, and was no more than a few days of effort by three or fewer people for all the projects (excepting the largest project: Internal Communications Ordering).

The Infrastructure projects discussed are now provided by the ITD as part of the company-wide electronic messaging system services. They have the largest potential application users, as would be expected for infrastructure offerings.

The shortest real-enough time was 2 hours, considerably in excess of the capability of the electronic messaging system with its 10-minute median delivery time. However, the lack of an absolute delivery time maximum (the ITD only provided mean and median measurements of message transit) also discouraged the use of the electronic messaging infrastructure for applications which were more time critical.

In this section a number of propositions are presented which emerged from the case analysis.

Proposition 1. The existence of the electronic messaging infrastructure enabled a broad range of systems to be built efficiently over a range of Values and Sophistications.

A series of successful projects using electronic messaging were developed at PhoneCo. Given the size of the firm and the complexity of the projects the typical cost was relatively low and elapsed time short. It was rare to see any software development project completed at PhoneCo in less than 12 months and under \$1 million dollars due to the complexities of ensuring compatibilities with existing systems. The relatively low cost and short time to completion were particularly impressive as the projects were generally built to be compatible with the firm-wide SOE.

Fig. 5 illustrates that the projects included systems that covered almost the full range of the Value and Sophistication possibilities. No completed projects had a Value of Enables the Business Process (i.e., D or E); however, two were planned. The Value and Richness framework appears to be a helpful tool to map and classify the use of electronic messaging.

Proposition 2. Application projects using electronic messaging are more integrated into the business processes (with higher Value and Sophistication) than infrastructure projects. There is a tendency for application projects to become infrastructure projects over time.

The split between applications and infrastructure is also illustrated on the Value and Sophistication framework (Fig. 5). The use of electronic messaging in infrastructure projects was at the lower levels of Value and Sophistication while the application projects were at higher levels. All projects classified as applications were Sophistication 2 or more, had a Value of B or C, and were positioned above the Infrastructure projects in Fig. 5.

Applications are generally more integrated into the business processes and have greater Value potential. Infrastructure was observed across the full range of Sophistication but with lower levels of Value potential. Applications tended to have larger project cost and have more impressive results as measured by process improvement.

Some of the applications actually utilised the lower-level Value and Sophistication infrastructure projects. The infrastructure projects, as a consequence of being shared, can be considered as part of the messaging services layer (3) in Fig. 1. The infrastructure projects on the Value and Sophistication framework (e.g., Report Creation) are more complex and tailored to PhoneCo than the more generic messaging services such as providing return receipt. However, over time it is likely some more of the useful electronic messaging applications will become infrastructure.

We posit this migration of application to infrastructure as a strong trend for electronic messaging-enabled systems. To be infrastructure the application will need to be broadly applicable, be consistent with the SOE and deliver either economies of scale or synergies between the business units (Weill et al., 1995).

Proposition 3. Electronic messaging’s contribution to the business is dependent on the presence of a standard technology infrastructure with sufficient Value potential and Sophistication. This requires centralised approval, design, development, deployment and maintenance standards and guidelines.

During the interviews all project managers stated that the presence of centralised corporate-wide standards and guidelines within PhoneCo was critical to the success of their electronic messaging use. We posit this is a necessary condition for utilising any IT infrastructure.

All projects examined relied on the presence of the lower three layers of the model of electronic messaging as infrastructure (Fig. 1). Project managers indicated they understood that the base standards below the messaging services layer (i.e., layers 1 and 2) were the uniform desktop environment (i.e., the Desktop Standard Operating Environment). In addition to providing a consistent base over which to layer the messaging services, this uniform environment had the benefits of enabling electronic messaging with high levels of Sophistication (i.e., 4 and 5).

There was also the quantifiable benefit of cost savings in purchase, education and support. The presentation facilities provided by the desktop standard ensured systems designers and developers could plan on a high-quality graphical user interface (GUI), not the lowest common denominator previously used of the character user interface.

The project managers agreed that the inclusion of messaging with a standard application programming interface into the base standards allowed them not to have to be concerned with the messaging architecture. This meant that they could concentrate on their business unit’s processes and appropriate applications of the infrastructure to the processes.

However, to achieve the funding and provision of the messaging infrastructure required a centralised approach. None of the business units would have provided a firm-wide messaging service on their own. If not centrally provided each of the business units would have built their own non-compatible electronic mail platforms. Several different electronic messaging systems did exist in PhoneCo before they were consolidated. If this diverse development continued the platforms would have been extremely difficult to consolidate into a firm-wide service and would have resulted in a competitive disadvantage (Keen, 1991, p. 34) when trying to deliver strategic initiatives such as a single point of contact for all a customer’s needs.

The provision of a centralised messaging infrastructure requires a centrally set IT architecture (Earl, 1989; Keen, 1991, p. 81). Within PhoneCo the relevant architectures were called an SOE, which initially was received with much resistance by the business units.

However, by not wavering from the policy of an SOE, except in exceptional circumstances, and offering price incentives gradually the SOE was established. The ISD group building the electronic messaging services took an infrastructure approach, and charged internally for the system as a corporate overhead. A clear business case for replacing all existing messaging systems with one corporate system was justified on the basis of messaging economies alone. Once the funding had been obtained centrally for the project, the users of the infrastructure saw the products as free. To have then purchased nonstandard products would cost money, versus this ‘free’ approach. This was key to gaining acceptance of the system.

The SOE allowed messaging projects to be undertaken with increasing Value and Sophistication over time. In addition to messaging the SOE covered the desktop, some applications, some shared data items and telecommunications networks where 27 Wide Area Networks were reduced to two.

The business units were also looking for as much guidance as possible to assist them to exploit the firm’s infrastructure. There was a sense that along with the construction of infrastructure came the firm-wide responsibility for ensuring it was well understood and used. This assistance was necessary to assist the business units in the development of standardised applications that would be shareable with other business units, who often had similar needs.

There were some problems due to inconsistent implementation of the SOE (e.g., LAN standards) and lack of change management across the company in the Internal Communications Ordering, After Hours Orders and Staff Suggestions projects. This was caused by a very broad standards definition promulgated by the ITD, and the liberties (and creativity) taken on their implementation by technically able LAN administrators and end users.

Proposition 4. Electronic messaging must be perceived as reliable to support applications that enable a firm’s business processes (Value D, Fig. 5) in contrast to uses that only aid business activities (Value C, Fig. 5).

In the Staff Suggestions and Internal Communications Ordering projects, there was a lack of trust in the electronic messaging services used as a foundation for systems. Project managers often ensured that backup systems were available to provide a perception of security in case of electronic messaging problems. When experience with the new technology provides sufficient evidence of stability, it is likely that focus will be reduced on these backup systems. The project managers also believed the infrastructure group should provide an absolute guarantee of system performance, which was considered to be not yet achievable by the ISD.

Due to the distributed nature of the electronic messaging infrastructure there was considerable dependence on local expertise for success. Variability of support available at each site meant that there was variability of success in projects such as Internal Communications Ordering. Many sites had thrust untrained or unskilled people into the role of systems administrator, causing considerable problems, usually despite the best intentions of the individuals concerned.

There have not yet been any cases in PhoneCo of applications that directly enable highvalue business processes, despite a huge effort to stabilise the electronic messaging system by the ITD infrastructure builders and substantial support provided by the vendor. Until the developers and project managers have greater faith in the manageability and robustness of the electronic messaging system it is unlikely that applications will be built that are above Value C.

There are two project managers who have a planned second stage where their projects will cross from Value C (i.e., an aid) to D (i.e., enabling to the firm’s business processes) (After Hours Orders and Internal Communications Ordering) (see Fig. 5). In discussions with the ISD it was decided that there was not yet sufficient ‘level of comfort’ with the infrastructure’s manageability to allow their next stage to be essential to the business and critically reliant on electronic messaging.

Proposition 5. Increasing exploitation of electronic messaging infrastructure is dependent on raising the level of awareness of the infrastructure’s potential.

To create projects which are more woven into the business process with higher Value and Sophistication requires an awareness of infrastructure potential. Ultimately it is the user who will have to recognise the applications where messaging can make a difference to their business. The infrastructure builders in PhoneCo’s ITD put in considerable effort to ensure that the majority of the firm were aware of major advantages of the electronic messaging infrastructure. These efforts included:

successes written up in print media internally (and considerable exposure was given to their successes externally to PhoneCo);

• technology updates given regularly to key executives;

• a ‘white’ paper broadly disseminated, focusing on business benefits of the electronic messaging system;

• showcase applications developed to tangibly demonstrate business relevance;

• project groups chosen to participate in the prototyping of a real system at no charge to the group concerned (to reduce their concern at apparent ‘experimentation’);

• an electronic messaging ‘culture’ was encouraged by rolling out the system top-down, considerable effort put into establishing a critical user mass (considered to be around one-third of the potential user base), and adequate user training and education made available to all.

Despite these efforts, there was a general lack of understanding of the infrastructure’s capability beyond person-to-person messaging, which means that it is unlikely to be fully exploited. Many of the projects took longer than was planned as this type of infrastructure exploitation is new to both the business and to IT professionals, who often have little awareness of electronic messaging potential.

The infrastructure group was not always aware of projects utilising the system, so that any necessary changes to the infrastructure could not be appropriately managed by applications. For example, the problems caused by insufficient awareness were shown when the implementation of file size restrictions on electronic messaging messages caused the Skills Database project considerable difficulties.

Proposition 6. Over time the existence of the electronic mail infrastructure provided organisational flexibility and created the opportunity to build new business functionality more quickly.

The adoption of electronic messaging at PhoneCo exhibits both the characteristics of the diffusion of new technologies and infrastructure. The above propositions and the placement of the projects on the Value and Sophistication framework support Fishman and Kemerer’s (adapted from Arthur, 1988) ‘five sources of increasing returns’. Electronic messaging is a complex organisational technology (Attewell, 1992) and to have projects that are more woven into the business process requires: (1) learning by doing, (2) economies of scale, (3) technological Interrelatedness (i.e., the emergence of supporting technical infrastructure), (4) informational increasing returns (i.e., lowering of uncertainty) and (5) network externalities (belonging to a larger network). PhoneCo was reasonably successful at achieving and encouraging these five sources of value.

Furthermore, the establishment of a firm-wide messaging infrastructure provided PhoneCo with a significant organisational flexibility offering strategic opportunities (Porter and Millar, 1985). The messaging infrastructure provided the opportunities to build business functionality quickly which resulted in spectacular process improvements. This is an example of an Enabling infrastructure (Weill et al., 1996) where the objectives are organisational flexibility to quickly respond to changes in market need rather than to cut costs. PhoneCo was able to achieve both an Enabling messaging infrastructure and cost savings by consolidating several systems into one.

## 7. Recommendations

This section makes recommendations and identifies insights from PhoneCo and the literature for organisations wishing to build applications on their electronic messaging system.

7.1. Attributes required for project use to increase the Value and Sophistication of messaging projects

For a messaging project to become more integrated into the business process there must be uniform and pervasive supporting infrastructure and architecture within the firm. The infrastructure must also be centrally managed (or outsourced), with appropriate system management.

For applications of electronic messaging to be essential to the firm’s business process electronic messaging technology must ‘grow up’. It must be more manageable and have a higher level of reliability than the common distributed PC-based shared file systems, which is still an immature technology compared with facsimile, telephone or on-line transaction processing. These capabilities should include the ability for the corporate system to be monitored and controlled from a single location, and 100% guarantees for delivery of mail within a specified time (guaranteed variance). The fundamental difficulty of software distribution (for installation of systems and ongoing maintenance) in large geographically dispersed firms must also be solved, to ensure sufficient change management capability for distributed applications. If present, this manageability will provide the confidence to use the infrastructure as a basis for applications.

7.2. Manage the evolution of electronic messaging application projects to infrastructure projects over time

As the infrastructure is built over time, the definition of what it comprises will change. In PhoneCo’s case the network, transport protocols, and messaging services of the model in Fig. 1 are all standardised and part of the infrastructure. The infrastructure applications (such as the FORUM system developed in PhoneCo) are beginning to provide services to a broader need than a specific project. The ITD must be aware of these applications, and ensure they are included in the corporation’s infrastructure management structure. This will be necessary when issues like standard of service and level of management required have outgrown the ability or resources of a project group, or when specific functionality is being duplicated by many projects.

## 7.3. Use the Value and Sophistication framework to find opportunities for new electronic messaging-enabled projects

The Value and Sophistication framework is one way to classify and document the existing projects using electronic messaging in a firm. Studying the framework complete with existing projects is a practical way to identify:

• other projects of the same Value and Sophistication to those already existing in the firm but in different areas;

• opportunities to use the messaging services for projects with Value and Sophistication that are technically feasible but not being utilised (e.g., cell D5 in Fig. 5 for PhoneCo).

## 7.4. Infrastructure must be justified separately from project-specific needs

Electronic messaging infrastructure should be justified separately from project needs. PhoneCo has built its electronic messaging infrastructure using this approach, which has allowed the firm to be very advanced in its use. With the previous approach taken, project or business unit-based justification encouraged the building of disparate unconnected and incompatible systems that only suited local as distinct from corporate needs.

Separately justifying infrastructure allows the firm to take into account the full range of projects the infrastructure would enable. Infrastructure of this type is making an investment which buys an option (i.e., similar to a Real Financial Option) to build systems faster and more cheaply than without it (Kambil et al., 1993). Also infrastructures are designed to be used by a number of different applications with a new application utilising the infrastructure at a low marginal cost. Thus infrastructures (e.g., electronic messaging) have different characteristics from applications and need to be justified separately.

Infrastructures can be justified by identifying the full range of applications they will enable in addition to any cost reductions relative to the existing service. In the case of PhoneCo there was a cost advantage to rationalising electronic messaging and the purchase of the option to build future applications not previously possible.

## 7.5. Business processes should be examined critically, and if necessary re-engineered, before applying the technology

In both the Internal Communications Ordering and After Hours Orders projects it was found to be easier to introduce new technology to assist an existing process and then re-engineer the business process. If this approach is followed, however, it is likely that there will be less enthusiasm to achieve the overall plan to change the business process.

Changes should be made to the business process before developing an IT solution, otherwise the technology is often just assisting a process to be done less than optimally but faster.

Once technology is introduced to assist business process improvements, it can be easier to define business process standards. This approach was taken in the Staff Suggestions project, where the project manager believed it provided considerable process gains. The Internal Communications Ordering project just simulated their existing system using technology, sped up the problems, but did not get to the core of the concerns. The Staff Suggestions project examined and changed the process.

Quinn (1992), p. 161) notes that this problem was present in many of the firms he investigated where they consistently:

attained neither significant organisational changes nor efficiency gains if they installed new desktop computers, LANs, or elaborate workstation software without totally re-engineering their processes.

## 8. Conclusion

The original justification for PhoneCo’s electronic messaging infrastructure was the reduction in costs due to dropping the number of electronic messaging systems existing in 1991 from 12 to one. The ability to leverage off the infrastructure was mentioned, but as it was a soft benefit it was not made essential to the justification. There is now concrete evidence of reduction of costs.

The Value and Sophistication framework was applied to the electronic messaging projects at PhoneCo. The framework illustrated where PhoneCo had utilised electronic messaging and where opportunities still existed. Projects were successfully completed relatively faster and more cheaply, providing evidence of the strategic implications of electronic messaging.

The opportunities were generally in the areas of using electronic messaging at higher levels of Value Potential. To achieve this level of use more time and effort will be required to manage electronic messaging as it follows the diffusion pattern of many ‘complex organisational technologies’.

Throughout the interviews and the analysis of the projects there was a common theme that electronic messaging has totally changed the way people in the organisation work. There was acknowledgement that it can be interrupting and distracting (as is probably true of the introduction of many new technologies), but also universal agreement that the business is much better off because of the improved communication.

Very large amounts have been invested in PhoneCo’s infrastructure in recent years, as PhoneCo buys an option for the future (Kambil et al., 1993), as it plans for long-term business benefits and in-built flexibility. PhoneCo’s ITD has been entrusted with forming the needed balance between building the centralised infrastructure and working with PhoneCo’s business units to develop the systems they require.

The project managers appreciated the necessity of the infrastructure approach taken, where the electronic messaging infrastructure is charged as a ‘corporate tax’ to each business unit based on the number of people in the unit. They appeared relieved that this responsibility was taken from them, allowing them to concentrate on their business needs, not on technology needs.
