# Government regulation through voluntary cooperation: a follow-up study of the strategic impact of information technology

Trevor A. Williams

School of Management, Human Resources and Industrial Relations, Queensland University of Technology, GPO Box 2434, Brisbane Q4001, Australia

Received April 1995; revised December 1995

## Abstract

A follow-up study of Australian tax agents' attitudes towards the Electronic Lodgement Service introduced by the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) found that perceptions of improved cooperation had declined. Despite further improvements at the level of routine transactions and a strong increase in perceptions that the benefits of the new system offset the costs to agents, divergent goals and greater agent uncertainty about how the ATO would use its monitoring capacity and regulatory authority appear to have restricted the system's positive contributions to regulatory strategy.

Keywords: Government Regulation; Voluntary cooperation; Information Technology

## 1. Introduction

This paper reports and discusses the results of a follow-up study of Australian tax agents' attitudes towards electronic tax return lodgement. Like many government agencies, the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) is developing a regulatory strategy based on securing the voluntary cooperation of the regulated to achieve compliance. A general problem of regulatory reform is that much regulation is intended to protect diffuse public interests while the costs of compliance are borne by particular private interests (Zald, 1978), and securing voluntary compliance depends on the combined effects of inducements towards and constraints on cooperation (O'Toole, 1988). The ATO's 'Modernisation Program' involves a combination of organisational and technological innovation in a strategy to reduce routine checking of tax returns, improve services which assist taxpayers to comply with tax laws, and strengthen the agency's auditing capacity as a deterrent to potential tax evaders. The strategic objective of the program is to ‘maximise voluntary taxpayer compliance with tax laws’.

The electronic lodgement service (ELS) was the first major initiative in this program. Tax agents lodge more than two-thirds of tax returns and play an important role in the administration of the Australian tax system and in the level of compliance. Prior to ELS, most agents were satisfied with their role of minimising their clients' tax obligations, giving independent advice to taxpayers, trying to ensure full compliance with tax laws, and acting as intermediaries between taxpayers and the ATO. However, many agents resented doing work which they thought should be the ATO's responsibility, many also felt that they bore too much of the risk of penalty for noncompliance, and agents wanted more input to tax policy and decisions (Williams, 1994). ELS was offered to agents first, and an important question was how it affected cooperation between agents and the ATO.

The first study (Williams, 1994) examines tax agents' perceptions of whether cooperation between themselves and the ATO had increased due to electronic lodgement and factors influencing these perceptions. Following Oliver's (1990) conceptual integration of research on interorganisational relations, it was hypothesised that ELS would:

1. Reduce the asymmetry which agents perceived in their relations with the ATO, by giving them more control over transactions because they entered tax return data direct without intervention by ATO staff and more influence in ATO decisions to the extent that the ATO accepted agents' assessments of their clients' tax liabilities.

2. Increase perceived stability in relations by reducing ATO processing errors and delays, providing agents with immediate feedback on returns accepted or rejected for processing, establishing shorter and more definite timeframes and reducing client anxiety.

3. Increase perceived efficiency through faster issue of assessment notices and refunds, improved cashflow for agents, less time spent making inquiries to the ATO, and business growth by attracting more clients and offering new electronic financial services (Venkatraman and Kambil, 1991).

4. Increase the legitimacy standing which agents perceived they had with the ATO, because ELS is not compulsory and agents had an opportunity to demonstrate their willingness to cooperate voluntarily with a regulatory initiative.

5. Increase perceived reciprocity by offering benefits to agents and creating greater commonality or mutuality of goals due to agents and the ATO sharing an information system that benefited them both.

A stratified random sample of 200 tax agents was interviewed in 1992, two years after ELS was introduced. Main findings were that:

1. On asymmetry, agents had more control over their transactions with the ATO, over processing errors and delays, and their own planning and organisation due to ELS, but the majority did not perceive that they had more input to ATO decisions, they thought that ELS gave the ATO greater ability to monitor agent compliance performance, and were uncertain whether the ATO used this ability to penalise or help agents.

2. On stability, the majority were more confident in how the ATO would treat their clients' returns and reported fewer delays with returns which made them more certain of outcomes, but doubted or denied that ELS gave them faster access to Tax Rulings and Advance Opinions, that ELS enabled them to help clients more to plan for tax, that ELS facilitated the integration of tax preparation with other financial services, and that clients were less anxious about their returns (although most also thought that ELS did not increase the probability that their clients would be audited by the ATO).

3. On efficiency, most respondents reported getting paid more quickly by their clients and spending less time making inquiries to the ATO, the sample was divided on whether it was less costly to lodge returns, and most did not think that they were getting more clients or that ELS created new business opportunities (although they did not think that ELS threatened their business).

4. On legitimacy, the sample was divided on whether the ATO had a more favourable attitude towards ELS than non ELS agents, but most thought that ELS helped their public images with clients.

5. On reciprocity, most respondents thought that ELS resulted in them doing more work for the ATO, their perceptions of whether the gains to them from ELS offset the costs were mixed, the sample was divided on whether the ATO did more for ELS agents, and most denied that they had more in common with the ATO due to ELS but also thought that there was now less tension and conflict with the ATO.

6. Almost two-thirds of respondents perceived that cooperation between them and the ATO had improved with ELS (Williams, 1994).

The findings provided qualified evidence that ELS was helping to promote voluntary cooperation between tax agents and the ATO which, given the important role played by agents, supported the ATO's strategic objective of maximising voluntary taxpayer compliance. However, it had not been possible to conduct a before-and-after study of the change. The agents' experience with ELS varied from those who had used it from the beginning to others who had adopted it more recently. ELS was still evolving with further enhancements to come. Tax laws and regulations were changing, and the tax system and tax agents had both become subjects of national reviews. Other technological and organisational changes were being introduced through the Modernisation Program. Therefore, it was important to conduct a follow-up study to test the stability of the results from the first study and identify changes.

One year after the initial interviews, in June and July 1993, 60 agents were re-interviewed. They were randomly selected from the original sample, but selection was restricted to agents who dealt with the ATO through four of the seven branch offices included in the first study. Background characteristics of respondents, including the question of which branch an agent dealt with, had not significantly affected responses in the first study and selection was confined to agents who lodged returns through the two central business district and two greater metropolitan branches. The 60 agents were asked most of the questions which had been put to them before as suggested by the conceptual categories of asymmetry, stability, efficiency, legitimacy and reciprocity (Williams, 1994). The questions are paraphrased in Table 1. Respondents were contacted by telephone to obtain their agreement and all interviews were conducted by telephone. Where an agent was not available to be re-interviewed the agent was replaced by the next one in the sample.

The next section compares the results obtained in the follow-up study with the 1992 responses of the 60 agents. The results are then discussed in relation to relevant findings of other studies which have been reported since this study commenced, and conclusions are drawn about the implications for the strategic role of information technology in regulatory reform.

## 2. Results of the follow-up study

Table 1 compares the percentage distributions of the 1992 and 1993 responses of the 60 agents. Significant differences between the 1992 and 1993 responses of the 60 agents, as determined by chi-squared analysis, are asterisked. The 1992 responses of the 60 agents were similar to those of the whole sample in most respects. More of the 60 agents thought that the ATO used its monitoring ability through ELS to help them, that it was less costly to lodge returns on ELS, and that there was less tension and conflict with the ATO as a result of ELS.

In 1993 respondents became more definite that ELS gave them greater control over their own planning and organisation (item 2 in Table 1) and there was a substantial increase in perceptions that the gains to agents from ELS offset any extra work which they did for the ATO (item 21). However, respondents had become more uncertain about whether the ATO used its monitoring ability through ELS to help or penalise agents (item 5) and whether ELS increased their clients' prospects of being audited (item 11). Perceptions of getting more clients (item 15) and of having less tensions and conflicts with the ATO due to ELS (item 21) became negative. Whereas in 1992 almost two-thirds of these respondents thought that cooperation with the ATO had improved due to ELS (item 25), such perceptions declined significantly in 1993.

## 3. Discussion

Comparison of the 1992 and 1993 results confirm the positive effects of ELS in giving agents greater control over lodgement and processing. However, respondents' uncertainty about eventual outcomes, in terms of how the ATO uses its ability to monitor agent performance and the prospects of agents' ELS clients being audited, appears to have increased. ELS is no longer seen as helping agents to obtain more clients, and the effects of ELS in reducing tension and conflict also seem to have diminished. On the other hand, perceptions that the benefits of ELS offset them doing extra work for the ATO increased significantly. Relating these findings to the issues in agent–ATO relations prior to ELS, ELS may have reduced agent resentment at doing work which they think should be performed by the ATO, but appears to have had less effect on the issues of responsibility and risk in tax compliance and agent participation in ATO policy and decisions.

Increased control over returns lodgement and processing should increase agents' influence in how the ATO treats their clients because most tax returns are unlikely to receive further attention from the ATO once they have been electronically processed and assessed (Joint Committee of Public Accounts, 1993, p. 292). However, particularly since the introduction of self-assessment (Williams, 1994), taxpayers have increasingly sought professional help with their returns and the knowledge required of agents has become more extensive and technical. At the same time, computer IT has enabled the ATO to target specific taxpayers and it may adjust a taxpayer's assessment for up to four years after the income year for which the return was lodged. Although most taxpayers will not be targeted, the enhanced ability of the ATO to target them increases the perceived possibility that they may be investigated and that they will be penalised for innocent errors in their returns (Joint Committee of Public Accounts, 1993, pp. 292–298). The uncertainty which this creates may be reflected in the changes between 1992 and 1993 in respondents' perceptions of whether the ATO uses its monitoring capacity to help or penalise agents and whether ELS clients face greater prospects of being audited by the ATO.

The 1993 results confirm the 1992 findings that ELS has not contributed to the sustained business growth of agents. The competitive advantages of electronic integration which were predicted by Venkatraman and Kambil (1991) were never recognised by these agents, and they had not expected early refunds obtainable through ELS to provide a competitive advantage because agents anticipated that most or all agents would use ELS eventually and that it would become available to taxpayers who did not use agents (Woolcott Research, 1991). In fact, the results of a recent taxpayer survey strongly indicate that the prospect of early refunds is not a primary consideration in taxpayers' decisions to use tax agents. Rather, it is the desire to lodge correct returns and avoid trouble with the ATO (Morgan Research Centre, 1994, p. v). The significant decline in 1993 in the proportion of respondents reporting more clients due to ELS suggests that any effects were short-term.

The business benefits of ELS are more evident in agents' reports of being paid more quickly by clients and of spending less time making inquiries to the ATO about their clients' returns. Almost two-thirds of respondents in both years reported that they spent less time on inquiries. The success of ELS in greatly reducing intervention by ATO staff in returns processing has lessened the need for agents to make inquiries about their clients' returns, and ATO staff are able to be more responsible to the inquiries that agents do make. ATO staff in nine branch offices said that ELS enabled them to advise agents of the exact status of returns, explain precisely where in the process errors had occurred, and offer more realistic estimates of when tax assessments would be issued. The only significant loss of control occurred with returns which had to be taken out of electronic processing for special attention such as Tax Rulings (Australian Taxation Office, 1993, pp. 30–31).

On the general question of costs and benefits, the ATO (1993, p. 57) points out that the costs of ELS to agents varied from a few hundred dollars for agents whose businesses were already computerised to several thousand dollars for agents who had to invest in computer equipment and training. The Joint Committee of Public Accounts (1993, pp. 129–131) concluded that overall ELS was ‘a popular and efficient system’ but that, while the gains outweighed the costs for most agents, the ATO had benefited more than agents had. On the other hand, respondents’ perceptions that the benefits of ELS outweighed the costs to them almost doubled. Woolcott Research (1991, p. 8) also noted a perception among agents that the ATO gained more from ELS than they did, but that perceptions became more favourable with increased usage and experience of ELS and that agents gained more benefits when they used modem transmission.

Even so, the large increase in perceived benefits exceeding costs is not reflected in increased perceptions of cooperation between agents and the ATO. In fact the results suggest a reverse trend in perceptions of fewer tensions and conflicts, of the ATO as doing more to help ELS agents, and of improved cooperation with the ATO due to ELS. The National Review of Standards for the Tax Profession Steering Committee (1993) emphasises that tax agents act on behalf of taxpayers and not the ATO but also have obligations to assist in the administration of the tax system. This duality seems to be reflected in divergent perceptions among respondents of how ELS affects reciprocity in the relationship. Disagreement about whether ELS had promoted greater commonality between agents and the ATO persisted across the period of the two interviews, suggesting that different generalised predispositions towards their roles and relations with the ATO affected agents' perceptions of how ELS affected reciprocity in the relationship (Woolcott Research, 1991). ELS does not seem to have made further inroads into perceived divergence between goals and its perceived effects in reducing tension and conflict appear to have weakened. The persistence of different goals and agent reactions to tensions between their efforts to represent their clients' interests and their obligation to assist the administration of the tax system appear to set limits to the practical scope for increased cooperation.

## 4. Conclusions

This study proceeded from the premise that, rather than predicting the strategic effects of new IT systems from what they are designed to do, implementation and operation of new systems are interwove with the other relations that exist between the organisations concerned. In this regard, advocates of IT and of regulatory reform both tend to emphasise the importance of economic benefits in promoting increased cooperation between organisations (Swann, 1988, chapter 1; Rockart and Short, 1989; Venkatraman and Kambil, 1991). The findings reported here suggest that economic benefits may be neither as straightforward nor as singularly important as is implied by these views. Some of the intended economic benefits, such as gaining new clients and business growth, seem not to have been as important as was supposed, and may have been outweighed by other considerations such as the desire for greater participation and certainty in ATO decisions affecting their clients. Perceptions of convergent or divergent aims also affect the reactions of the regulated to regulatory initiatives such as ELS. Rather than remaining stable or increasing, respondents' perceptions of improved cooperation due to ELS declined between 1992 and 1993. This was despite a substantial increase in perceptions that the benefits of the innovation offset any costs to agents, and despite system enhancements such as agents being able to lodge more types of tax returns through ELS and more agents transmitting returns via modem. However, the decline in perceived improvement in cooperation was consistent with declining perceptions of fewer tensions and conflicts and increased uncertainty about the ATO's use of its monitoring capacity and regulatory authority.

Where the goals of regulators and regulated are actually or potentially in conflict, effective participation by the regulated in regulatory policy and decision making may be necessary to gain their continued support for regulatory aims and methods (Selznick, 1945; Thompson, 1967, pp. 35–36). The conditions which are necessary for effective participation in public decision making have been examined at length (Ayres and Braithwaite, 1992; Emery, 1989). These analyses strongly suggest the need for face-to-face interaction where the contribution of IT may be limited (Keisler et al., 1988; Emery, 1989). While there appears to be a significant role for IT in supporting regulatory reform strategies at the level of routine transactions, governments generally have not pursued the issue of participation in regulatory decision making very far. IT may have less to contribute to this dimension of regulatory reform, although its role in supporting participative decision making requires further investigation. At the same time, lack of participation appears, from this study, to constitute a barrier between regulators and regulated which restricts regulatory reform strategies and the effective use of IT to support these strategies.
