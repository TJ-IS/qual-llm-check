# Assessing realized information systems strategy

Yolande E. Chan $^{a,*}$ , Sid L. Huff $^{b}$ , Duncan G. Copeland $^{b}$

$^{a}$ School of Business, Queen's University, Kingston, Canada K7L 3N6

$^{b}$ Richard Ivey School of Business, University of Western Ontario, London ON, Canada N6A 3K7

Received September 1996; received in revised form April 1997; accepted April 1997

## Abstract

This research developed and validated a tool to measure realized information systems strategy, or existing uses of information technology in organizations. It was recognized that intended uses of technology often differ from actual uses. The objective of the study was to determine a valid and reliable way of quantifying how information technology is actually used by organizations to provide support for business operations. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Information systems/technology strategy; Information systems strategic orientation; Information systems strategic fit; Strategic alignment

## 1. The importance of realized information systems strategy

Businesses face enormous challenges. Global competition, industry shakeouts, inter-organizational networking, company downsizing and other pressures make it important for companies to execute their strategies as well as possible. But in order to properly execute, it helps to understand the organization's strategies—including its information systems (IS) strategy. This is more difficult than it first appears.

All organizations have strategies, although not all organizations have formalized strategies. Some write their strategy down, some just talk about their strategy, while others never even mention the word. Mintzberg and others have differentiated between intended strategy and realized strategy (Mintzberg, 1978). The intended strategy is the organization's ‘official’ strategy (which may or may not be written down). In contrast, the realized strategy is the strategy as actually reflected in decisions made and activities undertaken by the organization's actors.

In the IS area, it is often incorrectly assumed that intended strategy is the only strategy of interest, and that strategic processes are conscious, analytic, top-down, control-oriented, and simply modeled (Ciborra and Jelassi 1994). However:

Unfortunately, in everyday practice strategy formulation differs from what is implied by such prescriptions and assumptions. Incrementalism, muddling through, myopic and evolutionary decision-making processes seem to prevail.... The rational bias towards conscious thought and full, explicit articulation of strategy assumes, implicitly, that the environment is highly predictable and the unfolding of events is itself sequenced, so as to allow an orderly alternation of formulation, deliberation and implementation. Often, however, during implementation surprises occur....

(Ciborra and Jelassi, 1994, pp. 8)

For IS researchers to have a complete understanding of an organization's IS strategy, it is useful to understand both the intended and the realized strategy, and to evaluate both what was planned and what actually happened. Understanding intended strategy usually involves examining the existing documentation (IS strategy statements, documents, minutes of planning meetings, etc.), coupled with asking questions of key informants regarding the organization's formal IS directions and strategic intent. A lot of past IS research has focused on shedding light on intended strategy, and has dealt with the matter of IS strategic planning (e.g. Earl, 1989; Keen, 1991; Raghunathan and King, 1988; Ward et al., 1990). However, very little research has examined realized IS strategy. Also, very little research has addressed the measurement of either intended or realized IS strategy. Researchers and practitioners have few tools with which they can assess alternative IS strategies. In this article, we ask and answer the following question: “How does one go about understanding and assessing realized IS strategy?”

We propose an approach for characterizing and quantifying company deployments and uses of information systems, i.e. the realized IS strategy of an organization. The approach is based on earlier research by Venkatraman, who introduced the notion of strategic orientation. It is part of our long-term research stream on IS strategy and alignment (Chan and Huff, 1992, 1993; Chan et al., 1997). Our approach enables managers and researchers to examine a portfolio of company information systems in terms of the types of support provided for business activities, and the adequacy of these forms of support. It provides a framework within which realized IS strategy and contributions to firm performance can be assessed. Areas of weak or inadequate support can be identified and corrected.

First, we briefly present the literature on the measurement of IS strategy. Then we present and evaluate a comparative measurement approach to assessing realized IS strategy. Next, we introduce an alternative taxonomy measurement approach. We close by describing key findings, management implications, and suggestions for future research.

## 2. Defining information systems strategy

Research in the IS literature on the concept of strategy has involved a variety of definitions and assumptions (Henderson and Venkatraman, 1992). One well-received definition of information systems and technology (IS/IT) strategy follows:

We conceptualize IT strategy in terms of three dimensions: (1) information technology scope — the types and range of IT systems and capabilities (e.g. electronic imaging systems, local and wide-area networks, expert systems, robotics) potentially available to the organization; (2) systemic competencies — those distinctive attributes of IT competencies (e.g. higher system reliability, interconnectivity, flexibility) that contribute positively to the creation of new business strategies or better support existing business strategy; and (3) IT governance — choices of structural mechanisms (e.g. joint ventures, long-term contracts, equity partnerships, joint R & D) to obtain the required IT capabilities. (Henderson and Venkatraman, 1992, p. 100)

In this research, our focus was on the first two dimensions — the types and capabilities of IS/IT, and the IS/IT support provided for business strategy. IS/IT governance issues were outside its scope.

## 3. Measuring information systems strategy

At the outset of the research, we looked to the literature on business strategy to identify alternative general strategy measurement approaches, and found several (Chan et al., 1997; Hambrick, 1980):

\- textual descriptions of strategy (e.g. case studies);

• deductive typologies (i.e. intuitive groupings or classifications of phenomena); and

\- empirical multivariate measurements of strategy, often involving multi-item scales (i.e. comparative measurement approaches) and/or taxonomies (i.e. groupings based on the results of empirical analyses).

Very few researchers employed multi-item scales in order to measure business strategy. These included Venkatraman (1985, 1989), Fredrickson (1984), Fredrickson and Mitchell (1984), Miller (1987a, b, 1988) and Miller et al. (1982). In our review of the business strategy literature, Venkatraman's study was especially noteworthy because of its rigour, high instrument reliabilities, and thorough documentation.

In the information systems literature, we found that although there had been several studies which assessed the adequacy of IS planning (Premkumar and King, 1992, 1994; Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1994), only a few studies had attempted to measure IS strategy and evaluate its adequacy. Most of these had qualitatively assessed IS strategy and its relationship to business strategy, taking the form of case studies and historical analyses, i.e. employing textual descriptions of IS strategy (Clemons and Row, 1988; Copeland and McKenney, 1988; Rochester, 1989; Sager, 1988). There had been very few empirical studies designed to quantitatively assess company IS strategy. ## The Jarvenpaa and Ives Study

Ives and Jarvenpaa (1991) and Jarvenpaa and Ives (1990, 1992, 1994a, b) employed a taxonomy approach to measure global IT strategy. They identified four generic strategies: (1) independent IT operations in firms' subsidiaries; (2) headquarters-driven IT strategies maximizing efficiency and employing a number of common systems; (3) synergy IT strategies conceivably involving several global systems, tailored locally and running under the control of subsidiaries; and (4) integrated IT strategies aiming at worldwide integration of core business processes, while promoting innovation by subsidiaries.

The current study employed both a taxonomy measurement approach and a multivariate, comparative measurement approach.

## The Henderson and Venkatraman Study

In their research stream on IS strategy and alignment, Henderson and Venkatraman (1991, 1992, 1993) investigated four important domains of strategic choice: business strategy, IT strategy, organizational infrastructure and processes, and IT infrastructure and processes. In the current research study, IS strategy and IS infrastructure were not conceptually separated. Instead, realized IS strategy was assessed partly by examining company IS infrastructure. The focus was not on formal IS plans, or stated (i.e. intended) IS strategy, but on observed IS deployments which provide evidence of actual (i.e. realized) strategy. Henderson and Venkatraman employed a comparative measurement approach in their study.

## The Sethi and King Study

Sethi (1988) and Sethi and King (1994) developed an instrument to assess the extent to which an information technology application provides competitive advantage. They defined the higher order construct competitive advantage from an information technology application, CAITA (or later CAPITA — competitive advantage provided by information technology applications), and operationalized eleven CAITA (and five CAPITA) dimensions. Sethi and King employed a comparative measurement approach.

## The Weill Study

Weill (1990, 1992) and Weill and Olson (1989) studied IT investment in manufacturing firms. Weill hypothesized that company strategy and performance goals determined many operational business decisions, including the decision about appropriate levels of investment in IT. He operationalized business strategy using the Porter (1980) cost leadership/differentiation/niche concentration strategy typology, and defined strategic IT investment as IT investment made with the purpose of gaining competitive advantage through increased sales. Weill used a comparative measurement approach.

In contrast with Weill's research, this study developed an IS strategy instrument intended to assess specific uses of (rather than dollar investments in) information technology.

## 4. STROIS — A measurement instrument for realized information systems strategy

Venkatraman (1985, 1989) created measures to assess the “strategic orientation of business enterprises” (STROBE). His conceptualization of strategic orientation represented a broad, holistic perspective of business strategy — one which examined “a broader (than product–market) package of managerial choices made to achieve the desired business objectives" (Venkatraman, 1985). Venkatraman argued that organizations can be viewed as having characteristic orientations shown in their marketplace behaviour. These orientations are stable, i.e. more enduring than individual marketplace initiatives, and are not industry-specific, thus permitting cross-industry comparisons.

Venkatraman proposed that business strategic orientation embodied the principle of realized business strategy. His studies showed that business strategic orientation could be reliably viewed as having the following dimensions:

\- aggressiveness;

\- analysis;

• defensiveness (Internal and External);

\- futurity;

\- innovativeness;

• proactiveness; and

\- riskiness.

The current study is, in part, a response to Venkatraman's call for “systematic extensions” to his findings. The research instrument developed to measure realized IS strategy was based on the STROBE instrument. This IS strategy instrument is named STROIS, as it measures the strategic orientation of the portfolio of information systems in an organization — i.e. existing company deployments or uses of information technology.

The STROIS instrument is designed to determine the ways in which information systems are used by an organization to provide support for business strategy and operations. It provides a snapshot view of the company's IS investments and capabilities at a point in time. In this research focused on realized IS strategy, we do not monitor IS strategy formulation or planning processes, or examine IS strategy or planning documentation. We are not assessing intended strategy. Neither are we evaluating past strategy or trying to predict future strategy. The goal is to determine current IS strategy as implied by the portfolio of information systems actually in existence and by the strategic support these systems provide.

Assessing the strategic orientation of the portfolio of information systems is but one of several approaches researchers can use to assess realized IS strategy. This particular approach appeals to us for a number of reasons. First, STROIS appropriately spotlights what systems have been created, not planned, in the IS area. Second, STROIS examines how systems are used and the business support they actually provide, not how systems are intended to be used. Third, STROIS is ‘generic’. It is not industry or company specific. It has broad applicability. Fourth, STROIS reveals the underlying, prevailing IS priorities and thrusts. It goes beyond a simple list of the systems in an organization. Instead, it emphasizes systems’ business contributions. Respondents think first about their business activities and then identify the systems support, if any, and not the other way around. Fifth, STROIS is based on STROBE, a reliable and valid measure of realized business unit strategy.

In developing the STROIS instrument, we paralleled the STROBE dimensions. For each STROBE questionnaire item, a corresponding item was created to determine the degree to which information systems existed that could be used to facilitate that particular aspect of business strategy. Thus, STROIS was intended to capture the same eight dimensions as STROBE.

## 5. Instrument development

The recommendations given by Churchill (1979) regarding instrument development served as guidelines for the research. Churchill advocates the following process:

1. Specify the domain of the construct.

2. Generate sample items.

3. Collect data.

4. Purify the measures.

5. Collect additional data.

6. Assess instrument reliability and validity.

The domain of the construct was realized IS strategy. Venkatraman's realized business strategy instrument was used as a base from which to generate sample realized IS strategy items. Pilot test data were collected and the measures were purified via the following activities:

\- Instrument Review by Other Researchers. The focus of this review by faculty, Ph.D. students and research associates was two-fold: to examine the substance of the instrument and ensure content validity, and to address presentation issues. Questionnaire items, directions, and layouts were modified after this review.

\- Questionnaire Item Sorting (Moore and Benbasat, 1991). Every item in the questionnaire instrument was printed on a separate card. These cards were shuffled and placed in a single, unsorted pile. Instructions were created to describe STROIS and its hypothesized dimensions. Participants were directed to separate the items into groups, each group to be associated with one hypothesized STROIS dimension. Faculty and Ph.D. candidates completed the sorting exercises. Their misassignments highlighted items to be scrutinized for possible rewording or elimination.

\- Instrument Completion in Pilot Companies. The research instrument was pilot tested in 12 North American companies. Senior executives in each firm were asked to review and complete the questionnaire. A key goal of this pilot testing phase was to check that practitioners understood, and could respond unambiguously to, instructions and questions. The instrument was checked for unreliable items as indicated by missing data or respondent questions, and for item scaling problems as indicated, for example, by end piling where respondents circle all 1's or all 5's on a 1–5 Likert scale.

\- Instrument Review by IS Consultants. As a final refinement procedure, suggestions to improve the content and layout of the questionnaire instrument were obtained from several senior Andersen Consulting personnel.

After the pilot testing activities had been completed, a mail survey of North American firms was conducted to gather additional data in order to assess instrument reliability and validity. The survey is described next.

## 6. Survey design and execution

## 6.1. Survey procedures

The survey was administered in accordance with the principles and procedures advocated by Dillman (1978). It focused on North American firms operating in the financial services (specifically, banking and insurance) and manufacturing (specifically, automotive parts and pharmaceutical preparations) industries. These industries were chosen because they were considered to be information technology intensive. That is, in general, firms in these industries processed high volumes of data and relied heavily on computer-related technologies, and were likely to be more aware of their realized IS strategies.

Dun and Bradstreet Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes were used to prepare a list of North American companies which operated in those industries and which employed 100 or more employees. After this list had been compiled, the following steps were taken:

\- A database of companies meeting SIC code requirements was created. The address and presidential information in this database was verified by telephone. Introductory letters describing the study were mailed to all company presidents in the database. Each president's office was contacted by telephone to ascertain the company's willingness to take part in the study.

\- Questionnaires and cover letters were mailed to all firms interested in participating. The instructions communicated the necessity that the questionnaires be completed by senior executives knowledgeable about the business unit's information systems. Generally this meant that the STROIS instrument was completed by a VP with IS responsibilities $^{1}$ .

\- Five reminder postcards, letters, packages, and phone calls followed the mailing of the questionnaires. These were sent to participating organizations which had not yet returned completed questionnaires. Thank you letters and final reports were later mailed to all companies providing data.

## 7. Survey results

A total of 170 STROIS questionnaires were received, amounting to an overall response rate of approximately 19% (Table 4). Of these, 164 were usable. Power calculations indicated that 80 questionnaires were required for the data analyses. Thus, adequate amounts of data had been gathered. Non-response bias analyses were carried out. The response from the larger pharmaceutical manufacturing firms was shown to be lower than that from other industry groups.

Also, firms returning questionnaires in other industry groups were smaller than average in size indicating that the research findings would be applied most appropriately to small and medium-sized business units. No other non-response bias was detected.

## 8. Analysis of the measurement models

Partial Least Squares analysis (Barclay et al., 1995; Lohmoller, 1982), a latent structural equations modeling technique increasingly utilized in Social Sciences research, was employed in this study to determine construct reliability and validity. Structural equation (i.e. causal) modeling is a way of simultaneously assessing the validity of measures of theoretical constructs and estimating the strength of relationships among these constructs. Unlike traditional, first generation methods such as multiple regression and factor analysis, second generation analytical techniques facilitate the examination of measures within specific theoretical contexts (Barclay et al., 1995).

## 8.1. PLS assessment of measurement models

As is commonly done in PLS analyses, the survey data were split into two equal-sized datasets $^{2}$ . The first dataset was used to finalize the trimming of the hypothesized models, i.e. to complete the purification of the measures. This process involved identifying and removing poorly performing questionnaire items. The second, independent dataset or holdback sample was then used to test the refined measurement models (Barclay et al., 1995, p. 300). No modifications were made while using this second dataset. The purpose of this holdback sample was to avoid testing the refined models with the same data used to refine them. Measurement assessments using the holdback sample are reported below. These assessments involve the examination of reliability, discriminant validity, and convergent validity (Barclay et al., 1995; Campbell and Fiske, 1959).

Reliability. Instrument reliabilities, or internal consistencies, of the STROIS instrument are reported in Table 5. Nunnally (1978) suggests that scale reliabilities of 0.7 and greater are acceptable. The reliabilities associated with STROIS are seen to be strong.

Convergent validity. Convergent validity can be defined as the degree to which different attempts to measure the same construct through maximally different methods are in agreement (Campbell and Fiske, 1959). Fornell et al. (1982) and Fornell and Larcker (1981) suggest that while maximally different methods are an ideal, they are not a requirement for the assessment of convergent validity. They suggest that convergent validity can be assessed by the degree of agreement among items measuring the construct. In particular, convergent validity can be assessed by checking to see that, for each construct (or single dimension), the average variance of the measures shared with the construct, i.e. the average of the squared loadings, is 50% or greater (Fornell et al., 1982). To examine this, average squared loadings were computed. These are also shown in Table 6. With the exception of a single dimension (IS to Support Company Futurity), which had an item which did not load highly, all the dimensions of STROIS had average squared loadings greater than 0.5, demonstrating satisfactory convergent validity.

## 9. Analysis of higher-order measurement models

In the analysis reported above, eight theoretical dimensions for IS strategic orientation were examined. In the analysis below, higher-order factors that group dimensions together are explored.

## 9.1. Higher-order STROIS groupings

In order to examine the underlying factor structure of realized IS strategy, a principal components analysis of all the items in the IS strategy questionnaire was carried out. An examination of the scree plot produced by the analysis indicated that four underlying orthogonal factors were present. These factors were interpreted and labeled as:

\- IS to Support Company Future Orientation $^{3}$ ('Anticipation')

• IS to Support Company Defensive Orientation ('Armour')

• IS to Support Company Analysis and Risk Aversion ('Analysis')

• IS to Support Company Action ('Action')

Table 7 depicts the loadings of key items used to label the four factors which altogether explained 52% of the variance in the data. IS to Support Company Future Orientation, i.e. Anticipation, and IS to Support Company Defensive Orientation, i.e. Armour, were quite similar to the IS to Support Company Futurity and IS to Support Company Defensiveness dimensions discussed earlier. IS to Support Company Analysis was a combined factor which represented IS being used to support risk aversion and provide more general analytic support. It appears that information systems that provide support for risk aversion, e.g. packages to compute expected ROI or payback periods, are also likely to facilitate other detailed analyses, e.g. periodic project reviews. Finally, IS to Support Company Action was a factor comprised of items from IS to Support Company Aggressiveness, Internal Defensiveness, Innovativeness, and Proactiveness. ## 10. Discussion of findings and management implications

## 10.1. The value of examining realized strategy

This research developed an instrument to measure realized or existing IS strategy, as opposed to planned or formalized IS strategy. Such an instrument can distinguish between companies' approaches to IS — their priorities and capabilities. Too often in the literature in the IS field, intended and realized strategies are blurred.

This study suggests that it is important to view systems not just as accounting, personnel, or marketing systems, for example, but in terms of their support for business operations. Some forms of business support may be seen as more critical (e.g. more closely tied to the bottom line) than others. An accounting system may provide IS to support analysis, while a cost control system may provide support for internal defensiveness. Viewing systems in terms of the business needs, activities and postures they support, and evaluating them in this light, is a potentially useful new perspective. This article discussed a measurement instrument that assesses realized IS strategy in these terms. The complete, annotated $^{4}$ research instrument is provided in Appendix. Summary information regarding company IS strategy scores obtained via the use of the instrument is depicted in Table 5. On average, IS in these business units provided limited support for Company Proactiveness, but a high degree of support for Company Analysis.

To further illustrate the use of the instrument, let us examine a hypothetical business unit situation where completed STROIS questionnaires suggest that appropriate scores for IS to Support Company Aggressiveness, Analysis, Internal Defensiveness, External Defensiveness, Futurity, Proactiveness, Risk Aversion, and Innovativeness are 3, 5, 4, 2, 1, 3, 5, and 3 respectively on a scale of 1 to 5 (where 1 is low and 5 is high) $^{5}$ . In this situation, IS support for External Defensiveness (e.g. partnerships with customers, suppliers, distributors, or competitors) and for Futurity (e.g. forecasting) appears to be lacking. IS resources to assist with Analysis and Risk Aversion appear to be excellent. IS resources to support other business activities appear adequate. The CEO and CIO can meet to discuss the business priorities of External Defensiveness and Futurity, and decide whether it is important to address IS deficiencies in these areas. Proposals for new/enhanced systems can be reviewed with an eye to whether they provide these forms of business support, or simply add IS capabilities to areas that are already well served by information systems. Investments in new systems can be reviewed in terms of their fit with the existing portfolio of systems, and current/future business priorities and needs.

## 10.2. Alternative approaches to measuring realized IS strategy

Another key research finding with implications for management practice was that certain kinds of information systems were inter-related. Venkatraman's research showed that business strategy can be modeled using eight dimensions. Our findings suggest that we can also speak in terms of eight distinct types of information systems support that can be provided for the dimensions of business strategic orientation. However, we also can speak in terms of a smaller number of information system types, some of which individually support several aspects of business strategic orientation. We are referring to IS Support for:

\- Action;

\- Analysis;

• Armour (or defensiveness); and

• Anticipation (or future orientation).

A given system in an organization may support multiple aspects of business strategic orientation (e.g. Proactiveness and Innovativeness). However, a system typically falls more clearly into one of the four A categories. A less complex four-dimensional view reduces the precision of the measurement of IS deployment but provides a useful, parsimonious IS strategy taxonomy. It also provides a measurement approach with reduced collinearity and redundancy because the categories are relatively independent.

STROIS, conceptualized with eight dimensions, performs quite satisfactorily but is somewhat complex. A given information system can provide support for one or more of the eight dimensions. Practitioners seeking to identify and evaluate their current realized IS strategy must examine each major system in their IS portfolio and consider the extent to which it supports company aggressiveness, analysis, innovativeness, etc. One system can provide multiple forms of business support.

STROIS, conceptualized using higher-order factors, portrays realized IS strategy more simply. A given information system is likely to provide substantial support for only one dimension. The task facing practitioners who wish to evaluate their IS strategies is made easier and more straightforward. For each system in their IS portfolio they can simply assess whether it provides support primarily for company action, analysis, anticipation or armour. The appropriate category needs to be determined. In order to examine the completeness of the IS portfolio, the manager simply reviews systems in relation to four large categories. For instance, s/he can create a two-dimensional matrix that lists categories on one axis and individual systems on the other axis. The completed matrix provides a summary of current IS priorities. It visually depicts answers to questions such as the following: “which systems support company action? what kind of IS support is a particular system primarily providing? Is there any strategic orientation (action/analysis/ armour/anticipation) that is poorly supported by the existing IS portfolio?” This review can provide new ways of examining the contributions made by IS. It can be used to influence current and future technological investments.

The eight dimensional realized IS strategy measure closely parallels Venkatraman's work. The four-category measure parallels the work by Miles and Snow (1978) more closely — technology to support the business strategies of Miles and Snow's ‘defenders’ can be seen as support for armour, technology to support ‘prospectors’ as support for anticipation, and technology to support ‘analyzers’ as support for analysis. In our discussions with managers about IS strategy, it has become evident that they relate to both of our realized IS strategy measurement models.

It is appropriate to point out a key difference between the business strategy measurement approaches developed by Miles and Snow (1978) and Venkatraman (1989), and between the four-dimensional model and the eight-dimensional model of realized IS strategy. Miles and Snow's assessment of business strategy was in the form of a typology. Venkatraman did not propose a new typology. Instead he introduced a complementary comparative measurement approach. He did not place companies into different categories (e.g. to identify a company as primarily a prospector). Instead, Venkatraman assessed fine differences between business strategies (e.g. “this company is more aggressive and proactive than the other”). In a similar manner, the four-category STROIS model allows a practitioner to readily identify the major use to which an information system is put (e.g. to provide support for analysis). The eight-dimensional model, however, allows a practitioner to make finer distinctions (e.g. “this system provides more support for internal defensiveness than it does for aggressiveness; we need a new system designed to further improve our ability to act aggressively in the marketplace”). Both taxonomy and ‘fine grained’ views of IS strategy have their place and can be very useful. In this research, we are pleased to be able to offer practitioners and researchers both.

## 11. Limitations of the study and suggestions for future research

This study has its limitations. First, the research made use of subjective data on IS strategy provided by a senior executive in each company. Although many researchers prefer objective modes of assessment, studies suggest that perceptual data from senior managers tend to correlate strongly with objective data, and are particularly useful when non-financial data are being examined (Chan and Huff, 1992). Our own pilot testing activities confirmed this. When the reasoning behind particular STROIS scores was explored, respondents generally were quick to describe specific examples of IS support or incidents where IS support was woefully inadequate.

It is our view that ideally STROIS data should be gathered from multiple respondents. A single STROIS respondent, per company, was used in this study in order to encourage a higher response rate. However, in our ongoing work with companies, multiple executives in each firm complete STROIS questionnaires and then meet to discuss and resolve differing perspectives.

Another limitation is that the study focused on the content and nature of realized IS strategy. It did not address process-related issues (see Das et al., 1991 for example). Yet another limitation is that the research findings apply primarily to small and medium-sized private-sector manufacturing and financial services business units only.

There is much additional work to be done in the IS strategy area. STROIS provides two new, potentially valuable approaches for assessing realized IS strategy — a fine grained comparative measurement approach and a simpler taxonomy approach. It is the authors' hope that researchers and practitioners alike will find the STROIS instrument helpful, and will use, test and improve it to further address research and practice in information systems strategy.

## Yolande E. Chan

Yolande E. Chan is an Assistant Professor at Queen's University's School of Business. She received a Ph.D. at the University of Western Ontario, an M.Phil. at Oxford University, and S.M. and S.B. degrees at M.I.T. She is a Rhodes Scholar. Her Ph.D. dissertation was a co-winner of the 1993 ICIS Dissertation Competition. Prior to her Ph.D. studies, she worked with Andersen Consulting for several years. Her research interests include IS strategy, IS alignment, business performance impacts of IS, and the structure of the IS organization. Dr. Chan has published her research in academic and practitioner journals such as the Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Information Systems Research, and Business Quarterly. She has co-authored a number of practitioner reports including, most recently, the Practitioner's Guide to Information Systems Performance Measurement, published by the Society for Information Management, and Developing Performance Measures and Management Controls in the Department of Defense, published by the US National Academy of Public Administration.

## Sid L. Huff

Sid Huff is a Professor at the Richard Ivey School of Business, the University of Western Ontario. His research and teaching address both technical and managerial aspects of information systems, emphasizing the organizational use of IT in a strategic fashion. Prior to joining the Richard Ivey School of Business, Dr Huff worked as a research engineer with Ontario Hydro, and as a management consultant with Woods, Gordon and Company (now Ernst and Young). He has published his research in numerous academic and practitioner journals such as MIS Quarterly, Communications of the ACM, The Journal of MIS, and Business Quarterly. He also consults in the information systems field with various Canadian and international firms. Dr Huff holds an undergraduate degree in mathematics, and graduate degrees in electrical engineering and in management, all from Queen's University in Kingston, Ontario. He holds a Ph.D. in Information Systems from MIT. He was recently selected to hold the Hewlett-Packard Chair in Information Technology Management at UWO.

## Duncan G. Copeland

Dr Copeland received his undergraduate degree in Business Administration from the University of Western Ontario and his doctorate from the Harvard Business School. Prior to his work as an Assistant Professor at the Richard Ivey School of Business, UWO, he worked for a management consulting firm in New York, introducing new technologies to firms in the financial services industry struggling to cope with the bull market of the 1980s. Today, he consults with companies in Canada and the United States in the financial services and transportation industries, as well as federal government agencies. Dr. Copeland has authored articles in leading academic and management journals, and is a co-author of Waves of Change: Business Evolution through Information Technology, recently published by the Harvard Business School Press.
