# EDI in the Scottish Health Service: inter-organisational systems and inter-organisational change

Graham Spinardi $^{a,*}$ , Ian Graham $^{b}$ , Robin Williams $^{a}$

$^{a}$ Research Centre for Social Sciences, Old Surgeon's Hall, High School Yards, University of Edinburgh, Edinburgh, EH1 1LZ UK

$^{b}$ Department of Business Studies, University of Edinburgh, Edinburgh, UK

Received 25 July 1995; received in revised form 1 June 1997; accepted 2 August 1997

Revised paper accepted for publication by Professor R.D. Galliers

## Abstract

This paper describes the introduction of electronic data interchange (EDI) into the Scottish Health Service during a period of organisational flux caused by the introduction of an internal market in health care provision. The Scottish Health Service's central provider of IT services—the Common Services Agency (CSA)—attempted to develop a coherent EDI strategy, which would also have supported its role in the organisational status quo. However, the very weakness of its organisational power base which it sought to bolster in this way meant that the EDI strategy did not proceed rapidly or smoothly, and this then provided the opportunity for supplier companies to advance their own strategic aims through the implementation of proprietary EDI systems. The case study thus raises doubts about the extent to which EDI can be promoted and guided by public administration. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Public services; Inter-organisational systems; Electronic data interchange

## 1. Introduction

Over the last decade the UK government has instigated radical changes in the organisation of public services. At the core of these changes are moves away from hierarchical control of public sector organisations towards the creation of internal markets, in which operating units compete to provide services to customers. In health-care these moves led to the creation of autonomous Hospital Trusts providing services for health boards and general practitioners. One consequence of these structural changes in the health sector is the freedom for Hospital Trusts to develop their own strategies for information technology (IT) use.

In parallel with these radical organisational changes, there had been increasing awareness of the arguments for using IT to restructure processes and improve operational efficiency, as proposed by Hammer in his concept of Business Process Reengineering (BPR, Hammer, 1990). The CCTA (1994), on page 3, argues for the central role of IT-based BPR within the public sector, specifically identifying it as a response to the introduction of market disciplines. One area in which the strategic use of IT is attractive for public sector organisations is in using Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) to improve the efficiency of supplies' ordering by more closely integrating the suppliers with their public sector customers, thus replacing paper based ordering with electronic ordering. This paper describes the implementation of EDI in the Scottish Health Service and considers how it has been affected by the wider organisational changes.

This specific case is analysed in the wider context of the use of EDI in public administration. Are public sector organisations able to implement EDI in ways which incorporate the radical restructuring advocated by the proponents of BPR? Does public administration provide a means for governments to promote EDI?

## 2. Electronic data interchange and inter-organisational change

Typically, EDI involves the exchange of business transactions (such as orders, acknowledgements, invoices, sales figures) through the use of structured messages sent from one organisation's computer system to that of another organisation using a Value-Added Network (VAN) or public telephone line (Emmelhainz, 1993). The benefits which have been credited to EDI include faster transmission of information, and, because of the elimination of re-entering data into computers, both reductions in errors and savings in staff time (Pfeiffer, 1992). More strategic benefits are said to be possible due to the organisational restructuring which EDI may both encourage and facilitate, and from the closer inter-organisational relationships that may result. For example, the introduction of ‘just-in-time’ delivery can be facilitated by the rapid transmission of orders via EDI.

Many EDI implementations simply automate existing paper-based transactions (Graham et al., 1994). However, to gain the maximum benefits from EDI it has been argued that the processes should be redesigned (Galliers et al., 1993; Bjorn-Anderson and Krcmar, 1995, p. 323). Discussion of the strategic use of IT has increasingly been subsumed within the discourses on BPR, in which the potential of EDI to improve interorganisational transactions has featured strongly. For example, in describing the General Motors Saturn plant, Hammer and Champy (1993), on page 91, claim that EDI “enabled Saturn and its suppliers to operate as one company”. Davenport (1993), on page 60, stresses the importance of combining EDI with the redesign of the processes which generate transactions, citing Gillette as a firm where the use of EDI without a redesign of these processes did not lead to the expected efficiency improvements. Venkatraman (1991) argues for a stage of IT use beyond BPR within the boundaries of the firm, defining the concept of Business Network Redesign (BNR) in which IT is used to radically redesign processes across networks of firms.

However, in practice BPR and EDI do not usually go hand-in-hand. Studies of communities of firms trading electronically show progressive incremental changes, starting with the automation of existing relationships. Although Bjorn-Anderson and Krcmar (1995), on page 322, identify BPR as a critical success factor in EDI implementation, they also note that all the firms in their study followed an evolutionary approach. Swatman et al. (1994) propose a five-stage model of EDI integration, progressing from stand-alone systems, through non-integrated software, EDI functionality integrated into other systems to the final stage in which EDI is a major factor in strategic and information systems planning. While explicitly relating EDI to BPR, this again implies an incremental rather than radical model of EDI implementation. In a study of the formation of electronic trading communities in the publishing sector, Spinardi et al. (1996) identified that the need to maintain consensus within a network of firms created a barrier to the agreement of using EDI to restructure an industry. It is not a coincidence that the cases cited of using EDI for BPR/BNR are usually in communities where one firm is strong enough to impose the changes without the need to agree it across a network, as, for example, in the Australian steel industry (Swatman and Swatman, 1993).

Such conditions generally exist in the public sector, where the state does not have to negotiate the form of electronic commerce with competitors, but can impose it by direction. Therefore, relative to commercial organisations, public sector organisations may be more able to develop and implement a strategic vision for EDI usage. While public administrations lack the incentive of using EDI to differentiate their services from competitors, they are under pressure to deliver services with less resources, and so the focus in EDI implementation will be more on gaining improvements in operational efficiency.

The Commission of the European Communities (CEC) has seen EDI as a means of improving the performance of public administration across Europe. The CEC supported projects to develop electronic commerce in public administration in the TEDIS programme (Klein, 1995), which has been followed by the Interchange of Data between Administrations (IDA) programme explicitly aimed at electronic exchange in the public sector. These initiatives are based on the assumption that it is possible to develop a strategy for IT usage in the public sector.

However, in the UK such a centralised approach has been eroded by the government commitment to the fostering of market disciplines in the public sector. This ideological shift has been mirrored in other liberal democracies, notably in the USA, where writers such as Osborne and Gaebbler (1992) have popularised the slogan that government should “steer and not row”, where “steering” is restricted to the setting of priorities and monitoring of outcomes.

One potential drawback of these changes is that they undermine the ability to plan a national IT strategy. This paper describes the introduction of EDI into the Scottish Health Service, a sector which has undergone radical structural changes, and considers the implications for the viability of strategic IT planning in the public sector. This case study draws on a series of tape-recorded semi-structured interviews with a range of actors directly involved in the implementation of the EDI pilots, including staff of the Common Services Agency, of the Health Boards, and of supplier companies.

## 3. Background: Health Service reforms, the Common Services Agency, and Health Service EDI

The Health Service reforms stemmed from a review of the NHS which was announced by the government in January 1987 (Holliday, 1992). The White Paper arising from this review (Department of Health, 1989) was heavily influenced by contributions from radical theorists from the Centre for Policy Studies (Goldsmith and Willetts, 1988) and the Adam Smith Institute (Pirie and Butler, 1988). These argued for the adoption in the UK of an internal market in health-care, along the lines first proposed in the US by Enthoven (1980, 1985), in which “health maintenance organisations” would purchase health-care services from hospitals and other providers on behalf of patients. The proposals in the White Paper were enacted in the NHS and Community Care Act, 1990, (DHSS, 1990), which created the purchaser-provider split between Health Boards (District Health Authorities in England), GP fundholders and private patients on the one hand, and Direct Managed Units (hospitals still under Health Board Control), Hospital Trusts and private sector units on the other. Discussion of these reforms has mainly focused on their most radical elements, the creation of fund-holding GPs and Hospital Trusts. However, the creation of the purchaser/provider split has also had profound consequences for central support services, which must increasingly persuade providers of health care to purchase their services in the face of competition from commercial firms.

The Common Services Agency (CSA) provides national services for the Scottish Health Service, which include the ambulance service, blood transfusion service, building and legal advice, health statistics, information and computer services, and some aspects of supplies purchasing. The work of the Supplies Division of the CSA is divided into three branches. Two of these deal with the procurement of major items of medical equipment and with the equipping of new hospitals, but the bulk of the Division's work by value is that of the Contracts Branch. This negotiates contracts for the procurement of supplies (food, energy, drugs, medical sundries, etc.), amounting to an annual value of over £90m.

The central contracts negotiated by the CSA make use of the overall ‘purchasing power’ of the Scottish Health Service to get better value. Prior to reorganisation, the supplies would be ordered and delivered directly between the suppliers and the fifteen Health Boards. The Supplies Division also provided information services which included a Scottish Health Service communication system known as GENSIS (General Supplies Information System), using an X25 network to link the Health Boards and the CSA.

The CSA's interest in EDI developed against a background with two main features. One was the various developments using EDI in health care that were already taking place, particularly in England and the USA. The other was the major changes being instigated in the organisation of the health service in the UK.

The English Procurement Directorate issued a report in April 1989, which included the goal of making “80% of trading electronic within 3 years” (NHS Procurement Directorate, 1989). A project called Trading for the Future (TF2) was established in October 1989 to coordinate and promote the use of EDI in the English Health Service. TF2 involved 3 hospitals and 12 major health-care suppliers, working with IBM’s VAN service. Another separate initiative was carried out at about the same time by the Regional Supply Centre of East Anglia Health Authority, working with the INS Tradanet VAN.

Since the initiative to introduce EDI into Health Service supply started later in Scotland, the CSA was able to draw on this English experience. Indeed, Terry Dunmore, Director of the CSA's Supplies Division, had direct experience as a participant in the English EDI initiatives. The CSA was also able to see what effects the Health Service reforms were having, and to develop its EDI strategy accordingly.

Interest in introducing EDI into the Scottish Health Service led the CSA to set up several pilots to investigate the feasibility of electronic ordering of both general supplies and pharmaceuticals. These pilots were particularly concerned with what might be called the ‘technical’ issues of getting EDI to work. The pilots were set, however, within the context of major structural changes within the Health Service, a context in which the role of EDI could be seen as ‘political’. Getting EDI to work thus meant two things to the CSA. At a technical level it meant getting orders sent electronically from the Health Boards to suppliers. At a political level it meant carving out a role for the CSA which would sustain it through the Health Service changes, and particularly the shift to trust status for hospitals. A third aspect of EDI—the question of economic viability—was likely eventually to become critical to the ‘selling’ of the CSA’s vision of EDI, but for the time being was assumed to have a positive answer.

## 4. Demonstrating technical feasibility

In mid-1991 the Supplies Division set up a project board to examine EDI. This also included representatives from other areas of the Scottish Health Service: Finance, General Supplies, Internal Audit, and Communications. It was decided to run six-month pilots using five sites in three Health Boards. The three Health Boards chosen were Ayrshire and Arran, Lothian, and Fife. The first would run a pilot for general supplies only, while the other two would conduct pilots for both General Supplies and Pharmacy.

It was decided not to follow the route taken by the English Health Service which chose a single VAN and bought its networking services. Instead it was decided to use the pilots as a test-bed for the VAN suppliers, and to trial each of the three—IBM, INS and AT&T—in one of the Health Boards. IBM was assigned to Ayrshire and Arran, INS to Lothian, and AT&T to Fife. Communications within the Scottish health system would use the X25 national network, which was already in place, but underused. This had originally been installed with an eye to future possibilities; one particular function envisaged was to allow access to Health Service statistics (traditionally circulated in “the dreaded Blue Book”) so that analysts and managers could manipulate it on-screen.

By keeping the VANs in competition it was intended not only to see which provided the best services for their own network, but also to exert pressure for the VANs to provide interconnection between their networks. There was a particular desire by the Director of CSA Supplies Division that such interconnection would mean that the Scottish Health Service purchasing via EDI would not become dependent on any single VAN, but could maintain a competitive market. Also, because of the very wide range of suppliers that the NHS deals with, reliance on a single VAN was seen as potentially limiting (in contrast, say, to EDI in a single sector, such as banking or automotive, where one VAN might link the whole community).

Although VAN interconnection is now a reality, the CSA had little success in exchanging data between VANs: “what we’ve done is bought their hardware, bought their software, and said, ‘please require your software to talk to your own system and anyone else’s we nominate.’ And that...has not worked”. The CSA was also very wary of suppliers keen to push proprietary EDI as a means of ‘locking-in’ customers. Instead, it was intended to pursue standard EDI technology and to avoid propriety approaches.

Purchase orders were to be generated by the Integrated Clinical Supplies Information System (ICSIS). ICSIS was developed in the late 1980s by the CSA to provide a complete stock control and purchasing system for Scottish Health Boards. In theory, individual Boards could have rejected ICSIS, but because it was funded by the Scottish Office it was provided free to them, whereas an alternative system would have had to have been paid for. One ICSIS system was run by each Health Board, and one by the CSA (which deals with purchasing different kinds of items, for equipping hospitals, etc.). Many of the Boards had only recently started using ICSIS; for example, Fife Health Board went ‘live’ with it in April 1991.

Although not developed with EDI in mind, the output of ICSIS could be down-loaded through a file transfer package onto a PC, and sent out through the X25 network onto the VAN, from which the supplier would pick them up at its mailbox. The decision was made that international EDIFACT standards were to be used, despite the fact that most health service suppliers, particularly the pharmaceutical companies, were using the UK retail sector TRADACOMS standards. The CSA's preferred product identification system was the National Supply Vocabulary (NSV) which is maintained by the National Supplies Authority in England.

An EDI manager was appointed at the start of November 1991 and the pilots were scheduled to run till the end of March 1992. Actually getting everything in place took till the end of February, at which point the transmission of orders could start. The initial pilot period was extended to run until the end of April. About ten major suppliers who were known to be keen on EDI were chosen for the pilots. Since only a small amount of transmission had occurred by the end of April, it was decided to continue the pilots for another 6 months, up until the end of November 1992. At this stage IBM was dropped because “they weren’t meeting our requirements for what was needed”. AT&T Istel were selected as the replacement VAN for Ayrshire and Arran.

It was recognised at the start of the pilots that because the CSA has no direct control over Health Boards the pace of progress could not be forced: “people in the Health Boards have got other priorities, they haven’t been able to put the input into it that I would have liked”. Although the pilots were funded centrally by the CSA, this did not cover local staff costs. Also, “some Health Boards have been more pro-active, shall we say, than other Health Boards, but that is purely down to personnel within the Health Boards”. A more detailed account of the pilots carried out in Fife highlights the problems that can hinder the implementation of EDI.

## 5. The Fife pilots

Two pilots were run independently in the Fife Health Board: one for general supplies and one for pharmaceutical supplies. Three main suppliers were chosen for the general supplies pilot: Johnson and Johnson, HMSO, and HM & S (Hospital Management and Supplies, part of Baxter). Of these, the largest number of items are supplied by HM and S, which is a distributor. After several months sending test transmissions, Fife went ‘live’, sending orders for general supplies at the start of July 1992.

The two central prerequisites for EDI are message standardisation and data harmonisation. The first of these—message standardisation—was not an issue because the decision to use EDIFACT had been made by the CSA. Since the suppliers chosen for the pilots were large organisations it was not a major problem for them to accept orders in EDIFACT format, even though most would be using other message formats (i.e. TRADACOMS) for their other EDI. Data harmonisation, on the other hand, was to prove more troublesome.

There was a requirement for unique codes to identify items, which could be recognised by the computer systems of both the Health Board and its suppliers. It had been the CSA's intention that the pilots should use the NSV numbers supplied by the CSA. However, there was difficulty getting up-to-date NSV information in time. General supplies decided instead “that we would code our items with our own local code numbers”. Nevertheless, general supplies' EDI file output from ICSIS did have a field which could contain the NSV number if one was available, which was the case for about 60% of items. In practice, the main identifiers used for EDI were either European Article Number (EAN) codes, if the suppliers could provide them for all their products, or, if not, the supplier's own product code numbers.

Pharmacy were even less keen on using the NSV codes for their pilot. Although NSV codes are used by the CSA in its contracts (for some, but not all items), Pharmacy did not want to use them. Their objections to the NSV codes were due not only to the feeling that they were not kept up-to-date enough, but also to their inability to distinguish between a single item and a pack: “The pack size is not relevant to NSV, whereas it is to the EAN. That uniquely identifies a product and a pack at the same time”.

This was an issue which caused problems for both the General Supplies and the Pharmacy pilots. Whether an order was for a single item or for the normal packaged quantity of that item was frequently a cause of uncertainty. For example, pens are supplied by HMSO in boxes of 50, which according to ICSIS would be one purchase unit. Thus orders generated by the general supplies' ICSIS would be for numbers of boxes—for example, two. However, if sent by EDI this would be read by HMSO's system as an order for two pens, and rejected because pens only come in orders of fifty. Previously, manual intervention at HMSO had provided the necessary interpretation which with EDI no longer happened. This could be solved by adding a conversion routine to ICSIS, but the view was taken that this is the supplier's problem and they should deal with it:

I was just being bloody minded because I was turning round and saying I was not going to get anyone to develop any conversion routines. Why should we? I could end up doing that for everyone...the thing is that we have got a system which we are trying to develop on a national basis...no-one in their right minds is going to develop a system which has to have a whole lot of sub-routines written in, depending on who it is you decide to trade with afterwards. Now HMSO knows the problem.... They were saying that their catalogue was so vast it was going to be such a problem looking at all these items. Well I mean, that doesn't wash I'm afraid. If they want to trade with us, it is up to them. What would Marks and Sparks do for example? ... They would say, if you want to deal with us, you deal on our terms.

While General Supplies were able to develop a usable system of coding during their pilot, pharmacy were faced with a much greater problem. Pharmacy differs from general supplies in that it is simpler as regards numbers of order point/delivery sites (only two in Fife, at Kirkcaldy and Dunfermline), but more complex regarding the numbers of products. The pharmacy stock file contains about 4500 items, compared to under 1000 for general supplies.

The suppliers chosen by CSA for the pharmacy pilot included the two main ones, AAH and Baxter. AAH are the wholesaler which provides most of Fife's pharmacy requirements. At the time of the pilot they supplied about 65–70% of Fife's supplies, but it was intended to increase this to 90–95%. An immediate problem in getting the pilot going with AAH was that they used the INS VAN and their own proprietary EDI solution, Mediate; and they were not interested in getting a mailbox on the AT&T VAN. An initial solution was that AT&T installed a microcomputer in AAH's Glasgow branch (to which Fife mails its orders) which could be used to pull Fife's EDI messages from a mailbox on the AT&T network. These orders could then be printed out. This was made to work, but AAH's system in Glasgow could not deal with the EAN coding, and so AAH were simply reading the printed orders as “descriptions and narratives”.

As with general supplies, ‘factoring’ was a problem because many pharmacy supplies come in packs or boxes of a certain number. For example, the infusion fluids supplied by Baxter come in boxes of 15, 20, or 24 bags. However, the product code used by Baxter referred to a single bag and did not match ICSIS orders, which would refer to the box.

The difficulties in achieving any meaningful transmission of orders meant that the internal Fife Pharmacy view of the pilot was quite negative. Fife's Chief Administrative Pharmaceutical Officer (CAPO) was reportedly dissatisfied with the progress of the pilot: "he's more or less said that we seem to be putting a lot of man-hours into the project, and we're not getting a lot out at the moment, and he's not too keen on putting a lot more effort in if we're not getting benefit". Although the General Supplies pilot was considered successful—in that orders sent by the Health Board were received by the suppliers—the Pharmacy experience led to a pragmatic decision to accept the offer of AAH's own solution. All the coding/factoring problems would then be handled by AAH's software. However, relying on AAH's product identifier codes could have made it difficult to buy from other suppliers. Adopting proprietary solutions ran completely counter to the approach that the CSA favoured, of developing an open and competitive electronic ordering system to avoid the danger of becoming dependent on a single core supplier. However, in line with what appears to be a general trend, AAH had come to see the advantages of allowing users to use its software to send electronic orders to other suppliers as greater than the competitive advantages of tying hospitals to them. The system was therefore adapted to allow hospitals to use the Mediate package to place orders to all their suppliers capable of receiving orders electronically. During 1993 Fife Pharmacy carried out a second pilot with the Mediate system which proved much more satisfactory than the initial CSA pilot.

## 6. Too little, too late

When the pilots were completed, they could be seen to have demonstrated the technical feasibility of a nationally coordinated EDI solution. This was what the CSA favoured, not least because it would consolidate its role as a central supplier of information services to the Scottish Health Service in the face of the splintering effect of the reforms. In practice, however, the CSA may have missed the window of opportunity for such an EDI solution, as the pace of the reforms made the central EDI vision redundant—despite the fact that steps were taken to protect the CSA’s role. These included a decree in mid-1991 from the Chief Executive of the Scottish Health Service that all central contracts drawn up by the CSA would be mandatory and should be used by Boards.

With the Health Service reforms, the freedom of Health Boards has grown, as has the autonomy of Hospital Trusts. In principle, the Chief Executive of the Scottish Health Service can mandate Trusts to follow particular practices, but this power is rarely used to constrain Trusts in developing their own IT strategies. Although the pilots had demonstrated the feasibility of the CSA's EDI solution, Health Boards and hospitals had to be persuaded that it represented a worthwhile use of resources.

The Hospital Trusts, released from Health Board control, needed IT systems for financial management. In specifying these systems, EDI facilities were often low on the list of functional requirements; the Trusts sought standard cost-effective packages which would meet their immediate needs. This led to the implementation of diverse financial systems. In Lothian the four largest Trusts, previously operating the same systems under the aegis of the Health Board, implemented three different systems with different levels of EDI capability. The trusts also followed the pattern set in the CSA's pilots in differentiating between the needs of Pharmacy and General Supplies. Fife hospital Trusts had set up a collaborative area purchasing unit and were continuing to develop EDI links based on the CSA pilot with twelve suppliers for General Supplies. In Lothian, however, trust pharmacies withdrew from the project and switched to using the AAH Mediate system, arguing that its tailoring to pharmacy overcame information harmonisation and standardisation issues, with the additional advantage of providing information on available stock levels.

The CSA's vision of a national EDI policy was thus challenged by the emergence of commercial solutions which met the immediate needs of hospitals. Crucially for the CSA, the adoption of a proprietary EDI solution meant the displacement of the Supplies Division by a commercial intermediary.

## 7. The future of EDI (and CSA) in the Scottish Health Service

By the end of the pilots there was some consensus that EDI could be made to work, but that it was not straightforward. Most aspects of the pilots had encountered difficulties, except the National Scottish Health Service network which “worked splendidly”. EDI is widely viewed as being ‘technically’ easy, but actual implementation generally takes longer than expected due to issues which are as much ‘social’ as ‘technical’. Standardisation of message formats is one such issue. In this case it was not a major consideration because the existing EDIFACT messages developed for the English EDI trials were selected by the CSA as the basis for their trials, and the participating suppliers were large organisations with the resources to maintain more than one standard if necessary. Data harmonisation proved a far more troublesome problem. The initial choice of the NSV for product coding did not prove successful, as these codes were neither very suitable for inter-organisational trading (they had been developed for internal health service use), nor well maintained. For pharmaceutical supplies—where products not only often come in multiples, with the problem of factoring, but also can, in the case of many drugs, exist in a variety of different guises—the coding problem was only resolved by simply using the codes defined in AAH's Mediate system.

Both these ‘technical’ aspects of EDI (message standardisation and data harmonisation) hinge on the wider issue of inter-organisational relations. It appears that not all of the relevant organisations (suppliers and VANs) gave their full commitment to the pilots. This seems surprising, given the importance of the Scottish Health Service as a customer. However, the impetus for EDI came from the CSA, not from the Health Boards. Although initiated and funded by the CSA, the staff time and effort for implementation lay with the Health Boards. The CSA could encourage, but not enforce, these efforts. No doubt this created an impression of a lack of urgency which conveyed itself to both the suppliers and to the VANs. From the outset there were thus doubts about whether the pilots would result in a major shift to EDI by the Scottish Health Service (rightly as it has so far transpired).

Several conclusions could thus be drawn from the pilots. Consideration of the technical issues suggested that EDI could be used for Health Service supplies ordering. However, implementation requires a major commitment by all partners. Significant efficiency improvements remained more an assumption rather than something demonstrable in any cost-benefit analysis. In any case, the over-riding question facing the introduction of EDI into the Scottish Health Service centred not on the narrow ‘technical’ issue of whether it would work, but on wider ‘political’ concerns.

Specifically, the health service reforms meant the creation of the ‘internal market’ within which Hospital Trusts would operate as independent financial centres. These reforms impinged on the CSA’s EDI plans in two ways. First, from a practical standpoint, they meant delay in moving forward. Until all the Trusts were fully established with management in place there was uncertainty as who would be the CSA’s customers. During 1993 the CSA could therefore do little to push forward their vision of EDI. Secondly, there was the way that the reforms affected how the CSA conceived this vision. The strategic view of the future role that EDI could play in the Scottish Health Service was tied in with maintaining the role of the CSA as a central service supplier.

The baseline assumption of the CSA was that EDI would be introduced into the Scottish Health Service. The experience of the English Health Service seemed to confirm such a view, whilst also suggesting that EDI implementation might be slower and more patchy than some predictions. The major choice as regards EDI thus seemed to hinge on two possible alternate scenarios. In one, EDI could develop haphazardly as individual parts of the Health Service (formerly Health Boards, but now Trusts) implemented their own preferred EDI approaches. In the other, EDI could be supplied as a central function developed under the coordination of the CSA.

This latter approach would build on the existing role of the CSA Supplies Division.

Individual Boards were sent details of CSA-negotiated central contracts, allowing purchase orders to be made simply by 'call-off' of the specified items by reference to the National Service contract number. The CSA is now developing an electronic catalogue of all supply information which could be provided electronically to Health Service customers. This catalogue could then provide the basis for ordering supplies through a centrally coordinated EDI hub developed by the CSA. Initial consideration of physically creating such a hub within the CSA has been superseded by plans to develop it by using an EDI service provider, such as INS.

The CSA argued that this central EDI approach would mean that the hard-won expertise gained in the pilots could be applied throughout the Scottish Health Service rather than everyone trying to ‘reinvent the wheel’ through local solutions. This would avoid the necessity for each Health Board and Trust to have the overhead of EDI expertise: “All they do is, they raise their purchase orders as normal, press the button, it’s away, it’s gone and they don’t know any more about it”. Moreover, just as the CSA can use the purchasing power of the Scottish Health Service as a whole to negotiate better contracts for supplies, so too could it negotiate better deals for EDI services.

This vision of a central EDI service maintained by CSA would then, of course, consolidate the role of the CSA. However, the question remained as to whether CSA could 'sell' its vision of a central EDI service, a vision which stands in contrast to other developments in hospital supply EDI, particularly in the USA. There, EDI developments in this area have evolved in the form of proprietary systems such as Baxter's ASAP, in which a 'prime vendor' provides one-stop purchasing through its own ordering system (Short and Venkatraman, 1992). Recent developments, such as Baxter's Valuelink programme, expand the role of the system supplier as a 'service provider' still further, supplanting many of the functions previously carried out by the hospital. Long-term contracts underpin 'strategic partnerships' in which Baxter takes over many of a hospital's purchasing, stock control and distribution functions.

These relationships constitute a transfer of procurement activities from the public sector to the private sector. The CSA's EDI programme can be seen as a strategic response to this threat. In adopting EDI, the CSA were aware that they had to sell the solution to users. It was therefore critical that the CSA addressed the needs of users in the pilots and demonstrated not only EDI's technical feasibility but also its economic value. In doing this the CSA came increasingly into competition with commercial firms offering their own solutions.

In the context of the new internal market such ‘out-sourcing’ of supply functions may strike a deeper resonance with the new Hospital Trusts than the centralised EDI hub approach favoured by the CSA. Although the EDI pilots were funded by the CSA, full EDI implementation is unlikely to be paid for centrally. Whether or not the CSA can convince the 60 or so Trusts that they can provide a competitive EDI solution centrally will thus be critical. The shift to Trusts has led to a more fragmented approach to Health Service purchasing, hindering rather than aiding the uptake of EDI. Within such a context, health care suppliers which can offer not just a ‘turn key’ EDI solution, but also outsourcing of supply management functions, may instead be preferred by those trusts seeking early EDI implementation.

## 8. Conclusion

The development of EDI in the Scottish Health Service highlights the importance of political and organisational factors in the development of IT. The apparently unambitious policy of using EDI to automate existing order transactions became embroiled in the free-for-all created by government Health Service reforms. On the one hand, the CSA, in its role of central service provider to the Scottish Health Service, was following a strategy of developing an EDI solution independent of vendors and service suppliers, and in so doing, seeking to consolidate its organisational existence. On the other hand, healthcare and pharmaceutical suppliers were able to offer their own EDI solutions which minimised implementation difficulties.

In this context, the CSA was unable to impose a coherent strategic EDI policy throughout the Scottish Health Service. This apparent failure (as we write) reflects both the CSA's limited hierarchical power and (possibly) presages its demise—at least as a central supplier of IT services. At the same time, the development of proprietary EDI systems by healthcare and pharmaceutical suppliers appears to be a successful strategic move on their part. Although these systems have become nominally more open (allowing customers to order from other suppliers) they nevertheless still involve some degree of ‘lock-in’ through the ease with which transactions are handled with the favoured supplier.

The CSA's EDI project was shaped by the conflicting policies of the government, the CSA, the Hospital Trusts, the suppliers and the private system developers. The raison d'etre of the CSA had been to plan and implement centralised national strategies for the Health Service in Scotland, but this role was undermined by the shift in government ideology. The product suppliers adopted strategic approaches to EDI, responding to the Trusts' autonomy by offering EDI as a means of providing hospitals with wider supply chain management services, while the software suppliers saw EDI as another function in the integrated systems they were offering to hospitals. The CSA's long-term vision of an integrated national EDI system appears to be losing the competition with the shorter-term and complementary visions of the suppliers and the software companies.

This case demonstrates a fundamental conflict between the UK government policy of introducing market forces into the public sector and the feasibility of a national policy for IT usage in public administration. This problem also affects the CEC's model of supporting pan-European solutions for introducing EDI in public authorities. Such efforts have been based on the belief that these authorities would be able to impose standardisation and harmonisation of EDI practices in public administration in their respective states. In practice, however, moves towards privatisation and market solutions in public sector services look likely to make the imposition of coherent IT strategies increasingly difficult.
