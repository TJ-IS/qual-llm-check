# Information systems management issues in Central America: a multinational and comparative study

Francisco J. Mata $^{a,*}$ , William L. Fuerst $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Earth Council, Apartado 2323–1002, San Jose, Costa Rica $^{b}$ Department of Business Analysis and Research, College of Business Administration and Graduate School of Business, Texas A&M University, College Station, TX, USA

Revised paper accepted for publication 4 June 1997 by Professor Jon Turner

## Abstract

As businesses move to increased levels of globalization, information systems (IS) management issues in the global economy become increasingly significant. This study identified and evaluated the key IS management issues in two Central American countries—Costa Rica and Guatemala—and compared those issues to two other countries—Australia and the US. Good agreement was found on the importance of the issues in the two Central American countries, while the comparison of the key issues with Australia and the US showed differences between the developing and developed countries. Some issues were found to be important in all the countries compared. Based on the findings, key IS issues are classified as developed-country, developing-country and universal. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Costa Rica; Guatemala; Information systems management; International IS management issues; Strategic IS issues

## 1. Introduction

Globalization is having a dramatic impact on the way organizations conduct business, forcing companies to become more aware of cross-cultural environments. This awareness is needed for all business functions, including the information systems (IS) function, and can be accomplished in part through internationally-oriented research. Although a considerable number of studies aimed at identifying IS management issues have been carried out in the US, few similar studies have been conducted internationally. IS management issues in other countries are likely to vary from those in the US due to factors such as availability of information technology (IT) personnel, national policies, level of IT sophistication, vendor support and technology awareness (Cash et al., 1992). Therefore, a study of IS international issues cannot be considered as “a replication of American research in a different country” (Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991, p. 35). Research on IS management issues in other countries is needed to determine differences and similarities in IS management between countries. Furthermore, to support a more global consideration of IS issues, Palvia et al. (1992) argue that “in today’s global society, the practice and research of MIS must be extended to include the broadest range of nations” (p. 2).

Knowledge about international IS management issues is important for multinational companies, IS researchers and local IS practitioners. Multinational companies may face different problems regarding IS management because of specific conditions in each country in which they operate (Selig, 1982a; Selig, 1982b; Deans et al., 1991; Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991; Cash et al., 1992; Deans and Kane, 1992). In this regard, Watson and Brancheau (1991) claim that “knowledge of regional similarities and differences can assist [IS] executives in effectively managing their increasingly global function” (p. 214).

Cash et al. (1992) argue that international IS management has been a “largely unreported, unstudied IT story” (p. 547). However, recently there has been a growing concern by IS researchers about this subject (see Deans et al., 1991; Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991; Watson and Brancheau, 1991; Deans and Kane, 1992). In international IT research, a major consideration is the differentiation between international and local IS management issues. International IS management issues deal with concerns that may be universal to most countries, whereas local IS issues reflect concerns of a particular country (Deans and Kane, 1992).

Although research on IS management issues has been conducted in Australia (Watson, 1989), Europe (Davenport and Buday, 1988), Singapore (Rao et al., 1987), Taiwan (Harrison and Farn, 1990) and the UK (Hirschheim et al., 1988), no related study has been conducted in Latin American. Latin America is becoming economically and politically important in the global economy (Drucker, 1990), and currently offers very good opportunities for multinational corporations and international investors. Most countries in Latin America have initiated reforms in their economic and political structures, making them very attractive for international business (Martinez et al., 1992). The importance of Latin America has increased with the creation of an American free trade zone. IS will play a major role in this economic integration, and thus IS management in Latin American is an important topic not only for IS specialists, but also for the international business community.

Consequently, the purpose of this work is: (1) to determine the IS management issues in two Latin American countries—Costa Rica and Guatemala; and (2) to analyze the similarities and differences in importance of these issues between the countries studied and with regard to other developed countries in which similar studies have been conducted. Costa Rica and Guatemala were selected because of their economic and political growth in Central America, and because they are the leading users of IT in Central America, reflecting the IS trends observed in the region. Specifically, the following research questions are addressed in this study:

1. What are the critical $^{1}$ or key IS management issues facing IS managers in Costa Rica and Guatemala?

2. Are there differences in the importance of the issues between these countries?

3. How do the key IS management issues in these countries compare with those previously reported in other developed countries?

By answering these questions, several benefits are provided. Researchers of international IS management will be provided information regarding IS management issues in developing countries. IS researchers in the countries studied will benefit by being able to understand better the critical problems facing IS managers, allowing them to focus their work to solve such problems. IS executives in the Central American countries and in multinational corporations will gain knowledge about IS management issues which may affect the transfer of IS technology and IS management skills at the international level.

This paper is divided into eight sections. Section 2 reviews previous studies on IS management issues. Section 3 discusses the state of IT in Costa Rica and Guatemala and compares it with IT development in other countries. The research hypotheses motivating this research are presented in Section 4, and Section 5 discusses the methodology used to collect the data. The results of the survey conducted in the two Central American countries are presented in Section 6. Section 7 provides: (1) a comparison between the issues found in Costa Rica and Guatemala; and (2) a comparison of the issues found in these two Central American countries with those found in two developed countries, the US and Australia. Finally, Section 8 presents the conclusions and implications of this work.

## 2. Previous studies on IS management issues

IS management studies have been conducted in the US by Ball and Harris (1982), Brancheau and Wetherbe (1987), Caudle et al. (1991), Dickson et al. (1984), Hartog and Herbert (1986), Herbert and Hartog (1986), Martin (1982) and Niederman et al. (1991). The first studies aimed at identifying IS management issues were conducted by Ball and Harris (1982) and Martin (1982). In 1980, Ball and Harris conducted a membership survey of the Society for Information Management (SIM), asking participants to rate 18 issues in the IS profession. Martin, on the other hand, conducted a survey of 15 chief information officers using two successive questionnaires to determine IS critical success factors.

The surveys of Dickson et al. (1984), Brancheau and Wetherbe (1987) and Niederman et al. (1991) can be considered a series of longitudinal studies. These studies, referred to in this paper as the SIM–MISRC studies, sampled members of the Society for Information Management (SIM), were conducted by researchers affiliated with the MIS Research Center (MISRC) at the University of Minnesota, and used a Delphi methodology to identify the key IS management issues. An important conclusion drawn from these longitudinal studies is that the importance of IS management issues varies with time.

Caudle et al. (1991) conducted a study of the key IS management issues in the public sector using follow-up discussions with selected respondents to clarify the issues. Hartog and Herbert conducted two surveys about IS management issues, the first study (Hartog and Herbert, 1986) from 107 companies in the St Louis, MO, area, and the second study (Herbert and Hartog, 1986) from managers in Fortune 1000 companies.

In contrast to the number of IS management studies carried out in the US, few similar studies have been conducted in other countries. IS management issues have been studied in Australia (Watson, 1989), Europe (Davenport and Buday, 1988), Singapore (Rao et al., 1987), Taiwan (Harrison and Farn, 1990) and the UK (Hirschheim et al., 1988). Deans et al. (1991) studied international IS management problems from the perspective of USA multinational corporations. Most of the previous studies have been conducted in developed countries with the exception of those conducted in Singapore and Taiwan, which can be considered developing countries. Also important for our study is the work by Watson and Brancheau (1991), who compared the results of the studies conducted in Australia (Watson, 1989), Europe (Davenport and Buday, 1988), Singapore (Rao et al., 1987), and the US (Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1987).

Since the importance of IS management issues does not only depend on time, but also on the location of the study, the research presented in this paper is valuable to advance the knowledge of similarities and differences among countries.

## 3. IT development in Costa Rica and Guatemala

The state of IT development in a country is dependent on its economic conditions (see Deans and Kane, 1992). Therefore, in order to understand the current state and role of IT in Costa Rica and Guatemala, their economic development needs to be examined, as presented in the next subsection. Section 3.2 discusses the differences in IT development between these two Central American countries and other developed countries based on the stage model established by Palvia et al. (1992).

## 3.1. Economic influence

Central America is composed of small countries which are, thereby, challenged to compete in the world economy (see Lindenberg and Ramirez (1991)). From the 1960s to the 1980s, these countries pursued an inwards development strategy aimed to protecting the domestic market mainly through a program to substitute imports with indigenous products. This is the opposite of an outwards development strategy, where the focus is on competing in the world market (see Chenery et al., 1986 for details). Therefore, closed economies with a strong governmental influence emerged in the Central American countries during that period. To overcome the limitation of their small markets and in an attempt to substitute imports, central American countries pursued economic integration in the 1960s. This attempt failed mainly due to relative differences in the capabilities of the countries to fully develop their economies under this economic philosophy (Delgado, 1984).

At the end of the 1970s, the Central American countries began to suffer serious economic problems. This economic crisis was the result of the productive inefficiencies fostered by the prevailing inwards development strategy in the region (see Fallas, 1981; Lindenberg and Ramirez, 1991). Then in the 1980s, most Central American countries took corrective actions to fix their economies. The key to these actions was the change to an outwards development strategy aimed at competing in the world market. This change has fostered a process of industry transformation in which companies have been forced to become more competitive. In the 1990s, notable progress towards this change can be observed in Costa Rica and Guatemala, although these countries are still far from achieving a highly competitive economy.

The economic conditions presented above have impacted the development of Central American organizations in at least three ways. First, most business organizations do not face serious competition. However, competition is increasing in some industries as deregulation has taken place. A notable example of this phenomenon can be found in the finance sector. Second, large organizations have mainly arisen from monopolies fostered by the governments in industries believed to have social importance. Consequently, the largest organizations in the countries tend to be very bureaucratic. Third, organizations not controlled by the government are mainly family businesses without, in many cases, a clear separation of owners and managers. This situation has lessened as capital markets have emerged in the countries. Due to these characteristics of the organizations in Central America, information systems are mainly used in the countries for operational support.

## 3.2. Comparison with developed countries

Palvia et al. (1992) presented a hypothetical three-stage model $^{2}$ for countries which was useful for this research. According to his model, IT is constrained by the national infrastructure in the first stage of the model. IS management issues in this stage are therefore infrastructural. This stage can be observed in underdeveloped nations, $^{3}$ such as some African countries, which do not yet have an appropriate infrastructure.

In the second stage, IT is used to support business operations. Therefore, the issues in this stage are concerned with the operational aspects of using IT. Developing countries provide examples of nations in this stage. Finally, IT is used strategically in the third stage. IS management issues in this stage are then concerned with the strategic use of IT to improve the competitive position of the organization and to enhance the organizational structure. This stage is characteristic of developed nations.

Based on the IT development presented in Palvia et al. (1992), Costa Rica and Guatemala fit well in the second stage (operational). Both countries have a mixed economy in which the government exerts considerable influence in economic activities. Therefore, governmental policies and laws — for example, regarding telecommunications, the protection of local markets, and taxes — notably influence IT development in those countries. In many cases, IT is primarily used to alleviate the bureaucracy prevailing in large organizations.

Furthermore, at the end of the 1980s, most organizations, both public and private, began to buy relatively large amounts of microcomputers in an attempt to rapidly introduce and assimilate IT. Microcomputers allowed, for the first time, many organizations to consider the use of IT in their operations. According to the Policy Commission on Informatics of Costa Rica, approximately 37 000 microcomputers were installed in the country in 1990, whereas in the same year it was estimated that around 500 minicomputers and 35 mainframes were in operation. Therefore, for a majority of organizations in the Central American countries, IT is relatively new.

## 4. Research hypotheses

Based on the research questions presented above, two research hypotheses were developed. These hypotheses are presented below along with their rationale for including them in this study.

4.1. Hypothesis 1: the importance of the IS management issues varies among the Central American countries studied

Rationale—The importance of the key IS management issues varies depending on the country considered, as can be concluded from the review of the studies presented earlier. Differences in aspects such as availability of IT personnel, national policies and level of IT sophistication and awareness can produce differences in the importance of the issues among countries (Cash et al., 1992). Furthermore, country-specific issues are likely to appear due to different social/cultural, economic, technological, and political/legal conditions in the countries (Deans and Kane, 1992).

## 4.2. Hypothesis 2: key IS management issues are more similar among the Central American countries relative to the key issues reported for developed countries

Rationale—According to the discussion in the previous section, differences in IS management issues between the two Central American countries and other developed countries are likely to exist. Based on the model of Palvia et al. (1992), issues for Costa Rica and Guatemala should be more similar due to their operational state of IT as opposed to the issues in developed countries which are strategic in nature.

Evidence of this difference in issues between developing and developed countries is revealed in Watson and Brancheau (1991). These authors found in their comparative study a good level of agreement among the key IS management issues in Australia, Europe, and the US. However, the key issues for these countries differed considerably from those found in Singapore. Two possible explanations for this difference, proposed by Watson and Brancheau, are the developing status of Singapore and the difference in culture between Singapore and the other countries. Considering that Costa Rica and Guatemala are developing countries which have a comparable culture, it seems reasonable to assume that their key IS management issues are more similar compared with those in other developed countries. Consistent with the operational stage of Palvia et al., we anticipated finding key issues related to disaster recovery, security and control maintenance, education, and end-user computing in the Central American countries. These reflect operational concerns aimed at using effectively IT.

## 5. Data collection

The data for this study was collected through a single-round field survey, similar to the studies of Ball and Harris (1982), Caudle et al. (1991), Davenport and Buday (1988), Deans et al. (1991), Harrison and Farn (1990), Hartog and Herbert (1986), Herbert and Hartog (1986) and Rao et al. (1987). The questionnaire was distributed in Costa Rica and Guatemala with the help of a major computer vendor in the Central American region, General Business Machines (GBM). $^{4}$ GBM was the ideal candidate to assist in administering the survey since it has the largest market share in the two countries studied. GBM's role was limited to distribution and subsequent collection of the questionnaire from IS executives. Completed questionnaires were sealed to protect the anonymity of the respondents.

Because of GBM's major presence in Costa Rica and Guatemala, and because of the relatively small size of each country, a good cross-section of these countries was possible. The reliance on a major computer vendor with this presence in each country to distribute the survey was preferred to the use of a mail survey for two reasons. First, mail surveys usually exhibit very low response rates. The problem would have been accentuated by conducting the mail survey internationally. Second, given the international nature of this study, the administration of the survey by mail would have been very time consuming and expensive.

Following the advice of Watson and Brancheau (1991), the questionnaire was addressed to the highest ranking IS executive in each of the Costa Rican and Guatemalan organizations selected for this study. A judgement sample $^{5}$ was used to choose the organizations. Organizations were selected based on their importance for the country, as determined by a panel of experts. (This panel's role is further discussed below). The questionnaire was also administered in Costa Rica to non-GBM customers by the researchers. Costa Rica is the Central American country in which GBM faces more competition, and thus administering the questionnaire to non-GBM customers allowed the coverage of important organizations in the country which were not customers of this computer vendor. Since GBM has a dominant position in Guatemala (approximately 80% market share), it can be argued that the reliance on this company to administer the survey to its customers did not introduce a significant bias in the sample for this country. In addition to the survey, semi-structured interviews were conducted with ten IS executives from Costa Rica and Guatemala to clarify and provide additional insights regarding the key issues in the Central American countries.

## 5.1. IS management issues

Issues from the studies previously presented were used as a starting point to develop a first list of issues applicable to Central America. Thirty-seven IS management issues were initially identified from these studies. These issues were reviewed by thirteen IS experts from Costa Rica and Guatemala, selected from university professors, IS executives and IS consultants, to assure their content validity. $^{6}$ Based on the recommendations of these experts, seven IS management issues were deleted and three new ones were added. Consequently, the final version of the questionnaire contained thirty-three issues (see Appendix A for a description of these issues).

Furthermore, the issues in the final questionnaire were tested for stability using the test–retest method. Thirty-six IS professionals from four Costa Rican organisations were selected for this reliability test. A time interval of 2 weeks, as suggested by Nunnaly (1978), was used and a reordering of the issues in the second test was performed to reduce the carry-over effect. $^{7}$ The reliability coefficients obtained from the stability test ranged from 0.53 to 0.90, which were acceptable (Nunnaly (1978)).

In addition to the ex-ante content validation of the questionnaire by the panel of IS experts, survey participants were asked to add issues not included in the questionnaire that they considered important. The low percentage of respondents suggesting new issues and the low frequency of those issues supported a good ex-post content validity in both countries.

The final questionnaire was given to the IS executives in Spanish. Similar to previous studies (Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1987; Watson, 1989; Niederman et al., 1991), the issues were presented to the IS executives in random order with a rationale aimed at explaining why each issue was included in the questionnaire. These rationales were adapted from these previous studies.

The participants were asked to rate the importance of the issues over the next 3–5 years using a ten-point Likert-type scale. Rating was selected over ranking because rating allows participants to rate two issues with the same value, showing indifference between them, or to rate them differently, where the interval between the rates becomes a measure of the relative importance among the two issues (Hartog and Herbert, 1986; Niederman et al., 1991). In addition, rating is less difficult than ranking (Watson, 1989; Niederman et al., 1991) and provides numerical scores for each issue which can be processed statistically (Hartog and Herbert, 1986).

The number of questionnaires obtained in Costa Rica was just five less than the number of usable responses obtained for the last round in the most recent US survey (Niederman et al., 1991). Regarding the issue of competitive advantage, the executives interviewed agreed that the importance of this issues could be explained by the economic changes made in Costa Rica from an inwards to an outwards development model, as previously discussed. As stated by Vitale et al. (1986) and Wiseman (1985), there is a direct link between IS strategic planning and the use of IS to obtain competitive advantage; therefore, it is not surprising to find that these two issues appear among the key issues in Costa Rica. The executives interviewed concurred with this finding.

Software development and disaster recovery tied for third. Software development can be considered as a universal IS management issue. It is well known in most parts of the world that software projects usually take longer and cost more than expected (see Price Waterhouse, 1992). Better tools (e.g. fourth generation languages and CASE) and methodologies (e.g. prototyping and object-oriented analysis and design) are needed to increase the effectiveness of the software development process. Although some of these tools and methodologies have been introduced in Costa Rica, the executives interviewed explained that in general they still are not being used properly.

Different from the first three issues, disaster recovery does not appear as a universal IS management issue as it has been barely mentioned in other studies. This issue appeared in the US only in the latest SIM–MISRC study (Niederman et al., 1991), which ranked twentieth. It was ranked seventh in Singapore (Rao et al., 1987), and was suggested, but not formally ranked, by participants in the study conducted by Harrison and Farn (1990) in Taiwan. The IS executives interviewed agreed that a plausible explanation for the importance of disaster recovery in Costa Rica is the high seismic danger that exists in this country.

Training IS personnel was considered as an issue in former IS management issues studies in the US. However, these studies usually combined this issue with career development or recruiting. In the most recent studies conducted in the US, training IS personnel was included in the issue of human resources for IS. Niederman et al. (1991) stressed the importance of training within IS human resources, which ranked fourth in their study. In our study, training IS personnel and IS human resources were regarded as distinct issues. Interestingly, training IS personnel ranked higher (fifth) in Costa Rica than human resources for IS (fourteenth), which is concerned with obtaining and retaining IS personnel.

The executives interviewed explained that university programs related to IS, mainly computer science programs, are relatively new in Costa Rica, dating back to the mid 1970s. Therefore, organizations have large numbers of IS professionals who do not have a university degree or have degrees from other disciplines. The accelerated change in IT in the last few years has forced the continuous retraining of these professionals. Training is also needed for new university graduates to get acquainted with their company's IT environment.

The use of the data resource can be considered as a universal IS management issue. The executives interviewed agreed that, most often, data as a resource in Costa Rica is unrecognized by managers, thereby often making it underutilized. This disregard for the data resource affects not only the effectiveness of the organization but also the functions of the IS department.

According to the executives interviewed, information security and control has become important in Costa Rica due to the two factors: (1) a pressure in organizations for more accountability; and (2) the electronic crimes which have been committed in past years. The first factor appears as a consequence of the economic changes that Costa Rica is undergoing. Before these changes, private organizations were primarily family-owned companies with little pressure for accountability. With the creation of money and capital markets and the increasing use of these markets to obtain financial resources, Costa Rican businesses have been forced to have more stringent auditing procedures. These procedures have permeated to the IS function. However, IS personnel in Costa Rica have limited knowledge about IS control and security, creating a problem for IS managers.

Additionally, several electronic crimes in Costa Rica have been reported over the years. This type of criminal activity has raised the awareness of the general public about the need for better security and control procedures in the IS function of the Costa Rican organizations.

Most studies on IS management issues did not consider the issue of supporting and training end-user computing, they only presented the issue of facilitating and managing end-user computing. Supporting and training end users was ranked higher in Costa Rica than the issue of facilitating and managing end-user computing, which is more concerned with the organizational control and definition of the role of end users vis-à-vis the IS department. The importance of training end users can be observed in Costa Rica in the number of training seminars for end users offered by different organizations. Training and support for end users tend to go together as more sophisticated IT users may demand greater support from IS departments.

Organizational learning and use of IS technologies may be considered as a universal IS management issue. According to the executives interviewed, the importance of the issue of organizational learning and use of IS technologies in Costa Rica can be explained by the process of industry transformation in the country (as discussed in Section 3). With the purpose of using IS technologies to improve competitiveness, organizations in Costa Rica are more aware of the organizational learning required to efficiently and effectively apply these technologies.

The issues of decision and executive support systems and end-user computing tied for tenth in Costa Rica. While the first can be considered an issue particular to the country, the second can be considered universal. A possible explanation for the importance of the issue of decision and executive support systems in Costa Rica, suggested by the executives interviewed, is the high uncertainty that exists in Costa Rica regarding the transition to the new economic model and the increased level of competition. Little experience exists in the country about competing in the world market. Therefore, companies are using or considering the use of decision support systems to help make decisions.

The importance of the issue of end-user computing has decreased in the US over time, as evidenced in the SIM-MISRC studies. However, end-user computing is “one of the critical...issues that managers are facing in developing countries in Latin America” (Montesinos-Delgado, 1992, p. 114). Perhaps the lag in IT transfer into the country is a plausible explanation for the end-user computing issue to be ranked high in Costa Rica. In the mid 1980s, Costa Rican organizations began to heavily invest in microcomputers, increasing the number of end users. According to the executives interviewed, this currently large and relatively new number of end users that exists in Costa Rican organizations has produced the same administrative and control problems that existed in the US during most of the 1980s.

## 7. Comparison of issues

This section addresses research questions 2 and 3, and is divided into two parts. The first subsection compares the issues found in Costa Rica and Guatemala. The second subsection compares the issues in these Central American countries with those found in other developed countries.

## 7.1. Regional comparison

As presented in Section 6, a remarkable concordance exists among the key issues in Costa Rica and Guatemala. This fact is confirmed by the results of a correlation analysis. The Kendall's tau-b correlation coefficient $^{9}$ considering the rankings for all the issues ( $\tau_{b}=0.6743$ ) reflects a good positive correlation between the rankings obtained. This coefficient is statistically different from zero at the 0.0001 level of confidence. Therefore, a good agreement in the ranking of the issues exists for the two Central American countries.

A MANOVA was also performed to analyze the similarity between the two Central American countries considering the ratings of the issues. Since this analysis is based on the ratings of the issues and not on their rankings, it circumvents the problem of the relevance of the rankings for the issues in the countries, as previously explained. In this case, only the key (top eleven $^{10}$ ) issues in both countries were considered to increase the power of the test, obtaining fourteen different issues. These issues were used as dependent variables, whereas the country was used as an independent variable in the analysis. The significance level for the MANOVA test was 0.1960 for the Wilks' lambda, Pillai's trace, and Hotelling–Lawley trace statistics. Therefore, no statistical differences were detected for the means of the ratings at the 0.10 level of confidence. A power analysis $^{11}$ for the sample used in the MANOVA test provided a power of 0.85 for a confidence level of 0.10 and an effect size of 0.13. Consequently, the results presented above do not support hypothesis 1: the importance of the IS management issues varies among the Central American countries studied. Therefore, we conclude, based on the data gathered in this study, that the importance of the key IS management issues in Costa Rica and Guatemala is statistically the same.

## 7.2. International comparison

In this subsection, we compare the key IS management issues in Costa Rica and Guatemala with those obtained in other studies in the US and Australia. We selected the US for this comparison for two reasons. First, the US is the leading country in the world in terms of using and developing IT. Second, it is common to use the US as a yardstick to compare the development of IS management in other countries (see Rao et al., 1987; Davenport and Buday, 1988; Watson, 1989; Harrison and Farn, 1990; Watson and Brancheau, 1991).

For the US, we selected the most recent study on IS management issues, conducted by Niederman et al. (1991), and the previous SIM–MISRC study carried out by Brancheau and Wetherbe (1987). Brancheau and Wetherbe's study, although a little dated now, was selected for the possibility that the Central American countries might have issues more similar to the ones revealed in an earlier stage of development in the US. These two studies used similar samples and methodologies as our study, making comparison possible.

The studies conducted in Australia (Watson, 1989), Europe (Davenport and Buday, 1988), Singapore (Rao et al., 1987) and Taiwan (Harrison and Farn, 1990) were also considered for comparison. From these studies, only the Australian study was selected. The other studies were not selected because of differences in their time frames with regard to our study, representativeness problems arising from few participants, or different wording of the issues which would have made the comparison difficult. Table 6 presents the key issues and their ranks for the selected studies. To provide a fair comparison, eleven top-ranking issues were considered for all the studies due to the tie of two issues for the tenth rank in Costa Rica.

To determine the similarities and differences among the studies, a correspondence analysis of the rankings of the key issues was conducted. Correspondence analysis $^{12}$ is an exploratory multivariate technique for displaying the rows and column of a data matrix as points in a two-dimensional space (see Greenacre, 1984; Hoffman and Franke, 1986). It is an appropriate and useful technique for this study as it shows associations among data points (i.e. the studies and issues) by grouping them accordingly.

To perform a correspondence analysis on rank-ordered data, the rankings of the issues had to be reversed (see Weller and Romney, 1990). Thus, the highest ranking issue (1) was assigned the highest numerical value of the rankings in Table 6 (28), and the lowest ranking issue was assigned a value of 1. Issues not ranked in a study were assigned a value of 0. The eleventh issue in Australia, data integrity and quality, was excluded from this analysis since it only appeared as a key issue in that study (see Table 6), leaving a total of 20 issues for the analysis. The correspondence analysis, according to the criteria provided by Hoffman and Franke (1986), determined that the issue of IT infrastructure (13) was an outlier, and thus it was included only as a supplementary point (see Greenacre, 1984; Hoffman and Franke, 1986 for details).

The numerical results of this analysis are presented in Table 7. The horizontal and vertical axes accounted for 83.66 and $7.61\%$ of the inertia (total variation in the data), respectively. Therefore, these axes explain a large proportion (91.27%) of the total inertia, and thus provide a good two-dimensional representation of the data. The weights of each point (i.e. each study and each issue) are represented in Table 7 under the column of mass. The contribution of each point to the total variation of the data is presented in the column labeled inertia. The contribution of a point to an axis is depicted in the column of contribution. The larger the contribution of a point to an axis, the more important the point is for determining the location of the axis in the geometrical representation of the data. Finally, the inertia (variation) explained by an axis for a point is presented in the column of correlation. The larger the correlation for a given point and axis, the better the representation for the point using only this axis.

## 8. Conclusions

This study identified the key IS management issues in Costa Rica and Guatemala and then compared those items with key issues previously identified from studies in other countries. Good agreement on the importance of the issues was found in the two Central

American countries, while the comparison of the issues in these countries with studies in Australia and the US showed differences. Thus, the analysis provides support for categorizing IS management issues as developing-country, developed-country, and universal.

Particular issues to Costa Rica and Guatemala were: training IS personnel, supporting and training end users, IS standards and controls, disaster recovery, information security and control and decision and executive support systems. These issues also have appeared among the key IS management issues in other developing countries: Singapore (Rao et al., 1987) and Taiwan (Harrison and Farn, 1990). Furthermore, with the exception of the issue of decision and executive support systems, these particular issues fit into the operational stage of the model of Palvia et al. (1992) model which is characteristic of developing countries. Therefore, these issues can be considered developing-country issues.

On the other hand, some issues appeared to be particularly important to the US and Australia: information architecture, human resources for IS, aligning IS department, telecommunications system, IS role and contribution and IS effectiveness and productivity. In general, these issues are more strategic than operational in nature, and therefore, they can be considered developed-country issues. In addition, in the four countries compared (Australia, Costa Rica, Guatemala and the US), common important issues were discovered: using IS for competitive advantage, IS strategic planning, software development, use of data resource and organizational learning and use of IS technologies. These common issues also frequently appeared as important issues in the studies discussed in Section 2. Consequently, they can be considered universal issues.

These universal issues can be attributed to global economic forces that are driving developed and developing countries alike to become more competitive. This competitive pressure is forcing organizations in these countries to increase their effectiveness and efficiency. We can speculate that such universal issues are common to all stages in the Palvia et al. (1992) model with the possible exception of underdeveloped countries in which infrastructure may impose limitations for competing.

The findings also support the stage model of Palvia et al. Key issues in the two Central American countries are more operational as compared with the issues in the two developed countries considered, Australia and the US, which are more strategic in nature. However, the appearance of issues such as using IS for competitive advantage, IS strategic planning, use of the data resource, and organizational learning and use of IS technologies among the key concerns of IS managers in Costa Rica and Guatemala reveal a transition of the Central American countries towards the strategic stage.

## 8.1. Implications for researchers and practitioners

This research has several implications for researchers and practitioners. Researchers interested in international IT management need to recognize differences in issues among developed and developing countries as well as recognize the existence of common or universal issues. These universal issues are likely to reflect the interdependency of the countries in today's world.

Most of the universal issues found in this study were strategic in nature. However, more research is needed to conclude that this will always be the case. It may be possible that some universal issues may be operational or even technological in nature, particularly when new technologies are introduced. Most IS management research has been conducted in developed countries where IS management issues tend to be strategic in nature. In order to promote an effective transfer of IT into developing countries, research is needed in these countries to address the impact of operational issues on their management practices. IS researchers in developing countries need to consider the key operational issues in their countries to suggest solutions or ways in which they can be addressed. In this way, they can help to accelerate the transfer of IT as well as the transition of their countries into the strategic stage. However, additional research is needed to address social, cultural, technological, political, and legal factors in the countries which may also affect this transfer of technology (see Deans and Kane, 1992).

The SIM-MISRC studies show that considerable change exists in the key IS management issues in the US across time. However, no similar studies have been conducted in other countries. Therefore, another area of research concerns the time stability of the key issues in developing countries and the factors influencing such changes.

IS practitioners must realize that useful IS technologies and methodologies in developed countries are not necessarily successful in developing countries. Developing countries may face operational and human resource limitations for adopting these technologies and methodologies. Furthermore, although IS practitioners in developing countries have to consider operational aspects of using information technologies, they also need to consider similar issues as their counterparts in developed countries for using IT in an increasingly competitive world. This situation forces IS managers in developing countries to concentrate on several fronts at the same time. This is probably the reason why clear priorities regarding IS management issues do not appear to exist in the two developing countries studied, as we mentioned above. More in-depth research, possibly involving more direct data gathering techniques, like interviews or focus groups, will be needed to understand the relationship of issues in the minds of IS managers in developing countries.

## 8.2. Concluding remarks

This study is valuable in supporting additional IS management research internationally and specifically in other Latin American countries. The fact that differences in Costa Rica and Guatemala existed with respect to the US and Australia signifies that differences might exist in other countries as well. To assure an effective use of IT in an eventual economic integration in a global environment, increased awareness of the country-specific and universal IS management issues must be a high priority.

Appendix A.1. Selecting and integrating packaged applications software

The changing relationship among costs of hardware, packaged software and human resources makes the selection and integration of packaged applications software a critical aspect of IS management. Using purchased application software can cut costs, boost productivity, and increase user satisfaction, but it can result in poor integration of systems and serious maintenance problems. It is necessary to develop better procedures to evaluate, select, and integrate packaged applications software.

## Appendix A.2. Migration to open systems

Open systems offer portability, compatibility and interconnectivity benefits. However, the migration to open systems is a difficult process. It involves reprogramming of the existing systems and training of technical personnel. The IS department must overcome administrative problems to justify the necessary funds required to perform this migration.

## Appendix A.3. Overcoming barriers for procuring equipment and contracting services

Governmental regulations may impose lengthy and complicated procedures for procuring equipment or contracting services. Such regulations may also affect the type of equipment that can be procured or the services that can be contracted. Changes are needed so IS departments can avoid spending a considerable amount of time dealing with these regulations.

## Appendix A.4. Facilitating and managing end-user computing

The proliferation of end-user computing through personal computers offers the promise of improved productivity, but also presents the dangers of inadequate management control. IS management must balance control against slack. This slack is needed for end users to experiment with IT. Clarification of the roles of the IS department and end users in the IS function of the organization is a necessity.

## Appendix A.5. Using IS for competitive advantage

In many businesses, long-term survival is dependent on using IS to gain competitive advantage. Competitive advantage results from the recognition of opportunities through creativity and innovation, followed by rapid implementation. It is necessary to improve the capabilities of the IS department to discover these opportunities as well as to accomplish a rapid IS implementation.

## Appendix A.6. Improving systems maintenance

The applications portfolio is rapidly increasing in size, complexity and maintenance cost. Despite the longevity of the systems maintenance problem, too little is known about managing it effectively.

## Appendix A.7. Facilitating and managing decision and executive support systems

Increasing the ability to exploit situations for competitive advantage in the organization depends on enhancing the ability of management to ‘experiment’ with decision possibilities. Decision support tools have long been viewed as a method to introduce models and quantitative tools to executives with the aim of improving their decision making capabilities. However, these efforts have been met with mixed success.

## Appendix A.8. Establishing effective disaster recovery capabilities

Downside risks are increasing daily from the potential loss of business due to a disaster. Risks become greater as organizational applications grow and become more integrated. Effective recovery plans must be in place and tested regularly to ensure losses are minimized.

## Appendix A.9. Measuring the effectiveness and productivity of the IS function

The measurement of the performance of the IS function is crucial to its effective management. This activity is becoming more important as organizations invest more and more resources in IS.

## Appendix A.10. Improving software development

The application development backlog remains at unacceptably high levels. The need for improved effectiveness in systems development is clear. More effective software tools and techniques are needed to improve the development of software and satisfy the systems demand from users.

## Appendix A.11. Facilitating organizational learning and use of IS technologies

Organizations that prosper are those that make use of appropriate new IS technologies in their entire operation. Business practices and organizational structures need to be modified in many cases. The IS department must demonstrate its own ability to learn and use new technology.

## Appendix A.12. Evaluating and testing new IS technologies

Firms should devote more resources to the evaluation and testing of new IS technologies. By doing so, organizations can avoid investing in inappropriate technologies and become better prepared to use relevant technologies.

## Appendix A.13. Developing standards and control mechanisms for IS activities

The IS function is a complex activity which requires standards and control mechanisms to be successful. The need for standards and control mechanisms increases with the presence of end-user computing. However, the development and enforcement of these standards and mechanisms are difficult activities. IS departments need to find better ways to establish and enforce efficient standards and control mechanisms for their activities.

## Appendix A.14. Integrating IS technologies

The capability now exists to integrate systems that are based on diverse IS technologies such as electronic data processing, office automation and telecommunications. However, as organizations try to integrate these technologies, technical and managerial problems may need to be solved.

## Appendix A.15. Supporting and training end users

End-user computing is important for the organization. More support and training is needed for end users so that end-user computing can live up to its promise of improved productivity.

## Appendix A.16. Improving relations between IS and user departments

Political problems or misunderstandings with the user departments makes the accomplishment of the mission of the IS department difficult. This department should develop skills to improve its relations with the user departments in the organization.

## Appendix A.17. Using and improving IS strategic planning

It is increasingly critical to an organization's success that it aligns its long-range IS plan with its strategic business plan. Rapidly changing business environments, increased involvement of end users and accelerated technological change underscore the need to use and continuously improve the strategic planning skills of the IS department.

Appendix A.18. Reorganization of the IS department functions due to the increment of outsourcing

There exists a preference towards computer services and information systems outsourcing. Due to this process, the IS department needs to reorient its function within the organization.

## Appendix A.19. Developing and managing distributed systems

Distributed systems present a variety of challenges including: establishing and maintaining consistent data; physically connecting computers linked by various networks; responding to increased demands for joint development projects with users; and controlling wasteful duplication of efforts.

## Appendix A.20. Facilitating and managing mainframe computing

Mainframe computing is an important activity of the IS department. In order to obtain an acceptable response time, the mainframe computer needs enough capacity to handle the workload. Qualified technical personnel are needed to operate this equipment.

## Appendix A.21. Making effective use of the data resource

The organization's data resource is growing in size, complexity, and value. Despite this, it remains largely unrecognized, inaccessible and underutilized. The IS function must developed a climate within its department and throughout the organization that values the data resource as a corporate asset.

## Appendix A.22. Developing an information architecture

A corporate/global information architecture is needed to identify the major categories of information used within the enterprise and their relationships to business processes. This architecture is required to guide applications development and facilitate the integration and sharing of data.

## Appendix A.23. Planning, implementing and managing telecommunications systems

Communications is the lifeblood of the organization. Using IS for competitive advantage often depends heavily on telecommunications. Rapid and major changes in the telecommunications industry makes the task of planning, implementing and managing telecommunications systems very complex.

## Appendix A.24. Aligning the IS department with the organization of the enterprise

The effectiveness with which IS can support the enterprise's information needs is dependent on the IS department's position within the enterprise. Too often, this department is not located appropriately within the organization.

## Appendix A.25. Coping with national infrastructure limitations

Problems with the electricity, telecommunications and transportation systems in the country may seriously affect the operation of IS in the organization. A significant amount of time and effort should be devoted by organizations to cope with such problems.

## Appendix A.26. Training IS personnel

IS technologies and methods are always evolving. These changes make it necessary to train IS personnel in the latest technologies and methodologies available.

## Appendix A.27. Improving information security and control

As organizations increase their dependence on IS, there is a greater risk from destruction and alteration of data, disclosing of sensitive information to outside sources and disruption of information services. Tight security controls and fault-tolerant information delivery are becoming a necessity.

## Appendix A.28. Increasing understanding of the role and contribution of IS

The IS department is sometimes viewed as an overhead expense with little appreciation of its contributions to the organization. This can lead executive management to make infeasible demands and cut IS funding, resulting in missed opportunities for the organization.

## Appendix A.29. Overcoming language barriers

IS personnel and end users often have problems understanding technical documentation that is not written in Spanish. This situation makes the appropriate use of the IT resources in the organization difficult.

## Appendix A.30. Facilitating and managing office automation

Office automation is being implemented by many organizations through facilities for word processing, electronic mail, electronic conferencing, etc., to improve ‘white collar’ productivity. The IS department should take a leadership role in planning, implementing and managing office automation technologies.

## Appendix A.31. Specifying, recruiting and developing human resources for IS

Possible shortages of qualified IS personnel may threaten the IS department's ability to keep up with the needs of the organization. IS professionals need to be selected carefully based on their technical and managerial skills. Better salaries are needed for IS personnel. Career paths need to be defined for IS professionals within the organization.

## Appendix A.32. Preparing for IS use in a global economy

There exists the tendency towards the integration of national economies. The European Economic Community (EEC) and the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) are clear examples of this tendency. IT is a fundamental instrument to realize this integration. Companies must develop abilities in the use of IT to be prepared to enter a global economy.

## Appendix A.33. Building a responsive IT infrastructure

The combination of maintenance problems, evolving IT, package integration, user-developed systems, quality of the application portfolio and the existence of multiple hardware and software platforms in the organization impose limitations to the IT processes needed to manage the business. Building an integrated IT platform is the key to organizational and industry productivity.
