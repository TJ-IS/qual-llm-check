# Differences in national information infrastructures: the reflection of national cultures

M.J. Garfield\*, R.T. Watson

Department of Management, University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602-6256, USA

Accepted 21 January 1998

## Abstract

This paper reports on the different paths seven countries (the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Japan, Singapore and China) each took in forming a National Information Infrastructure (NII). While many factors play a role in the development of an NII, this paper examines the pervasive role national culture had in the policy decisions and formation of the various NIIs. The main lesson from observing these seven countries is that when a country is forming an NII it should look to similar countries, in terms of national culture, for guidance in the creation of an NII. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: National information infrastructures; National cultures; Data communication

## 1. Introduction

Designing and implementing a National Information Infrastructure (NII) has become a high priority for many leading industrialized nations (Blake and Tiedrich, 1994; Cats-Baril and Jelassi, 1994; Chee-Kai, 1994; Gore, 1994a, b, c). An NII is of strategic importance because it is potentially a very significant contributor to economic wealth in the emerging age of electronic commerce. Implementing an NII requires political leaders to make key decisions on its structure and management.

Infrastructure can have a profound effect on a region's prosperity. The Roman road system, for example, transported Rome's conquering army and enabled efficient administration of the Empire. The highways also enabled the great migrations into the empire and facilitated the spread of Christianity (Britannica Online, 1996). Transportation (e.g. canals, railroads, and highways) and communication (e.g. postal and telephone) infrastructures are critical to economic development. As more communication take place online there is every expectation that an NII will be essential for continued national prosperity (Information Industry Committee of Industrial Structure Council, 1994).

As we enter into the new economic era of information and knowledge, the ability to communicate and transfer information will become the cornerstone to a successful national economy. In order to compete, a country will require a communication infrastructure that facilitates the movement of information (Florida, 1995). Telecommunication policies are designed to help a country be more competitive in the knowledge economy. Telecommunication infrastructures allow public and private industry to share information and learn from one another; they dictate what can be easily communicated with external entities and what knowledge will be made readily available to the world at large. They also touch individual lives by enabling specific types of electronic interactions and defining an individual's right to communicate with others.

Many definitions of an NII have been offered. Some include cable television, plain old telephone service (POTS), and data communication infrastructure. For the purposes of this paper, we are limiting our study to data communication infrastructure, which in some cases, may include the use of POTS and cable television. However, these forms of communication are included only when a country has intentionally designed a national data communication infrastructure based on these services.

We are fortunate to have diverse national cultures and approaches to creating wealth because nations arrive at different solutions to common problems. As a global society, we can learn from this diversity. The extent of our learning depends on our willingness to examine carefully and openly each nation's efforts. Failure to observe how other nations develop and deploy their information infrastructures could be detrimental to the development of a country's NII.

We expect there will be fundamental differences in some approaches to the planning and development of an NII. For example, the idea of providing 500 cable TV channels to each home, an early proposed application of the American NII, clearly depicts an emphasis on satisfying consumers. By contrast, Singapore emphasizes developing applications for improving healthcare to improve its society as a whole. Thus, we believe the alignment of technological policy with cultural norms will play a major role in the shaping of NII policy.

This paper compares the type of NII policies present in culturally dissimilar countries. It is the first report of an ongoing project to monitor NII development. It is meant to be a descriptive paper that provides a structured comparison of the NIIs of seven countries. To provide an organizational structure for comparing different national efforts, a detailed framework that outlines the various components of an NII is used. This framework groups the NII components into three basic categories: network services, administration, and applications that reside on the NII. Each of these categories in turn encompasses a range of specific details necessary for an NII.

## 2. Frameworks for evaluating nii options

We selected two frameworks as the basis of comparison: national culture and NII components. National culture was used to select the countries included in this study;

the NII components were used to break the policy down to identify discrete portions of the NII policy.

## 2.1. National culture

National culture, the collective programming of the mind, distinguishes members of one nation from another (Hofstede, 1991). Since each culture has unique values to guide human behavior (Triandis, 1993), national culture strongly affects management practices, including policy making (Earley, 1993, 1994). The creation and direction of economic units, technological infrastructure, and industrial policy will be affected by cultural ideologies (Porat, 1978; Hofstede, 1991; Fallows, 1994). Thus, the alignment of cultural norms and technology policy may play a major role in shaping NII policy.

Based on a survey involving more than 120 000 respondents from 50 countries, Hofstede (1991) proposes a model of national culture with five dimensions: individualism–collectivism, power distance, time orientation, masculinity–femininity, and uncertainty avoidance. Other studies have confirmed the validity of these dimensions (e.g. Ronen and Shankar, 1985; Shackleton and Ali, 1990) and employed them to account for empirical observations (e.g. Earley, 1993; Straub, 1994; Tan et al., 1998). This study uses the model of Hofstede (1991) because it has been shown to be stable and useful by numerous studies across many disciplines [see review by Sondergaard (1990)].

Individualism is the relationship between the individual and the group and the power the group has over the individual. Gender-related values are those norms tied to the social implications of the gender identity that the individual possesses. Power distance incorporates the ways individuals interact with others with respect to authority and the social equality within a group. Uncertainty avoidance describes the ways in which individuals within a cultural group deal with uncertain or unknown situations, frequently in terms of the control of aggression and the expression of emotions. Power distance and uncertainty avoidance are useful in describing organizations and structures whereas “individualism and masculinity affect our thinking about people in organizations” (Hofstede, 1991). Since this paper seeks to understand the structures that govern the components of the NII rather than the individuals within the structures, we will focus on the power distance and the uncertainty avoidance dimensions of cultures. We have also decided not to include the time horizon dimension of culture because it was not as thoroughly investigated by Hofstede, and thus we are unable to identify where to place the different countries on the time horizon scale.

In high power distance societies, subordinates defer to superiors and rarely question their authority. Corporations in countries with high power distance tend to be hierarchical, with an unequal distribution of power. They are more centralized and a few individuals make the majority of the decisions. In low power distance societies, subordinates and superiors consider each other as essentially equal. Corporations tend to be flatter and more decentralized. The roles of boss and subordinate are flexible and change rapidly, so that someone who is your boss on one project may work along side you on another project. Countries with small power distance believe that everyone has basically the same rights. Those with large power distance have an unequal distribution of rights, with those in power controlling the rights of the ordinary people.

Strong uncertainty avoiding societies tend to rely on rules and defined procedures to enable them to act clearly. They prefer structured means to get to an end over loosely defined means to reach the same end. Uniformity in the process is important in strong uncertainty avoidance societies, but it is less important in low uncertainty avoidance societies. The higher the uncertainty avoidance, the more rules and regulations employed in a country and its organization.

Using the dimensions of power distance and uncertainty avoidance, Hofstede (1991) identifies four distinct organizational models: the village market, the family, the pyramid of people and the well-oiled machine (see Fig. 1). The village market is characterized by authority lying neither within a person nor a set or rules, but within a situation. Market conditions, not people or formal rules, dictate what should take place. In the family structure, power is unevenly distributed so that a few people hold most of the power. However, these people must also operate within a certain set of rules or boundaries, based on personal relationships. The pyramid of people is best characterized by the importance of personal power and formal rules within an organization. A good leader in this culture has personal authority and deploys formal rules and regulations to guide his employees. Organizations, often labeled bureaucracies, in which the real authority lies within rules, not people, are the well-oiled machines. Cultural differences impact organizational and economic structures. Therefore, NII policies should reflect national ideologies.

## 2.2. NII components

Singapore decomposes its NII (Chee-Kai, 1994) into three levels: the applications, the middleware, and the network. Some of the key components of an NII include terminal design and provision, system architecture, service provision, billing system and regulatory and political environment (Schneider et al., 1991). We construct a model that incorporates the features of the Singaporean model and key NII components defined by Schneider.

This model allows us to look at NII policies at two levels; a higher level with the three layers (network services, administration and applications) and a more refined level by looking at the specific components within each of the three broad categories (see Fig. 2). Network services describes the physical connection between servers and clients. The transport mechanisms used include basic twisted pair, optical fiber, and satellite networks. Servers are the hardware used to control network traffic. Clients are end-customer terminals, which may be designed and manufactured specifically for use on the NII (e.g. Germany), or they may be items already available in the market, such as personal computers (e.g. USA).

The administrative level controls the customers and uses of the network, including the method for billing. It is also responsible for determining who has access to the NII, and how this access is controlled. This may include the monitoring of customer activities (e.g. Singapore).

Finally, the applications component of the NII framework includes all services offered on the NII. Graphical interfaces and screen design fall into this category, as do the specific communications software and protocols. Services provided on an NII range from personal chat sessions to news lines, home shopping, and databases.

## 2.4. The NII in the village market

The village market tends to exhibit governing and organizational structures that take a hands-off approach in the creation or the formation of any project that may be driven by the marketplace. In these countries the market tends to drive policies and programs (Hofstede, 1980). As such we would expect the components of the NII to be created to meet market demands. Private entities may compete to provide the various services and these entities would be driven by a market pull. Therefore, consumer demands will shape the services and products the private entities will offer.

Traditionally, most countries have had high levels of government intervention in network services because the significant up-front cost for the installation of the infrastructure makes it difficult to support competing market forces. As technology improves and the main infrastructure (phone lines) is laid, the village market will be the first to deregulate the marketplace. As for the administration of the NII, we would expect the governing entities to be less involved than the governing entities of other countries (Hofstede, 1980). We may, however, still see some oversight mechanisms playing an active role to ensure that the rights of the people (i.e. freedom of speech and at least minimal universal access) are not infringed. The applications component of the NII will have the least amount of government interaction, and applications will be driven by market demand. The potential profitability of each application will drive its creation.

## 2.5. The NII in the family

The family structure aims to protect the head of the family and is less concerned with equality. Family oriented governments and organizations tend to be very centralized and guided by visions rather than rules (Hofstede, 1980). We would expect the network services of the NIIs in these countries to be guided by the government, with specific implementation methods selected by either the government or private industry. The government would be most likely to play an active role in designing the playing field for the network services (e.g. by providing the guidelines and expectations that the network services must meet), but it may allow the specific features of the services to be determined by other organizations. The government may use the administrative component of the NII as a tool to reign in power. Thus, it may try to promote group harmony and to regulate the types of discussions that are permitted to take place in electronic forums. The government could also use the applications component of the NII to deliver applications that serve the needs of the family (e.g. health and education).

## 2.6. The NII in the pyramid of people

The pyramid of people is best described by its likeness to a full bureaucracy where “the relationships both among people and between people and the work processes tend to be rigidly prescribed, either in formal rules and laws or traditions” (Hofstede, 1980). This could manifest itself in the creation of an NII that is fully controlled by the government, which sets the rules for its implementation and use based on what it perceives to be the appropriate path to take. The market may play a role insofar as it would provide the services the government decided are pertinent for the NII to succeed. Therefore, we would expect to see the network services to be designed and owned by the government with market forces perhaps selling their services to the government to implement the network. The administrative component may be similar to the family in that it may be used to protect the top of the pyramid. The rights of the masses may take a back seat to the desire to protect the pyramid’s leadership. The applications would most probably be designed by the government to serve the public good.

## 2.7. The NII in the well-oiled machine

The well-oiled machine runs smoothly because work process are well defined, not because the parts of the machine (people) have relationships with one another (Hofstede, 1980). The NII in such a country may be designed to be the ultimate communication machine, where the engineering aspect of it is of superior quality (though not necessarily designed to meet market needs). The government tends to play a role in defining national projects, like the network services of the NII, but it may be more of a sponsor for the project than the entity that actually creates it. Because of low power distance, we would expect that the administration of the NII would focus on protecting individual rights (such as personal privacy and having at least minimal access to the NII). The government may play a more intrusive role in the applications development due to the strong uncertainty avoidance, but again it would keep one eye towards the market, and thus the design of the applications may be driven by their profit potential.

## 2.8. Summary of culture and NII fit

We would expect that countries that fall into the same quadrant in the organizational model will tend to be more similar to one another than to those that fall into a different quadrant. Nevertheless, no two countries will be exactly the same, just as no two people within a country are identical. The guidelines and assumptions about what we would expect to see in NII policies are generalizations and as such may not be as precise as those based on considerations of a specific country.

## 3. The success of an NII

The intent of this paper is not to determine if an NII is successful. However, to enable the policy-makers of various countries to learn from one another, they need to be able to assess to some degree the relative effectiveness of an NII. This is a difficult task. What one country may perceive to be a success, another may consider irrelevant (e.g. 500 channel TV). Success can be measured in a wide variety of ways (e.g. number of customers, improving society as a whole, and universal access). Applying one set of measures to all NIIs would not be a fair reflection of the success of the NII. The goals of the NII must play a role in measuring its success. Because these goals and objectives vary greatly between countries, one measure for success is not feasible. For instance, the UK hoped to attract as many customers as possible, whereas Japan had loftier goals of using the NII to improve societal ills (i.e. health and education). Therefore, a country that is looking for successful examples of NIIs needs to find countries that have similar goals and objectives, which will commonly mirror a nation's culture.

## 4. Research method

This paper is a descriptive case study of seven NIIIs. Content analysis (Babbie, 1995) is a suitable research method to use for such case studies when documents are available. In this case, we gained access to government NII policy documents, many of which are electronically available. We relied on archival records and secondary interview sources to collect relevant data. Specifically, we used written records of speeches made by government officials and documents identifying NII policies. We did not attempt to code these documents. Rather, our intent was to gain a broad understanding of NII policy.

We originally selected two countries, with a published NII policy or current NII, from each of the organizational models (see Fig. 3) identified by Hofstede (1991). This sample was biased towards advanced economies for which NII policy information was readily available in English. After further examination, Finland was dropped due to a lack of sufficient information in English.

## 5.1. United Kingdom

The British government's early attempt at a national videotext system, Prestel, was introduced in 1979. By 1990, Prestel had attracted only 150 000 customers, a small portion of the number of customers the government had hoped to attract. By most accounts, this system fell far short of its goals. In more recent years, the UK has greatly deregulated the telecommunications network, and more private companies have entered the market. Britain's overall objective for its NII, according to the Office of Public Service and Science (OPSS), is to be viewed as successful by the consumer—a market orientation.

## 5.1.1. Network services

Initially Prestel's infrastructure, which relied on a network of centralized servers each responsible for a given region, was solely owned and run by the government. In 1981, the Telecommunications Act liberalized some of the telecommunications industry. Telecommunication service was separated from the UK Post Office, and a new state-owned corporation, British Telecom, was established to operate the UK's telecommunications network. At the same time, private corporations were permitted to compete with Prestel. In 1984, the market was further deregulated when British Telecom was privatized, with the government retaining $49\%$ ownership.

The major infrastructure problem in the UK is that the last half mile (0.8 km) of cabling to the home is twisted pair wires and only 20% of all homes have a cable television connection (Dowling et al., 1994). The government feels that through market competition, this structural problem will be corrected.

Prestel uses adapted television sets created by industry and purchased by the customer. The television-based videotext configuration is considered expensive and has hampered the dissemination of terminals within the UK (Schneider et al., 1991). The future generation of the NII could rely on a variety of system platforms including personal computers, telephones, or televisions. Personal computers will probably not be the customer system because only 15% of the people in the UK have one (Waldegrave, 1994), and they are too expensive for most people.

## 5.1.2. Administration

Customers of Prestel are charged a monthly rate (as determined by the government) and may incur additional charges levied by market provided services. But as new networks challenge Prestel in the marketplace, the billing rates are sure to change.

Private industry is competing heavily in the telecommunications and NII marketplace but not without some government intervention. The Office of Public Service and Science (OPSS) (along with several other government entities) still oversees major policy issues for the NII. As the telecommunications industry continues to be privatized, the British government has made it clear that new forms of communication networks need to ensure privacy and must have the ability to interface with one another without the use of separate devices. According to the OPSS, it is important that the system selected for the future have an interface that is readily accessible and affordable.

## 5.1.3. Applications

The limited success of Prestel has been attributed to a low quality database (the design of which is hindered by the government's control of the database storage facilities). Although most of the information accessible on Prestel has been supplied by private information providers, it has been stored in government-operated centralized servers. As of 1993, there were about 1300 services offered, but growth was negligible.

As new competing systems arise more applications are being provided to entice customers to the new systems. The success of these systems will be determined solely by market forces. The government has been encouraging private enterprise to make their services affordable for the majority of the people. It has also stressed the need for the communication protocols used by the system to be interoperatable for a range of providers of consumer services.

## 5.1.4. Concluding remarks

The deregulation of the telecommunications network will have a profound effect on the future of the UK's NII. Prestel has become a network of the past, though what the network of the future will be is still uncertain. The government played an active role in designing and implementing Prestel, which turned out to be fairly unsuccessful in meeting the objectives set out for it. Since that time, the government has deregulated the market and allowed private enterprise to play a role in the development of various telecommunications systems.

Although the government is a major player in the telecommunications market, there is no central champion for an NII policy, and it has lacked a unified marketing effort (Schneider et al., 1991). The government is making suggestions for the future NII, but it appears as though it will only be a referee in the marketplace. The UK market is now perhaps the most deregulated and competitive market in the world (Noam, 1992; Dowling et al., 1994). The telecommunications field has been opened to other countries to encourage further competition (e.g. Bell Atlantic, US West and PacTel have a stake in the British telecommunications industry). “Given also the socio-political constraints imposed by a deep-seated liberal tradition of discrete state intervention...the British approach follows almost naturally from these conditions” (Schneider et al., 1991).

## 5.2. United States

The current marketplace and communications laws in the USA play a major role in establishing NII policy. Congress and the Executive Branch are working to change the restrictive and outdated laws that govern private industry. Although NII policy statements have been market driven, regulatory policies have been slow to change. The general thrust of the recently passed legislation (the Telecommunications Act of 1996) is to increase competition among the competing sectors (e.g. cable companies, regional Bell operating companies, and long distance carriers) without allowing any one sector to monopolize the market.

In the USA, there are currently two distinct types of computer information infrastructures. The first, the Internet, a loosely bound organization of tens of thousands of computer networks in more than 100 countries, grew out of the US Defense Department's project in 1969 and is the de facto US NII. The second is provided by the commercial information service providers (e.g. Prodigy, CompuServe, and America Online). These companies have splintered the marketplace and have had varied success.

## 5.2.1. Network services

The current network topology in the USA includes lengthy stretches of fiber optic wiring, owned by long distance providers, and two primary forms of wiring that reach the house: twisted pair wiring owned by regional Bell operating companies (RBOCs) and higher capacity coaxial cables owned by the cable companies. The government's only policy stance on the NII topology is that every American should have equal access to the NII and that the network be fast and flexible. Although deregulation of the industry is beginning to take place, there are still restrictions on the cross-fertilization between cable companies and phone companies.

While the USA has considered using a centralized approach to building an NII (Cohen and Donow, 1989), it has been recognized that such an approach would run against “the very grain of American culture” (Sawhney, 1993). The National Telecommunications and Information Administration (NTIA) echoed this feeling when it said, “We can best achieve that objective, not by mimicking the deployment plans and organizational styles of other nations, but by doing what we do best” (US Department of Commerce, 1991). For this reason the USA has put forward a market driven plan to build an NII, but it has not moved very quickly to deregulate markets. “These (regulatory) changes promise substantial benefits for the American people, but only if the government understands fully their implications and begins working with the private sector and other interested parties to shape the evolution of the communications infrastructure” (NTIA NII Office, 1993).

## 5.2.2. Administration

The government sees its role is “to protect the public interest and to promote private competition” (Gore, 1994c). It hopes to raise interest in the NII, but not to create, design or implement the NII. This is left to the private sector. To do this the proposed budget calls for a \$5 billion investment over 5 years to help promote the NII, as well as tax incentives for companies who invest in the formation of the NII. The government plans to aid in the research and development stages of the NII and help update current regulatory laws. The government believes “the only viable path is towards competition” (Gore, 1994c) in the telecommunications marketplace.

## 5.2.3. Applications

The US government has not played a significant role in the development of applications. It has had a hands-off approach to the types of applications put on the NII, though it has placed a few of its own applications on the Internet for the public to use (for instance, passport applications material, information dissemination sites, etc.). A wide variety of applications have been made available, many of which have raised public concern over the uses of the internet (pornography, gambling, dissemination of potentially dangerous information about bombs, etc.). At this point, the government has not taken a formal stance on curbing the various applications available electronically, though it has enforced laws that apply to other media on the electronic applications (e.g. pornography and encryption).

## 5.2.4. Concluding remarks

The participatory style of government in the USA may be slowing the deregulation process, since there are at least a dozen players in the government, including the Vice President, the Federal Communications Commission, the 27 member NII Advisory Council, the Commerce Secretary, various Congressional Committees (i.e. the Senate Commerce Committee, the Senate Judiciary Committee, the Senate Subcommittee on Communications, and the House Telecommunications Subcommittee), and several key industries involved in the development of an NII. It is also possible that the slow pace of change reflects a stronger uncertainty avoidance than is generally seen in the US. It has been observed that “the good news from Washington is that every single person in Congress supports the concept of an information superhighway. The bad news is that no one has any idea what that means.” (Blake and Tiedrich, 1994).

## 5.3. Germany

In 1984, a year later than originally promised, the West German government agency, Bundespost, introduced Bildschirmtext a national teletext service. In 1987, when Bundespost was divided into three government-owned agencies (TELEKOM, Post-dienst, and Postbank), TELEKOM retained control and operating rights of Bildschirmtext. In 1989, a telecommunications reform law liberalized a portion of the telecommunications industry, including mobile phones, enhanced services, and the retail sale of customer equipment. In 1993 personal communications networks were liberalized.

## 5.3.1. Network services

The government TELEKOM agency continues to have monopolistic control over the public network. In 1993, TELEKOM did revamp Bildschirmtext, renaming it Datex-J and enhancing many of its features. This network is still solely controlled by TELEKOM and has retained the centralized system architecture that gives the German government control over the network while also giving information providers equal access to the network (Schneider et al., 1991).

The initial terminals were expensive television sets sold by private corporations. The government began marketing simple terminals to provide lower cost customer systems to achieve TELEKOM's objective of reaching the mass market. TELEKOM has since offered an inexpensive card that allows personal computers to interface with Datex-J. Telecommunications regulations exist for all terminal producers in Germany.

## 5.3.2. Administration

Customers are billed a monthly and volume-based charge, both of which are regulated by the German government, but not necessarily levied by it. Some private industries opt to pay for the transmission costs of some of their pages to encourage customers to view them. All German states have enacted special media laws with respect to content control, equity of access, and consumer and data protection to regulate Bildschirmtext or Datex-J, but there is no one federal government stance on these issues. Prior to the redesign of Bildschirmtext, business on the network had been largely unprofitable, mostly due to the lack of network subscribers (only 250 000 at the end of 1990). Since the redesign of the system, the number of customers has grown to 579 687 (as of May 1994).

## 5.3.3. Applications

Private industry provides the services available to customers of Bildschirmtext. In 1994 there were 5434 services provided by 2545 companies. Advanced interactive applications must be connected to the network through IBM-designed gateways and networks, which are too costly for most service providers. The government has neither financed the offering of many applications nor played a role in giving incentives for private industry to provide specific types of applications.

## 5.3.4. Concluding remarks

This system is one of the most heavily regulated NIIs in terms of network services. Although Bildschirmtext has not been profitable, TELEKOM wants the network to continue to offer more services (via private industry) to continue a relationship with the customer base it feels will influence innovation in telecommunications (Dowling et al., 1994). While there seems to be general consensus that TELEKOM needs to be privatized, the political process is moving very slowly (Dowling et al., 1994). This hesitancy to explore new and uncertain regulatory situations illustrates Germany's strong uncertainty avoidance. Nevertheless, Germany has recently accepted bids for a license to compete against the stateowned Deutsche TELEKOM.

## 5.4. France

In 1984, the government deployed Télétel, France's national videotext service, in an effort to become a technological leader. By 1991 Télétel had more than 5 million subscribers and has surpassed 6 million customers today. By most accounts Télétel has been very successful.

## 5.4.1. Network services

Télétel relies on government-owned telephone lines and a network of decentralized servers using packet switching technology (Transpac). This network design cuts the cost of sending messages and makes cost independent of distance. “In an approach typical of French industrial policy, the government, rather than the consumer electronic industry, decided the specifications of the videotext terminals” for use with the network (Cats-Baril and Jelassi, 1994). The dumb terminal clients are provided free of charge, but for more sophisticated applications customers can purchase a higher quality machine or rent second-generation terminals from the government. Customers can also use personal computers with software for emulating Minitel menus and function keys. Portable Minitel terminals that can be used over the cellular telephone network are also available.

## 5.4.2. Administration

Customers are charged for the amount of time they use the system. Private industry decides which of eight levels of pricing it wishes to impose. Although private industry selects the pricing level for accessing its application, the government controls the billing and determines the pricing options through a system termed the Kiosque.

The French government has shown little concern with respect to the privacy of the customers on Minitel. It neither infringes upon it nor takes excessive steps to ensure the privacy of the customers. The government has, however, been very active in making Minitel accessible to all, with the distribution of the free terminals.

## 5.4.3. Applications

France created the first Télétel application, an on-line telephone directory, to spark interest in the system. By 1992, Télétel hosted over 20 000 services, largely due to the low connection cost and a common standard protocol (CCITT X.29). The government is now primarily a common carrier that provides the topology and administers Télétel. While it is not very involved with the development of new applications for Télétel, the French government does encourage the creation of new applications by private industry. France also announced full Internet access for all Télétel customers by March 1996.

## 5.4.4. Concluding remarks

The government has taken a clear leadership role in Télétel that seems to suit French citizens. There is no question about who is controlling this market, and there is no jousting for the leadership role. This reflects France's need for a structured environment, due to its strong uncertainty avoidance. France's Télétel strategy is not novel, “it is very much in keeping with the pattern underlying the development of earlier infrastructure networks” (Sawhney, 1993). The government has the authority to quickly implement changes in regulations and tariffs (Cats-Baril and Jelassi, 1994) without opening the decision to public comment. This is a trait of its culture's large power distance, which emphasizes the authority of the leaders as opposed to the ideas of the masses (Hofstede, 1991). What made Télétel so successful was “a government with deep pockets, a clear purpose, and a national technology strategy.” (Maital, 1991). “France Télécom, aligned it [Télétel] with the political and social values of the time...[and it] was highly compatible with the values of French society” (Cats-Baril and Jelassi, 1994). Had Télétel been implemented in such a fashion in a different country, it may not have been so successful and could have faced strong opposition because of governmental control of the system.

## 5.5. Japan

Japan, yet to implement an NII, has proposed a national information and communication infrastructure and has set out the basic guidelines for the promotion of an advanced information and telecommunications society. The plans are somewhat vague, with various government agencies involved with the planning and design of the over-arching policy. The Japanese effort is led by the Ministry of Posts and Telecommunications (MPT). The objectives for the NII include dealing with an aging population, reforming economic structures, and addressing environmental problems.

## 5.5.1. Network services

In June of 1994 the Telecommunications Council of the MPT announced that it plans to undertake a multi-billion dollar project to connect all homes with fiber optics by the year 2010, in order to help the economy shift from a manufacturing to service economy. The government hopes the network is deployed to the main districts of prefectural capitals, and designated ‘teletopia cities’ by 2000, followed by all cities with populations of 100 000 or more by 2005, and finally to the rest of the country by 2010. The government has also established an advisor system for providing local governments with suggestions on how to promote information and communications capabilities in their regions.

Public and private companies are currently upgrading the telecommunications network. The MPT continues to encourage favorable conditions for the development of the telecommunications industry through the establishment of a special loan system, tax incentives, and government investment to stimulate private sector investments in the network infrastructure. The MPT has also pledged to implement deregulatory policies for the information network industry. Not only is the government concerned with wiring Japan, but also it is also concerned with interconnecting with other countries.

## 5.5.2. Administration

There has been little discussion on how the network would be administered. Japan has stressed the importance of standards and the need to protect consumer interests by adopting policies that minimize cost and service differences caused by geographical location and usage rates. The government will likely have to be responsible for the network in the less profitable domains (e.g. rural areas) that private firms will be less likely to service. This philosophy leads to the assumption that the government is interested in equality of system access to promote an information society.

## 5.5.3. Applications

The government also plans to develop seed applications, especially in health and education, to trigger future development and customer interest. To oversee the development of policies that will lead to the creation of an advanced information and telecommunications society, in August 1994 the Cabinet established the Advanced Information and Telecommunications Society Promotion Headquarters, with the Prime Minister as chairman and the Minister of Posts and Telecommunications as vice chairman. The Japanese government feels that the transformation to an advanced information infrastructure society should bring about significant changes in all industries and economic activities as well as in people's daily lives. Public and private companies are working together to develop ways to deliver a wider range of multi-media services.

## 5.5.4. Concluding remarks

“Japan is ‘supply-pushed’, with heavy government direction guiding what is seen as the interests of the nation and its industry” (Maital, 1991). The government is to “foster an environment conducive to the dissemination of information technology led by the private sector” (Information Industry Committee of Industrial Structure Council, 1994). “Moves toward the advanced information infrastructure society should be led by the private sector as clearly stated in the NII plan, while the role played by the government will be to supplement and reinforce the initiatives taken by the private sector” (Information Industry Committee of Industrial Structure Council, 1994). Although this policy is in its infancy, the government has clearly taken the lead and provided direction for the development of Japan’s NII.

## 5.6. Singapore

As early as the 1960s, Singapore realized the strategic importance of information technology. In 1990 the Economic Development Board released a report, The Next Lap that puts forth IT goals, including the creation of an NII. This proposal was detailed in 1992 by the National Computer Board (NCB) when it released IT2000: Vision for an Intelligent Island. IT2000's objective is to improve the quality of life and enhance the Singapore economy. In the past the government has been widely criticized for its repressive politics (Sisodia, 1992) and critics have questioned how Singapore's political policies will affect the NII. How Singapore currently controls the NII precursor, Teleview, provides some insights as to how it may control its future NII.

## 5.6.1. Network services

The network layer will be based on the current government-controlled telephone networks. Both circuit switching and packet switching technologies are being examined for this layer. Singapore already has one of the most advanced networks in the world, achieving 100 percent ISDN availability in 1989 (Sisodia, 1992). In the future, cable companies, as well as the government, will enhance the network topologies.

The government has emphasized its intentions to partner with local and international R&D firms to implement IT2000. It has also put together a core set of principles the NII should incorporate, which include the need for the network to be distributed, ubiquitous, reliable, secure for multi-service, easy to use, and open to competition. To achieve these principles, it has suggested that a fault tolerant distributed system with secure communication lines, gigabit networks, and bandwidth on demand be deployed. To encourage growth of the NII, the government feels the network should incorporate standards to allow for interoperatability. Standards will be essential in creating an open NII, and the NCB will help create those standards. The policy issues and general guidance for the development of the NII will be resolved by the National Information Infrastructure Department (NIID) within the NCB, and the National IT Committee (NITC) will oversee multi-agency processes.

Teleview, the de facto NII currently running in Singapore, is an advanced videotext system created by Singapore Telecom, the government-run phone company. The system is a public, interactive information system that integrates television, computer, telephone, and a radio receiver to deliver text and color graphics (Sandfort, 1993). This is a very promising foundation on which to build an NII. Teleview is run by the government but has been adaptable enough to allow for a wide range of private industry products to be used as clients for the customers.

## 5.6.2. Administration

The government aims to use a common system for billing customers and for the security measures employed by the network. Those components common to more than one application, the ‘middleware’ for the system, will be based on reusable objects. Singapore is looking to the leading IT suppliers to create these common components. “However, to integrate the pieces to form a holistic middleware layer for the NII requires substantive intellectual work and investment” (Hu, 1992). The government may provide or fund the integration effort necessary for the components to work together.

To encourage Singaporeans to utilize the technology, the National Computer Board (NCB) formed a team (the IT Culture Promotion team) to reach and equip every Singaporean with the knowledge and skills to deal with the new information technologies. These educational efforts are designed to encourage all Singaporeans to become customers of the future NII.

Every Singaporean can subscribe to Teleview, and by 1993 Teleview had nearly 17 000 subscribers (of a population of 2.8 million). Though Singaporeans can use Teleview to communicate with each other, they cannot use it to reach anyone outside the country. Teleview subscribers must also agree not to use the service, “...for sending to any person any message which is offensive on moral, religious, communal or political grounds…” (Sandfort, 1993). This calls into question the level of privacy. It appears as though the Singaporean government will continue to put the protection of the government first and the privacy of individuals second.

## 5.6.3. Applications

The government has targeted IT application development for 11 sectors of the economy including: trade, real estate, education, and medicine. Applications will be designed by the government and developed jointly by public and private entities. Application interfaces and communications will be provided by the private market but must be approved by the government.

Teleview services are provided primarily by private industry and include: services to pay bills, check stock prices, chat with other Singaporeans, and exchange email. The government regulates most components of the network, even personal messages. Thus it is quite possible that there will be many applications available on the NII that are not directly funded by the government but that are still governed and inspected by the government to protect itself and its citizens.

## 5.6.4. Concluding remarks

If the government controls IT2000 as it does Teleview, the market will be defined by the government and run by private industry. Government policies may delineate boundaries for the creation of an NII, but they are only general guidelines for competition. The government feels the “answers to new questions on policies and legal framework become as important as developing the technologies because misguided use of technologies is believed to cause more harm than good” (Chee-Kai, 1994). “Singapore’s story [about Teleview] demonstrates…what can happen when a government assumes an instrumental position in shaping and managing the economic environment” (Sisodia, 1992).

## 5.7. China

China is a newcomer to the field of information networks. It is eager to enter the twenty-first century by embracing the use of electronic networks, yet it is concerned about the potential abuse of such networks. China has been a relatively closed environment and the impact an NII would have on such a country could be monumental.

## 5.7.1. Network services

China is building internal networks and focusing on Internet connections. In 1993, it began to offer Internet connections. China has offered limited access to the Internet via educational networks (CERNET, China Education and Research Networks, being the largest of these kinds) and a few semi-commercial networks (ChinaPac and China Public Data and Digital Network funded by the Beijing Telegraph Administration).

Through funding provided by a World Bank supported project, China is establishing a more comprehensive NII-ChinaNet, which is managed by the National Computing and Networking Facility of China (NCFC) and Computer Network Center (CNC), both of which are government agencies. This network was established to first link the three major institutions in China: The Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS), Peking University (PU), and Tsinghua University (TU), to the Internet. It has now grown to connect over 20 different networks, mostly university-owned, to the Internet.

China is looking to build an NII by meshing together the public switched voice and data networks owned by the Ministry of Post and Telecommunications (MPT) and fiber optic, microwave, and satellite systems. The MPT is spearheading the effort and plans to develop a national network, having all major cities linked by fiber optic networks. The MPT will collaborate with other agencies and the private sector to form the NII. Although the government controls most of the network facilities, it does not produce much of the equipment used for the network architecture. Industry bids on providing such equipment and the government contracts with outside vendors for servers, routers, and other component of the network. Three major computer manufacturers, Cray, DEC and IBM, have been selected as candidates for the second stage of bidding on the development of the NII. China has also set up web servers, FTP sites, and gopher servers, but is currently restricting access to many newsgroups. China faces a major infrastructure problem with only 17 percent of urban homes having phones and only an estimated 40% will have them by 2000.

## 5.7.2. Administration

Due to the high cost of using these networks, most people cannot afford to access them. Customers are often billed for both the time that they use a connection and for the volume of data they transport. Furthermore, long distance calls (which are necessary to connect to some of the networks) are prohibitively expensive for even universities to justify their use of the networks. Not only does the high cost of the connections reduce the number of people who can use the network but also the threat of monitoring what the customers read and send has reduced the number of people who are willing to use the various networks.

China has acknowledged that “as a sovereign nation, China must strengthen information management,” yet not all information on the Internet would be allowed to flow into the country, said Wu Jichuan, Minister of Posts and Telecommunications. In February 1996, China instituted new regulations that required any network offering Internet service be subject to close supervision by the Ministry of Post and Telecommunications or one of three other designated government agencies. The regulations are intended to ensure “healthier development of the exchange of international computer information” (New China News Agency). Customers of the network in China “are also forbidden to produce, retrieve, duplicate or spread information that may hinder public order” (Faison, 1996).

## 5.7.3. Applications

China has not targeted the specific creation of applications to be used on the NII. It has, however, put limitations on what types of applications or information will be permissible. The government controls all access to the networks in China, and through this control it has blocked access to certain newsgroups. It is struggling to find other ways to control the content of network transactions that flow into the country. “China is not closing the door to all information,” said James Chu, CEO of the China Internet Corporation. “It’s just requiring that all information coming in has to follow Chinese laws” (Associated Press, 1996).

## 5.7.4. Concluding remarks

Given its culture, it is expected that if the Chinese leadership decides to aggressively pursue an NII, it will be able to deploy one with few policy difficulties. China's weak uncertainty avoidance will allow it to move into these largely uncharted waters at a rapid pace. However, the Chinese government appears to be hesitating about who can have access to the networks, at what price, and for what use. The government is currently controlling all growth and access to the country's networks. The main focus of providing Internet or network access to the people of China has been to improve education and research exchanges with a dual focus on improving the economy. The private citizen has not been a driving force for the establishment of the networks.

## 6. NII development and fit with culture

We expected that village market countries would have the most market-driven NII policies (Table 4). This is true for the USA and the UK. Conversely, we expected that pyramid of people countries would be the most hierarchical. Clearly, France's Télétel, in its original form, was hierarchical, but it has become more market-driven over time. Initially, France designed an NII as predicted, but the current status of its NII is not in line with what we would have expected. Japan, on the other hand, has continued to promote a fairly hierarchical proposal for an NII. The family countries were most likely to have intrusive government policies, which is true for both Singapore and China. And, finally, the well-oiled machine was expected to take a hands-on approach by the government but one that is less invasive than the family and less altruistic then the pyramid of people, which was true in the case of Germany. But the manner in which Germany implemented its NII was not in line with what we proposed. For instance, we would have thought the government would have guided the development of applications to reap more economic profits than they did.

Note that the goals and objectives of various NIIs are not always similar. For instance, France's goal was to become a world leader in information technology, whereas Japan sees the NII as a tool to rectify current social ailments (e.g. to reduce air pollution and address the aging population).

The type of market structure employed may affect the probability that the NII will meet the objectives established by the government. Less governmental control of the market may mean that it is less likely the NII will meet the government's objectives. Countries that deploy more hierarchical structures to govern the various components of the NII, therefore, may be able to meet the national objectives of the system better than those that are more market-driven. Furthermore, the degree to which a government intends to use the NII to address societal issues may affect the amount of government control placed on the components.

Although there appears to be an association between culture and NII policies, it is by no means a conclusive relationship. Those countries that developed, or are developing, an NII that does not fit their culture may be less successful. This is certainly the case for Germany. Although the government continues to refine the systems it has put forward, it has not been able to meet its national goals and most observers conclude that it has been unsuccessful.

## 7. Discussion

Culture seems to play a significant role in the development of an NII. Countries that design NII policies that are appropriate for their culture are likely to be more successful in the creation of an NII. As countries look to create an NII, they would be wise to look towards culturally similar countries to learn what works. Mimicking the successful NII policies of a culturally dissimilar country may results in policies that are culturally inappropriate and doomed to limited success or failure.

Since NII policy is still in its infancy, it is not possible to gather adequate data for determining the level of success of the various NIIs. Furthermore, it is premature to determine whether the success of an NII is strongly correlated with its ability to match its culture. Further research is required to understand other issues that affect the development of an NII. Through our exposure to the various NIIs, we discovered other factors which affected their implementation and design. These are potential variables that should be considered in future research in this area.

## 7.1. Other explanations for NII development

## 7.1.1. Length of time NII is in place

Perhaps there is a cycle, or set of cycles, that a nation may progress through as its NII policies mature. Over time, an NII may evolve and take on different characteristics than originally intended. This evolution will have the most significant impact on countries with high uncertainty avoidance because they will become less uncertain and more comfortable with the NII once it is established and in use. This may be the reason Minitel no longer fits our predictions for France. Minitel, in its original form was tightly controlled by the French government, but as it evolved and France became more comfortable with its presence, the government relaxed its control over the network and more private industries became involved in it. It may also shed light on what may occur in Japan over time.

## 7.1.2. Date of NII implementation

If a country is a late adopter of an NII, it may face less uncertainty in that it is able to observe other countries' NIIs prior to implementing its own. Furthermore, the technologies available today are very different from those that were available ten years ago. As technologies continue to evolve, the types of NII that can be implemented are changing and options that are available today may not have been available yesterday. Some of these technologies may also provide features that will impact the possible permutations of an NII for the government to manipulate (i.e. better ways to monitor e-mail, web site access, use of particular newsgroups, improved security protocols for commercial uses of the NII, etc.). As these technologies become more commonplace, governments and the customers of the NII will have new tools to meet their goals (i.e. to protect their privacy).

## 7.1.3. Implementation method: leadership and funding

How a government decides to implement an NII may not reflect the true goals of the government's NII policies. If there is not strong leadership for an NII, it may fall short in what the government wishes to occur (e.g. the UK) or it is possible the NII will surpass the initial goals of the government when there is strong leadership (e.g. Singapore). As is the case with many technological endeavors, project leadership can have a significant impact on success.

Furthermore, without adequate funding the efforts of the sponsoring agency may fall short of its goals. This funding may not reflect the idealistic wishes of a sponsor but instead the economic realities of a nation. Without leadership and funding, the path of an NII may not be very predictable and may take turns that are not in line with the culture of a nation.

## 8. Epilogue

This is the first report of a longitudinal project to track the development of NIIs. Since many policies are in the early stages of development, interpretation should be made with caution. We expect to see considerable change over the next few years as we observe the rapid development of different policies in different cultures.
