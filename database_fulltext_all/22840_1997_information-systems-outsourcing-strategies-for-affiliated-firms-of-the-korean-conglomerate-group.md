# Information systems outsourcing strategies for affiliated firms of the Korean conglomerate groups

Jae-Nam Lee, Young-Gul Kim\*

Graduate School of Management, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology, 207-43 Cheongryang, Dongdaemoon, Seoul, 130-012, Korea

Received 20 June 1996; received in revised form 16 August 1997

Revised paper accepted for publication by Professor Marianne Broadbent

## Abstract

Information systems (IS) outsourcing has received little academic attention in the non-Western context. This paper reports on IS outsourcing strategies for the affiliated firms of the Korean conglomerate groups which possess their own IS subsidiaries. Aggregating the IS departments of the groups' firms into a separate IS division has been a major trend among the Korean conglomerate groups. The IS division is a separate corporation wholly owned by the parent group. The IS companies of the conglomerate groups occupy a major share of the Korean IS outsourcing market. This is due to the 'guaranteed' IS outsourcing contracts they secure from their groups' affiliated firms.

From the affiliated firms' perspective, however, this arrangement prevents them from selecting the best IS solution provider and potentially undermines their information technology (IT) strategy. Using a contingency model based on an organization's information intensity and group influence, this paper offers a set of outsourcing strategies for the affiliated firms of the conglomerate groups. Subsequently the model and the recommended strategies are verified through multiple case studies. Results from these case studies support our model in that user satisfaction is higher when the firm's current outsourcing strategy matches the recommended strategy. © 1997 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Information systems; IS outsourcing; Mid-sourcing; Outsourcing strategy

## 1. Introduction

Organizations need diverse and high quality information services to survive and excel in the rapidly changing business environment (Barney, 1991; Ives and

Learmonth, 1984; Willcocks et al., 1993). Frequently, we find that the future of an organization relies heavily on the quality of information services it is using. Attempts to achieve sustainable competitiveness through information systems (IS), however, puts much burden on organizations as the scope and complexity of IS expand (Attewell and Rule, 1984). Many organizations attempt to solve this problem by increasing their investment in hardware and human resources, only to face exploding IS expenditures and an inability to catch up with fast changing information technologies.

An organization's overarching objective in managing its information resources, then, should be to maximize flexibility and control in order to pursue different options as its circumstances change (Lacity et al., 1995). To accomplish this objective, more and more organizations look to IS outsourcing by external service providers rather than pursuing in-house development. In Korea, IS outsourcing is a relatively recent but rapidly growing business practice adopted primarily by the large, conglomerate-affiliated firms. The integration of the affiliated firms' IS departments into a subsidiary IS company has been a major trend among the Korean conglomerate groups. Despite the short outsourcing history and the lack of expertise to support customer requirements effectively, IS companies of the conglomerate groups occupy a major share of the Korean IS outsourcing market. This phenomenon results from the ‘guaranteed’ IS outsourcing contracts they secure from the separate firms in the group. From the affiliated firms' perspective, however, this arrangement prevents them from selecting the best IS solution provider and potentially undermines their information technology (IT)-based competition and opportunities.

The purpose of this research is to gain a deeper understanding of the outsourcing phenomenon in Korea. First, we focus on situations where the extant IS departments of the affiliated firms have been integrated into a separate IS company, after which the affiliated firms outsource their IS operations to that firm. In particular, we develop a contingency model of IS outsourcing strategies for the affiliated firms of the Korean conglomerate groups, based on each firm's information intensity and group influence. Secondly, we focus on the differences between the Korean approach and many Western schemes. The result is a set of insights regarding current assumptions and conditions for outsourcing in Korea.

We then verify the validity of the proposed contingency model and the recommended strategies by analyzing case studies of outsourcing among Korean firms. The results of this research may be applicable not only to Korean conglomerate firms, but also to any firm which has a special relationship with a specific IS outsourcing company. An example lies in the USA in the former connection between General Motors and EDS. Other classes of examples relate to outsourcing environments similar to that in Korea, as illustrated by Finland (Reponen, 1993).

The paper is organized into seven sections. Section 2 reviews the current outsourcing environment in Korea. Then two decision making models are described: unconstrained outsourcing and restrained outsourcing in the case of the 'in-house' IS firm. Section 3 introduces the research framework and contingency model of IS outsourcing for the affiliated firms of the conglomerate groups. In Section 4, the research method is presented. Section 5 reports the results of the case studies. Section 6 discusses the

Korean outsourcing phenomenon based on the case studies, while Section 7 concludes with the implication of the results, limitations of the research, and future directions.

## 2. Research background

Until recently, research on IS outsourcing have been conducted primarily in the US and the UK: Loh and Venkatraman (1992; 1995), Grover et al. (1994), Grover and Teng (1993) and Lacity and Hirschhein (1993) in the US; Fitzgerald and Willcocks (1994), Willcocks et al. (1993; 1995), Cross (1995), and Pinnington and Woolcock (1995) in the UK. These research endeavors necessarily reflect the business environments and organizational cultures of anglo-american firms. However, outsourcing, like many other facets of management, has different features and characteristics depending on particular the business environment and organizational culture.

In Korea, IS outsourcing is a relatively new but fast-growing phenomenon. Until the mid 1980s, Korean firms outsourced only specific applications development projects. They were reluctant to consider large-scale IS outsourcing projects due to the potential exposure of confidential information and lack of reliable IS service providers. In the late 1980s, however, the conglomerate groups in Korea began to integrate the IS departments of their affiliated firms into an in-house IS company. Major examples are found in LG-EDS Systems of the LG group, Samsung Data Systems (SDS) of the Samsung group, and Hyundai Information Technology (HIT) of the Hyundai group. This trend suggests first of all that the large groups in Korea recognized the importance of the IS service business; the strategy also indicates a perception that the quality of IS service will improve with the economies of scale resulting from such integration. In this way, the affiliated firms of the large groups in Korea tended to outsource their entire IS operations to their in-house IS company.

In other words, the group IS company operates as a monopoly provider of IS services to the affiliated firms without any competitive pressure from external IS firms. In general, monopolies exist due to barriers which prevent other firms from entering the market. These barriers consist of three types: legal barriers, as in the case where a firm has a patent that prevents other firms from entering; sociological barriers, where entry is prevented by the invisible handshake; and natural barriers, where the firm has a unique ability to produce what other firms cannot duplicate (Colander, 1995). In the Korean outsourcing market, the close relationship between the conglomerate group and its affiliated firms acts as a sociological barrier.

As of 1995, the IS companies of the conglomerate groups occupied about 80% of the Korean IS outsourcing market and continued to expand their IS services beyond their group boundaries into other private and public sector organizations (Park et al., 1996). While the rapid growth of the group IS company may support the group's IS strategy, the affiliated firms of the conglomerate groups tend to suffer from their lack of choice in IS solutions and the poor quality of service provided to 'captive' customers. This unique outsourcing pattern in Korea raises the following questions: why has this been done; has the integration of the affiliated firms' IS departments into a single IS company been successful, and is the symbiotic relationship between the affiliated firms and the group IS company sustainable over time?

In this paper, the term ‘IT’ refers to a set of hardware, software, and networks used for creating, storing, and transmitting information. On the other hand, ‘IS’ denotes specific organizational applications designed to satisfy the information needs of decision makers. For conceptual understanding, we define and use the term ‘IS outsourcing’ as ‘the process of commissioning part or all of an organization’s IS functions — planning, development, and management — to external service providers’. IS outsourcing is an umbrella term which covers many information services such as transaction processing, facility management, systems integration, etc. (Apte, 1990). For the remainder of the paper, the term ‘outsourcing’ will be used in lieu of ‘IS outsourcing’, except in cases leading to ambiguity.

## 2.1. The process model of outsourcing decision making: general

The motivation for outsourcing has been explained in terms of three types of expected benefits: strategic; economic; and technological (Loh and Venkatraman, 1991; McFarlan and Nolan, 1995; Grover et al., 1996). First, strategic benefits refer to the ability of a firm to focus on its core business by outsourcing routine IS activities (Schiffman and Loftin, 1991). Second, economic benefits accrue when a firm can utilize expertise and economies of scale in human and technological resources of the service provider and manage its cost structure through clear contractual arrangements (Huff, 1991; Apte, 1990; Schiffman and Loftin, 1991). Third, technological benefits refer to the ability of a firm to gain access to leading-edge IT without the risk of technological obsolescence that results from dynamic changes in information technologies (Child, 1987; Apte, 1990).

Because of these perceived outsourcing benefits, many organizations in the 1990s look to outsourcing by an external service provider rather than pursuing the traditional strategy of in-house development. Even so, there have been few papers that illustrate the general process of outsourcing (Rockart and Scott Morton, 1984), despite the numerous studies on specific areas of outsourcing such as bidding, contract management, and package selection (Buchowicz, 1991; Chaudhury et al., 1995; Venkatensan, 1992). Fig. 1 illustrates the process model of outsourcing decision making. The various stages in the model are recommended, not required, for firms considering outsourcing. While the model does not cover all outsourcing situations, it provides a rational model for making outsourcing decisions. Such a model may also provide insight on the difference between the general model of outsourcing decision making and the model for the Korean context (Fig. 2).

First, a company decides whether to outsource or not, considering its internal IT environment and the external service provider's environment. In this phase, the company identifies the risks and benefits of outsourcing (Loh, 1994; Loh and Venkatraman, 1995; Earl, 1996), while carrying out an internal assessment of the existing IS resources and capabilities (Cheon et al., 1995; Clermont, 1991).

Second, if the company decides to outsource, the outsourcing strategy must be selected. Outsourcing details must be decided in this phase, such as the degree of outsourcing — total or selective outsourcing (Lacity et al., 1996; Lacity and Hirschheim, 1993), the period of outsourcing — long term or short term (Pinnington and Woolcock, 1995), the number of service providers — single or multiple (Willcocks et al., 1995), and the type of outsourcing — service or asset outsourcing.

Third, the company can select several service providers as candidates for outsourcing and evaluate their proposals. To select an appropriate set of service provider(s), the outsourcing firm must have full understanding of its internal IS resources and the service provider's capability (Clermont, 1991).

Fourth, after evaluating the service providers, the company selects the best service provider(s) for outsourcing. Here, the company should not only consider its strategic business objectives but also prepare for the detailed strategy of contract negotiation in the next phase.

Fifth, once the service provider(s) is selected, the process of defining the dynamics of the outsourcing relationship begins in the contract negotiation phase (Richmond et al., 1992). According to Fitzgerald and Willcocks (1994), there are three outsourcing contract patterns that client companies employ to overcome problems with service providers: the first is the involvement of both internal and external legal expertise in the contract negotiation, the second is to enter only short term contracts, and the third is the establishment of partnership relationships based on trust between the customer and service provider, as in the USAA–IBM partnership or the Kodak–IBM partnership (Fitzgerald and Willcocks, 1994; Grover et al., 1996; Lasher et al., 1991).

Sixth, the service provider implements the outsourced services after the contract negotiation is over. To manage the implementation process more smoothly and solve any unexpected problems, the customer and service provider usually set up an interorganizational coordination unit (Lasher et al., 1991; Henderson, 1990).

Seventh, a number of activities are required to ensure effective and successful outsourcing: contract management including liaison with users; control of service level; performance monitoring; and contract administration. In this phase, the outcome of the outsourcing decision is measured against contract clauses such as the service level, performance goals, etc. It is common to measure the outcome in terms of user information satisfaction as well as financial or technical performance (Nam et al., 1996; Grover et al., 1996).

Finally, the measured performance in terms of business and user perspectives are applied iteratively to each phase of the feedback process. We consider it a major input into the contract renewal decision or to the change of outsourcing strategy, such as from total outsourcing to selective outsourcing or from a long term to a short term arrangement. Depending on the results of the outsourcing, insourcing — which takes outsourced IS functions back into the customer organizations — may be considered (Pinnington and Woolcock, 1995; Reponen, 1993; Willcocks et al., 1995).

## 2.2. The process model of outsourcing decision making: Korean context

Fig. 2 represents the process model of outsourcing decision making for the affiliated firms of the conglomerate groups in Korea. We can note a key difference between the Korean model and the general model of Fig. 1. The affiliated firms of the conglomerate groups do not have a choice in outsourcing decision, nor in the evaluation and selection of the service provider. Since the group decided to integrate the affiliated firms' IS departments into a group IS company, affiliated firms must outsource their IS functions to the group IS company by default, and do not have much control over outsourcing contract negotiation, implementation, or recontract decisions.

The main reason for the difference between the two models is that the Korean conglomerate groups were very reluctant to expose business information to external companies. Therefore, most conglomerate groups in Korea established their own group IS companies. These group IS companies differ from the independent IS service providers in the western countries in that they provide IS services mostly to the affiliated firms of the same group, and focus on generic systems management services rather than create unique and innovative IS solutions tailored to each customer.

This phenomenon can be viewed from both the affiliated firms and the conglomerate groups' perspectives. Affiliated firms may interpret it as 'outsourcing' because all their IS functions are transferred to the group IS company, while the group may consider it 'insourcing' because the group IS company manages all IS functions of the affiliated firms. Consequently, outsourcing of the affiliated firms of the Korean conglomerate groups belongs somewhere between pure outsourcing and insourcing, perhaps fitting the term, 'mid-sourcing'.

As the group IS companies begin to expand their IS services beyond their group boundaries into other private and public sector organizations, however, the symbiotic relationship between the affiliated firms and their group IS companies has begun to falter. Affiliated firms perceive that, as ‘captive’ customers, they are not treated by the group IS company as ‘real’ customers, like the external clients. Hence, while the rapid growth of the group IS company may be conducive to the group’s IS direction, some of the disgruntled affiliated firms are now seeking ways to acquire more voice in their choice of outsourcing.

## 3. Research framework and model

## 3.1. Research framework

Effective strategies are those that achieve a fit between the environmental conditions and organizational factors (Drazin and Van de Ven, 1985). When companies decide to outsource their IS operations to external service providers, they need to consider many factors related to their business environment and organizational characteristics (Teng et al., 1995). These factors can be broadly divided into two classes: firm-specific factors such as organizational resources, organizational structure, financial status, information requirements, IS capability, IS policy, etc. (Arnett and Jones, 1994; Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978; Lacity and Hirschhein, 1993; Pinnington and Woolcock, 1995; Reponen, 1993); and service provider-specific factors such as breadth and depth of technical skills, industry experience, reputation, availability of trained manpower, hardware and software, etc. (Chaudhury et al., 1995; Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978; Lacity and Hirschhein, 1993). Firm-specific factors are reflected in the firm's outsourcing decision and outsourcing strategy selection process while service provider-specific factors are mostly examined during the evaluation and selection process of the service provider.

Affiliated firms of the conglomerate groups, for instance, assess the influence of their group as well as their own information requirements and information processing capability. The outsourcing strategy for the affiliated firms of a groups can be classified into three categories: the default strategy that designates the group IS company as an outsourcing service provider; the limited strategy where the group IS company and external IS companies work together; and the free strategy where the best outsourcing service provider — internal or external — is selected. Since the goal of this research was to identify the best outsourcing strategy for the affiliated firms of the Korean conglomerate groups, we decided to examine the organization's information intensity and the group influence as two potential determinants of the organization's outsourcing strategy using a contingency model. To assess the effect of fit between the firm's current outsourcing strategy and the recommended outsourcing strategy, we will measure outsourcing performance through the user information satisfaction construct (Bailey and Pearson, 1983; Baroudi et al., 1986; Ives et al., 1983) indicated in Fig. 3.

## 3.1.1. Organization's information intensity

Many researchers have used the term information intensity, but there seems to be no consensus on what it means (Busch et al., 1991; Glazer, 1993). Some have defined it as the amount of intellectual work done by people as they conduct their work (Sabherwal and King, 1991), while others equate it with the information content of the product or services provided and of the firm's value chain associated with producing those products or services (Porter and Millar, 1985). In this paper, the concept of information intensity is defined as the combined level of business information requirements and organizational IS capability. According to this definition, firms with an advanced IS capability for dealing with diverse and dynamic information requirements will have a high information intensity.

The following factors are introduced to measure information intensity on the information requirement dimension.

3.1.1.1. Dependency on information systems. We define the dependency on IS as the combination of the breadth and depth of daily IS usage and the expected degree of damage on the business if IS service is unavailable. The higher the firm's dependency on information systems, the higher will be its information intensity (Porter and Millar, 1985; Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993).

3.1.1.2. Industry Environment. There are two extremes in the competitive environment of an organization, static and dynamic, depending on its changing circumstances (Blanton et al., 1992; Zmud, 1984). A static industry is not particularly influenced by the changes in the external environment, while a dynamic industry is sensitive to environmental change. Thus, companies in a more dynamic environment are more dependent on information technology for dealing with environmental opportunities and threats (Blanton et al., 1992; Porter and Millar, 1985); consequently, the organization's information intensity must be higher to appropriately respond to this dynamic environment.

3.1.1.3. Organization type. An organization's information intensity is affected by its type. Formal organizations have general rules and standard procedures to effectively perform business activities while informal organizations have very low standardization of business procedures. This leads to higher information intensity because standardization and formalization facilitate the implementation of information systems (Kim and Lee, 1991; Olson and Chervany, 1981). So, formal organizations tend to have higher information intensity than informal organizations.

The IS capability of the firm is measured by four variables: distribution of information systems; maturity of information systems; IS understanding by the end user; and IS investment.

3.1.1.4. Distribution of information systems. This dimension indicates whether information systems are centralized or distributed. Centralization means centralized business authority and responsibility while distribution is accompanied by delegating authority and responsibility to lower level units (Dearden, 1987; Tavakolian, 1989). Information systems with more distributed IS capability tend to be more sophisticated than those with more centralized IS functions (Kim and Lee, 1991). Consequently, if the degree of distribution is high, we expect that the organization's information intensity will be high (Kwon and Zmud, 1987).

3.1.1.5. Maturity of information systems. This factor represents the maturity of technology and IS people in an organization. The adoption of a new technology and system in an organization will be easier if the organization's maturity of information systems is high (Raymond, 1985; 1990). Therefore, organizations with mature information systems display more flexibility towards changing circumstances (Grover and Teng, 1993), and the information intensity of these organizations is expected to be high.

3.1.1.6. IS understanding by the end user. Even if the maturity of an information system is high, the effectiveness of an IS can be low if the end users do not understand the newly adopted technology or system. The adaptation of new technology by end users implies that they have the ability to understand and manage the IS introduced. A new IS will have a greater chance of succeeding when it is used by more competent users (Kim and Lee, 1991; Raymond, 1985; 1990). Thus, the higher the end user's understanding of the information technology and system, the higher will be the organization's information intensity.

3.1.1.7. IS investment. We can also identify the information intensity of an organization by the firm's level of IS investment. Pursuing the right IS strategy can be a key factor in sustaining corporate prosperity, leading to more effective and efficient use of IS resources and to the improvement of IS capability (Bacon, 1992; Ives and Learmonth, 1984). Therefore, an organization with a high IS investment ratio will likely have higher information intensity.

## 3.1.2. Group influence

In the outsourcing context, the influence of the group is defined as the level of central control or involvement in IS operations of the affiliated firms. That is, it represents the degree of group influence between the conglomerate groups and its affiliated firms, and also shows the strategic direction of IT in the conglomerate group. We can recognize the degree of group influence to affiliated firms through four factors: the affiliated firm's position in the conglomerate group; the existence of a group IT infrastructure; the group's support of the group IS company; and the existence of a positive or negative relationship between the affiliated firm and the group IS company.

3.1.2.1. Position in the conglomerate group. An affiliated firm's position within the conglomerate group is based on many factors such as its relative rank in total sales, its total profits, the power of its chief executives, and its contribution to the group, etc. An affiliated firm might manage business operations, including IS structures, relatively independent of central interference or control if the affiliated firm's position is strong. Accordingly, a company with a strong position in the conglomerate group will experience low group influence.

3.1.2.2. Existence of group IT infrastructure. Does a conglomerate group have or plan to build its group-level IT infrastructure? If the group has such an IT infrastructure and requires its affiliated firms to comply with it, the affiliated firms' IS operations will be constrained by the group's infrastructure. Without such infrastructure, affiliated firms may establish and manage their IS operations rather independently. As a result, group influence is higher if a strong group IT infrastructure exists and is enforced on the affiliated firms.

3.1.2.3. Group's support of the group IS company. We can also estimate central influence by the group's level of support to its group IS company. If the group strategically supports the group IS company, the affiliated firms may have to select the group IS company as the default outsourcing alternative. Consequently, if the group formally and explicitly supports its IS company, then the affiliated firms will encounter higher group influence.

3.1.2.4. Relationship between the group IS company and affiliated firms. Group influence is affected by the nature of the relationship between the affiliated firm and the group IS company. In a positive relationship, the affiliated firms would consider the group IS company prior to considering the external service providers for outsourcing services. In contrast, in the negative relationship, they would try to avoid the group IS company as much as possible. Therefore group influence is higher in a positive relationship between the group IS company and the affiliated firm than in the opposite case.

## 3.2. Contingency model for outsourcing

Many affiliated firms have outsourced their IS operations without fully considering the available alternatives and strategies. They simply followed the group's outsourcing strategy without stressing their unique organizational characteristics. To ensure more successful outsourcing, however, we need to analyze the affiliated firm's information processing requirements and capability as well as its group influence. Hence, this study proposes a contingency model for outsourcing strategy selection based on an organization's information intensity and group influence (Fig. 4). By considering each firm's situation, we recommend one of three outsourcing strategies; default, limited; and free competition.

When the organization's information requirement is simple and its IS capability is limited, the firm may find it difficult to make an outsourcing decision by itself. ## 3.2.2. Limited competition strategy (employ group and external IS companies)

In some circumstances, information systems are outsourced to both the inhouse IS company and to external service providers. As seen in Fig. 4, a strategy of limited competition is recommended in two situations. In the first situation, the organization's information intensity and group influence are low; the affiliated firm will have difficulty in creating an IT strategy by itself but will not be severely affected in this context by the group since the group influence is low. For this type of situation, the tendency of asset outsourcing is frequently observed. It is difficult, however, to simultaneously control both the group IS company and the external service providers. Second, when the organization's information intensity and group influence are both high, the affiliated firm has strong IS capability but must comply with the group's IT directions and strategies. Due to the strong group influence, it must maintain some links to the group IS company. Here we find a strong tendency to outsource services without any asset transfer. This is because the strong group influence discourages the asset transfer to the external service providers.

The strategy of limited competition makes it easier for the affiliated firm to access and learn the latest technology and split the outsourcing cost and risk. For the conglomerate group, it increases the challenge for the group IS company. This scheme often yields coordination problems among the involved companies — the affiliated firm, the group IS company, and the external service providers. A drawback of this strategy lies in an increase in dependency on external service providers (Table 1).

## 3.2.3. Free competition strategy (use the best)

This strategy is likely to become the main outsourcing strategy for the affiliated firms in the future. It is recommended when the organization's information intensity is high but group influence is low. With high information intensity and low group influence, affiliated firms have a wide range of choices in determining what and how to outsource as well as by whom. If they find an appropriate external service provider, they can employ the firm without worrying about the group or the group IS company. We find both asset and service outsourcing occurring under this strategy.

Advantages of this strategy include: easy access to leading-edge technology; faster development cycle; cost reduction; high quality service for the affiliated firm; and high quality of group IT infrastructure and increased competitiveness for the independent IS company. Disadvantages are: incompatibility with the group IT infrastructure standards; jeopardy of information safety; and potential clash of cultures for the conglomerate group. Other liabilities include a loss of control and lack of flexibility for the affiliated firm (Table 1).

## 4. Research method

To gain further understanding of the outsourcing phenomenon in Korea, eleven affiliated firms were selected as case studies. These firms belong to six of the conglomerate groups which have their own IS companies. Here, we describe how the data was collected and analyzed, and then present some conclusions.

First, we conducted a series of personal interviews with six IS professionals, using open-ended questions, to validate the proposed research framework. These interviews were concluded when there were no further substantive comments for the framework. The interviews confirmed that our proposed framework and the contingency model were suitable in the Korean outsourcing context. Second, we developed a questionnaire based on the outsourcing literature and the authors' previous experience in the Korean outsourcing industry as well as our research framework and the comments gathered from the interviews. Since one of the authors has worked for a large Korean group IS company for over five years, we were able to grasp an in-depth understanding of outsourcing practices in Korea. Third, we interviewed three to ten individuals in each affiliated firm who were representatives or senior executives in charge of the firm's IS operations to collect data on each organization's information intensity and group influence. We also interviewed end-users for user satisfaction regarding the outsourced information systems. The interviews using the prepared questionnaire were semi-structured and quite detailed. The interviews were held at each affiliated firm and were conducted for two to three hours per session. During the interviews, we solicited and recorded each firm's current outsourcing strategies, information intensity, group influence, and user satisfaction as shown in Table 2, using a five-point Likert scale (see Appendix A). In addition to the personal interviews, we collected and analyzed each firm's relevant documentation of outsourcing such as the list of current IS projects, copies of outsourcing contracts, the list of current hardware and software, and so on. Current outsourcing strategies for each affiliated firm, in case several were employed, were estimated according to the proportion of the outsourcing projects implemented under each strategy.

## 5.1.2. Samsung group

Samsung Data Systems (SDS), the group IS company of the Samsung group, ranked first in Korea in 1995, with annual revenues of 708 million US dollars and a yearly growth rate of $43.8\%$ . It was founded in 1985 as a joint venture with IBM Korea. The Samsung group began to integrate the existing IS departments of its affiliated firms from 1991 to 1992 after the LG group led the way. In 1993, SDS dissociated itself from its partner with IBM to provide its customers diverse open solutions without being constrained by IBM's proprietary services and products.

5.1.2.1. Case E: SS-1. Located at cell (1,2) of Fig. 5, this electronics company, founded in 1969, had annual revenues of 14 billion US dollars and over 50 000 employees as of 1995. The level of user satisfaction is not good perhaps because the current outsourcing strategy — limited competition — does not match our recommended strategy of free competition. This firm, however, has recently been attempting to select an external service provider who can provide the best IS solution.

5.1.2.2. Case F: SS-2. It has high user satisfaction under the limited competition strategy, which coincides with our recommended strategy. User satisfaction will be higher when this strategy is adopted more extensively.

## 5.1.3. Hyundai group

Hyundai Information Technology (HIT) was founded in 1993 as a group IS company of the Hyundai group to integrate the isolated firm-level networks into the group-level centralized network. HIT was formed from more than 40 IS departments, employs more than 2000, and had a capital base of 32 million US dollars as of 1995. The integration of IS departments started relatively late compared to other groups. Hence they are investing 10% of total revenues on human resources and new technologies to be more competitive both inside and outside the group. However, inter-organizational problems and resistance currently exist among some of its primary affiliated firms.

5.1.3.1. Case G: HD-1. This automobile company with annual revenues of 11.6 billion US dollars in 1995 had the highest information intensity among the surveyed companies, and did not take part in the group's IS department integration process. It is proud of its IS department with its reliable human resources and deployed technologies, standing ready to respond to the external changes. This company tends to be largely free of group pressures because of its high information intensity and low group influence. Consequently, it picks the best solution provider among any service provider, including the group IS company. In spite of the friction with the overall group during the inter-departmental integration process, user satisfaction for IS is acceptable due to the match between the current and recommended outsourcing strategy (free competition). However, to enhance user satisfaction, the company should retain its current strategy and clarify the uncertain relationships between itself and other affiliated firms in the group.

5.1.3.2. Case H: HD-2. This affiliated firm in the heavy industry was founded in 1973 and had annual revenues of 4 billion US dollars in 1995. At this company, group influence is low because of its high position in the group, and information intensity is high. Most information systems are supported by external service providers while some routine activities are internally served by its IS company. User satisfaction is moderately high, as seen at cell (1,2) of Fig. 5 where current and recommended strategies (free competition) match.

## 5.1.4. Daewoo Group

1993 to early 1995, involving 22 affiliated firms. The competitiveness of DIS is hard to measure despite its solid record — annual revenues of 111 million US dollars in 1995 and eighth among Korean IS service providers — for most of its revenue was generated almost exclusively within the parent group.

5.1.4.1. Case I: DW-1. Information intensity and group influence for this affiliated firm are high. The main products of this heavy-industry company are internal combustion engines, construction machinery, ships, etc. As shown in Table 4, the default strategy was used as the current outsourcing strategy, while the limited competition strategy is recommended by our contingency model. Nevertheless, user satisfaction is acceptable because DIS, unlike other group IS companies, concentrated solely on serving its internal group customers. In other words, it enjoys high group influence despite its high position and total sales in the conglomerate group. However, this company may have to change its current outsourcing strategy to limited competition in order to improve user satisfaction while maintaining a balanced relationship with its group IS company.

## 5.1.5. Dusan group

This group made a decision to integrate the internal IS departments of its affiliated firms in order to enter the outsourcing business in 1994. While a limited competition strategy is recommended for this company, the default strategy was selected for outsourcing, which obviously led to significantly low user satisfaction.

## 5.1.6. Hanjin group

The Hanjin group owns Korea's largest airline and shipping businesses. This group established the Hanjin Information System and Telecommunication (HIST) company in 1989. HIST later merged with Hanjin Data Communications, which provided airline reservation and information network services. As of 1995, HIST had 640 employees and ranked ninth among Korea's IS service providers with annual revenues of 95 million US dollars. But most sales are to its affiliated firms, and HIST should increase outside sales to be more competitive.

5.1.6.1. Case K: HJ-1. The holding company of the group was founded in 1945 and had annual revenues of 328 million US dollars with about 3500 employees as of 1995. This company, with a very high level of information intensity, is outsourcing its entire IS to HIST because of the high group influence. The employees of the firm complain about the quality of the information services provided, but it is not easy to hire an external service provider due to the pressure from the group. In spite of this situation, this company is trying to find and evaluate external outsourcing service providers to best satisfy its information needs and to increase the low level of user satisfaction.

## 5.2. Summary of case studies

We have described the IS outsourcing status and strategies of eleven affiliated firms. Most affiliated firms of the conglomerate groups in Korea outsource their IS operations to their group IS company by default. Among them, however, affiliated firms with strong group positions or with high information intensity levels employed external service providers without worrying about group interference or the group IS company. In addition, even the affiliated firms with weak group positions or low information intensity levels were attempting to pick the best service providers for their information needs. As a result, it appears that there will be an increasing trend toward adopting a strategy of free competition for outsourcing.

Fig. 5 shows the relative positioning of the eleven firms in the two dimensional outsourcing contingency model according to the organization's information intensity and group influence. The open circles denote firms' positions, enclosing the character 'L' for a limited strategy, 'F' for the free strategy, and 'D' for a default strategy. Each label represents the current outsourcing strategy of each firm. The circles and labels highlight the match between the current and recommended strategies for the firms.

Based on the match between the current and the recommended strategies, we split the eleven affiliated firms into two subgroups as shown in Table 5, then compared the average values of user satisfaction using t-statistics. A comparison of user satisfaction between two subgroups resulted in a t-value of 2.40 (P < 0.05). This result shows that the affiliated firms whose current and recommended strategies match tend to have more satisfied end users compared to the firms without matches.

There were no organizations located at cell (1,1) in the contingency model where organization's information intensity and group influence are both low. Either we were unable to find such companies in our study or — more likely — most of the large, independent organizations tend to have high levels of information intensity.

## 6. Discussion

As of 1995, there were 132 service providers in the Korean outsourcing market, while the total market volume was over 2 trillion US dollars with an annual growth rate of 37.5% (Kum, 1996). The IS companies of the conglomerate groups occupied about 80% of the Korean outsourcing market share and continued to expand their market (Park et al., 1996). Although the present research concentrated on outsourcing strategies for the affiliated firms of the Korean conglomerate groups, it provides a deeper understanding not only of the current conditions, characteristics, and assumptions regarding outsourcing in Korea, but also of future trends. We can summarize the outsourcing phenomenon based on multiple case studies as follows.

In the process of integrating the affiliated firms' IS departments into the group IS companies, some affiliated firms fought against such integration as in the case of HD-1, HD-2 and DW-1. Several reasons may be offered regarding the ability of these firms to resist their groups' central authority. First, the within-group positions of these affiliated firms were very strong; second, since the information intensity of these affiliated firms was very high, they had developed capable IS departments with reliable resources and technologies; and finally they were concerned about the loss of technical expertise accumulated over the years due to the integration of their IS departments into the group IS company. Therefore, most of the conglomerate groups in Korea — such as the LG group, the Hyundai group, and the Daewoo group — failed to integrate all their affiliated firms' IS departments into their group IS companies. The relationship between the conglomerate groups and the affiliated firms that did not take part in the group's IT integration is still strained.

In the future, we predict that there will be increasing momentum for the free competition strategy for outsourcing. On the one hand, the affiliated firms become more eager to select the best service provider and to be treated as real customers, as in cases of LG-1, LG-2, SS-1, HD-2 and HJ-1. On the other hand, the group IS companies of the conglomerate groups try to expand their IS services beyond their group boundaries so that they can reduce their dependence on the ‘guaranteed’ affiliate firms and become globally competitive IS solution providers.

We can also find evidence of this trend from the advertising industry in Korea. Most conglomerate groups in Korea have their own advertising agencies such as Cheil Communication of the Samsung group, LG-AD of the LG group, Oricom of the Dusan group, and so on. Every affiliated firm had to hand over its entire advertisement business to the group advertising agency as a default policy, as with the groups' outsourcing arrangement for IT. However, this practice in the advertising industry is rapidly changing from the default strategy to the free competition strategy as the industry matures. The affiliated firms of many conglomerate groups in Korea now can select the best advertising agency for their needs without their parent group's interference.

Similarly, the outsourcing industry in Korea will also change from group-by-group segmentation to open competition based on ability in the near future. Each firm, not its parent, will decide where and how to outsource its IS functions.

## 7. Conclusions

This paper reports on a research study on outsourcing practices for information systems in Korea, where conglomerate groups had formed group IS companies from their affiliated firms' extant IS departments. The main focus of this research is to propose the best outsourcing strategy for affiliated firms of the conglomerate groups using a contingency model. The results support our model in that user satisfaction is higher when an affiliated firm's actual outsourcing strategy and the recommended strategy match. It indicates that affiliated firms should consider not only the group's influence, but also the organization's information intensity to select the best IS solution provider. Currently, it is difficult for most affiliated firms of the conglomerate groups to freely choose their outsourcing strategy, ignoring the group's influence. This research, however, proposes the strategic directions to help the affiliated firms in planning and implementing its future IS outsourcing projects.

The empirical findings of this study need to be further validated due to the following limitations. First, the results from the multiple cases are too exploratory to be generalized: the sample was relatively small. Second, the organizational environment was analyzed along only two dimensions. A more sophisticated model requires additional dimensions covering the organizational environment. Third, both the information intensity and group influence constructs were measured without a rigorous test of their validity and reliability; this should be rectified in the future. Future research issues include the validation of the current measurement instruments, a generalization of the recommended model to ordinary firms unlinked to conglomerate groups, and an evaluation of the outsourcing process and strategy from the service provider's perspective.
