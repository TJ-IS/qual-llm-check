# A classification of success factors for decision support systems

Paul N. Finlay\*, Morteza Forghani

Loughborough University Business School, Ashby Road, Loughborough, Leicestershire LE11 3TU, UK

Received 3 February 1997; accepted 6 February 1998

## Abstract

Whilst considerable experience in the design and development of decision support systems (DSS) has been accumulated, the expected benefits from these systems are often not realized. To help correct this deficiency the knowledge about the factors for success in consultant-led DSS development has been structured within a classification that has been motivated by three major considerations: the needed alignment between the DSS and the business requirements; the wide range of actors who play a role, both in the development of the DSS and in its continued use; and the two phases to DSS development whereby an appropriate environment for development is ensured and the commitment of the major actors is maintained as the required DSS is evolved. Field research was carried out to develop and validate the classification. The implementation of 39 DSS was reviewed in 27 organizations that together were using 22 DSS generators. 131 factors were verified in the field research as impacting on the successful implementation of DSS. A second field research investigated the importance of these factors in one specific situation, and demonstrated the use of the classification in providing guidance to practitioners operating in similar contexts. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Decision support system; project selection; IS development; IS implementation

## 1. A classification of success factors for decision support systems

## 1.1. Introduction

A decision support system (DSS) is a computer information system (IS) that provides information in a given domain of application by means of analytical decision models and access to databases, in order to support a decision maker in making decisions effectively in complex and ill-structured (non-programmable) tasks (Klein and Methlie, 1990). Whilst the number of DSS has grown at an accelerating rate during the last decade, the expected benefits are frequently unrealized (Alavi and Joachimsthaler, 1992). Meta-analysis has identified some of the significant factors for success in DSS implementation (Alavi and Joachimsthaler, 1992) but the overall picture regarding the most appropriate development route for DSS remains unclear. Decision performance studies are unlikely to have assessed all of the potentially relevant factors that determine DSS impact (Todd and Benbasat, 1992).

A major problem in determining DSS success factors has been the difficulty in establishing appropriate DSS success measures. Many measures were suggested (examples from many are: profitability (Garrity, 1963); widespread use (Swanson, 1974); better communication, better control, cost savings, time savings, better teamwork, response to 'the new' (Keen, 1981), repeat use, and time taken in decision making (Nunamaker et al., 1989)). No set of success measures has found universal acceptance. Although not sufficient in themselves, two measures would seem to set necessary conditions for a DSS to be considered a success. Since DSS use is generally not mandatory, the first of these is whether the system is indeed used (Swanson, 1974; Nunamaker et al., 1989). The second is user-satisfaction (see, for example, Kim, 1989). Given that there are no universally accepted success measures, the approach taken in this study is to accept the two generally agreed necessary conditions for DSS success and to seek the factors that lead to their achievement: thus success is being equated with repeat use and user-satisfaction.

Researchers do not yet understand sufficiently well the interaction between success factors and context to allow theory to be built. This lack of clarity hinges chiefly on the relative importance of the success factors: it is likely that most factors play some part in most DSS development success but their importance is contingent on the context. Whilst we see the overall purpose of DSS research is to devise an empirical-theoretical system—a system that is able to predict what the appropriate course of action might be in a specified situation (Denzin, 1970, quoting Parsons and Shils, 1959)—our work does not attempt to achieve such an ambitious goal except in one specific context; it is merely a step towards it. The aim of the research reported in this article is to establish a classification $^{1}$ that encapsulates present knowledge of the factors that lead to success in external consultant-led DSS applications and is capable of acting as a repository for new knowledge as this is developed. The restrictions in this study to DSS and specifically to those developed with the aid of consultants external to the organization arose for the following two reasons. First, there is evidence from the literature that the form and value of user participation is dependent on system type, and specifically that DSS are different from other forms of information system (IS) in these regards (Garrity, 1994). Second, there has been a large increase in the use of outsourcing as an avenue for building IS of all kinds (Booker, 1994 and Willcocks and Fitzgerald, 1994) and it seemed apposite to focus on this increasingly important activity.

The research stance we take has been labelled ‘internal realism’ (Archer, 1988). This is a position intermediate between the positivist and interpretivist approaches where it is accepted that facts and values are intertwined and that ‘reality-for-us’ is a construction of the shared human cognitive apparatus. This position allows us to tend towards a positivist approach in some instances and an interpretivist one in others. In particular, we have taken a mainly positivist stance in deriving the major facets of our classification and in our testing of the full classification that we constructed from the literature and our own experiences: we have taken a mainly interpretivist stance when adding facets to the classification and a mixed stance when establishing the importance of success factors in the one particular context that we investigated in detail.

The article is structured into four main sections. Initially, the theoretical support for the classification is developed leading to the structure of the classification itself. The initial set of success factors identified from the literature and our own experiences are then placed in this classification. In the third section the first field research is discussed and the classification is refined as a result. In the fourth section the application of the classification to one particular situational context through a second field research is described. Summary and conclusions then follow.

## 2. The theoretical support for the classification

## 2.1. Approaches to DSS development

Organizational change theory supports the interpretation that there are two phases associated with a realized DSS. It has been argued that the change process model (Lewin, 1947; Schein, 1961) offers a basis for developing and implementing computer-based IS (Ginzberg, 1978; Keen and Scott Morton, 1978). The model involves three stages: unfreezing, moving and refreezing. Unfreezing involves establishing the necessary conditions for change. Many of the situational factors, such as top management support or client-felt need are evaluated and manipulated, if necessary, during the unfreezing process. Moving involves design, development and implementation. Finally, the change model explicitly confronts the problems of institutionalizing (refreezing) the new situation: in the case of DSS this is ensuring repeat use and user-satisfaction. As will be described in more detail later, unfreezing is one fairly distinct phase in DSS development (ensuring the appropriate environment) with moving and refreezing intertwined in a second phase (maintaining commitment).

In the initial phase the idea for the proposed DSS is introduced, its value to the organization is assessed and support is gained for the associated resource spend: in effect this is following the alignment model whereby a major emphasis on information systems (IS) planning is the alignment of IS with business goals (Scott Morton, 1991). The decision makers in the organization are answering the question: Is the proposed DSS perceived to be aligned to the needs of the organization and to those people associated with the DSS?

In assessing whether the conditions within an organization are conducive to any form of innovation, the organization must answer three questions: whether the proposed change is acceptable, whether it is feasible and whether it is suitable (Johnson and Scholes, 1993).

To the extent that a proposal fails to satisfy any of these three tests, the likelihood of a successful innovation will be compromised.

In broad terms, acceptable means that the powerful actors in the organization are favourably disposed towards the innovation. For DSS in particular, this means taking into account the benefits and disbenefits of the proposed DSS to the key actors and their commitment to the proposal. This is alignment of the DSS with the people.

Feasibility is concerned with whether the organization has the appropriate capability to implement the DSS. This includes technical ability—whether the software and hardware is available or can be made so—and whether the actors possess the appropriate technical capabilities. It also includes consideration of the relationships between the key actors and of their workloads.

Suitability is concerned with the extent to which a proposed course of action would sustain or improve the effectiveness and efficiency of the organization, whether it assists organizational learning (Stein and Vandenbosch, 1996) and whether the perceived benefits outweigh the associated disbenefits. This is alignment of the DSS with the organization in general.

These three areas of concern and their main subdivisions are set out on the left-hand side of Fig. 1 entitled ensuring the appropriate environment (akin to establishing a suitable climate for change, Tait and Vessey, 1988). One feature associated with the use of consultants external to the organization is that the very early stages of conception and discussion have often been undertaken before the consultant is employed. In the study reported here these very initial stages associated are not considered. (Although dealt with in more detail later, it is worth noting here that the relationships amongst key actors are considered when we discuss maintaining commitment.)

The systems lifecycle approach to IS development (see, for example, FitzGerald and FitzGerald, 1987) was initially applied to DSS development. The appropriateness of this approach relies on the belief that the final system can be defined at the beginning of the development process. This belief in turn implies that the precise context in which a DSS is to be used and the proclivities of the people who will be using it can also be defined in advance. Such an approach is inappropriate where the situation is fluid, unstructured and context dependent and where the requirements of the system cannot be known with any precision at the outset—exactly the domain of DSS. Here the concern is also with process and not simply with the product.

More recently, prototyping and breadboarding approaches have been favoured. A prototype may be defined as an early version of a system that exhibits the main features of the later operational system (Sprague and Carlson, 1982). The term ‘prototype’ can be applied to any system that is developed rapidly and which is exposed to the user for interactive development and modification based on user experience of the system (Hopkinson, 1986). Prototyping allows the user and designer together to make incremental changes to the system to better fit the needs of the user. Breadboarding is similar to the way an adaptive system would be designed (Simon, 1977): it is a more extreme version of prototyping in that the whole system is created in an incremental fashion.

Prototyping, breadboarding and more formal, structured approaches may all be utilized in the creation of a DSS. For example, it could be that any substantial database associated with a DSS would best be built and maintained using very formal and structured methods, whilst the interface might be prototyped and the logic model breadboarded. Thus the formal and informal approaches co-exist, with informal methods building on more formally developed corporate databases and operating within formal reporting lines. In the development and implementation phase individuals work together in an evolutionary manner to develop the DSS. Again, there is a continuing need to follow the alignment model (Scott Morton, 1991).

## 2.2. The elements of DSS

The well-known split of DSS into data-oriented and logic-oriented systems identified two of the important elements in DSS—the data model and the logic model (Alter, 1977) or alternatively the data base and model base (cf. Sprague and Watson, 1996). DSS combine tools from three generic application areas: data management, analysis and presentation (Sprague, 1980; Panko, 1988), and this explicitly adds the human-computer interface to the two component model. Building on these approaches, DSS are viewed as being composed of a data model, a logic model, the presentational (human-computer) interface and additionally the IT infrastructure that supports the DSS. The IT infrastructure consists of the hardware and operating systems platform and any applications generators associated with a specific DSS (Sprague, 1980). However, since DSS are socio-technical systems rather than simply technical ones, the success of a DSS relies also on the people involved with the DSS. That the major problems in IS implementation stem from failures to give adequate regard to behavioural, political and organizational factors motivates the view people should be treated as central to the development and implementation process (Li, 1997).

The realization that a mere informal, people-based approach is needed in DSS development than in other forms of IS (Garrity, 1994; Janson et al., 1993) means that it is very important to identify all the actors who could play a significant part in the life of a DSS. One obviously key actor is the user (or 'end-user', Welsch, 1981) who has been defined as the person who must take action and be responsible for the consequences (Sprague, 1980). A second major actor is the consultant who is taken to be the person responsible for delivering the DSS and maintaining it. The consultant is akin to the technical intermediary (Keen and Scott Morton, 1978) and systems builder (Finlay and Wilson, 1987).

Users may involve intermediaries in their use of a DSS (Sprague, 1980). The term ‘integrating agent’ was used (Welsch, 1981), whilst the terms ‘chauffeur’ and ‘intermediary’ were used interchangeably (Keen and Scott Morton, 1978). Two types of intermediary were posited: technical intermediaries who help with the design, the formalization of ideas and with personalizing the DSS, and staff intermediaries who act as the interface between the user and the system, translating questions, operating the DSS and providing analyses (Keen and Scott Morton, 1978). The authors’ experiences with building and maintaining DSS indicate the importance of the staff intermediary. Experience also suggests that there is the clerical intermediary, performing simple routine tasks such as data entry. The role of the technical intermediary is considered to be taken by the consultant.

A toolsmith has been considered a significant player in the building of DSS (Sprague, 1980). Whilst this may have been true in the late 1970s, the development by commercial organizations of new infrastructural platforms for DSS is now rather rare. Where new capabilities are required of the DSS generator it would be the consultant who would undertake the task. Since many DSS need structured data from corporate databases, one important aspect for the design and operation of a DSS is access to these databases. This in turn suggests that the custodians of the databases (data custodians) are likely to be important for DSS success $^{2}$ .

Whilst the above list of actors contains those people who are directly involved in the technical aspects of DSS development and implementation, the political and organizational dimensions must also be considered. The organizational behaviour and innovation literatures have strongly linked the success of technological innovations to the support of top management and, in particular, there is the link to the presence of champions (Beath, 1991). DSS champions are managers who actively and vigorously promote the idea of a DSS among potential systems users and management (Vitale and Ives, 1988). There are also the sponsors who have the funds and authority to accomplish their goal of getting a DSS implemented (Vitale and Ives, 1988).

The authors' research consultancy activities suggested that two further personnel have political and organizational significance. There are other supporting political actors whose influence is needed to secure political support for the DSS and there is the direct superior of the user. These people are close to, but not quite the same as, top management and functional management (Zmud and Cox, 1979).

## 2.3. The classification

Three powerful strands from the above discussion of DSS experience motivate the structure of the classification within which we place knowledge of DSS success factors. The first strand derives from the realization that there are two main phases in the conception and construction of DSS and that these have very different characteristics: an initial phase that we have termed ensuring the appropriate environment and a development phase that we have termed maintaining commitment. The second strand is the realization that building and using a DSS engage socio-technological systems in which a wide range of actors play significant roles. Intertwined with these two strands is the third—the widespread acceptance of the alignment model whereby a major emphasis in IS planning is the alignment of IS with business goals.

Essentially there are two classifications, one applicable to each of the phases. Both are faceted classifications. Faceted classification is accepted as providing a sound theoretical basis for the construction of classification in today's environment of rapid development of knowledge (Rowley, 1992). The process by which a faceted classification might best be achieved has been detailed in Rowley (1992) and has been followed in this research. (The structure of the classifications is set out in Tables 2 and 3.) With ensuring the appropriate environment the alignment model and the strategic management literature together suggest the common facet Business Focus. The view that a wide range of actors is involved in the DSS suggests the second common facet of Actor. Included in the Actor facet is the Consultant him/herself and Organization which includes all the 'infrastructural' aspects associated with the DSS. The Major Factors for Success were derived from the field research and provide the third facet. The Lower-Level Factors for success are differential facets, being subsets of the major success factors. An initial list of these had been obtained from the literature and later grouped under the appropriate major factors for success.

With maintaining commitment the DSS literature again suggested Actors as the common facet. The Major Success Factors and the lower level factors were derived in the same way as already described.

## 3. The initial set of success factors

The literature on contributory success factors for computer-based IS was examined and together with our own consulting experiences, provided the elements of the third facet of our classification—the major factors for success. These factors when ensuring the appropriate environment were: benefits and disbenefits to the major actors, their commitment to the DSS, the relationships between the major actors, the technical and organizational stability, the technical capability of the actors and of the organization in general and staff work loads. The major success factors when maintaining commitment were: adaptability of the DSS, the decision-making benefits arising from the DSS, ease of use of the DSS, the learning and support infrastructure surrounding the development of the DSS, the match of the DSS to the issues facing the user and the organization, the quantity and quality of the information provided by the DSS, the time between the consultant delivering additional functionality to the user, understanding of the DSS by the actors, and understanding by the actors of the importance of their contributions. Associating the lower-level factors for success with these major factors produced the initial classification.

## 4. The first field research

The literature review identified the factors that theoreticians had postulated and/or researchers had found were significant to DSS success. The aim of the field research was to weed out those factors that were of little significance and to unearth further significant factors. To secure wide generalizability of the research findings a good spread of DSS within a wide range of business settings was investigated. Both tactical and strategic issues were addressed and financial, inventory, production, sales and marketing decisions were included. In all, 39 cases were investigated involving 27 organizations and using a total of 22 different DSS generators. The characteristics of the research sites are given in the Appendix.

The IS used in this study was a filtered subset of the many IS that were initially considered. Several of the discarded cases were management information systems rather than DSS whilst others did not satisfy the criterion that were adopted for this study that a consultant must have helped the user or the intermediary to build the DSS.

It was initially thought that a questionnaire would be a suitable research instrument. However, the testing of a pilot showed that this was not the case, since it proved difficult to specify the questions in a loose enough way to prevent strong leading of the respondents and to capture the richness of the situations in which the DSS had been developed and utilized. Thus face-to-face interviews were considered more appropriate. Access to the principal actors in DSS development and implementation was obtained through the vendors of DSS generators $^{3}$ . These software houses were willing to use their good offices to secure access to client firms, seeing the research as a means of obtaining publicity for their products and obtaining insight into how best to use their products in their own consultancy activities. An important side effect of using software houses was that the authors were able to attend user meetings.

Considerable effort was made to interview more than one of the key people involved with each DSS development $^{4}$ . This was done to obtain a balanced view of the situation and to cross check the information collected from different sources. In a few cases the key people were interviewed as a group. This could have provided problems in combining responses but this was not the case in practice. Before each interview the researcher became familiar with the DSS generator that had been used for the DSS.

After initial trials, all interviews were conducted in the same way, allowing the researchers to have a considerable degree of control over the direction and depth of the interviews, but not being overly restrictive. The interviewees were first asked to focus on one successful and one unsuccessful DSS with which they had been involved (however, some interviewees couldn't think of an unsuccessful DSS). The success/lack of success of the DSS was confirmed; i.e. was there repeat use and user-satisfaction? The interviewees were then asked to identify what they thought were the important associated success factors. Generally, five or six factors were offered and these were then discussed in detail. Then the researcher introduced further major factors using a pre-defined checklist of factors obtained from the literature review and earlier interviews. This was followed by open-ended discussion that sought additional success factors associated with further DSS and a richer understanding of the success factors already identified (getting words to fly; Glesne and Peshkin, 1992). Then the interviewees were asked to comment on the structure of the success model and to offer comments on the factors included under each section. Where consultants were being interviewed the discussion was then widened, with the interviewee being asked about his/her more general experiences with DSS development. In almost all cases the interviews were recorded.

## 4.1. Results from the first field work

The result of the first field work was a revised set of factors for success derived from the original list by removing those items that were not supported by more than one interviewee and adding to the list where this level of support was forthcoming. The final elements are listed in Tables 2 and 3. There are 131 items in all.

The model of the factors for success was validated by re-interviewing two consultants who had been interviewed previously (associated with scheduling and linear programming) and interviewing two ‘new’ consultants not previously interviewed (associated with simulation and networking). These consultants were sent a copy of the model prior to interview and asked to review the model as to the appropriateness of the factors that had been included, the model’s completeness and the relationships between the success factors. At interview, all the consultants agreed that the factors included were appropriate (they didn’t suggest any deletions) and complete (they didn’t suggest any additions). There were comments about the phraseology of some of the success factors and some rephrasing occurred as a result.

Considerations of acceptability, feasibility and suitability provided a suitable framework for establishing whether an appropriate environment for DSS development and implementation has been ensured. Within the criterion of acceptability, only the benefits and disbenefits to the consultant, staff intermediary, user and the user's superior were of concern: it was considered that personal benefits for the other actors were of little significance. Apart from the champion and the clerical intermediary, the commitment of the actors was a significant factor. Within the criterion of feasibility, the technical capabilities of the consultant, staff intermediary and user were considered important. There appeared to be no need for either the user or staff intermediary to know the DSS programming language nor the technique underlying the DSS (although this need was evident in the development phase). The importance of many of the relationships between key actors was confirmed. Within the criterion of suitability, it was surprising that the permanence of the sponsor was not considered a significant feature.

It was found that the role of the champion appeared much less significant than the literature would suggest for both phases (Beath, 1991). Our interpretation was that where a sponsor exists, which would usually be the case where a consultant was called in to provide a DSS, championing was not required or was already successfully accomplished: thus the champion was no longer a significant actor.

When DSS development is underway, the emphasis on maintaining the commitment of the key actors in the development process was generally supported by the field research except for the two roles of champion and supporting political actors.

Maintaining the commitment of the user and the staff intermediary are considered paramount. The DSS must match the issues being addressed, show decision making benefits and the quantity and quality of the information provided must be high. A suitable learning and support infrastructure is needed and the DSS must be easy to use. Of great significance is that both user and the staff intermediary must understand the DSS—the model complexity must match their capabilities, they need to be involved in the DSS development and be able to interpret the results.

## 5. The classification and the success factors in one situational context

It is likely that most success factors always play some part in determining DSS development success: however, their importance will be contingent on the context. The importance of the factors in one particular context has been derived in a second field research using the classification set out in Tables 2 and 3. This work shows how the classification can be operationalized for specific cases to provide guidance to practitioners operating in similar contexts.

One of the authors held discussions over a period of 12 months with 15 consultants working in a consultancy that developed financial applications using the DSS generator Financial Management System. $^{5}$ Around 100 implemented DSS were considered which in the main involved large companies. The type of DSS was defined from the dimensions that have been used as the basis for definitions of DSS: the amount of the problem ‘solved’ by the system (Alter, 1977), the decision situation (Donovan and Madnick, 1977), the application (Keen and Scott Morton, 1978), the approaches to model development (Sprague, 1980; Ginzberg and Stohr, 1982), and the user (Freyenfeld, 1984). These dimensions are shown in Table 4.

A typical development schedule was 2–6 months spent identifying the systems requirement, designing the DSS and completing the first implementation, with a further 12 months of full implementation to all organizational areas $^{6}$ . The ‘passive’ users tended to be the CEO, main Board members and senior managers one level below the main Board. The ‘active’ users, who would both use the DSS and call for enhancements, were such people as product managers, accountants, financial controllers and some senior managers.

In almost all cases the following people from the client organizations were involved.

In ensuring the appropriate environment 25 success factors were considered ‘most important’: 10 associated with establishing Acceptability, 11 with Feasibility and four with Suitability. Within Acceptability, the commitment of the consultant him/herself, the sponsor and user were highlighted. Within Feasibility, the importance of the permanence of the staff intermediary was noteworthy. Technical capability was important, but only of the consultant, staff intermediary and user. It was only the relationship between the sponsor and consultant that was considered to be important—all the other relationships were considered much less significant. The consultant’s interpersonal skills were considered important. Within Suitability, the place of the DSS, within the corporate and IT strategy, efficiency, effectiveness and costs were highlighted.

In maintaining commitment 25 factors were considered ‘most important’ with only the staff intermediary and the user being involved. The staff intermediary provided 12 items and the user 13. Their shared need for training and support, their need for confidence in the information displayed and their ability to interpret this information were highlighted. Convenience of access, flexibility and involvement in system development were considered to be of major significance to the staff intermediary. It was considered important to the users that the DSS matched the issues facing them (included the ‘must haves’), was adaptable and provided decision making benefits. Also it was important to ensure that the consultant provided ‘deliverables’ to the user at appropriate times.

## 6. Summary and conclusions

This article reports on the development of a classification of success factors for consultant-led DSS. The form of the classification has been motivated by three major considerations: the two phases to DSS development, the wide range of actors who are significant to both of these phases, and the needed alignment between the DSS and the business requirement. The classification is strongly empirically based and, due to the number and type of people interviewed and the consequent triangulation, it has high internal and content validity. Although the validation of the factors established from the literature survey and the first field work was not as strong as we would have liked, factor validity was augmented, albeit in an indirect fashion, during the second field work. A total of 131 factors were established as being significant to successful DSS development in general. The classification and these factors have been derived from a review of 74 academic articles, our own consultancy experiences and face-to-face interviews with 46 people associated with 39 DSS and involving 22 DSS generators. The range of applications, sites and generators investigated give the results high generalizability.

It is likely that most success factors play some part in most DSS development success: however, their importance will be contingent on the context. The ‘most important’ success factors in one particular context were derived in a second field research. It was also worth noting that although the main thrust of this research was to identify important elements in one specific context, it also provided an opportunity to informally check out the total classification. It was gratifying that no one in the second field research commented adversely on the classification. This work shows how the classification can guide practitioners operating in contexts similar to that investigated.

The classification allows for the systematic incorporation of research findings as they become available. This has two major implications for DSS research. First, there is a need to extend the range of organizational contexts from which the classification was derived in order to extend its generalizability. Second, the classification can be used to establish those success factors that are important in further specific contexts.

We see two major implications for systems development practice. The general approach should encourage the ‘softer’ issues in DSS development and implementation to be more fully considered: in particular, it should prevent the importance of the political and organizational factors being underestimated. The classification has established the important factors in one particular context, and this information can be used directly by practitioners.
