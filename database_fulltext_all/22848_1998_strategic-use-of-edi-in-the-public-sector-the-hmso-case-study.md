# Strategic use of EDI in the public sector: the HMSO case study

Benita Cox\*, Sherine Ghoneim

The Management School, Imperial College of Science, Technology and Medicine, 53 Prince's Gate, Exhibition Road, London SW7 2PG, UK

Revised paper accepted for publication by Professor R.D. Galliers 3 February 1998

## Abstract

There has been widespread academic discussion in recent years on the potential of information systems to provide organisations with a strategic advantage. To this debate has recently been added a further controversy, i.e. the extent to which information systems (EDI in particular) and the concept of business network redesign are compatible. The aim of this paper is to contribute to these debates, by arguing that the implementation of EDI may still provide strategic advantage to an organisation, but is dependent upon the industry sector in which the organisation is operating, its position within that sector, the dynamics of the market place, its ability to build on its existing strengths and its ability to exploit the available technology innovatively. Support for these arguments is provided by documenting the case of Her Majesty's Stationery Office (HMSO), where the implementation of EDI has resulted in financial gains estimated at between £5 million and £10 million per annum since 1993. This has been achieved through the integration of EDI with the corporate strategy and results from a process re-design programme reflecting the changing environment of the British public sector. The paper also explores how the traditional role of EDI as a document exchange system has been extended to that of systems' integrator and corporate change agent, thus facilitating future business and technical change requirements. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Strategic systems; Electronic data interchange; Public sector; Systems integration

## 1. Introduction

Much of the academic discussion on the topic of Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) has focused on the strategic potential of EDI and its role as an enabler in business process re-engineering. Yet, despite the development of a number of EDI implementation frameworks, there remains a paucity of case studies and empirical research to support the validity of these frameworks. Detailed accounts of how successful implementation of EDI is achieved, in particular in organisations gaining large-scale financial advantages from its introduction, are not widely documented, and there is even less evidence of this occurring in the public sector. This lack of evidence contributes to the prolongation of the academic debate on the potential of information systems to gain or sustain a strategic advantage with well-rehearsed arguments being raised both for and against (Bakos and Treacy, 1986; Robson, 1997; Ives and Vitale, 1996). To this debate has recently been added a further controversy, i.e. the extent to which information systems and the concept of business network redesign are compatible. Spinardi et al. argue that EDI and business network redesign “don’t go together” (Spinardi et al., 1996), whilst Swatman argues that EDI can be used to reengineer the business processes of its adopter and hence confer long-term, strategic and comparative advantage upon the adopter (Swatman, 1994). The aim of this paper is to contribute to these debates, by suggesting that the implementation of EDI may still provide a strategic advantage to an organisation, but is dependent upon the industry sector in which the organisation is operating, its position within that sector, the dynamics of the market place, its ability to build on its existing strengths and its ability to exploit the available technology innovatively. Support for these arguments is provided by documenting the case of Her Majesty’s Stationery Office (HMSO), where the implementation of EDI has resulted in financial gains estimated at between £5 million and £10 million per annum since 1993 (Currie, 1994). This has been achieved through the integration of EDI with the corporate strategy and results from a process re-design programme reflecting the changing environment of the British public sector. The paper also explores how the traditional role of EDI as a document exchange agent has been extended to that of systems’ integrator and corporate change agent, thus facilitating future business and technical change requirements.

## 2. EDI and strategic advantage

The term ‘strategic advantage’ is used differently by a number of authors in the academic literature. In particular, there is some ambiguity between the terms ‘competitive advantage’ and ‘strategic advantage’. In this paper, ‘competitive advantage’ is considered to be a short-run advantage based on such factors as first-mover influence. ‘Strategic advantage’ refers to a longer-term advantage which enables the organisation to improve business performance permanently.

It is our contention that opportunities for organisations, operating in developed nations, to gain a competitive advantage from the introduction of EDI are limited, save in a few market sectors. In the UK, e.g. within the retail and motor manufacturing sectors, EDI usage is widespread and is no longer a source of differentiation but simply a cost of staying in business. However, in those sectors, e.g. the pharmaceutical industry and public sector, where EDI is less widespread, the potential to gain a competitive advantage from its implementation remains (Cox and Ghoneim, 1995). Further, the ability of EDI to integrate disparate external and internal information systems provides opportunities for both competitive and strategic advantages, through its potential to change the entire structure of the organisation and the business network in which it operates.

In so far as strategic advantage is concerned, there are a number of factors which will influence the degree of success achieved. For example, the position of an organisation within the supply chain will determine its market power and hence the potential to gain a strategic advantage through EDI. Clearly, focal organisations have the power to ‘strongly encourage’ EDI trading with their suppliers and, where suppliers themselves are not operating in highly differentiated markets, they have the power of persuasion over their customers.

The strategic potential of an EDI implementation will also be influenced by the prevailing market conditions. For example, the volatile market conditions resulting from policy changes in the British public sector during the early and mid 1990's, created new opportunities for introducing technologies previously used predominantly in the private sector and thus a window of opportunity.

Finally, the potential to gain a strategic advantage from a technological implementation is influenced by the extent to which the technology enhances an existing advantage already enjoyed by the organisation. The widely quoted successful introduction by Merrill Lynch of their Cash Management Account is an illustration of this (Cash et al., 1993). Merrill Lynch already had a unique range of banking products which it offered to its clients, but was able to use Information Systems to integrate these products to provide even more sophisticated services which could not be replicated by competitors as they could not supply the same range of base products.

The case of HMSO described in this paper shows how the organisation exploited its position within the public sector, taking advantage of the volatile British political environment as well as its reputation as a supplier to central government, to successfully grasp the opportunity to integrate disparate systems and achieve a strategic advantage through EDI implementation.

## 3. Literature survey

There has been a great deal of academic research in the area of EDI. To date, the research has focused on a number of perspectives, each of which is typically addressed independently of the others. The first perspective considers the impact of EDI on inter-organisational structures (Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Benjamin et al., 1990). It is concerned with EDI's ability to facilitate the restructuring of the industry as a whole as well as its ability to transform individual partnerships, e.g. EDI's potential to transform relationships between trading partners by bringing them closer (Baker, 1991; Johnston and Carrico, 1988; Holland et al., 1992b).

The second perspective focuses on the role of EDI in business process re-engineering, its role as an enabling mechanism, and vehicle for business process re-design and business scope re-definition (Swatman, 1994; Benjamin et al., 1990; Fowler et al., 1994; Swatman et al., 1994; Venkatraman, 1991). The extent of EDI integration with internal and external business processes as well as with core business activities has been identified as a determining factor in achieving maximum business benefit (Bergeron and Raymond, 1992; Cox and Ghoneim, 1996). However, the relationship between EDI and business network redesign has recently been questioned. Spinardi argues that there is little evidence for “the much touted vision of EDI and BPR” (Spinardi et al., 1996), whilst Swatman argues the reverse (Swatman, 1994).

The third perspective is concerned with the impact of EDI on organisations. In particular, organisational benefits associated with EDI implementation have been widely discussed (Reekers and Smithson, 1994). In recent years, the literature has reflected widespread debate as to the potential of EDI (and information systems in general) to either gain or sustain a strategic advantage.

The fourth perspective concentrates on implementation issues. The role of Strategic Information Systems (SISP) planning in the successful adoption of EDI has been highlighted (Galliers et al., 1995). Critical success factors in adopting EDI have been explored, and various implementation models have been described which highlight both internal and external organisational requirements (Cox and Ghoneim, 1996; Holland et al., 1992a).

A final perspective takes a technological approach to the discussion of EDI (Borthick and Roth, 1993). Here technological solutions are sought to technological obstacles which are viewed as being the primary barrier to successful adoption.

Each of these perspectives highlights important aspects of the EDI implementation process. However, there is no single, coherent theoretical framework which covers the complexity of the entire process. As such, the HMSO case study described in this paper provides support for issues addressed by a number of these frameworks rather than a single theoretical approach. In particular, however, the case does supply material illustrative of the achievement of a strategic advantage through the judicious use of EDI. It also provides support for the work undertaken by Swatman illustrating the use of technology in achieving business transformation in the private sector by illustrating this effect in the public sector (Swatman, 1994; Swatman et al., 1994). Finally, it demonstrates the use of EDI as an enabler of business change and a systems' integrator.

## 4. Research approach

A survey was conducted by the authors in 1993 to investigate whether industry differences existed between various sectors in terms of the drivers, benefits and barriers to EDI adoption (Cox and Ghoneim, 1996). The results highlighted the need for in-depth case studies to complement the quantitative aspects of the research. Amongst the areas identified for further research was that of the potential of EDI to provide a competitive advantage and its role in business process re-design. In particular, additional information was needed on the benefits achieved from EDI integration with core business activities in a non-retail organisation. HMSO was selected for the study on the grounds that it was a non-retail organisation where the use of EDI had provided strategic benefits. In investigating and constructing this case study, a range of qualitative techniques was used. Four semi-structured interviews, each ranging from between 1 h and 1.5 h in length, with both business and technical representatives from different levels within the organisation were undertaken. In addition, four meetings were held with outside organisations, including the third party service providers. Detailed internal documentation and published references were also examined.

The case study described in this paper does not purport in itself to represent a testimonial to the success of EDI in general, or to the particular method of its adoption. It does, however, provide an acaically researched case that documents the strategic benefit gained from the adoption and its role as a facilitator in a business process re-design programme.

## 5. Market dynamics and SO

Until September 1996, H. SO was a British government organisation with a turnover approaching £400 million per annum. It supplied stationery products, office supplies and equipment, printing and publishing facilities to the public sector, as well as publishing services to the private sector. The public sector from 1786 till the early 1980s was dependent on HMSO for the sourcing and supply of office stationery. The commercial environment was one of a protected customer community bound by government commercial agreements. In 1980, the government changed purchasing policies, altering HMSO's status to that of 'Trading Fund', thus granting the customer base the freedom to choose suppliers on the basis of competitive price and quality. Whereas previously, HMSO operated in a protected customer environment, these policy changes forced it to move to a position of open competition. At the same time, large scale 'value for money initiatives' introduced by the government meant that the public sector agencies, which constituted a major percentage of HMSO's customer base, gained greater control over their budgets. As a result, HMSO faced the risk that its established and guaranteed customer base might move away from wholesale suppliers such as itself, to retail suppliers already operating in the private sector. HMSO was faced with a crisis situation in which it was in danger of losing its customer base. In 1987 a review was undertaken at HMSO to improve performance, control costs and increase competitiveness. The review resulted in the implementation of a Total Quality Management (TQM) programme and the adoption of Supply Chain Management Techniques (Sawhney and Williams, 1994). The review particularly highlighted the need to increase efficiency and the quality of customer service. It was decided to implement EDI technology as the enabling mechanism to achieving these objectives. This strategy proved to be successful and in 1992, when senior management adopted a business process re-design strategy, the potential role of EDI in developing new markets and/or changing the nature of the business environment was assessed and the extension of HMSO's existing electronic trading endorsed. The following paragraphs describe the history of EDI implementation from conception through to its current state.

## 6. Early implementation of EDI

Sawhney and Williams detail the early EDI implementation history at HMSO as follows (Sawhney and Williams, 1994). In 1987 the first implementation of an on-line order receipt system was developed to reflect the needs of commercial bookshops and accredited agents, and the implementation was restricted to this community. This improved ordering and delivery, and provided uniqueness to both products and services thus increasing the customer's cost of switching to alternative suppliers (Bakos and Treacy, 1986). This successful outcome indicated that electronic capture facility could provide significant benefit to HMSO and had a clear application to the Business Supplies Division, where the goods supplied could be identified by discrete coded numbers and the exchange document information structured. HMSO was aware of the fact that the majority of their customers were not EDI capable, and, therefore, an on-line order entry system was implemented. This system, HMSO Enquiry Access and Rapid Trading (HEART), based on customer requirements, was developed in 1989 and provided on-line access to an electronic catalogue with screen-based order creation and authorisation facilitation. In parallel, HMSO's EDI-capable customers communicated their requirements through Value-Added Network Service (VANS) providers.

## 7. Analysing and extending the role of EDI

By 1992, HMSO was conducting in excess of £30 million worth of electronic trading. However, arrangements were ad hoc and unfocused across the organisation and did not provide the necessary security, data validation, audit and control required for the increasing amount of valuable information it was handling. Communication links were also beginning to show signs of strain, with occasional slow downs (Currie, 1995). Investigations were undertaken to consider how electronic trading could deliver new business opportunities for HMSO and allow them to provide value to what was increasingly becoming a commodity market. The investigation focused on the trading and finance divisions within HMSO. An analysis of the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats (SWOT) for each trading division was initiated, detailing the potential benefits of electronic trading to HMSO and its trading partners together with any dependencies. Consideration was given to whether electronic trading could enable the organisation to develop new markets or change the nature of the business, thus reducing its dependency on existing markets. To assess the trends within the private retail sector, discussions were held with a large retail outlet which had been exchanging information with suppliers since the 1980's.

## 8. Adding value to existing business strength through EDI

Amongst the more apparent strengths of HMSO was its position within government as well as its expertise both in central government issues and electronic trading. From its existing customers' point of view, it was perceived as the supplier to central government and therefore 'safe'. However, this position needed to be consolidated and strengthened in the light of changing government policies. A unique service was required in order to both protect its existing customer base from competition from the private sector as well as attract new customers. Opportunities existed for a number of options, e.g. the introduction of 'one stop shopping' providing the customer with a single source for publications and printing materials. Other opportunities included the potential for the provision of a legislation database for solicitors and material for local authorities which would provide information on the latest Acts which they could subsequently distribute to relevant parties. A further opportunity existed for HMSO to provide consultancy and management services. It had a great deal of know-how in the area of procurement in the public sector which could be exploited. It also had expertise in the development of software for specific information databases. In order to evaluate the range of opportunities available, HMSO assessed each one against a range of criteria. Included in these criteria were the associated costs and benefits, how speculative each option was, the ease of introduction, the extent to which the customers' requirements were known, as well as the benefit to the customer and the extent to which the process was core to the business. Finally, a technological opportunity existed to extend the existing HEART system as a base on which other facilities could be developed. The existing catalogue system could be extended to cover all the services provided by HMSO, thus supporting the concept of one stop shopping for customers.

## 9. Extending EDI functionality

It was decided that in order to meet the business objectives, the existing EDI implementation would be extended to logistics and supply activities and controlled centrally within HMSO through the implementation of an ‘EDI Corporate Gateway’ (Fig. 1).

The EDI Corporate Gateway was a response to both the business requirements outlined above as well as to technical requirements. It comprises a number of modules providing the management and control facilities for the exchange of information communications (Fig. 2). Orders received (via HEART, value-added networks, telephone or post) are integrated by the on-line capture and placement systems, which in turn interact with the warehouse system or raise a supplier order. Some of the most important features of the gateway are as follows:

Single access capacity: a single trading interface to connect HMSO internally and externally to all trading partners both within and outside the UK. The gateway includes a built-in translator to convert data from in-house applications into EDI format (e.g. TRADACOMS, EDIFACT or ANSI X12) and vice versa;

A comprehensive and flexible technical platform. In order to cater for the varying trading community technical setups, it is necessary to provide open systems communications and multi-network connect facilities to access the various VANS. It is also necessary to ensure system resilience, security and reliability. When purchase orders are delivered to HMSO, the gateway information flow control system invokes HMSO-specific routines to carry out data integrity checking to ensure the correctness and validity of the messages exchanged. The Gateway also manages trading partners' profiles and HMSO's relationships with them;

Enhanced administrative functions are required to maintain the information needed for business and legal purposes in a single auditable repository as well as management and billing information. This information details trading profiles and statistics. In addition, a number of audit and status reports are generated to track details of a document's delivery and receipt;

Agent tasks: the functionality of the construction and translation agents was technically adapted and extended to co-ordinate the exchange of internal as well as external business applications through establishing a standard means of business communication. This central facility referred to as ‘agent tasks’ minimised the requirement to change existing systems. These ‘agent tasks’ referred to in programming terms as ‘user hooks’ are built into the trading relationships and allow for assigned tasks to be implemented whenever the relationship is invoked. This functionality allows for greater flexibility, power and accountability, because the scope of the agent tasks is limited only by the scope of the programming script created by the user. Tasks can thus be defined to validate internal or external trading relationships, conduct security checks, add or extract data from a database, trigger routines in a different system, route message destination, setup audit information or archive pre-specified messages, thus building in intelligent information management as well as applications and systems integration. Further, ‘agent tasks’ provide a central capacity to accommodate future change by simply adding or modifying existing script files.

## 10. Innovative use of EDI technology—systems integration

The foregoing discussion illustrates how HMSO utilised the HMSO EDI Corporate Gateway to integrate systems as well as set and define trading relationships. This served to:

1. minimise the requirement to change internal existing systems, and where change was necessary to conduct these changes centrally through the use of the Gateway using agent tasks functionally capable of invoking the specific pre-defined routines and tasks associated with a particular application, system or trading partner;

2. automatically validate and route documents to one of the four internal operating systems, covering printed goods, publications, office supplies and financial systems, or to an external supplier according to the pre-defined parameters in their respective trading relationships. As a result, the corporate gateway automated the entire process of creation and dispatch of HMSO's orders to suppliers originating either from customers orders or to replace warehouse stock;

3. position the gateway as the central backbone for corporate systems serving as a change agent, enabling and managing potential future corporate systems and/or applications change requirements. By absorbing any potential change requirements centrally, i.e. ensuring data coming into or moving across HMSO is dealt with in a standard format, costly and complex conversion code is eliminated and may be re-used across the organisation.

## 11. Relationship with the trading community and suppliers

A primary objective throughout the implementation of this project was to enhance trading partners' co-operation and establish closer trading relationships within the relevant business community. The initiation of the electronic trading strategy provided an opportunity to understand trading partners' requirements, re-negotiate terms in the light of exploiting the technology and communications capacity, and further enforce collaborative efforts. For customers, HMSO incorporated the use of EDI in bid offerings as a positive business differentiator. This provided the opportunity to work more closely with the trading partners in understanding their corporate and EDI requirements from the beginning as well as the opportunity to support non-EDI users and assist them in adopting and implementing EDI.

In order to address the needs of suppliers, HMSO adopted a formal rollout program in partnership/collaboration with a Value-Added Network supplier. Senior staff members addressed suppliers at seminars specifically organised to introduce the benefits of EDI implementation. The implementation itself was undertaken with a number of key suppliers so that the objectives of both parties could be understood and achieved.

## 12. Strategic benefits of the system

HMSO reported organisation-wide technical, operational and strategic benefits. The operational and efficiency benefits reported focused on ‘improved customer service’, ‘improved cash flow’ and ‘reduced clerical error’. HMSO states that EDI transactions are 10 times less likely to contain an error than traditional document processing and exchange methods. However, it is the strategic benefits reported which are the most relevant. A senior representative for HMSO stated “in an extremely competitive field, HMSO has been able to retain and expand its customer base” (Currie, 1994). Financial gains, including the value of business won and cost efficiencies resulting from the introduction of EDI are estimated at between £5 million and £10 million per annum. “In 1993 the annual value of HMSO’s business leaped to £45 million, a tenfold increase on 1991. Customer benefits comprise faster and more accurate ordering, reduced costs, greater control over cash flow and improved management information” (HMSO, 1993). In 1995, a major tender with Inland Revenue worth in the area of £4 million would not have been awarded if HMSO had not been EDI capable (Currie, 1996).

Further, the EDI technology also provided a platform for flexibility and change in managing the business. HMSO was originally organised on the basis of four divisions. Today, it comprises 15 fragmented businesses with EDI functionality serving as a central corporate gateway accommodating the individual units and enforcing a central corporate standard for conducting business. Through the introduction of the Corporate Gateway, which integrated applications and systems, the role of EDI has been extended from that of an operational tool to one of systems' integrator and corporate change agent providing flexibility to technical and business requirements.

## 13. Technological benefits

HMSO has used EDI as a solution to a technological problem. The introduction of the EDI Corporate Gateway facilitated the transfer of structured data internally within the organisation as well as externally amongst HMSO suppliers and customers, but it also provided an integration enabler for in-house applications (integrating various organisation applications, whether ordering, accounting or other) as well as systems (connecting diverse IT platforms). HMSO connected three mainframe computers and one UNIX operating system to the EDI corporate system utilising the EDI technology as a systems integrator. EDI has been traditionally perceived as an inter-organisational means of exchanging structured data, however, HMSO's Corporate EDI Gateway applied the same functionality to the individual fragmented intra-organisational systems. This provided for a sophisticated systems integration capacity. The implementation scenario was designed to encourage continuous modification and enhancement at minimum cost, and positioned the gateway as a central corporate change agent for managing future business and technical change requirements. On the basis of the flexibility of the system, HMSO are expanding their use of electronic trading to include invoicing, order acknowledgement, dispatch advice, self billing and forecasting, and are planning to pilot the use of electronic funds transfer and generic credit cards with central government authorities. A further HMSO initiative introduced in 1994 utilises voice response technology to allow customers to generate electronic orders using touch-tone telephones.

## 14. Business re-engineering benefits

Venkatraman's IT-induced transformation describes the challenge of re-engineering as not only to reconfigure the business to fully exploit the available IT, but also to differentiate their operations from their competitors (Swatman et al., 1994). The implementation of EDI at HMSO has enabled the re-configuration of the business using IT as the central lever. The introduction of EDI within the Publication Division and its initial restriction to trading partners within this community marked the initiation of the business process re-design stage, transforming the traditional means of conducting business within that particular community of customers. This initial process re-design was then extended to reconfigure the scope and tasks of the business network involved in the creation and delivery of products and services. The extension of the use of EDI to the Business Supplies Division and the implementation of a Corporate EDI Gateway to maximize the benefit of the existing investment in EDI and to enable links to be developed with the wider trading community resulted in a ‘virtual business network’. The initiatives were to include non-competing products, e.g. orders relating to the supply of industrial uniforms and clothing on HEART, and to provide EDI consultancy often as a free service to enhance transfer bids, differentiate HMSO’s service, generate new sources of income and enhance customer service profiles, resulting in an extension of the business mission and scope of the organisation. Further, parallels can be drawn with the EDI team whose role developed from one of support and co-ordination to one of adding value, service and products.

## 15. Managing problems

As may be expected, HMSO experienced a number of organisational and technical problems in implementing the system, as well as a number of difficulties associated with introducing EDI to their trading community (Sawhney and Williams, 1994).

Organisational problems included constraints on staff reduction, limited capital resources, fear of the unknown and the complexity of changing traditional business processes. Technical problems focused on the integration of EDI with internal and external systems. There were also problems associated with giving adequate weight to the needs of the different departments, as well as the limited state of EDI adoption within HMSO's trading community.

In order to address these problems, HMSO took the following steps:

1. An Electronic Trading Unit was established that provided technical assistance, training and support to the various internal trading divisions. The unit was also responsible for providing regular updates on the strategy and achievements. This served to alleviate anxieties, keep staff informed and promoted the ‘Electronic Trading Culture’ within the organisation;

2. A Total Quality Management (TQM) strategy was adopted, which highlighted the achievement of specified objectives and milestones;

3. Detailed analysis of internal processes was conducted, and the planning and management of systems modifications were carried out in-line with the corporate requirements;

4. Training and awareness sessions were given to promote the use of EDI with their trading community.

A major factor in the success of the implementation was the alliance between HMSO and a major EDI service provider who was able to tailor the EDI gateway platform to meet corporate requirements.

## 16. Critical success factors

The HMSO experience is a good example of a business proacting to the opportunities afforded by technology to meet the requirements of customers, competition and the changing environment (Hammer, 1993). The introduction of EDI at HMSO was not a reaction to trading partner requirements, but perceived as a mechanism for obtaining a competitive advantage in a changing business environment. However, the EDI strategy closely reflected the needs of the trading partners. The business activities of the organisation were re-organised to maintain consistency with organisational strategy and customer expectations, and to maximise the productivity of organisational resources through the use of EDI (Hammer, 1993; Davenport, 1993). HMSO established the business vision and objectives for enhancing and differentiating their customer service, by clearly identifying and focusing on the core business processes and supporting them through EDI implementation (Cox and Ghoneim, 1995). Thus, EDI was viewed not only as an automating or mechanising force, but also as a tool serving corporate objectives and enabling the integration of various functional systems. The entire strategy for implementation of EDI at HMSO thus demonstrates the importance of reflecting market dynamics, organisational position and strengths, and innovative use of technology. EDI provided HMSO with a solid IT foundation for managing changing market and business requirements and gained them competitive advantage. Further, it provided the technical capacity to develop new products, services and markets.

In summary, the following factors were critical to the success of HMSO:

1. A corporate-wide, coherent and integrated, both upstream and downstream electronic trading strategy, reflecting the industry structure, vigorously supported by senior management and pioneered by dedicated leadership;

2. An EDI strategy focused on customer requirements and organisational business processes, aimed at adding value to core business (buying-distribution-logistics), eliminating time, cost and error from HMSO's internal processes, and maximising benefit to HMSO and trading communities;

3. Awareness of the industry-specific requirements;

4. A sound technological infrastructure;

5. A comprehensive review and assessment of processes to provide for further exploitation of EDI both as an integration enabler and a corporate change agent within the business context of increasing customer satisfaction and hence, profitability.

## 17. Conclusion

Controversy exists in the academic literature as to the relationship between EDI and business process re-engineering, and the potential for information systems in general to achieve a strategic advantage for business. It has been argued on the one hand that EDI and business network re-design are incompatible (Spinardi et al., 1996), and on the other hand that real benefits can be obtained from inter-organisational electronic trading strategies founded on EDI, but that these benefits are unlikely to be achieved without commitment to Business Process Redesign. Swatman has detailed the case of BHP Steel in Australia where such benefits have been achieved (Swatman, 1994). The case study described in this paper documents the adoption of EDI in the UK public sector by HMSO to gain strategic advantage. It describes a close integration between business planning and EDI implementation, and the role of EDI as an application and systems integrator, allowing for future independent systems and applications enhancement with minimum impact on existing IT platforms and applications. This use of EDI as a systems integration enabler extends the role of EDI beyond business process support to one of co-ordination and control. It provides the technological flexibility for business decisions, dependent on technological solutions to be made, thus providing the basis for a corporate change agent.

In describing the case study of HMSO, the authors have endeavoured to set the topic in the context of existing research into EDI. In reviewing the literature it was found that the research focused on a number of perspectives, each of which is typically addressed independently of the others. Details of these perspectives have been outlined in this paper. It is our contention that the case study of HMSO highlights the need for a far more theoretically integrative approach to this topic. Defining potential benefit from EDI cannot be viewed independently from the context within which such benefit derives, be that as a result of its impact on inter-organisational structures or from its role as a facilitator of process re-engineering. Likewise, the implementation process cannot be viewed independently from either the cultural context or the technology. A more integrative theoretical approach to the study of EDI would more realistically convey the true complexity of the subject area.

Finally, in concluding, it is interesting to note that in September 1996 the British Government announced the sale of HMSO to Electra, an investment trust, and Robert Fleming, an investment bank, for £54 million (The Economist, 1996). The sale was controversial. The Economist reported that this sale price was at least £10 million lower than other final offers and a third of the original figure forecast. It was suggested that it was “a sweetheart deal” for friends of the Conservative government in power in Britain at that time. However, there were also rumours that the organisation’s accounting systems were a shambles and that it was therefore not surprising that the final offers were lower than the original ones. Roger Freeman, the minister handling the sale, when confronted with the view that HMSO should not have been sold in such an unsatisfactory condition defended the timing by pointing out that the HMSO urgently needed a massive change which only privatisation could provide. Whichever of the preceding arguments is true, whether HMSO was sold off cheaply or whether it was in reality not worth more than the price achieved, there is, in our view, little doubt that EDI improved the organisation’s position as it moved from public to private sector. In the process of conducting the interviews for the case study described in this paper, we were informed that the efficiencies and commercial gains afforded by the exploitation of EDI stood HMSO in good stead as it moved into uncharted private waters (Currie, 1996).
