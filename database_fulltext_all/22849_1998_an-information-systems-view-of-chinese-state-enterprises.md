# An information systems view of Chinese state enterprises

Dorothy G. Dologite<sup>a,</sup>\*, Mai Qi Fang<sup>b</sup>, Yu Chen<sup>b</sup>, Robert J. Mockler<sup>c</sup>, Chiang-nan Chao

<sup>a</sup>City University of New York — Baruch College, School of Business, 17 Lexington Avenue, New York, NY 10010, USA

<sup>b</sup>Renmin (People’s) University of China, 11 Lin Yuan 2, Beijing 100872, People’s Republic of China <sup>c</sup>St. John’s University, College of Business, Grand Central and Utopia Parkways, Jamaica, NY 11439, USA

Revised paper accepted for publication by Professor Jon Turner 27 May 1998

## Abstract

This study evaluates information systems (IS) in four large Chinese state enterprises (CSEs) located in Beijing. The IS areas considered are IS planning support, IS planning methodology, data resource management, and the IS manager’s role. While this study observes that these CSEs, among the largest in all China, have IS support only marginally better than that which existed 4 years earlier, it also finds evidence of slowly emerging change. An approach for evaluating IS in CSEs is proposed that focuses a lens on IS assets as an intermediate variable in benchmarking a CSE’s strategic adjustment toward reform. q 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information systems; Chinese state enterprises

## 1. Introduction

China’s path from a closed Communist party dominated economic system toward an open free market driven economy is creating a major opportunity for global organizations. Foreign companies are being encouraged to invest and share their technology with China’s traditional state run enterprises (Harding, 1997).

Investors have questions about how their current or future partners and possible competitors view key management issues. This study addresses one key management issue, the information systems (IS) function in Chinese state enterprises (CSEs).

Through case study analysis of five CSEs in 1991, Franz et al. found that because of the economic and political structure of China, IS does not play the same role within the corporate structure of Chinese enterprises as it does in the USA. Specifically, CSE managers do not understand IS as a vehicle for strategic competitiveness (Franz et al., 1991).

As the market economy is being slowly integrated with the Chinese way of doing business, it affects the motivations and incentives for all firms, which need to compete and survive. Is the role of IS changing as information becomes more important to support the attempts to conduct market economy business tactics and strategies? This study expects to find more of a role for, and use of, IS to support business objectives as China progressively shifts its basic economic activities more toward market-oriented practices.

Recent literature indicates that some Chinese industries have begun to use IS for strategic advantage. The banking sector, as an example, is developing a reliable national telecommunications infrastructure, electronic data interchange (EDI) systems, and switches to allow settlement of credit and debit card transactions (Iacobuzio, 1993; Day, 1994; Business China, 1995). The hospitality and transportation sectors have also begun to implement IS with similar value adding applications (Zhou, 1992; Kuby, 1995). The organizations involved demonstrate a high level of IS awareness and use as practiced in western economies. It is likely that this process will continue to influence IS use throughout China.

This study evaluates IS in four large CSEs located in Beijing. The IS areas considered are IS planning support, IS planning methodology, data resource management, and the IS manager’s role, the same IS areas considered in 1991 (Franz et al., 1991). While this study observes that some of the largest CSEs in China have IS support only marginally better than that which existed 4 years earlier, it also finds evidence of slowly emerging change.

The discussion of this study begins with a brief background section on CSEs. Then the development of the propositions for this study are discussed, followed by sections on research methodology, case descriptions, and an analysis of findings. These are amplified in a discussion and implications section. Another section proposes a conceptual framework for evaluating IS in CSEs that focuses a lens on a CSE’s strategic adjustment toward reform. A summary and conclusion ends the paper with suggestions for further study.

## 2. Background

Industrial reform began in 1979 in China with a shift in responsibility for business planning and economic performance from the government to the top management of CSEs (Tung, 1982; Cohen, 1988). Under the ‘contract-responsibility system’, managers were given more autonomy in personnel, finance, marketing and foreign-trade decisions. As long as public-sector firms fulfilled their so-called contracts — handing over a prenegotiated return to their state owners — they were free to reinvest their residual earnings and deploy their own resources. In some cases, residual earnings led to international expansion (Kaye, 1995). But as of the mid-l990s, only a fraction of CSE managers opted for the responsibility system (Nafziger, 1997).

In practice, large CSEs are managed much like they were before reform (Nafziger, 1997). China’s central planning commission sets targets on an annual basis for the amount of output required in each industry and the inputs that will be required to achieve that output. The planners convey these targets to the individual CSEs, which recommend changes based on local conditions. The cost of raw materials is fixed and the enterprises products or services must be delivered to government authorities at a fixed price.

CSE management focuses on improving planning and control activities to ensure that various targets are met. Performance is measured as attainment of pre-set production or service goals to generate the pre-negotiated return to the state.

Harding (1997) points out that CSEs are weighted down by the legacy of inefficient communist management. Managers reportedly understate capacity and overreport production to superiors to receive the greater reward given to by those who meet or exceed plan fulfillment (Nafziger, 1997). Rewards can be special subsidized pricing of goods, management benefits, increases in worker wages, contract advantages in the market, and more flexible pricing policies on finished goods (Franz et al., 1991). This management approach fosters a system of personal relations, known as guanxi, which forms an invisible network that often provides the most expedient way of getting things done (Chen, 1995).

A CSE’s general manager, or chief executive officer equivalent, must also factor in two main government mandated social issues. One is known as the ‘iron rice bowl’ and refers to the tradition that workers hired by state enterprises cannot be fired (Chen, 1995). The other is the charge to supply social welfare services, such as housing and feeding employees, retirees and their families, and providing schools for employees’ children. The burden of supporting these traditions is part of the reason most CSEs are losing money and are pursuing joint venture initiatives (Harding, 1997).

## 3. Development of propositions

To guide the investigation and analysis of the four case companies, this study builds on the study of Franz et al. (1991) by exploring the same four constructs: IS planning support, IS planning methodology, data resource management, and the IS manager’s role. Each is described in the sections that follow along with propositions that helped frame this study.

The propositions are oriented around linking IS functions to business requirements. They represent a synthesis of USA literature and findings from the 1991 study, since virtually no other research exists on internal IS functional activities in CSEs.

## 3.1. Planning support

It was found in 1991 that CSEs adopted an IS alignment approach to IS planning support. This approach attempts to match a portfolio of IS applications with business goals and objectives to improve the procedures and management of the business (Nolan, 1979; Parker and Benson, 1988, 1989). Essentially, IS resources supported building management information systems that enabled making appropriate operating decisions necessary to achieve, and control for, preplanned quotas.

By contrast, in the free-market USA economy, many organizations are motivated to use IS resources to build applications that support strategic positioning and competitive advantage within an industry (Porter, 1980, 1985; Cash et al., 1988). Based on reports mentioned earlier about strategic implementations of IS in China, we expected that industries with a growth orientation would be using an impact approach to IS planning support. The impact approach focuses on identifying a few strategic applications to create advantage over competitors (Lederer and Sethi, 1988). The work of Porter (1980, 1985) and Porter and Millar (1991) provides the lens for identifying strategically-oriented applications. Considered are primarily applications that support an organization’s ability to deal with external threats or opportunities, and those that enhance primary value chain activities. Proposition 1. CSEs striving toward free market reform will adopt an impact approach to IS planning support, as evidenced by strategic applications.

## 3.2. IS planning methodologies

Various IS planning methodologies can help an organization discover opportunities where IS can support important business requirements. Because Chinese organizations are short-term results oriented, it was discovered in 1991 that an approach similar to identifying critical success factors (CSF) best characterized the Chinese IS planning methodology. CSF focus on identifying key business measures that must go right for business success. It emphasizes monitoring and improving existing areas of business by developing the information systems to support the corresponding critical management decisions (Rockart, 1979).

Because of the trend toward infusing market practices in China, the current study expected IS to play a role in streamlining outmoded business processes in CSEs. We expected more recent approaches such as business process reengineering (BPR) might be taking hold in some part of the case companies studied. In BPR, business procedures are assessed, simplified and redesigned in order to be more responsive to competitive market conditions (Hammer and Champy, 1993; Davenport, 1993). Proposition 2. CSEs interested in market-oriented reform will invest in an IS planning methodology such as BPR to support their commitment to strategic change.

## 3.3. Data resource management

Data resource management encompasses activities that identify business objectives, set the data management scope, implement a data planning methodology and produce a data management product (Goodhue et al., 1988). It was found in 1991 that the data resource management approach adopted by CSEs resembled technical database modeling (Franz et al., 1991). It is characterized by narrowly scooped data sets designed to support the monitoring and control functions of individual operational managers.

For the current study, it was expected that CSEs focused on growth would demonstrate movement to some form of integration or enterprise-wide modeling of databases. Enterprise-wide modeling stresses top-down planning and links strategic business objectives with data usage and a database architecture (King and Zmud, 1981; Martin, 1989). Proposition 3. CSEs will evidence a move toward integrated or enterprise-wide data modeling that is congruent with linking important data sets to strategic business objectives.

## 3.4. IS manager’s role

The 1991 study found that the IS manager in CSEs primarily managed technical issues and remained detached from formulating business strategy. This manager was found to emphasize acquiring hardware and software, developing databases, and performing programmer and analyst functions.

In the USA, the IS manager’s role has evolved from that of a custodian over data processing to a higher-level information services officer who reports directly to the chief executive officer (CEO) (Doll and Ahmed, 1983; McNurlin and Sprague, 1998). It is accompanied by a shift in planning perspective from management of IS technology issues to management of IS usage in the strategic lines of business (Dixon and John, 1989; Parker and Benson, 1989).

Cash et al. (1988) point out that there is a relationship between the ‘distance’ of the IS manager from an organization’s CEO and the strategic value of IS. The IS function is perceived to be strategically important when the IS manager reports directly to the CEO, participates in the executive management meetings and communicates frequently and openly with the CEO.

In line with other change assumptions in IS functional areas, this study expected the following: Proposition 4. The IS manager will participate in formulating how IS can be used to support the CSE’s business strategy and have a close reporting relationship to the general manager.

## 4. Methodology

The research design that guided this work originates in the case study guidelines identified by Yin (1993, 1994), and is given an IS dimension by Cash and Lawrence (1989) as well as Lee et al. (1997). It attempts to be a descriptive multi-case study of IS in Chinese CSEs. It further adopts the positivist view of Eisenhardt (1989) of case study work in that it is based on predefined research propositions.

The cases considered are four public-sector enterprises located in Northeast China. Three are in Beijing, the capital of China, and one is in nearby Tianjin with its headquarters in Beijing. The four CSEs, two manufacturers and two service organizations, stand out as being among the top organizations of their type in all China. They are a steel company, a petrochemical company, a general hospital, and a harbor port authority.

The cases were selected because two of the Chinese researchers served as consultants, from 1984 to 1993, to all the CSEs. Each consulting project lasted between 4 and 5 years, and some projects overlapped.

To systematically investigate the status of IS in these CSEs, a team was set up with the two Chinese researchers and three researchers located in the US, who collaborated on field work in China and the USA. Field work involved open-ended interviews and participant observation, as well as work with document and archival records.

The original field work was followed by three return visits to the subject enterprises for additional data collection and reliability checks between Fall 1994 and 1995. The last wave of data collection consisted of loosely-structured interviews with the general manager, the top manager in charge of IS, IS staff professionals, user department heads and user department employees. Within any CSE, those interviewed ranged from being very familiar to somewhat familiar with IS functional activities and usage.

Additional data collection consisted of free-form taped narrative histories by the researcher consultants about their experiences at case companies. The triangulation made possible by multiple data collection methods provided stronger substantiation of the results reported in this study.

Constructs were operationalized as items on interview guides developed to collect data for this study. Additional forms provided uniformity in collecting background data and a technology profile on each organization.

To increase reliability, this study used consistency as well as member and outsider checking. Consistency across respondents encouraged confidence in interpretation of the data. Member checking involved going back to check interpretations with those who were being studied. Outsider checking involved having the final reports for this study evaluated by individuals in positions to pass judgment. One is a partner in a major international consulting organization who works in Southeast Asia and provides consulting services to Fortune 500 companies investing in China. Another is a business professor who retains ties to his native China for research and executive development.

## 5. Case descriptions

The following section begins with CSE case descriptions, focusing on the IS functional areas of interest to this study: IS planning support, IS planning methodology, data resource management, and the IS manager’s role. This background provides perspective for the discussion that follows.

## 5.1. Steel CSE

The steel CSE is one of the top two steel enterprises and is among the top five enterprises in China with 260 000 employees. It was picked to be a ‘model’ of public-enterprise reform in the late 1970s and served as a demonstration site for the ‘contract-responsibility system’ (Kaye, 1995). This led to international expansion, primarily with a Hong Kong company, into shipping, construction, electronics and tourism. With the CSE’s privileged access to raw materials and markets, success followed (Kaye, 1995).

From an industry perspective, this CSE is well positioned because China, along with Korea, are forecasted to dominate Asian steelmaking as the Japanese steel industry withers (Jacobson, 1994). For the present, however, it is part of a national industry known for producing substandard products (Kaye, 1995). To modernize its plant and operations, it is pursuing at least one major joint venture project with a USA steel maker (Iron Age New Steel, 1994).

The focal point of the CSE’s IS infrastructure is a modern IBM mainframe that functions as the central database repository for the enterprise. The database mainly contains public data, statistics and applications to produce government and management reports about products, sales, orders, inventory, finances, and personnel.

In 12 branches around Beijing, the enterprise uses IBM AS/400 servers for Novell networks linking 2000 PCs (486 and 386s). Every branch manager has a PC to access information from branch databases and sees a summary screen of data first thing every morning. The databases support management information systems for monitoring and controling sales, contracts, and profits, as well as costs, production, raw materials, spare parts, inventory, energy, and personnel. Decision support systems are available for both cost and production analysis and forecasting.

Data collection occurs through 5000 PCs distributed throughout the enterprise facilities. Data is entered in batches. Individual operational managers control the data that gets entered, processed, and reported. Because none of the data collection PCs are networked, output reports are manually passed to branch consolidation sites. An application in development will automate the flow of summary branch data to headquarters.

All databases contain files in many formats and duplicate data persists. While the researchers implemented an integrated database pilot project, the results of the integration have not yet been demonstrated.

A major planning effort in the mid-1980s was initiated to provide more information to managers at different organizational levels. This introduced a top-down IS planning methodology that decomposed critical factor monitoring along the organization hierarchy.

The top IS person at this steel enterprise reports to a Vice-General Manager who reports directly to the general manager. A subordinate described as the ‘day-to-day IS manager heads the ‘Electronics Department’ that consists of over 1000 hardware and software engineers and other staff. Most are involved in factory floor automation activity. This activity dominates computerization at the organization.

Two applications have special interest. One is a system to support market forecasting. It is expected to have increased importance as the steel CSE moves more aggressively into foreign sales with the USA as well as Southeast Asian countries. Another system in development tests the marketability of various grades of steel.

As a result of trips to Pittsburgh, the USA steel-making capital, among other locations, the enterprise principals are paying increased attention to external sources of information and consider it important input for strategic decision making purposes. Is systems to support this need are being formulated.

## 5.2. Petrochemical CSE

The petrochemical CSE is one of the top two petrochemical enterprises and ranks among the top ten enterprises in China. Its main products are oil and by-products such a gasoline, paraffin, coke, polymers, chemical fibers and synthetic fabrics. It employs 47 000 people.

China would like to become self-sufficient in petrochemicals and is encouraging technology transfer through joint ventures (Young and Wood, 1994a; Oil and Gas Journal, 1994; Wood, 1994). Currently, chemicals remain China’s largest import sector (Young and Wood, 1994b). Imports were essential in 1994 when some chemical CSEs were forced to halt production completely because of a lack of money to buy raw materials and pay electricity charges (Young and Wood, 1994b).

The petrochemical CSE studied uses Honeywell and Japanese computers to support almost all production equipment, including monitoring temperature, pressure, and rate of chemical reactions to reduce the risk of explosions. The CSE also uses IBM and DEC minicomputers as servers for management information systems that consist mainly of applications for production, sales, supply, inventory, and financial management. Because oil is such a critical material resource, the central government directly controls and monitors production and sales. IS needs to provide current situation reports and deliver them to government authorities on time. The entire IS function, therefore, revolves around meeting critical government reporting requirements.

Over 1000 PCs (486s) are partly networked for data access and acquisition. Like the steel CSE, data acquisition is batch oriented and under line management control. Output totals are manually passed to the next level of management.

Also like the steel CSE, data is scattered in many files, with duplication, throughout the enterprise. The concept of integrating databases remains locked in a planning stage.

IS is managed by the director of the ‘Computing Center’, who spends most of his time managing IS hardware and software acquisitions. This position is several levels below the general manager and non-influential in strategic issues.

The actual programming and system analysis, as well as database administration, activities are handled by deputy managers in a separate group called the ‘Automatic Department’.

## 5.3. General hospital

China’s health care system is loosely organized along ministerial and geographical lines. The Ministry of Public Health, along with other state level organizations, including the military and various industrial ministries, all administer their own systems (Pemble, 1992).

Reforms intended to encourage efficiency required health institutions to earn revenues sufficient to cover their operating costs. The policy resulted in ‘provider-encouraged consumption of health services. For example, the price for treatments or services set well above cost may be favored above those that cause losses (China Business Review, 1992). Institutions depend on this ‘profit’ to provide bonuses that compensate for low wages. Profits are also used to buy new equipment and support the workers’ welfare fund (China Business Review, 1992).

The general hospital studied for this project is owned by the military, services military and government staff, and is considered one of the top comprehensive, and best equipped, hospitals in China. It has 2000 employees, 800 of whom are doctors, and has 1300 beds. This not-for-profit, government-supported entity services 3000 patients a day. Some are fee-paying patients, or public traffic, taken in to earn extra revenue (Pemble, 1992).

The hospital CSE has some computer-controled medical equipment, such as scanners and imaging devices. Its IS infrastructure consists of two Hewlett Packard minicomputers linked to 20 terminals and three 486 PCs servers networked with 300 PCs (386s). Doctors, managers, and clerks access applications and retrieve information using the HP terminals and PCs. Major applications consist of management information systems for patients, equipment, and pharmaceutical inventory management, and statistics. There is also an application for fee calculation. These applications mainly service government reporting requirements.

Information comes from more than 40 databases, some of which are called ‘public data and include patient records and medical test results. Data duplication and information retrieval problems exist. Integrating databases, however, is not being considered although it has been proposed.

IS management is handled by the Director of the ‘Computing Room’ who implements recommendations handed down by ‘The Leading Group’. The Group consists of department heads of the hospital’s main units. The Group sets the IS budget and plan. The IS plan is designed in top-down military fashion, to satisfy the information and report needs of the hospital’s command hierarchy.

## 5.4. Harbor port authority

China’s main ports — stretching along 11 000 miles of coastline — look for major infrastructure upgrades with help from foreign joint ventures (Bow, 1995). The port facilities are widely regarded as inadequate to handle the trade volume increases projected for the coming decade (Lim, 1994; Lam, 1994; Bow, 1995). The government’s lack of investment in the water transport sector, combined with the inefficient use of port resources, results in traffic bottlenecks that can make other Asian ports look more attractive (Bow, 1995).

The port studied for this project services the northeast part of China. It is one of the top two ports in China with 20 000 employees. It handles over 25 million tons a year, mainly exporting grain and importing machine tools. It is the only one of the four CSEs evaluated that is not described as ‘rich’ by employees. It is not at the top of the pecking order to get government subsidies.

This port was part of a successful 1984 pilot project to decentralize away from direct control by the State Transportation Ministry (Bow, 1995). This expansive trend is evident in management’s interest in the global marketplace, demonstrated through yearly exploratory trips to ports in, for example, Singapore, Seattle, and Japan.

The CSE has eleven DEC minicomputers and 200 partly networked PCs (386s) located at Beijing headquarters and in branches. The PCs are mainly for data entry. Data duplication exits in over 100 public databases where incompatible file formats and data duplication are prevalent. An integrated database has been piloted, but results have not yet been demonstrated.

The applications portfolio includes programs for port-use fee calculation that is based on equipment and people employed for a task. The main part of the portfolio, however, consists of management information systems to inform the government about vessel activity in the port, as well as about receivables, payables, equipment and supplies inventory. There also is an application that enables sending an operations report by modem every 10 days to the Ministry of Transportation. A recently implemented decision support system optimizes pier allocation and equipment use.

The IS planning methodology used resembles that found in the other CSEs. It remains largely critical issues based with a focus on filling the needs of top managers before others lower on the organization’s hierarchy.

A Vice-General Engineer of Shipping is in charge of IS management and development. This position has no influence on managing the strategic lines of business.

## 6. Analysis

Table 1 summarizes findings in the four areas of IS planning support, IS planning methodology, data resource management and the IS manager’s role. As indicated, only the port authority and steel CSEs demonstrate the beginnings of change to strategic use of IS as found in the USA.

## 6.1. IS planning support

The first proposition stated that CSEs striving toward free market reform will adopt an impact approach to IS planning support, as evidenced by strategically oriented applications. This proposition was not supported.

As in 1991, an alignment approach to IS planning remains prevalent. The CSEs studied remain committed to support management operational and control decision-making. Their IS effort is focused on providing numbers for government reporting of services billed or products finished with supporting cost figures.

Some evidence, however, suggests a shift is in progress. The management of the port authority is beginning to identify applications for IS implementation designed to move the organization toward establishing competitive services. One example of movement in this direction is an application for the optimization of pier allocations. Evidence of an IS impact approach to IS planning support is also emerging at the steel company. Initiatives include the development of systems to test the marketability of various grades of steel, to support market forecasting, and to scan external markets.

## 6.2. IS planning methodologies

Proposition 2 stated that CSEs interested in market-oriented reform will invest in an IS planning methodology such as BPR to support their commitment to strategic change. The cases studied gave no evidence to support this.

As in 1991, in all CSEs some form of critical success factors was used to identify information systems needed to fulfill business requirements.

Critical success factors are communicated to IS from powerful operational managers, except at the hospital where it is a committee function. These sources drive the IS agenda.

IS must be responsive to support factors that managers know are critical to achieving target objectives. It precludes devoting the time required to design an applications portfolio using theoretically based planning methods that require a commitment of learning and development time backed by resources for implementation.

None of the CSEs were interested in exploring the benefits possible from a methodology like BPR. In particular, they were unwilling to explore any methodology that cut across organizational processes. Such a methodology would be disruptive to the in-place powerful hierarchies.

## 6.3. Data resource management

The third proposition stated that CSEs will evidence a move toward integrated or enterprise-wide data modeling that is congruent with linking important data sets to the strategic business activities. This proposition was not supported. Basically, sharing data among managers is not part of a CSE’s organizational culture. Data redundancy, therefore, is tolerated.

All organizations studied store narrowly focused data which are often replicated in files with incompatible formats. Nonetheless, all CSEs offer data access services to managers through the programmed interactive query feature built into database management systems such as DB2, Oracle, Powerbuilder, Access and Foxpro, among others.

Although the researcher-consultants introduced a form of database architecture planning and supported the design of database integration in all four cases, pilot implementations took hold in only the steel and harbor port authority CSEs. Neither have yet produced substantive results of the integration.

## 6.4. IS manager’s role

The final proposition (proposition 4) stated that an IS manager will participate in formulating how IS can be used to support a CSEs business strategy and have a close reporting relationship to the general manager. This proposition was not supported.

In all cases studied, the IS manager has no role in business strategy planning or decision-making, and ranks, except for one CSE, three or more levels below the general manager.

As in 1991, the IS manager is the motivation of opportunities for computerization and attempts to sell top management on the benefits of IS. But as in the earlier study, the urgency associated with striving to meet report requirements that justify annual production, sales or services seems to explain most of the pressure the IS manager faces that lead to a focus on technology rather than strategic use issues.

## 7. Discussion and implications

The primary finding of this study, that Chinese state enterprises demonstrate little evidence of strategic use of IS similar to that of the USA, replicates findings from a similar 1991 study. This reduces the probability that the present findings resulted from a temporary environmental factor. Also, because it includes organizations from different industries, it has the advantage of ruling out inter-industry differences in explaining IS functional support in CSEs.

Managers operating in, or planning, ventures with CSEs will find IS does not automate operational level systems, such as transaction processing, as their first step. Instead, they place highest priority on implementing management information systems that provide support for decision making to meet planned business performance objectives, such as achieving sales or service quotas.

Generally, investment in transaction systems is made with the goal of reducing labor costs by substituting capital for labor. In the case of China, inexpensive labor makes this unnecessary. In addition, as Davis (1997) notes, installing accounting applications in western countries were based on existing accounting discipline plus clerical cost displacement. In China, installing a computer system for accounting or inventory control would entail new work discipline and result in little cost displacement.

In the CSEs studied, computer-based technology was found to support production and machine tool processing more than information processing. This is consistent with findings that highly mechanistic contexts facilitate adoption (Wilson, 1966; Zaltman et al., 1973; Ouchi, 1979).

Powerful managers drive IS decisions about what is to be data processed and reported. They wield control over batch total accumulation and entry activity. IS support intervenes generally only to service the roll-up of figures to totals and to produce computer printed output. The heavy duty hardware power, therefore, remains underutilized, a finding that recurs in other studies (Tate and Maier, 1987; Franz et al., 1991).

Other findings of interest to managers and researchers grounded through this study are as follows.

• The size of a CSE does not appear to significantly influence its use of IS for strategic purposes. All the CSEs in this study were much larger than those in the 1991 study and the results are similar.

The modernization or quantity of the technology available in a CSE are no indication of an advanced strategic IS policy. All the CSEs in this study use far more advanced, in quality and quantity, IS resources than those in 1991, with no discernible difference.

• IS managers in CSEs with exposure to the global market economy will be the most experienced in using IS for strategic applications.

• Foreign enterprises planning to work in China and using information technology for strategic purposes should be willing to educate their Chinese managers and work force about what this requires.

While government policy effectively promotes use of information technology as a key objective in achieving modernization goals (Tate and Maier, 1987), management has been assessed as lacking the training needed to understand computer applications (Ishman et al., 1994). Furthermore the work force itself has been described as virtually computer illiterate (Franz et al., 1991), providing a strong barrier to receptivity.

Other cultural impediments to both management and organizational acceptance of innovation prevail. For example, innovation exists in organizations where members are encouraged to adopt broad perspectives and develop access to relevant information sources (Aiken and Hage, 1968; Allen, 1977). Such an open climate does not characterize CSE management. Also, China’s organizational culture awards prestige according to the number of workers supervised. Anecdotal evidence tells of a computer center manager prevented from implementing an accounting system by the chief accountant because of the potential reduction in his work force (Ishman et al., 1994).

Further, the Chinese system of communication is highly social and informal. The concept of a paper trail is foreign. Access to information is viewed more as a privilege than a necessity.

## 8. Proposed approach to future studies

With insight drawn from the cases, it is possible to propose an approach to help both practitioners and researchers evaluate IS in CSEs. Fig. 1 is structured, starting at the bottom, with an input, processing, output dynamic. It assumes that whatever IS investment is made and processed through the various IS functional areas considered, it results in an output called IS assets. These assets can be used, in a comparative analysis, to estimate a CSE’s strategic adjustment to reform itself in the direction of growth toward a free market economy.

Markus and Soh (1993) and Leow et al. (1996) find information technology (IT) investment is related to a firm’s performance only in relation to its ability to convert that investment into IT assets consisting of applications and infrastructure. This intermediate outcome, according to Weill (1992), Markus and Soh (1993), and Leow et al. (1996), is a significant moderator of the relationship between strategic IT investment and firm performance.

Focusing on a CSE’s IS assets, we suggest, could serve as an intermediate outcome to evidence compliance with a perceived goal, such as a strategic adjustment in the direction of growth toward a free market economy.

The direction of strategic adjustment in this approach builds on the study of Franz and Klepper (1994) of state enterprises in another developing economy. Their classification provides the range anchors: survival strategy at one end to signify minimal change, and growth at the other end to signify maximum change to a global customer-driven strategy that is evident in free market economies.

The present study provides preliminary evidence to suggest that both the steel and port authority would be located toward the growth end of the strategic adjustment continuum while the petrochemical and hospital CSEs would situate closer to survival.

For example, the steel and the port authority enterprises have strategic applications in their IS portfolio. Such intent is apparent in the steel CSE’s application to test the marketability of various grades of steel. It marks a departure from providing a commanded product line to doing what market-driven companies do: find out what customers want to buy. Another demonstration of strategic adjustment is an application to support market forecasting. In development is yet another application that supports external market scanning.

The harbor port authority also demonstrates movement toward growth as evidenced mainly by a major application to optimize pier allocations for cargo using its busy facility. By focusing on the CSE’s primary activity, this application aims to provide more value to customers in order to keep them from patronizing alternative Pacific Rim ports.

In the study of Leow et al. (1996), the aspect of IT infrastructure that was a significant intermediate outcome was the electronic linkages a firm has with business partners, such as parent, subsidiaries and government agencies. This dimension is evident in the growthoriented CSEs in this study. For example, the port authority has a modem link to its parent Ministry. The steel CSE is developing links between its branch subsidiaries and headquarters and is exploring external links for market scanning.

Pennings and Harianto (1992) extend the idea of linkages that has implications here. They find the more a firm commits itself to technological networking, the greater its propensity to innovate. Once again, in this study, both the steel and port authority demonstrate active outreach, especially through exploration of international competitive sites. Both seek international investment and the technology transfer it brings.

By contrast, neither strategically oriented applications or infrastructure electronic linkages were major presence at either the petrochemical or hospital CSEs. From such a perspective, therefore, these CSEs would fall on the survival end of the strategic adjustment continuum.

This proposed approach for focusing on the IS assets output by a CSE’s information systems function could prove insightful for organizations looking for benchmarks on how a CSE is strategically adjusting to market-oriented reforms.

## 9. Summary and conclusion

This study evaluated four areas of IS in four Chinese state enterprises: IS planning support, IS planning methodology, data resource management, and the IS manager’s role. While it finds IS is not used as strategically as it is in the USA, it does find evidence of emerging change. It further proposes an approach to evaluate IS in future practitioner or research studies that would focus a lens on determining a CSE’s strategic direction.

The current study has several implications for future research. Desirable are multiple studies using the same propositions with the proposed extensions to provide a more substantial body of evidence. Studies focusing on homogeneous organizations (for example, by industry sector) are needed to better understand the influence of environmental and organizational variables. The findings from different industry or organizational sectors could be integrated to develop a general theory of IS support in Chinese CSEs. A potentially illuminating research area is how IS impacts organizational performance. Finally, studies need to examine organizational contingencies that moderate IS support such as structures of control and interests served. Included in this would be the variety of implications Chinese management culture and the Chinese culture itself brings to the IS environment.

One area of special interdisciplinary interest to explore is what controls are replacing the old, centralized controls? Are there any replacements at all? An exploratory study might consider the role of legalism as a replacement control in transitioning CSEs (Sitkin and Bies, 1993).

Our research contributes to an emerging area that focuses on the importance of understanding IS support in Chinese CSEs. For organizations planning to do business in China, a country forecasted to be the world’s biggest economy in the twenty-first century, an assessment of how IS is used there has significant bottom-line impact (Naisbitt, 1994).
