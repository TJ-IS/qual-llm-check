# An exploratory study investigating transnational information systems

A.L.M. Cavaye

Department of Information Systems, University of Southern Queensland, Australia

Received 1 November 1997; revised 1 March 1998; accepted 1 March 1998

## Abstract

Transnational information systems (TIS) are information systems that cross organisational as well as national boundaries. This paper describes an exploratory study which gathered case study data from four TIS projects. The data was used to describe problems encountered during TIS development and management and to discuss solutions found for those problems. Problems and solutions were categorised according to domain (technical, organisational, and institutional issues) and according to development phase (identification, building, implementation, and maintenance). It was found that organisations tend to address difficulties by lowering the level of aspiration of the TIS rather than addressing problems head-on. © 1998 Elsevier Science B.V.

Keywords: Transnational information systems; IS development; IS management

## 1. Introduction

Transnational information systems (TIS) are information systems that transcend both national and organisational boundaries. Developments in information and communication technology make it feasible for many processes and tasks that cross company and national boundaries to be supported by information systems. At the same time, economic and political developments create demand for transnational information systems. Benefits of TIS include greater efficiency (in the form of lower costs and increased speed of inter-organisational, international processes) and effectiveness (due to new, improved ways of achieving the processes' objectives). Examples of TIS include international payments systems, systems linking public administration agencies, international traffic control, and logistics systems linking different countries.

Demand for TIS is clearly evident within the European Union in the context of a European market for persons, goods, capital and services. Similar developments can be seen on a global scale owing to increased internationalisation. The total number of TIS currently in use of under development is not known. However, within the public sector in the European Union alone about 30 transnational systems are operational or under development (Kroon, 1997). Add to those the systems in use in the private sector and those outside the European Union context and the total number of TIS is likely to considerable.

Development and management of TIS is not straightforward. The inter-organisational and international nature of these systems introduces various difficulties and problems. One major reason for this may be that the participants are independent in most aspects and autonomous with regard to their own proprietary information systems. Another potential cause of difficulties is the international context which introduces cultural, legal and language difficulties among participants.

Most TIS have a number of local, intra-organisational components and have an international, inter-organisational component (see Fig. 1). The local (national) components of the TIS are usually controlled by individual organisations within a country; the transnational component is superimposed on various individual, local systems to create the TIS. There is an overlap or connection between the local, organisational system and the transnational system. Development of this connection and management of the overall transnational information system were the subject of the investigation reported in this paper.

Most individual TIS to date are being developed on an ad hoc basis. There are many methods for system development. However, currently there are no known general techniques, tools or guidelines to aid development of information systems involving independent partners from different cultures and nationalities. Practical experience and empirical research show that many problems and solutions at first glance appear to be specific to a particular development effort, but often they are not unique at all. It should be possible to accumulate evidence and to start compiling generalised knowledge to aid the development and management of TIS.

This paper describes a study which attempted to take some early steps towards guidelines for the development and management of TIS. In view of the fact that very little existing research is available about TIS, this study focused on gathering information from practice as a basis for theory building. The study set out to not only describe problems during TIS development and management, but also to identify solutions tried and found useful in practice.

The paper proceeds as follows. In Section 2, relevant literature is briefly reviewed and categories for research issues are identified. Next, in Section 3, the research method is explained. Section 4 summarises findings from the empirical study of four cases. Lastly, in Section 5, findings are discussed.

## 2. Research issues concerning transnational information systems

A search through the literature revealed a lack of studies addressing the combined inter-organisational and international IS setting. There was no existing research model to guide this study. Hence, previous research was used not to build a research model, but to identify issues likely to impinge on the development and management of TIS. For the purpose of this study we decided to investigate TIS development and management according to two dimensions: (1) organisational domain; and (2) phases in the system development process.

## 2.1. Issues identified in related research areas

One research area in IS concerns the study of global systems. These are information systems linking parts of multinationals located in different countries. Several books (Deans and Kane, 1992; Palvia et al., 1996; Roche, 1992) and journals (Journal of Global Information and Technology Management; Journal of Global Information Management) are dedicated to this research area highlighting the main drawbacks and difficulties when developing systems that cross national boundaries. Previous work on information systems for multinationals highlights issues concerning any systems that cross national boundaries: problems with language, legal differences (Oz, 1994), different cultures (Shore and Venkatachamlam, 1996), different abilities in terms of IT practices (Grover et al., 1994), differing sophistication of IT infrastructure and telecommunications (Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991), and different views concerning appropriate IS structure and configuration (Cheung and Burn, 1994).

Apart from research about global systems, there is also a substantial body of writing about inter-organisational systems. These are information systems that cross company boundaries. Much of the early discussion about inter-organisational systems (IOS) focused on competitive benefits of developing IOS (Porter and Millar, 1985) and on facilitating and inhibiting factors at different phases in the development process (Reich and Benbasat, 1990). More recently, research has taken on a more thoughtful note, pointing out that not all IOS provide benefits to all participants (Webster, 1995), that IOS may involve risks and conflict (Kumar and van Dissel, 1996) and that IOS involve management of relationships among the participants (Meier, 1995). Previous research suggests that major difficulties concerning systems crossing organisational boundaries include problems of responsibility, co-ordination, sharing of costs and benefits, and concerns about competitive position.

Previous research on global systems and on inter-organisational systems is clearly relevant for the study of transnational information systems.

## 2.2. TIS development and management issues relating to organisational domain

One way of studying TIS issues is to distinguish issues in the technical, organisational and institutional domains. Parsons (1960) first discussed these distinct, but complementary, areas of responsibility and control; he argues that they are three quite separate domains within an organisation. Challenges to TIS development are not necessarily purely technical, but are also likely to involve difficulties of co-ordination and change management. Parson's identification of different domains provides a useful framework for categorising difficulties and potential solutions during the process of development and management of TIS.

Parsons used the term ‘technical domain’ to denote the technical sub-organisation which includes the control over data, procedures and people as well as the technical aspects of hardware, software and communications. The ‘organisational domain’ refers to the way in which individual organisations need to adapt organisational structures, processes, regulations, skills, standards and ways of working to conform to the TIS. Issues in the ‘institutional domain’ concern policy identification, political or competitive power struggles, and role models. In addition, institutional issues include the formulation and development of decision making arrangements among participating organisations in order to ensure that the new order is accepted, adhered to and continues to operate effectively.

2.3. TIS development and management issues according to phase of systems development process

TIS issues can also be categorised according to the various phases of the systems development process—from identification of the need for a TIS through to the management of an operational TIS. It is likely that different problems surface in different phases of the system development process and that not all problems are evident up-front. Hence, a categorisation of potential problems according to phase of development was considered to be useful for the purpose of identifying guidelines for TIS development and management.

Various authors provide different names for separate development phases, divide the system development process into different numbers of phases, and describe the development process with or without iteration and feedback loops. However, authors generally agree that: (1) the need for a system should be identified and requirements have to be drawn up; (2) logical and physical design and building of the system has to take place; (3) the system has to be implemented; and (4) the operational system has to be managed, updated and adapted over time.

## 3. Research methodology

Although there is no literature specifically addressing transnational information systems, there is substantial practical experience with such systems. For instance, Kroon (1997) has identified 30 TIS in the public sector in the European Union. This experience and knowledge tends to remain with the organisations involved unless an attempt is made to collate and generalise the lessons learnt by individual organisations. It was decided to conduct an empirical study, gathering data from practice to start building theory.

## 3.1. Selecting the research method

Case research was selected as an appropriate research method for a number of reasons. Firstly, because of the lack of existing information about, transnational information systems, this would be a theory building exercise. Carrying out a number of case studies helps in the process of theory building because of the rich contextual data that is collected during case research (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 1994). Secondly, for each system studied data needed to be gathered from different perspectives. Case methods would enable the researcher to personally collect the information from different informants and would give the researcher the freedom to determine if further information or further avenues for data gathering were required. Thirdly, case research would enable us to uncover issues and relationships not envisaged prior to starting data collection (Glaser and Strauss, 1967). In all, studying the development processes of existing TIS, being guided by existing constructs, keeping an open mind to uncovering new relationships, and subsequently conceptualising from findings was considered to be a sound method for investigating the phenomenon of transnational information systems.

Selection of cases for this exploratory study was based on several criteria (summarised in Table 1).

The unit of analysis was a transnational information system (TIS), but individual cases had to be sufficiently dissimilar to collectively provide a rich picture of the problem area. Hence, the first criteria was that cases would be selected from different sectors. Secondly, TIS in the cases had to support a primary process in the organisations; TIS that are peripheral to an organisation might not be accorded high importance and as such might not yield adequate and sufficient data for the research project. Thirdly, cases had to involve systems which were operational even if only in an early version. The project aimed to investigate development and management of TIS; it would not be possible to cover all aspects unless the TIS had completed all development stages. Hence, the criterion that systems had to be operational.

A fourth criterion was that at least one TIS would have to involve a non-automated system. By including a non-automated TIS among the cases to be studied, the study would be able to ascertain whether TIS development difficulties are related purely to technology or not. A further criterion was that cases need not be highly successful; in fact, the preference was for TIS cases that had encountered or were facing difficulties. Less successful cases were likely to yield a better insight in the difficulties concerning development and management of the systems. A final criterion was the requirement that organisations had to be willing and able to participate in the study.

## 3.2. Gathering the research team

A multi-disciplinary research team was put together consisting of experienced researchers from computer science, information systems and organisational science $^{1}$ . The reason for a multi-disciplinary team was to strengthen the research by drawing on knowledge and experience from a variety of disciplines related to the research topic. Nelson and Clark (1994) suggest that a multi-disciplinary approach is particularly appropriate in international settings. The multi-disciplinary team approach meant that the study had to be prepared meticulously.

In order to co-ordinate the individual efforts a number of documents were prepared (see Table 2).

Following Eisenhardt (1989), Miles and Huberman (1984) and Yin (1994), a case protocol, an interview list and a framework for writing up individual cases were drawn up and agreed upon. The interview list highlighted the research issues that each researcher needed to address in every case setting and to indicate the specific questions that the researcher would have to find answers to. The list could be used by researchers as a 'master' from which to compose individual interview guides for face-to-face sessions with informants.

The framework for individual case analysis included a number of items. Each case analysis wag to provide a factual description of the events and experiences concerning the case. Then, an evaluation of the project's major research issues (identified in the proposal) would be included. Apart from these pre-determined components of the case analysis, each researcher was encouraged to include any extra, noteworthy piece of information in a separate section to the case analysis. This latter addition enabled the presentation of issues that had not been identified prior to entering the case, but that had bubbled up during data collection as important and relevant research issues.

In addition, a list of notions and terms was drawn up. Individual researchers came from different disciplines which meant that all had different definitions for even the most basic of terms. Early on in the project it became obvious that individual researchers held different views about the meaning of concepts and constructs in the study. Hence, the need for discussion (and agreement) on the terms to be used and their definition. The product of such discussion was a 'list of notions and terms'. From that moment on there was a reference document detailing agreed-upon definitions of major, relevant research constructs. The list included constructs like information, information system, transnational information system, local system, local information system, and others. The list was referred to constantly.

## 3.3. Gaining entry to cases

The final criterion for case selection implied that case organisations had to be willing and able to participate in the study. The case had to provide the opportunity to investigate all aspects of TIS development and management. It was necessary to be able to gain access not only to the local (national and intra-organisational) case site, but also to the transnational site where the international and inter-organisational component was managed.

Through personal contacts four cases were identified: EUCARIS, TRANSIT, GEIS and TAPS (details of the cases are provided in Appendix A). EUCARIS is a TIS that enables personnel in different countries to have access to other national car and driving licence registration systems; the primary aim of EUCARIS is to lower the number of stolen cars being registered in another country as a legal vehicle. TRANSIT is the TIS that is being developed to speed up and facilitate custom administration concerning goods that are imported into the European Union and to which a suspense regime is applied; TRANSIT should also lead to more effective fraud detection and prevention. GEIS is the case concerning the collection and aggregation of international greenhouse gas emissions; the aim of the system is to monitor individual country's emissions which should help to stabilise emission levels. TAPS is the acronym for transnational ambulatory payment systems. This is an embedded case where four different systems were studied; each system enables private customers to make non-cash payments internationally. The aim of TAPS is to facilitate trans-border payment processing.

The major sponsor of the study in each case organisation provided names of potential informants for study of the national system. The sponsor also facilitated researcher entry into the transnational case site. Since this was an exploratory study only one national system was investigated for each case. As the study took place in the Netherlands, the national component studied was the Dutch information system feeding into and connecting with the transnational component of each TIS. The transnational components of the TIS could all be studied within Europe: the EUCARIS transnational system is managed from the Netherlands; the TIS for TRANSIT and TAPS are managed in Belgium; the TIS for GEIS was managed from Brussels (for the European Union) and Geneva (for the United Nations).

## 3.5. Analysing the case data

Individual case studies were written up, read by other researchers on the team and discussed. Once the team felt the individual cases were written and analysed effectively, the cases were sent to informants for review. Where possible the case study and the conclusions drawn by the researcher were discussed with informants in a subsequent interview. It was not possible to re-visit informants in Brussels and in Geneva (the international sites), but we were able to discuss the case studies with informants involved with the national process and the national IS. The individual case reviews resulted in minor corrections.

Cross case analysis was discussed by the multi-disciplinary team and an analysis framework was agreed upon. One of the researchers carried out the initial cross case analysis; all findings and results were discussed during research team meetings in order to check and refine the conclusions. Insights from the organisational science member of the team were particularly valuable; his knowledge about organisational politics and about consensus-building helped to make sense of our findings.

## 4. Research findings concerning TIS issues

For each case three documents were prepared: a full description of the case containing the history and facts concerning the case; a completed interview guide with answers from all informants to the various questions in the interview guide; and a single case analysis. These documents were subsequently used for cross case comparison.

The cases were compared along the two basic dimensions mentioned earlier: the particular domains of individual issues (technical, organisational or institutional) and the phases of development where particular TIS issues surfaced. In addition to these pre-determined dimensions for data collection and analysis, issues emerged during the course of the study as data was collected and collated. Findings of the cross case comparison are summarised below.

## 4.1. Issues according to organisational domain

Table 3 presents a summary of the problems faced by the four TIS categorised according to the technical, organisational and institutional nature of the problem. The summary in Table 3 shows that development and management of TIS concerns difficulties in all three categories of problems: there are technical as well as organisational and institutional problems. The table also shows how case organisations coped with the particular problem. An interesting point to note is that problems from one domain are not necessarily solved by solutions from the same domain. In the four cases technical solutions were used regularly to address organisational and institutional problems.

Technical issues need not be permanent bottlenecks. Difficulties like lack of sophistication in data collection abilities or lack of IT infrastructure tend to be dissolved over time as individual organisations or countries develop their systems. Similarly, technological limitations tend to be overcome as time passes and new developments become commercially available. Diversity of systems among partners is also overcome by increasing standardisation of IT products. Case evidence suggests that technical problems are real, but that they are not necessarily long-term difficulties.

Organisational issues are of a different kind. Inequality in costs and benefits of TIS and the lack of transnational bodies to develop and manage TIS are co-ordination and co-operation issues. Co-ordination of individual organisations is necessary for the concept of a meaningful TIS to evolve. Case evidence showed that, in order to gain co-operation of individual organisations, it is important to ensure benefits are shared or that costs are borne by those that stand to gain most of the TIS. Co-ordination among the organisations is necessary for the development and management of the actual TIS itself. The four cases highlighted the fact that the absence of transnational organisations inhibits fast or easy TIS development; a transnational body is particularly valuable for co-ordinating day-to-day TIS affairs.

Institutional problems may have been latent prior to TIS development and are highlighted during the discussions about development and management of the TIS. Such problems are not necessarily directly concerned with the TIS and may affect TIS indirectly. Clearly, existing problems have to be addressed if the TIS is to be successful. An example of an existing problem that affects TIS indirectly is ‘disagreement among various local organisations'. (Potential) participants of the TIS can have disagreements about data, technical matters or policy with parties outside the TIS and such disagreements can affect the ability of the participants to participate fully. Political struggles or business competition among TIS participants or between TIS participants and outsiders are important determining factors for the success of the TIS. A system that is sound from a technical point of view may turn out to be not feasible owing to institutional issues.

## 4.2. Issues during the various phases of the system development

The development process of every TIS moves through a number of recognisable phases; each of the phases may cause and may have to address different problems. Table 4 summarised the problems (and solutions) faced by the four cases categorised according to the various system life cycle phases.

Case evidence shows that problems in the early stages of development are not necessarily addressed head-on. Instead, development may be delayed, development may continue with a few participants only, the TIS may be built allowing for various levels of entry or various levels of functionality; implementation may be phased. During the ongoing management and maintenance phase those original problems tend to re-appear. It is during the ongoing management phase that there the wish to upgrade the system (to include more participants or greater functionality) surfaces.

## 4.2.1. System identification

Difficulties in the identification phase appear to be inversely related to the apparent urgency of the problem that is to be addressed by the TIS. When the issue is considered important and urgent, identification of and agreement about the concept of a TIS is relatively easily reached. However, when the problem that TIS addresses is not considered a high priority, difficulties seem to mount up and the TIS does not get beyond the ‘nice idea’ phase. ‘Delay’ was a common solution to problems at this early development phase.

## 4.2.2. Designing and building of the TIS

The difficulties at this phase of development are primarily technical in nature. The technical issues are made more complicated because of the great number of participants and their different objectives. The difficulties are addressed by finding a technical solution or by improved organisation of the development process. Technical solutions included decentralised system configuration and allowing for various the levels of entry. Although the problems in this development phase might seem difficult, finding solutions to the problems did not seem a major issue in any of the cases.

## 4.2.3. Implementation

When the TIS has been properly identified and has been agreed upon by participants, implementation does not pose major difficulties. Implementation may be laborious and costly (as in the TAPS case), but user resistance to implementation of the four cases was not high. In both the TRANSIT and the GEIS case there was large diversity among the participants when it comes to informational and technical sophistication and facilities. This inhibited implementation of the full TIS by all participants. Instead, a phased approach to TIS implementation was taken by allowing lower levels of entry or by allowing late entry for less sophisticated partners.

## 4.2.4. Ongoing management and maintenance

Not all of the four TIS in the study had reached this phase of development. However, evidence from the EUCARIS and GEIS cases suggest that management and maintenance issues include ensuring that participants make sure their local systems interface correctly with the TIS; enforcing standards and continuing use of the TIS; developing new functionality and increasing the scope, of the TIS.

## 4.3. TIS issues which emerged during the study

Apart from issues which we had anticipated and had specifically looked for in the cases, issues emerged during the course of the study as empirical evidence was collated.

A major issue which emerged was the level of aspiration of the TIS. Under rational norms we would expect organisations to aim at maximising the opportunity offered by a TIS, building a system to optimise efficiency and to maximise shared benefits. In practice, though, successful TIS organisations show satisfying behaviour by adopting a TIS which is acceptable or ‘good enough’. TIS development involves difficulties and TIS participants address difficulties by lowering the level of aspiration of the TIS.

From a systems development perspective, an ‘optimum’ TIS requires high levels of cooperation among partners and involves consensus on many aspects of the system. In the context of co-operation among independent partners it is not always feasible to achieve high levels of consensus and commitment. There are many potential disagreements and mismatches which might mean that the optimum TIS is never built. Rather than facing the difficulties head-on and risking failure, efforts are made to find a compromise which all potential partners feel comfortable with. Hence, instead of the ideal TIS, a less than optimum TIS is developed and implemented.

Evidence of the four cases indicates that there are a number of strategies which are used to reach consensus among participants about a proposed TIS. The most drastic strategy is to decide to delay development; this strategy was used twice in the TRANSIT case. Other strategies include limiting the number of participants to those who are most enthusiastic and/or most capable of participating in the TIS (EUCARIS) and lowering the level of proposed functionality of the TIS (TAPS). Another way forward is to allow different levels of entry or different levels of functionality for different participants (EUCARIS, GEIS).

Participants may have agreed to a diminished level of aspiration for the TIS, but the initial problems tend to resurface at a later stage. When the ‘simplified’ system is operational, attempts are usually made to improve and enhance the system by adding functionality, adding further partners, increasing the scope of the TIS. At that stage the problems which led to a reduced level of aspiration in the first place can surface again. It would seem that reducing the level of aspiration at early stages of development may postpone the moment of facing difficulties with the TIS, but it does not eliminate the problems.

## 5. Discussion

Transnational information systems (TIS) combine aspects of both global (or international) systems and inter-organisational systems. It would seem appropriate at this stage to discuss whether the international or the inter-organisational nature of the systems posed particular difficulties in the four cases.

The usual problems of operating across national boundaries were evident in all cases. However, many organisations and the people within them recognised and accepted the international differences and were willing to work towards overcoming potential difficulties. The international context did not present particular difficulties for TIS development and management in the four cases investigated.

It is likely that the European Union context of the four cases in the study reported here may have moderated the usual difficulties associated with development of systems that cross national boundaries. There is some previous research that poses international system development is not significantly different from local, domestic system development (Emery, 1991). Our case evidence does not support this suggestion. Other research suggests that development of systems to be used in countries which share cultural elements is likely to face less difficulties than systems development for use by more diverse and varied countries (Tractinsky and Jarvenpaa, 1995).

All cases in this study were TIS developed in the European Union. This presents a unique context that all TIS had in common. In Europe individual countries and cultures are used to differences among themselves and have learnt to live with those differences whilst in close proximity to each other; within the European Union there is a tradition of several decades of international co-operation. The European context of the TIS cases may have made it easier for participants to find solutions to international difficulties; it may be more difficult to develop TIS in a wider context.

Our case findings would indicate that there are more and more difficult problems relating to the inter-organisational context of TIS. Some of the inter-organisational issues are technical and systems issues—and in all four cases these types of problems were overcome fairly easily. Other issues concern autonomy and responsibility of organisations and equality among organisations. It is these latter issues which are difficult to address. Inter-organisational information exchange requires common decisions, consensus and commitment. Individual organisations may feel that their autonomy is threatened or that they are not gaining benefits on a par with other organisations. In such a situation, an organisation can withdraw participation or can become a difficult partner in the TIS. In each of the four cases, difficulties concerning autonomy, control, and responsibility were found and each of these problems proved to be difficult to solve.

One of the cases (GEIS) concerned a TIS which was non-automated and did not make extensive use of information technology to pass information across borders. The selection of a non-automated case had been a deliberate one; this would allow us to determine whether the IT component of a TIS was a major or the only problem for development and management of these types of systems. It was found that the non-automated TIS encountered many of the same problems as the three other TIS cases. It would appear, therefore, that many of the difficulties facing TIS development are not just related to the IT component of the system. This finding confirms and underlines the fact that many challenges in the TIS development process do not fall in the technical domain, but are related to organisational and institutional issues.

Development and management of TIS requires the wisdom to make the right trade-off between desirability and feasibility of solutions. In each case participants had to try and find the right balance between, on the one hand, the benefits, technical possibilities and positive effects of implementing a TIS and, on the other hand, the costs, changes in power, new responsibilities associated with TIS involvement. In some cases, a transnational organisation may be able to encourage individual partners to continue with TIS participation even when individual organisations are not convinced of the benefits (this happened in the TRANSIT case). In other cases, where there is no existing transnational organisation to act as mediator and co-ordinator, individual organisations have to make their own decisions about the trade-off.

## 6. Concluding comments

This study set out to investigate issues concerning the development and management of TIS. Focus was not only on problems, but also, and especially, on solutions to those problems. The overall objective was to take initial steps towards the development of guidelines to aid TIS development and management. Data was collected from four European cases.

Case findings showed that the technical component of TIS does not pose major difficulties. Greater challenges are presented in the form of organisational and institutional issues. It may interest practitioners that we found technical solutions can be used to alleviate some of the organisational problems. For instance, a decentralised TIS configuration can calm potential fears of lack of autonomy for individual participants.

The early phases of development appear to be crucial in terms of the level of aspiration of the TIS. According to our case evidence an optimum or ideal TIS is easily compromised during discussions prior to actual design and building of the system. TIS development requires consensus and co-operation among participants. In the four cases in this study TIS participants seemed eager to avoid or to overcome disagreements. This meant that participants readily agreed to lower the level of aspiration of the TIS. However, addressing difficulties by lowering the levels of functionality or participation does not solve the problem: at later phases of development the difficulties re-appear.

Earlier in the paper, two areas of IS research were identified as contributing to a study on transnational information systems: research on global IS and research on interorganisational IS. In many ways, TIS resemble systems in a large, decentralised, multinational organisation. The difference between TIS and global systems is that TIS are built between completely autonomous organisations. This means that there is no means at all of enforcing TIS development; our findings show that in all cases TIS development depends on the goodwill and consensus between participants. TIS also resemble interorganisational IS (IOS). The difference between TIS and IOS is that TIS includes an international aspect while IOS do not necessarily cross national boundaries. In our study, we found that many of the problems involved in IOS development are also found in TIS development; in our cases the main difficulties affecting TIS development were related to inter-organisational issues. Having studied TIS we would suggest that TIS are a special case of IOS, rather than a separate type of system.

This study was case based and focused on TIS cases in Europe. It was found that the challenge of the international nature of TIS was not a major issue and that problems relating to international issues could be overcome. The European Union context may have played a moderating role in this respect. Further empirical evidence from cases in other settings may indicate if this finding was context dependent.

The study can be extended and improved by studying TIS not only from one national location, but by investigating several national systems feeding into a TIS. This would involve researchers in various countries studying the same TIS from different country perspectives. Combining findings from several empirical studies should enable us to formulate guidelines and concepts to aid TIS development and management.

## Appendix A. Brief descriptions of the four cases

## Appendix A.1. EUCARIS—European car registration information system (Zuurmond, 1996)

The incidence of car theft has increased alarmingly during the past 10 years. Often stolen cars are taken across European borders to be re-registered (and thus white-washed) in another country. Registration authorities are obliged to register a car unless they can be prove within a short period of time that the car is registered elsewhere and, therefore, is a stolen car. When in doubt about the legality of a car that is being offered for registration, authorities traditionally write, phone or fax to foreign registration authorities. This process is inadequate as it is time-consuming and difficult to manage.

Computerised access to registration databases in other countries would facilitate the process of checking for legal registrations. It would be much faster, more accurate, and more likely to combat re-registration of stolen cars. The Dutch car registration authority conceived the idea for building a TIS to enable instantaneous, electronic searching and checking of car registration data across Europe; the system was called EUCARIS (European car registration information system).

Very early on, it became clear that it would not be possible nor necessary to integrate the registration systems of various countries into one large system. There is, at present, no single European car numbering system to identify individual cars Europe-wide. Also, current registration systems each use different data definitions which complicates the possible development of a single system. In addition, individual registration authorities value their autonomy and a central registration system would threaten that autonomy. Hence, it was decided to develop a relatively simple system: a common shell to provide a query interface to the separate systems of participating countries.

EUCARIS has been operational since 1995. Currently six countries are on-line (the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, UK, Hungary and Poland); 14 other countries are preparing to go on-line; another 10 have enquired about participation.

## Appendix A.2. TRANSIT—European union customs ‘goods in transit’ information system (Mantelaers, 1996)

Goods that are brought into the European Union (EU) are subject to taxes and duties. Since payment is not necessarily required at the point of entry, European customs officials have to keep track of goods moving within Europe and, in particular, have to be able to trace what duties and how much taxes have been paid.

Building a TIS to computerise this international customs process was first considered in the early 1980s. A properly working TIS would facilitate tracking and tracing of goods; it would enable immediate comparison of goods and duties paid, speeding up the clearance process; it would produce readable, accurate and up-to-date information at any customs office. The private sector would also benefit because of faster clearance procedures. Disagreements among different EU partners withheld a TIS from moving beyond the ‘nice idea’ phase.

The current project to build a TIS, called TRANSIT, is the third attempt at developing computerised procedures. This time a serious effort has been put into TIS development: fraud problems have reached alarming proportions with fraud concerning goods in transit within the EU adding up to billions of \$US. Computerisation of the transit procedures has now been placed high on the list of priorities of the EU.

EDI messages have been designed and national gateways are being set up. In order to account for diversity in local systems and to respect autonomy of individual customs agencies no attempt has been made to integrate the various systems. Instead, for each country one customs application host is built which connects the central TIS system with local systems in various countries. In this way, local systems and procedures need not be adapted to a single European-wide system; similarly, the diversity in local systems and procedures needs not affect the central TIS. At the end of 1996 the TRANSIT project is in its pilot phase; customs authorities aim to have the system fully operational as soon as possible.

## Appendix A.3. GEIS—greenhouse gas emission information system (Cavaye, 1996)

The global community has become increasingly concerned with the emission of dangerous gases into the atmosphere and the effect of those gases on the environment. It is generally recognised that combating dangerous emissions should not be a unilateral effort from individual countries. Sustainable reduction in emissions can only be achieved by commitment from and co-operation among countries. In the early 1990s both the EU and the world community at large (through the United Nations) have agreed to address the rise in greenhouse emissions.

Information is a vital ingredient in any effort to combat dangerous (greenhouse gas) emissions: information is required about the state of climatic change in different countries, about technologies to combat dangerous emissions, and about projects to counter the amount of and effects of dangerous emissions. This information has to be passed from individual countries to transnational organisations which collate and monitor the data.

A separate organisation (within the UN) was set up to deal with information about emissions and climatic change. The system for international information exchange is non-computerised, but involves detailed agreements about the exact data to be passed on to the transnational organisation. Guidelines have been written and accepted as the standard procedures and rules according to which each nation will collect and present international greenhouse gas information. Not all countries are able to collect detailed information; hence, in the cases of developing countries standard procedures are simplified. Even among western countries it is difficult to ensure that all countries adhere to standard reporting rules; monitoring is therefore an important function of the transnational organisation managing the system.

Appendix A.4. TAPS—transnational ambulatory payment system (Berg vande and Zuurmond, 1997)

Banks have had to agree on a transnational system to process and complete payments made in one country by a customer holding accounts in another country. Customers travel across border and wish to obtain cash or to pay for products in foreign countries by debiting their account in the home country. Such payments made by individuals in other countries are called ambulatory payments. In Europe there are currently four systems in place to support ambulatory payments.

The oldest system, introduced in the 1970s, is the Eurocheque—an internationally recognised cheque where the customer has to enter not only the amount payable, but also the appropriate denomination for payment. The second payment system is the internationally usable Europass—a magnetic strip card requiring PIN identification at all times. The third system is the Eurocard creditcard which requires signature and PIN for identification. The fourth system has been introduced in 1996: a chipcard with magnetic strip.

These four systems, although distinct, are processed in the same way by banks. Individual countries have a single channel for international transactions so that all foreign currency processing is centralised. The organisation operating the central channel maintains links to the European-wide organisation Europay which operates transnational card and Eurocheque transactions. Europay performs international clearing and links with national clearing houses in different countries.
