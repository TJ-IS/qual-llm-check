# Financial securities transactions: a study of logistic process performance improvements ✩

Roland Toppen\*, Martin Smits<sup>1</sup>, Pieter Ribbers<sup>2</sup>

Tilburg University, School of Economics, PO Box 90153, 5000 LE Tilburg, Netherlands

## Abstract

The goal of this paper is to develop a better understanding of the intra- and inter-organisational processes related to financial securities transactions, and, ultimately, to provide guidelines for the design of inter-organisational systems in information-intensive markets and networks. A framework is presented to evaluate the influence of eight factors on the performance of the securities transaction process. These factors are stakeholders, competition, information technology, market network design, perceived risk, process design, information, and trust. The framework is used in a case study of one of Europe’s largest asset management organisations (the Robeco Group). It is concluded that improvement of the logistic process for securities transactions implies a balancing act between process design, risk perception of the stakeholders and trust. q 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Securities transactions; Logistics; Performance

## 1. Introduction

In the last two decades, there has been an enormous growth in the number and volume of transactions in financial securities (stocks, bonds and derivatives), currently well over US\$15 000 bn in the USA alone (Federal Reserve, 1996). Derivative transactions, in particular, have grown enormously (GSCS Benchmarks, 1997). Derivatives transactions are transactions in investment instruments that are derived from unlying investment instruments (Euromoney, 1992; Group of Thirty, 1993). With regard to derivatives, a distinction can be made between exchange-traded derivatives and those that are traded directly between two parties, the over-the-counter derivatives. This paper focuses on securities that are exchange traded.

In addition to the consequences of the large growth in financial securities transactions, the industry must deal with changes related to the stakeholder interests of various financial institutions, with increased competition, with the developments of information technology, and with the emergence of electronic markets. The financial securities sector is characterised by the variety of stakeholders (for instance, brokers, custodians, and regulators) (Dale, 1996; Institutional Investor, 1996; SWIFT, 1997). Increased competition has led to consolidations and diversifications within organisations, differentiation in the services offered, and the increased importance of risk management (Group of Thirty, 1993; Weber, 1995; Dale, 1996; Federal Reserve, 1996). Developments in information technology create opportunities for organisations to communicate electronically, thus improving the speed and quality of information exchange (Dale, 1996; Venkatraman and Zaheer, 1990), and supporting the globalisation of the industry.

The time needed to process an inter-organisational securities transaction has been reduced significantly over the last few years: in 1991, it used to take about 15–30 days to settle a transaction after it had been executed on the exchange; in 1997, it took, on average, not more than 3 days. In the UK, the transaction throughput time was reduced from 10 days to 5 days in June 1994. In Hong Kong, it was changed back from 1 day to 2 days in order to be more in accordance with the industry standard of 3 days, and in the USA, it changed from 5 days to 3 days in June 1995. Within the next few years, the industry standard of 3 days is expected to be reduced further to 1 day or a few hours (Freund, 1991; Group of Thirty, 1993).

A reduction in the transaction times is important for organisations to remain competitive and to avoid or limit high risks for the various stakeholders. For instance, the risk of nonpayment or non-delivery was reduced from 15 days to 3 days. However, along with this reduction in the processing time, a high level of security and controllability of the interorganisational processes must also be guaranteed (Group of Thirty, 1993). The question is how inter-organisational processes can be (re)designed, given requirements such as adequate internal control measures that might tend to lengthen the processing time.

The goal of this paper is to analyse the processes related to financial securities transactions, and to identify and analyse factors that affect logistic process performance. The logistic process performance is determined by factors such as throughput time, reliability of the throughput time, and security. A descriptive conceptual framework is presented to provide insight into how these factors can positively or negatively influence the logistic process performance. Ultimately, the aim is to provide guidelines for the design of interorganisational systems in information-intensive markets and networks. This paper and the framework has built on the concepts of ‘trust’, taken from transaction cost theory (Williamson, 1993), ‘stakeholder analysis’ (March and Simon, 1957), ‘electronic commerce’, taken from IOS theory, ‘process redesign’ (Hammer and Champy, 1993), ‘control and coordination’ (Malone and Crowston, 1994), and ‘internal control measures’, taken from auditing theory. The inter-organisational market network design sets the general possibilities, the rules, and the regulations for each stakeholder and is closely related to process design (Short and Venkatraman, 1992; Holland et al., 1992). The focus of this paper is on internal process (re)design efforts.

The case study method was used because it makes it possible to capture the ‘reality’ in considerably greater detail than other methods, and also allows the analysis of a considerably larger number of variables (Yin, 1984; Galliers, 1993). The case study supports the exploratory stage of the study. Interviews were held with key officials. Relevant reports were analysed and archival data were obtained.

In this paper the first results of the research are presented. Further research will aim at refining the framework and providing guidelines for redesigning the processes employed in inter-organisational financial transactions. The processes and the risks related to financial securities transactions are described in Section 2, the stakeholders interests are analysed in Section 3, the framework is presented in Section 4, the Robeco case is described in Section 5, and is analysed in Section 6.

## 2. Financial securities transactions and risks

## 2.1. Financial securities transactions process

The financial securities sector consists of many organisations involved in the various phases of a securities transaction, i.e. execution, confirmation, settling, and clearing. Before a more detailed analysis of the operational, coordination and control processes is given, a general overview of a securities transaction is presented by considering the Securities Transaction Life Cycle (STLC) (Fig. 1). Basically, the STLC is a combination of a trading process and a settlement process.

Fig. 1. Overview of the Securities Transaction Life Cycle and its nine phases.

The STLC starts with the decision by the investor or investment management organisation to either buy or sell a specific security (phases 1 and 2). The order to buy or sell is given to a broker who executes the order (phase 3) on the exchange where the security is listed (the order can also be executed directly with another organisation over the counter). The fourth phase is to match the confirmation of the transaction details with the order, to check the execution of the deal, and to initiate the settlement of the transaction. When the transaction information matches, a matched trade report is received (phase 5) to update the portfolios. To settle the securities transaction, it may be necessary to move securities to a central place (securities may be held at different locations) (phase 6). Before a transaction is settled, the delivery and payment of the securities are checked. A (global) custodian takes care of the settlement and clearing of the transaction based on the instructions it receives (phase 7). The payment will not be made unless, simultaneously, the corresponding securities are exchanged. It is also possible to separate the payment from the delivery, but this means taking the risk of non-payment or non-delivery. When the settlement process has been completed successfully, the investment manager of the controlling department receives statements, which are the basis for keeping the administration (payments and holdings) up to date. The final phase of the STLC consists of handling various corporate actions (like dividends, coupons, splits, mergers, rights issues, etc.) the moment they occur. The processes in the final phase take care of keeping the existing portfolios up to date. This completes the STLC.

It must be realized that, because of the many different stakeholders involved, each phase in the STLC employs different ways of communicating, using different information and communication systems (Morgan, 1994).

## 2.2. Risks

The STLC process description provides a general overview of the phases of a securities transaction, but it does not provide insight into the issues relating to a successful completion of a securities transaction.

One of these issues is risk management. In the last few years, risk management has become more and more important in the financial sector. This is partly due to the fact that some organisations have experienced incredible losses, sometimes resulting in bankruptcy. Another cause is found in newly developed and more complex investment instruments, like derivatives. In general, the risks related to financial securities transactions can be analysed on an aggregate level or on an individual transaction level. The risks related to individual transactions have to do with, among other things, the risks related to the investment instruments and market prices, counter party risks, and settlement risks. On an aggregate level, three causes of (uncontrolled) losses related to securities transactions can be identified: (i) insufficient knowledge at management level regarding the transactions, their processes and their consequences; (ii) a lack of adequate internal control procedures (like segregation of functions); (iii) the complex interaction of settlement times and interdependences between activities in the process of executing and settling securities transactions.

There are only a few known examples of financial disasters that were caused by securities traders who made use of settlement times. It can be concluded from these disasters and the developments in financial securities transactions that there is a need for improving the process design, the coordination, and the risk management of financial securities transaction processes. Lowering the throughput time, the combination of integrated risk management and information technology, as well as improving the reliability and controllability of the processes are suggested in order to decrease the number and size of financial losses (Group of Thirty, 1993; Morgan Guaranty Trust, 1993; Dale, 1996).

## 3. Stakeholders interests

In the previous sections, a general description was given of the financial securities transaction process and some examples of financial losses were presented. The main seven stakeholders and their interests are now described in more detail.

1. Investors: individuals or organisations that buy or sell securities. Investors are interested in obtaining shares at the best possible price with the lowest costs (commission), as soon as possible after an order has been placed.

2. Investment management organisations: organisations (e.g. mutual fund companies, insurance companies or pension funds) that professionally manage assets on behalf of investors. To serve their clients, investment management organisations are interested in obtaining the best return on their investments at the lowest possible costs and risks.

3. Brokers: organisations that are licensed to execute trades ordered by investors or investment management organisations on the securities exchange. The primary interest of brokers is to obtain as high a volume of transactions as possible because of the commission.

4. Exchanges: places (physical or electronic) where transaction orders of the various investors are executed (except over-the-counter transactions). Exchanges are primarily interested in facilitating the market (issuing and monitoring licenses, new securities issuing), providing systems for efficient and effective trading and providing administrative services, for instance, administering the transactions that are executed, and providing information about securities, transaction volumes and prices.

5. Custodians: organisations that take care of settling the transactions executed (exchange traded and over-the-counter transactions). Custodians keep the securities on behalf of the client and take care of, among other things, the administration of dividends or coupon payments. For instance, their primary interest is to provide an optimal service (fast and reliable processing) to the clients.

6. Central security depositories (clearing organisations). These organisations are responsible for tracking every transaction executed on the exchange and checking if it is cleared on time (the standard settlement time for specific types of securities). Their primary responsibility is to facilitate the transaction execution at the exchange.

7. Regulatory organisations. Organisations in the financial sector are subject to regulatory control by the central banks. The regulatory organisations issue licences to the organisations involved and control their activities. The primary interest of these organisations is to provide protection against organisations that do not act professionally or potentially mislead clients.

## 4. Conceptual framework

The conceptual framework in this paper focuses on eight factors, that influence STLC logistic process performance (Fig. 2). Four are exogenous factors: stakeholders, competition, information technology and market network design. These were identified in the first sections of this paper and have an impact on the organisations that operate in the financial sector. The four endogenous factors are perceived risks, process design, information, and trust.

Before going into the interactions between exogenous and endogenous factors, first a brief description is given of the four endogenous factors.

1. Perceived risks. Over the last few years, various stakeholders (especially regulators and central banks) have advocated the development of comprehensive risk management measures to prevent disasters like those described in Section 3. Because of the high interdependency between stakeholders carrying out activities to complete the STLC processes successfully, the perception of various risks (for instance settlement and counter party risk) has become more important for selecting organisations involved in STLC processes. Next to this external risk perception, more attention has been paid to internal risk perception (Group of Thirty, 1993; Dale, 1996).

2. Process design. This consists of patterns of decomposition into subtasks (division of labour) and coordination (for completion of the entire task). Process design can be analysed at different organisational levels: the inter-organisational level (subject to market network design); the intra-organisational level (within the boundaries of the organisation); and the group or task level (Malone and Crowston, 1994; Hahn et al., 1997). Depending on the level of analysis, different theories have been developed outlining the factors which determine efficient and effective organisational designs (Van der Heijden, 1995; Mintzberg, 1979; Tushman and Nadler, 1978; Nadler and Tushman, 1988). The total throughput time of STLC processes consists of (i) real processing time (i.e. the total time needed to complete the process steps); and (ii) queuing time (i.e. the total waiting time between processing steps). The queuing time normally takes up to 95% of the total throughput time (Morgan, 1994). Information systems and the redesign of business processes enabled by modern information technology can have a dramatic impact on the process performance in terms of throughput time through integration of steps and activities. For this reason, in the early 1990s it was already recognised that the strategic impact of existing and future information systems would be high for organisations in the financial sector (Cash et al., 1992). Modelling techniques can be used to analyse the current processes and to look for ways of improving process performance. Various modelling techniques are available. In this study, the Process-Graph (P-Graph) modelling technique (Steneskog et al., 1996) was chosen, because it emphasises stakeholder positions, interactions, and process throughput time. The further analysis of available modelling techniques and their application to financial transaction processes are beyond the scope of this paper.

3. Information. Financial institutions are, basically, information processing organisations. Their processes are information-intensive, and their endproducts and services are, for the greater part, represented by information. Two types of information processing activities can be distinguished in these organisations: information processing to produce the final products and services; and information processing to produce management information for the various management levels. The two process types are interrelated: management information is to a great extent derived from data which are generated by the production processes. Information processing and the delivery of final products and services have to meet demands posed by the market. These demands relate to logistic performance, as indicated above, and to quality. Here, quality concerns, in particular, correctness, completeness and reliability of the data stored and processed in the systems, and information presented to the customers and users. Quality failures affect the endproducts, the logistic performance, because of correction activities and the management and control processes. Here the ‘garbage in, garbage out’ rule applies. Low quality of base data (e.g. data received from customers), poor integrity of existing data in the systems and faulty processing of data will all result in poor quality of the final management information and/or endproducts. It has to be stressed that low quality, e.g. low reliability, can either be the intended or unintended result of human activity. The former relates, for example, to fraud, the latter to mistakes made. The logistic performance of STLC processes and the quality of information involved in these processes are both related to process design. The impact of process design on logistic performance has been discussed above. Quality, and in particular the reliability of information, is assured through internal control procedures like the segregation of functions. In particular, the segregation of functions may have a negative effect on logistic process performance. Using IT can result in faster and more aggregated information which enables faster decision making and reduces processing and queuing times.

4. Trust. Buyers and sellers may be confronted with the opportunistic behaviour of their counterparts. Trust deals with the belief, or willingness to believe, that one can rely on the fairness, goodness, strength, and ability of others (e.g. the seller or the buyer). Trust is the expectation that arises within a community of regular, honest and cooperative behaviour, based on commonly shared norms, on the part of the members of that community (Fukuyama, 1995). Trust applies to different parts of a financial transaction. Buyers expect delivery of the right securities with the right quality and at the right time. Sellers expect payments as agreed. In recent literature, the element of trust has received considerable attention. Recently, Sanner (1997) defined three bases of trust. Trust can be ‘person-based’, i.e. tied to the characteristics of a person; ‘enterprise-based’, where trust is tied to the enterprise as an entity; and ‘institution-based’, where trust is tied to formal mechanisms based on law and norms in a society. Enterprise-based trust and institution-based trust will need special attention, especially in global electronic markets, where the only contact between buyers and sellers may be through databases and the telecommunications network. As Fukuyama argues, ‘‘people who do not trust one another will end up cooperating only under a system of formal rules and regulations, which have to be negotiated, agreed to, litigated and enforced, sometimes by coercive means’’. This legal apparatus, serving as a substitute for trust, entails what economists call ‘transaction costs’ (Williamson, 1979, 1993; Fukuyama, 1995).

The four endogeneous factors can influence the STLC logistic process performance in several ways. The success of organisations involved in the STLC processes depends on meeting performance measures dictated by the market and competition. As mentioned before, actual competition in the financial industry is driven by the throughput time (T þ X). The logistic performance of a process relates to the throughput time and the reliability of throughput time planning. If an organisation agrees to settle a transaction within two days, will it be two days? Or maybe four? Reliability of the throughput time is also related to security. When an organisation promises to settle within one day, how secure is that process, and to what extent is the organisation (and the stakeholders) protected against uncontrollable developments (like system failures).

The proposed framework describes the exogenous factors in the financial industry and the endogenous factors that influence the STLC processes. Exogenous factors affect endogeneous factors; endogenous factors eventually affect process throughput time. The relationships between these factors are to be determined.

## 5. Case description

This case study deals with the Robeco Group, one of Europe’s largest asset management organisations, managing around 140 billion Dutch Guilders (US\$70 billion) of assets (Robeco Group, 1998). In managing mutual funds, the Robeco Group executes hundreds of thousands of financial securities transactions all over the world. Until about 1994, the entire transaction process was mainly paper-based. Various information systems were used to support the process. The systems were hardly or not integrated. Communication with stakeholders was based on telex and facsimile; information had to be rekeyed into different systems, and instructions to stakeholders had to be generated manually, several times. Needless to say, there was a need for tight and extensive checks and control measures because the entire process was prone to errors.

Analysis of the transaction processes showed that problems occurred in 23% of all transactions. There were ‘internal’ problems consisting of incorrect or non-timely information relating to the transactions. Also, communication with external stakeholders was analysed. It became obvious that electronic integration, e.g. by using standard SWIFT messages (SWIFT, 1997) and by integrating messaging systems with existing information systems, would make human intervention superfluous and would lead to more efficient processing. The latter was necessary to face the increased competition from ABN-AMRO, Fidelity, Fleming and others, and to comply with the previously mentioned general trends in the industry.

The exchange of information in these processes used to be mainly paper-based. Although Fig. 3 shows that by the end of 1996 most of the settlement instructions were sent by SWIFT, it does not show the ‘straight through processing rate’. This rate is determined by (i) the correct reading of the instructions (syntax) by the receiving organisations; and (ii) the correct processing of the instructions (content) without delays. Analysis shows that the first component more or less equals the number of instructions that are sent through the SWIFT network. The second component depends on whether the instruction can be carried out without manual intervention. For instance, manual intervention is required when there is not enough credit to pay for securities to be bought (in case of a buy), or when there are not enough securities to be delivered (in case of a sell).

Implementation of the SWIFT system resulted in a significant 25% reduction in the error rate (for instance, those due to a wrong account number or a wrong payment amount, probably because of no more rekeying), thus leading to the situation that about 97% of all instructions were error-free. As a result, the cost of the processing of settlement instructions was reduced from about US\$60 to US\$20. Two-thirds of this cost saving relates to fewer manual activities, saving employee costs. The other costs were saved because of reduced expenses for repairs (the receiving organisations had to add or ask for additional information; repair costs had to be refunded to them).

The internal information system played a crucial role in achieving the improved performance of the STLC processes. By integrating the entire transaction process in one system, it became possible to process certain parts of the STLC processes in a parallel way instead of sequentially. For instance, the moment the transaction details (after execution) are entered into the system, the settlement department can already generate the instructions to the appropriate stakeholders. When a transaction would generate a problem, the settlement department would be informed about two days sooner than occurred before the system was used.

## 6. Case discussion

The Robeco case shows that many factors influence the securities transaction process, especially when operating on a worldwide scale. The perception of risk can change when another custodian is used, for instance, a USA-based custodian versus one in an emerging country. Furthermore, the perception of risk and the process design can change because of changes in international guidelines, changes in regulations for risk management, and shorter throughput times (settlement times). Information technology enables the stakeholders and the organisations involved to cope with these challenges and to reduce throughput times by redesigning the process and coordination. When using the proposed framework to evaluate the Robeco case, the following analysis can be made.

Perceived risk influences the process design, information, trust and logistic process performance. The case shows that a high number of stakeholders leads to ‘risk diversification’. This means that organisations reduce risks by dealing with many stakeholders (many brokers and custodians). On the other hand, the high number of stakeholders and the fact that each stakeholder uses a different process lead to an increased risk perception and to an increased demand for risk controlling measures. Adding control activities to the process means adding extra steps, thus changing the process design and lowering the process performance. Although risk diversification decreases the risk perception on an overall level, the process performance is affected negatively by an increased risk perception, due to differences between stakeholders. For example, when risk perception relating to the STLC processes is high, then it is expected that the process throughput time will rise. However, it is not clear whether risk perception directly affects the process performance (e.g. by selecting other stakeholders), or indirectly through process design or trust. A high risk perception poses more demands on process design and will require more trust in stakeholders.

• Process design influences perceived risks, information, trust, and logistic process performance. The industry has forced organisations operating in the financial securities sector to meet shorter throughput times of T þ 3 (market network design). The case shows that use of IT can reduce the processing time, especially the queuing time. The total throughput time was reduced from 3 days to 1 day for transactions without problems. At this moment, 2 days remain after the 1 day standard throughput time to solve the settlement problems. The case also shows that by using integrated information systems, 97% of all transactions are completed within 3 days. The reliability and controllability of the STLC process have improved because the process can be monitored on a real-time basis. Two elements improved the process performance considerably. The first relates to the automation of the existing processes (reduced rekeying activities) and the second to redesigning the processes in a way such that potential problems are identified earlier in the process (more time to solve). The case shows that redesign efforts in the form of integration of the systems and providing on-line and real-time information can have opposite consequences. On the one hand, the perceived risks are reduced and the process performance is improved, because integration of the systems leads to fewer errors (less rekeying), more efficient processes, faster responses (e.g. transaction confirmations), and improved quality of the standardised (SWIFT) messages. On the other hand, integration of the systems leads to less segregation of functions, and fewer opportunities for internal control procedures, thus leading to a lower reliability of the process and lower quality of management information about the process. Countering the negative effects by adding control procedures may again lead to negative effects on process performance.

Trust influences the perceived risks, process design, information, and logistic process performance. Trust is determined by many factors and has many aspects. One aspect is the trust between the stakeholders involved. The redesign efforts of intra- and interorganisational processes in this case made it clear that the willingness to work together increases trust and positively affects process performance. Enterprise-based trust is increased by the reduction of errors in the STLC process due to the integration of systems and less rekeying of information. Also, the trust that stakeholders have in the intra- and inter-organisational processes is increased by the fact that the information systems provide an on-line, real-time overview. Trust in stakeholders and trust in the information system have reduced the perceived risks. Increased trust positively affects the STLC logistic process performance. With regard to information and trust, it is expected that trust decreases the need for information, or, conversely, lack of trust will increase the need for information (e.g. regulatory control).

## 7. Conclusion

The aim of this paper is to develop a better understanding of the intra- and interorganisational processes relating to financial securities transactions, and, ultimately, to provide guidelines for the design of inter-organisational systems in information-intensive markets and networks. The Robeco case shows how organisations involved in securities transactions can respond to competitive challenges. Robeco responded through IT which enabled the re-engineering of intra- and inter-organisational business processes, aiming for improvements of the logistic process performance.

Improving logistic performance implies a balancing act between throughput time, reliability of throughput time, and security. The case shows that redesigning the logistic process in STLC is influenced by complex interactions between process design, stakeholders’ perceptions of risk, the availability of reliable and timely information, and trust.

The framework and the Robeco case are important for the various stakeholders in their efforts to follow the globalisation trend. Changing processes has become a competitive necessity. If stakeholders do not comply, they will probably not survive.

The issue of logistic process performance has been subject to research in both goodsflow industries (e.g. the production of cars and the reduction of inventories) and informa tion-intensive industries (e.g. the insurance sector and airline reservations). Specific factors relating to the finance industry are the complexity of the processes, the high number of stakeholders involved, and issues relating to trust and risks. Further research is needed to identify the implications of the proposed framework in other industries. We suggest that the next step is further development of the framework. Exogenous factors, for instance market network design, were beyond the scope of this paper, but should be included in further research.
