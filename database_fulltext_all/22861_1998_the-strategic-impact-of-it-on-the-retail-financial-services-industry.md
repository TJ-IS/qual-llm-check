# The strategic impact of IT on the retail financial services industry<sup>q</sup>

Derek F. Channon

The Management School, Imperial College, Exhibition Road, South Kensington, London SW7 2AZ, UK

## Abstract

This paper is concerned with the strategic impact of information technology on the retail financial services industry. Today, in deregulated markets notably in Europe and especially the UK, traditionally separate industry sectors such as banking, insurance, housing finance and brokerage have become increasingly integrated since the late 1980s. In the USA and Japan regulation has prevented all these historically segregated sectors from integrating although the same patterns observable in Europe have attempted to emerge. In addition, around the world institutions such as retailers, brokerage houses and the like, not subject to the constraints of those officially licensed institutions such as banks and insurance companies, were free to add financial services to their product portfolios subject to certain constraints. As a result it is these new entrants which have often pioneered the development of new ways of providing traditional services with lower levels of cost but offering superior consumer quality. These innovations have almost invariably been driven by information technology. q 1998 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information technology; Strategic impact; Retail financial services

## 1. Introduction

The paper was restricted to retail financial services. Important information based changes have also occurred in wholesale markets but these were excluded. In part, this differentiation has only been made possible for example in banking over the past twenty years or so in the most developed markets where branch based banking was the norm. Historically in these markets the product offering of such institutions was relatively simple. The institutions primarily collected retail deposits which were then largely lent on in the form of commercial loans. Other services were in their infancy or not established. By the mid 1990s the product offering of these institutions had changed dramatically as illustrated in Fig. 1 which shows the degree of increased dissatisfaction of the leading British commercial banks first in 1968.

Then by the mid-1990s these organisations had diversified dramatically and offered a very wide range of financial services including brokerage and insurance products. As a result, during the 1980s many banks reorganised in the developed economies, away from geographic based organisation structures operating full service branches, toward customer based structures. One critical factor in this change was to separate retail and corporate activities, hence the differentiation advanced in this paper where even in retail activities complexity has also increased substantially.

Similar patterns of increased diversification, although not to the same extent, occurred within the insurance industry, especially into investment management products aimed at increased segmentation of the market. From the late 1980s the market for retail insurance products was also transformed by the advent of direct writing leading in part to the elimination of the broker market in some instances.

## 2. The introduction of the Cash Management Account

After over one hundred man years of systems design in late 1978, Merrill Lynch the leading USA brokerage house, introduced the first IT based product which was to begin the revolution of the USA consumer banking industry, and in particular the savings and loan sector. The Cash Management Account (CMA) was launched after the company had carefully checked with the Federal Reserve that the service did not constitute a bank account and therefore could be offered nationwide unlike bank accounts which had limited geographic cover usually confined to the home state of the individual bank. Moreover, Merrill Lynch should not be subject to USA Banking Law as it had no banking licence. The new product was deemed to be a brokerage account and same under the supervision of the Securities Exchange Commission and any loans granted would be awarded at the brokerage loan rate while any surplus funds, ‘deposits’, in such an account would be treated as investments by the brokerage house investing to client instructions.

![](/api/attachments/F78E89BA/fulltext/images/d0867c61b46b0237fafbb34f12758643f7e44b770a087b376f7e499634d1b329.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Merrill Lynch Cash Management Account operation.

The CMA however, provided a comprehensive range of services unrivalled by any bank. It was designed to appeal to the moderately well-off but not necessarily rich. To open a CMA investors needed at least US\$20 000 of cash or marketable securities to place in the account for which they received a range of services. First, account holders were given a cheque book and a Visa card. As Merrill Lynch was not technically a bank these facilities were operated by Banc One of Columbus, OH, itself a pioneering high information technology bank. Merrill Lynch cheques, although having the brokerage house name on them, also carried the Banc One sort code and were processed by the bank.

Account holders received a comprehensive monthly statement of all transactions using the CMA. Funds placed in the CMA could be in the form of cash, stocks and bonds, dividends and interest received and the like. All cash was computer swept into one of a number of money market funds on which interest at money market rates accumulated daily. When cheques or Visa debits were incurred, these were first deducted from any cash awaiting computer sweep and, when this was insufficient, by the automatic sale of assets held in the money market fund. Should this also be insufficient then a margin loan was generated up to a value of 50% of the value of the securities, stocks and bonds held in the account. Such loans were charged at the brokerage loan rate.

The effect of the CMA for the investor was to provide a facility to concentrate most of the financial services he or she required into one comprehensive, cheap and highly convenient account with the attractive features of having the automatic facility to monetarize fixed assets and to offer a highly competitive, money market interest rate on surplus funds.

For the monetary authorities the CMA was also a potential nightmare in that the account was technically invisible as only one transaction occurred each day between Merrill Lynch and Banc One which cleared all the CMA holders’ transactions processed that day. The process is shown in Fig. 2.

Each morning the Merrill Lynch computer would update that of Banc One on the current status of all CMAs. The bank would process all transactions received that day and update the account files which it would then advise the Merrill Lynch computer by 1.00 p.m. At the same time the bank would advise the brokerage company of the total debits incurred by all CMA holders that day which Merrill Lynch would settle with a single wire transfer to clear the outstanding debt before the bank’s close of business. The accounts of CMA holders were updated overnight also to include any funds receipts ready for the operation again the following day. Moreover, as the brokerage concern was outside the banking industry it was not required to report the activities of CMA holders, hence the authorities had no knowledge of fixed assets that might be monetarized. Later imitators of the CMA even compounded this problem by allowing account holders to trigger automatic loans against the outstanding equity value in personal property.

The CMA was copied by a number of other USA institutions and was introduced by Merrill Lynch on an international basis. It was one of the first dramatic examples of the use of information technology to revolutionise the financial services industry. Moreover, despite the efforts of imitators it proved very difficult even for advanced IT companies such as Citibank to fully replicate its service offerings. Indeed even today around the world there are few financial institutions capable of offering such a fully integrated service without at some stage being forced to operate manual systems at great expense and loss of efficiency.

Within the USA the effect of the new product was even more significant in that the CMA and similar products were largely responsible for the catastrophic collapse of the US Savings and Loans institutions (S and Ls) during the 1980s. Depositors, limited to a fixed rate of interest paid by law, withdrew their funds and placed them in accounts such as the CMA or the less sophisticated money market funds. These also paid interest on a daily basis at money market rates and were themselves low cost operations, utilising either telephone or mail delivery systems as distinct from branch based systems. The result was a massive drain on the retail deposit base of banks and S and Ls, where in the latter case assets were made up largely of fixed rate mortgages. To replace lost deposits the S and Ls were forced to pay money market rates and to engage in risky property ventures which yielded higher returns in order to cover what had become a serious interest rate mismatch.

The resulting difference between the cost of funds and interest received resulted in the collapse of the USA industry during the mid to late 1980s at enormous cost to the USA taxpayer as deposits with these institutions were insured by the Federal Government. The CMA therefore serves as a classic example of how innovative information technology can transform an entire industry within a remarkable short time and where for several years the industry itself did not realise it was under competitive attack.

## 3. The transformation of motor insurance

More recently the motor insurance market in the UK was similarly transformed with the advent of direct writing. Commencing only in 1988, Direct Line Insurance began selling motor insurance by phone directly to consumers so bypassing the broker, the traditional intermediary between customers and insurers. As with the banks and the CMA, for several years traditional insurers failed to perceive the new threat to their traditional way of operating.

The savings achieved from bypassing the broker were largely passed on to consumers in the form of lower prices and enhanced claims service quality. Rather than waiting for cash flow and paying claims as slowly as possible, Direct Line wrote insurance to make profits from underwriting and paid claims rapidly. By 1994 the company was the only leading motor insurer out of 500 in the UK to actually achieve profits from underwriting.

This seeming aberration was achieved by the highly sophisticated use of information technology. The initial thrust of the company was to develop its information technology base to simplify the process of policy operation by assessing risk by car, type, socio demographics, geography and the like, to produce computer generated quotations rapidly by telephone operators obtaining answers to a checklist of computer generated questions. When quotations were accepted they had to paid for by credit or debit card or via direct bank debits. No paper was allowed into the transaction process. As a result cover could be provided instantly and formal policies were despatched within 24 h via a laser print out from the initial customer screens. The company’s risk portfolio was continuously updated on-line in real time.

The result of this system was that Direct Line grew at a compound annual growth rate of over 70% per annum and by 1996 the company was the largest private car insurer in the UK with over 2 million active policy holders. Moreover, the company’s policy holder retention rate at around 80% was by far and away the highest in the industry where the average retention rate was around 50%. The company was a favourite amongst consumer groups for both its competitive pricing and its perceived service quality in the event of a claim.

Direct Line also represented a rare example of a horizontal organisation structure. As a result of its sophisticated IT systems the company felt no need to add to its management structure, hence the hierarchy between the board and telephone sales was minimised with little or no middle management – the Chief Executive and top management being able to key in instantly to what was happening in practice in real time. Moreover, the linkage between operating systems data and the requirements for management information was fully integrated and available for interrogation.

The company had subsequently entered the market for property insurance which data base was also fully linked to the motor insurance data base. While initial results had not been as dramatic, the loss ratios had been gradually improved as more information was fed into the data base in order to improve the quality of quotations. During the mid-1990s Direct Line provided a clear example of the strategic convergence of the financial services industries by offering first consumer loans, secondly mortgage products and most recently has indicated its intent to offer investment products, deposit products and a credit card. These diversifications were offered with a minimal increase in staff, of which around 85% interface directly with customers and virtually no increase in the small number of top management personnel. As a result Direct Line can and does offer an array of financial services at a cost/income ratio of around 20% compared with an average cost/income ratio of around 60%–70% to offer a similar pattern of services via a branch based delivery system. Similarly, in its offering of insurance services Direct Line enjoys a cost advantage of around 30%, a lower loss ratio and can afford to operate a conservative investment policy compared with conventional insurers where ‘‘profits’’ tend to be generated more from investments than from underwriting. ## 4. The impact on branch based delivery systems

Until the reorganisations of the mid-1980s the traditional mechanism for delivering retail financial services in banking was via branches. While historically these were universal in terms of their product offering with no differentiation, in the mid-1980s to the present time, individual branches were redesignated to provide services either to retail and small business accounts or to commercial and corporate accounts. Some branches, essentially those seen as hub operations in hub and spoke combinations as illustrated in Fig. 4 may continue to offer a full range of services albeit segmented within the hub branch.

However, most branches have taken on a specialist, mainly retail function. Nevertheless, the use of technology including information technology, is increasingly making conventional branch structures too expensive. While it was clear for a number of years that branches should not all provide a full range of services, recent re-engineering moves have introduced much more radical change whereby most branches, previously offering the full service range were closed, downgraded to specialist activities, or replaced by automated teller machines (ATMs) in order to contain costs. As a result, the attribution rate for traditional bank managers, the middle management line, has reached up to 80% as suggested by management consultants and as shown in Fig. 5.

This is a result as a redefinition of the role of the branch manager; the need to reduce the number of branch based service delivery points in the light of alternatives, and the changes in cost structure as a result of new branch oriented service delivery configurations. To illustrate the latter, Wells Fargo has moved to a micro market approach which identifies retail banking catchment areas and attempts to provide the appropriate service delivery system to meet customer needs. Such a micro market is illustrated in Fig. 6 which shows that only in a central hub location is a full service offer required while in other branch locations the traditional product offer can be deleted, substituted for a limited, but automated service offering or replaced by an automated teller machine. As illustrated the resulting cost saving might represent as much as 65%–70% of the existing structure.

In addition the provision of non conventional service delivery points at low cost was introduced by innovative banks such as Banc One and Wells Fargo. Both have introduced ‘‘supermarket’’ prefabricated branches at minimal cost inside supermarket chains. One such is illustrated in Fig. 7. Again using information processing to minimise back-office paper work, such branches come fully on line, prefabricated and at minimal cost. If they do not work, the cost of withdrawal is small compared with conventional branches where premises costs are prohibitive and closure costs even higher as traditional branch architecture makes alternative usage difficult.

Wells Fargo indeed has experimented with using traditional branches as franchise outlets for drug dispensers in addition to bank services.

## 5. The retailers challenge

A problem faced by banks is that consumers do not like to enter their premises. By contrast, retailers do not suffer the same resistance. Consumers like to shop, hence in the UK Marks and Spencer has built an attractive retail financial services business around its retail operations. The same is true of Sears Roebuck in the USA. A number of outsourcing service providers such as General Electric Capital Services, Banc One, EDS and American Express have also developed successful businesses as outsource operators for other financial brokerage and retail institutions in the USA and Europe. These service providers do not stress their own brands but rather that of their clients. They however own the business and can potentially capitalise on their knowledge of the spending profiles of the clients they serve.

Major efforts have also been made to use technology to reduce back office operating costs. Much of this effort was made to eliminate paper based transactions. For example in paper driven systems document encoder readers were introduced to prevent the necessity of keying in paper based data several times. The use of imaging technology, while still not fully accepted, offers the promise of conveying data electronically rather than physically. Similarly EFTPOS offers an electronic cashless payments method.

In Scandinavia, transaction and paperless payments were developed since the 1970s. As a result of the elimination of paper from the system banks operate with approximately half the back-office labour than is found in many Western branch based banking systems. Within the Swedish system, while most simple payments were made via the Postal Giro system, the banks had substantially reduced the cost of cheque handling by cooperating with one another to redeem each others paper. As soon as a cheque was presented it was automatically truncated by converting the data into electronic form. All cheques were standardised regarding account number, identification description, layout and the like. Cheques were filed by the cashing/bank and the issuing bank received only the accounting data plus a code to tell it where the cheques were filed.

An interesting example of retailing/financial services integration is offered by the Saison Group in Japan. A services industry conglomerate operating railroads, fast foods supermarkets, department stores, travel and hotels, the group also operates the second largest credit card in Japan and within its department stores an area named Saison Square which offers consumers a comprehensive range of financial services including insurance, consumer credit, stocks and shares purchase, post office facilities, travel arrangements, real estate and share purchase facilities and the like as shown in Fig. 8.

Other retailers have also entered the financial services market. In the state of Florida for example the supermarket group Publix took control of the Florida switch; in the UK the supermarket group Tesco launched a loyalty card in 1995. By 1996 this had attracted database for the supermarket company to interrogate to identify the shopping habits of its customers. In 1996 the company entered the financial services market by introducing a revised version of its card as a debit card with processing being outsourced to one of the major British clearing banks.

These moves by non-traditional financial service industry competitors, many operating with a built in cost advantage, knowledge of their customers’ buying habits as a result of transaction records taken at the till, enable them to launch financial services with a competitive advantage. Indeed these institutions in many cases enjoy substantially superior customer information as most banks and insurance do not have integrated databases or management information systems. Rather the computer systems developed in banks for example were designed to accurately record transaction details rather than to provide management information.

## 6. Integrated databases and strategic segmentation

Advanced information technology sometimes appear in unusual places. Banco Comercial de Portugese (BCP) (Channon, 1993a) was the first private bank created in Portugal in the late 1980s as the country returned to democracy and joined the European Union (EU). At the time the existing state banks which had been rationalised with the advent of a communist government, provided in many cases a low quality and inefficient service to consumers. BCP on its formation adopted the highly unusual strategy of spending the majority of its shareholders funds on information technology. Advised by McKinsey and Company, the new bank opened a number of branches throughout Portugal catering for high net worth customers and medium sized corporate accounts. Customers were carefully segmented and provided with a substantially superior set of products and services than those available from the state owned sector. Moreover, each customer had a personal relationship manager who could be visited by appointment at the branch or via telephone and who could conduct a comprehensive service of transactions for his clients via a screen based system which showed all details of client accounts.

The system was enthusiastically received within Portugal and BCP grew at a compound growth rate of between 70% and 100% per annum. In the early 1990s the bank increased its customer coverage to include moderately wealthy accounts. This was achieved unusually by opening an entirely new branch network, Nova Rede. These branches were designed to be high technology, machine and IT driven with the minimum of personnel. The branches were of modular design fitting easily into conventional retail sites and if unsuccessful could be easily moved. Unlike the BCP branches, which although also high in IT, were conservatively furnished in traditional mahogany and conservative colours, the new chain was fitted with bright red, green and white plastic fascias.

BCP operated an integrated database across both its retail and corporate customer bases; had added insurance and telephone banking services as well as entering the large corporate and private banking markets. By 1996, the bank had developed to become the second largest bank in Portugal by its application of an IT based segmentation strategy as illustrated in Fig. 9. This same strategy has also been introduced, especially by McKinsey, around the world especially in newly privatising markets such as Eastern Europe.

## 7. New delivery systems

Despite the use of technology to reduce operating costs and enhance cross-selling conventional branch based banking systems and paper based insurance concerns are under serious threat in the developed economies from new delivery systems which are transforming each of the main industry sectors. These new systems are almost invariably driven by information technology and telecommunications, often in combination, to create new low cost, high quality services. The prophecy made by Walter Whiston in the early 1980s that the customer would determine ‘‘the time, the place and the method’’ of the banking transaction had largely arrived by the mid-1990s.

The use of ATMs was one of the earliest uses of machine driven technology to substitute capital for labour. Throughout the world ATMs were installed in increasing numbers. While initially such systems were proprietary, in many countries ATMs were linked into common access systems. In the USA the extensive introduction of ATMs which were linked into a number of nationwide systems, provided one of the earliest mechanisms for establishing interstate banking facilities. In addition, many ATMs are now linked on an international basis and provide service in multiple currencies.

Apart from the USA, extensive ATM penetration has occurred in Japan where some 80% of cash withdrawals occur via ATMs. Moreover, Japanese ATMs also take in most cash deposits as the currency is machine readable and can be recirculated so reducing the machine float by a substantial degree. Indeed the latest Hitachi ATMs actually take soiled notes received as deposits and steam clean and iron them prior to recirculation. In fully automated Japanese branches it is common to find 20–30 machines supported only by a telephone link to the operations centre.

New generation ATMs which can provide a substantially wider range of services including loan dispensing, share dealing, travel and theatre ticket dispensing and social security payments, are now being introduced. However, cash dispensing remains their primary usage.

## 8. Telephone banking

In the USA already some 25% of banking transactions are made by phone. During the 1980s and early 1990s telephone based banking, insurance writing and investment have grown apace. While some such systems were introduced to support existing delivery systems others such as Direct Line in insurance and First Direct in banking are full service operations. The latter organisation established as a subsidiary of the Midland Bank, now itself a subsidiary of Hong Kong and Shanghai Bank offers a personalised full service banking operation, with the exception of cash delivery where the bank relied on access to a nationwide ATM network. The service is available 24 h per day, 365 days a year and by 1996 had over 50 000 customers growing at around 15 000 new accounts per month. Similar operations occur in a number of banks around the world and it is anticipated that this mode of service delivery will increase its share of retail financial service delivery at the expense of branch based delivery in the future.

## 9. Card based service

Credit cards were originally established in the USA by American Expresses and Diners Club. These early travel and entertainment cards were soon joined by bank based credit cards introduced by the Bank of America and a number of other pioneers. Credit cards were rapidly adopted and two rival collective organisations, Visa and Mastercard, were established which today dominate around the world. The early card services have also been expanded substantially with the introduction of debit card variants, affinity cards, different interest rates for different repayment systems and the like. Moreover, cards may today be issued by non-banks when acceptable and individual banks may issue both Visa and Mastercard variants.

For the future, existing credit cards seem likely to be overtaken by the gradual introduction of smart cards. Although these were in existence for some years, no global standards have yet been established. The power of smart cards lies in their ability to store and manipulate data, to handle multiple applications in one card and to perform secure transactions.

One particular application of smart cards of interest to the banking industry is to use them as a store of value or electronic purse which may be recharged and used as a substitute for cash. A number of trials are underway around the world. In the UK a trial of the Mondex system began in 1995. Mondex, a collaboration between the NatWest Bank, Midland Bank, British Telecom and a number of hardware suppliers in which some 40 000 people were able to draw money from their bank accounts onto their cards by using a card reader connected to their bank via telephone. The electronic cash then held on the card could be spent at a wide range of outlets each equipped with card readers.

In the USA Citibank was offering a similar electronic money system (EMS). This system was designed to work on a computer network and thus to allow secure, immediate transfers of cash. The EMS cards can be inserted into PCs and contain their own tamper proof security and transaction processing software. In addition, unlike mondex, it allows banks and customers to keep track of the electronic money being issued. As users fill their electronic wallets with cash, either by drawing down an existing balance or by borrowing, the bank creates digital bank notes that then travel around the electronic economy. Each note carries with it an electronic tag that allows the issuing bank to identify its money.

Visa and Mastercard are also developing their own smart cards for small transactions. In the USA Visa is working with a consortium including First Union, Nationsbank and Wachovia banks. Visa was also working with other institutions in other countries including Denmark, the UK and Australia.

## 10. Home banking, PCs and the internet

During the 1980s many experiments were made to provide home banking services which allowed consumers with either a PC or a special terminal to conduct banking services in their own time from home. In the main these service offerings were failures as they were too expensive, complicated and failed to achieve a sufficient level of consumer acceptance.

With the advent of the internet great interest and anticipation was being revived in home and terminal based financial services. Bill Gates of Microsoft predicted that many banks would utilise the net to provide on line banking services which permitted customers to carry out transactions via a direct connection to the banks core customer account functions. Customers would be able to review all their data, cheques and credit details. They would even be able to fill out electronic forms and send in questions via e-mail (Gates, 1996). Despite these predictions however, it seemed unlikely that home banking/internet usage would become significant delivery mechanisms until early in the next millennium.

## 11. Retail service globalisation is difficult

Although IT has clearly had a dramatic impact on the strategy of retail financial service providers around the world, the emergence of global service providers is very rare. While many companies shave entered markets other than their home market and brought with them systems, they have mainly operated multi domestic strategies, rather than integrated cross border strategies, although there are some signs this may be developing within the EU.

Global services have developed in the private banking sector (the definition of private banking is arbitrary but is here taken to apply to individuals with US\$1m of credit to their accounts). It can therefore be argued that the only truly global player in the sub US\$1m market is Citibank (Channon, 1993b).

Whilst Citibank has established a major position in the private banking market, it has also established a global retail presence for high net worth individuals predominantly outside the USA. With a limited branch network in most countries around the world Citibank offers a predominantly telephone/card based series of services for its clients which provides 24 h, 365 day services which can operate in multiple currencies and gold to suit the needs of its customers. Moreover, services usually technology driven, produced for one market were adapted and introduced in other markets where they were perceived as desirable. As such the development a global retail service for selected clients supplements a multi domestic strategy which varies in strength marketing from country to country. Thus in Europe, Citibank is strong in retail banking in Germany and Spain but weak in the UK and has exited from Italy.

It seems unlikely that globalised retail financial services will develop strongly in the near future as most customers do not require such a service and when they do mainly use card based products. Multi domestic strategies are, however, developing relatively strongly although with only limited success.

## 12. Conclusion

This paper has attempted to sketch key areas where information technology based services and operations have significantly impacted on the strategies of financial service industry institutions over the past two and half decades and to point the way to the future. After half a century of relative stability information technology was a major contributor to transforming and forcing convergence of what were previously a series of segregated industry segments.

The changes, however, were often pioneered by either new entrants or by maverick operators within the industries concerned. Major strategic advantage has often been attained by first movers who have gained positions of competitive advantage whilst scarcely being observed by traditionalists. Moreover, the boards of many traditional players have failed to recognise the strategic advantage to be gained by the creative use of information technology, rather than using computer power to automate existing operations and failing to re-think the way business can be done. For future success, those organisations that wish to survive and prosper in a rapidly changing environment must look further than the narrow confines of their traditional operations and open their eyes to a broader horizon in which information technology is a key driver of corporate strategy.
