# Contours of diffusion of electronic data interchange in Finland Overcoming technological barriers and collaborating to make it happen

Jan Damsgaard<sup>a,</sup>\*, Kalle Lyytinen<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Computer Science, Aalborg University, Fr. Bajersvej 7E, DK-9220 Aalborg Ø, Denmark <sup>b</sup>Department of Computer Science and Information Systems, University of Jyva¨skyla¨, PO Box 35, SF-40351 Jyva¨skyla¨, Finland

Revised paper accepted for publication by Professor R.D. Galliers 19 June 1998

## Abstract

Electronic Data Interchange (EDI)—despite its basic simplicity—forms a complex and interorganizational innovation. This necessitates multiple points of observation and the use of multiple theoretical frames in accounting EDI diffusion processes. Based on field study data we deliver a multi-level account of EDI diffusion in Finland. The study clarifies how factors located on three levels of analysis can be applied to understand the unfolding of EDI adoption in different organizational constellations, here called diffusion patterns. We examine three families of diffusion patterns: local dyadic patterns; industry-wide networks; and national initiatives. Overall we discern five distinct diffusion patterns. The analysis of the five patterns demonstrates that EDI diffusion is a complex interplay of organizational, industry and institutional factors. On the theory plane our study calls for the need to orchestrate multi-level approaches to study the diffusion of complex, and networked technologies. q 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Electronic Data Interchange; Diffusion; Diffusion theory; Institutional theory; Organizational impacts; Finland

## 1. Introduction

Bounded in the north by Norway, in the east by Russia, in the southwest by the Baltic

Sea lies Finland. Nearly a third of the country lies north of the arctic circle. Finland’s population is ca. 5 million and its area totals 338 145 km<sup>2</sup>. This makes Finland one of the largest, and one of the most sparsely inhabited countries in Europe. Moreover, more than two-thirds of its population reside in the southern part of the country. Except for public utilities, industry and business are privately owned in Finland. Until recently the Finnish government has exercised considerable control over large parts of economy by means of numerous regulations, but this has changed rapidly. For example, the Finnish telecommunication sector is currently one of the most competitive ones in Europe, with three different tele-operators. Finland is also one of the most advanced countries in the world in regard to telecommunication infrastructure and the diffusion of telecommunication services. Electronic funds transfer (EFT) is common and it started already in 1968. Today some 80% of all economic transactions are performed electronically and Finland has ca. 600 000 users of Internet-based banking services. Several industries are also sophisticated in their applications of it. For example, the retail sector has applied EDI for almost two decades. Nevertheless, not much is known of how, or why, telecommunication services in general and EDI services in particular are so advanced and have started so early in this small country with no technical superiority before the 1990s. Due to the early take-up of EDI and her strong traditions of governmental involvement Finland thereby offers an excellent laboratory to study institutionalized aspects of EDI diffusion. Due to the high levels of both diffusion and infusion it also provides an opportunity to understand unfolding of various diffusion patterns at a more advanced stage than in most other countries.

In this paper we seek to answer the following question: what are typical diffusion patterns of EDI in Finland, and how are these patterns affected by organizational, industry and environmental factors? Moreover, what has been the success rate of various diffusion patterns in institutionalizing the EDI service?

To examine the dynamics of EDI diffusion fully we will consider alternative adoption patterns that operate beneath specific institutional incentives and programs. In other words we shall probe individual organizational cases within specific industrial contexts. Such an analysis is expected to dissect interactions between multiple factors that are likely to affect the diffusion of EDI.

The paper is structured as follows. First, we characterize EDI and discuss its traits from a diffusion of innovation point of view. Second, a concept of diffusion pattern is introduced and we explain the need for adopting a multi-level approach to study diffusion patterns. Section 4 describes a field study and its research design. Section 5 presents five identified diffusion patterns, and links the identified patterns to the pertinent literature in terms of how well it fits with other observations. In Section 6 we condense our findings of EDI in Finland and compare our findings with the innovation diffusion literature. The paper concludes by suggesting promising areas for further research.

## 2. Definition and traits of EDI

We define EDI as follows: EDI can be defined as standardized interorganizational communication between independent computerized information systems (IS) and associated technological components. It also covers the know-how of integrating information systems and information handling practices within computerized interorganizational communication.

Key features of EDI are: (a) the use of electronic transmission medium; (b) the use of structured, formatted messages based upon agreed standards; (c) relatively fast delivery from sender to receiver; and (d) direct processing by application software, generally resulting in a response to the sending organization (Wrigley et al., 1994). The following seven points depict EDI’s distinctive traits:

1. EDI is inter-organizational in nature; intra-organizational EDI does occur (Schalch and Griese, 1995), but it is not the focus of this investigation.

2. It links organizations by electronic means so that organizational ‘‘boundary’’ is lowered (Ciborra, 1995) and the organization’s interior is somewhat exposed to business partners (Webster, 1995).

3. It relies on an advanced and reliable telecommunication infrastructure (Kimberley, 1994), which when not in place seriously slows down the diffusion rate (O’Callaghan and Eistert, 1995).

4. It forms a complex, innovative and abstract innovation that requires considerable skills and know-how to implement and operate (Webster, 1995). Moreover, adopting organizations must have their internal information systems connected and their data systematically organized to reap full benefits of EDI. Thus adopting organizations must be mature in their in-house application of IT (Nygaard-Andersen and Bjørn-Andersen, 1994).

5. Standards<sup>1</sup> are essential in its adoption and they must be necessarily recognized in developing the EDI system (Swatman et al., 1994). Therefore EDI creates a high degree of organizational interdependence between participating organizations (Hørlu¨ck, 1994) and necessitates institutional regulation (Swatman and Clarke, 1990).

6. Network externalities are important in adoption decision about EDI, as it requires a considerable critical mass to be efficiently deployed (O’Callaghan and Eistert, 1995; Pfeiffer, 1992). This means that one organization’s decision to adopt EDI has implications for other organizations’ decision to adopt (Katz and Shapiro, 1985; Oliva, 1994).

7. EDI implementations are often built on third-party operated Value Added Networks (VAN). This is often the only economically feasible solution because creating and maintaining direct links to all partners form a very expensive one-to-many relationship (O’Callaghan and Turner, 1995). At the same time third-party services complicate the provision of EDI services (Ciborra, 1995).

It is important to notice that EDI use does not entail electronic markets. In electronic markets customers shop to find a product among a number of sellers (Bakos, 1991a,b;

Malone et al., 1987) through an infrequent and simple market exchange (Kambil and Short, 1994). EDI, on the other hand, requires a long-standing relationship between business partners. This relationship is relatively stable and partners operate using highly integrated business processes (Kumar and van Dissel, 1996), which demands frequent communication through a customized linkage (Kambil and Short, 1994).

Direct benefits of EDI include better efficiency of internal operations, better responsiveness to customers, improved trading relationships and increased competitive capability due to rapid and less error prone request/answer ratio, a quick and accurate placement and cancellation of orders, automation of repetitive administrative processes, and the optimization of inventory levels (Krcmar et al., 1995; O’Callaghan and Turner, 1995). Many argue that indirect benefits are more substantial and involve profound changes in supply chain structures and relationships (Kambil and Short, 1994; Malone et al., 1989; Short and Venkatraman, 1992). These include demonstrations of competency, increases in organizational reliability, improved accountability, or better monitoring of the environment (Iacovou, 1995). No wonder, that many observe EDI adoption to be necessary to sustain the competitive capability of nations, regions and industries (Farhoomand and Boyer, 1994; Keen, 1991; King and Konsynski, 1990a; MacKay, 1993; Swatman and Clarke, 1990; Swatman et al., 1994).

From view point of diffusion of innovation EDI has some prominent features that make EDI adoption a case in point in analyzing the adoption of complex networked technologies. Items (1), (3), (4) and (7) imply that EDI adoption can involve path dependencies with earlier innovation decisions. Items (1) and (6) suggest that the decision to invest in EDI is not solely dependent on singular adopters, but on ‘‘herd’’ effects of creating sufficiently large scale simultaneous adoption decisions that create positive network externalities (Katz and Shapiro, 1985; Oliva, 1994). Items (1), (5) and (7) entail that the benefits of EDI adoption do not depend on a singular adopter’s goals and desires, but as well on the effectiveness of broader institutional regimes in creating a favorable momentum for diffusion. Finally, items (2) and (4) imply that due to its originality there are high knowledge and learning barriers for its diffusion (Attewell, 1992). Difficulties in understanding what EDI is, can do, and does, create an additional hurdle for adoption.

In this paper we assume that due to these features we multiple theoretical accounts to understand EDI diffusion. Focusing just on the individual adopter’s behavior, like in some diffusion accounts following Rogers’ analysis (Rogers, 1983) or more recent diffusion models (e.g. the Bass model) (Mahajan et al., 1990; Mahajan and Peterson, 1985), will miss items (1) and (6). Likewise a sole institutional analysis (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 1996; King et al., 1994) would lead to an underestimation of item (4).

## 3. A framework for the analysis of EDI diffusion

In analyzing the diffusion of complex technologies such as EDI we need a framework that organizes the analysis into a set of separate, manageable, but yet interrelated issues. It must help organize data of each diffusion process and provide theoretical and methodical guidelines of how to carry out investigations diffusion processes. The analysis steps suggested must form a systematic, but yet flexible approach to make sense of diffusion processes. In the following we shall outline the framework which was established for this study.

## 3.1. Concept of diffusion pattern

A key notion in our framework is the concept of a diffusion pattern. In the case of simple and relatively stable innovations, such as pesticides or TV sets, the diffusion analysis has traditionally focused on two analysis units: individual adopters and adopter populations (Lyytinen and Damsgaard, 1998). Moreover, these analysis units are fixed for the whole analysis and over time. In the case of complex and inter-organizational technologies, this choice of analysis units alone is not sufficient, though it can help obtaining interesting insights of how individual adopters react, or behave in relation to available complex technologies (see for example Bouchard, 1993; Krcmar et al., 1995). Simply, when adoption of innovation involves two or more independent adopters and often requires the involvement and/or availability of several other actors in the adoption process (like standard setters, value added service providers, software houses etc.) we have to use network based concepts. Thus the whole network of adopting actors and their dynamic behavior over time should form the unit of analysis: not individual adopters. This constellation, moreover, can and will change over time. Also, the initial constellation of the network can vary from simple dyadic relationships to complex industry wide or service based networks. The network based analysis unit we call here a diffusion pattern, and it is defined here as a dynamic constellation of players, institutional and technical factors that are structured into a specific EDI diffusion trajectory.

We can visualize the differences between these two approaches by a simple figure (see Fig. 1). The part on the left (Fig. 1a) illustrates the traditional model, while the model on the right (Fig. 1b) illustrates the diffusion pattern based analysis. On the left in the figure we can see the traditional adoption model in operation: here each small square represents an independent socio-economic decision-maker forming the unit of analysis. Those that are colored at time point $t _ { \mathrm { k } }$ are adopters, while white squares represent the non-adopters (so far). The whole circle—in turn—represents and delimits the adopter population. With this model one can study properties of adoption units and properties of the whole system (like penetration levels and dynamics).

The exhibit on the right depicts by colored graphs three different types of adoption units that have adopted or are adopting EDI at time point $t _ { \mathrm { k } }$ and which are possible in the study of its diffusion: a single dyadic relationship (a); an industry wide network (b); or a community spanning network (c). The lines between, within the circle, depict natural diffusion scopes (or arenas) for each such diffusion pattern. For example pattern (a) is delimited to one industry sector which is exhibited by the line, while pattern (c) may span potentially over the whole adopted population. Hence, in our analysis EDI diffusion should take place through identifying and analyzing typical (or ideal-type like) families of diffusion patterns. Moreover, these families, and actors’ behaviors within these patterns, should be analyzed separately. Needless to say that in this type of diffusion study the analysis unit is not fixed a priori, but discovered during the analysis of the field study data. In fact, identification of the alternative diffusion patterns can be one valuable outcome of the study. Therefore this theoretical concept excludes the use of structured and quantitative instruments in early stages of the research process before the set of patterns in the arena has been identified.

## 3.2. Levels of analysis

The concepts of diffusion pattern set up new requirements for the types of analysis we must carry out in relation to each observed diffusion unit. For example in EDI diffusion patterns we need to address the following prominent traits: the nature of inter-organizational linkages involved, the scope of network externalities, the dependency and criticality of the infrastructure, the scope of standards and the nature of institutional regimes, and the impact of the complexity of the EDI service. In other words we need to mobilize several frames of reference to account for the observed behaviors and events in each diffusion pattern. Moreover, the breadth and depth of the frames may vary from one pattern to another, due to the complexity of the studied pattern and its linkages.

Overall we suggest examination of EDI adoption patterns using three broad levels of analysis: EDI adoption as an uptake of technology standards and policies in some regulatory regime; EDI adoption as a strategic/technological move in some industry; and EDI adoption as an investment and uptake decision in a user organization. These we call macro, meso and micro levels, respectively. Each one of these frames can be cast as a theoretical net of concept over the observed diffusion pattern. Separating analyses into these specific levels helps us focus on specific traits of EDI diffusion process and to discern distinct mechanisms that affect each diffusion pattern and to identify different players and their roles that can affect the evolution of the diffusion pattern. Each analysis level thus brings into the foreground incommensurable factors that complement each other in understanding the evolution and dynamics of the diffusion pattern. Adoption behaviors observed through these levels are seen as interdependent, mutually supportive and open-ended. Naturally in many cases some of these levels may be brought to bear to explicate the observed behaviors only indirectly, and thus the emphasis placed on each level may vary from situation to situation. Next theoretical concepts and vocabularies related to analyses at each level are clarified.

## 3.3. Micro level analysis

The micro level analysis focuses on characteristics of individuals and/or organizational units that use or might adopt EDI (the squares in Fig. 1). Specific features of EDI that necessitate the use of micro level concept are: the complexity of EDI; its abstract nature; and its capability to exhibit a large number of path dependencies. Concepts adopted from economics and innovation theory (Perrow, 1986; Rogers, 1983) help understand individual’s diffusion behaviors in each pattern. These concepts single out normally factors that affect individual EDI adoption decisions but ignore factors that prevail for longer periods of time and in broader scopes (Kambil and Short, 1994). Typically micro level studies have examined the rate of the innovation diffusion (or differences in such rates), and how differences in communications influence adoption decisions (Rogers, 1983). In particular, the studies concentrate on intra-organizational characteristics including user-awareness, or what type of user hinders or furthers adoption (Galliers and Sutherland, 1991; Gurbaxani and Mendelson, 1990; Nolan, 1973, 1979). Other factors considered are: characteristics of the innovation; type of innovation decision; communication channels; and the nature of the social system (Rogers, 1983; Tornatzky and Klein, 1982). Usually adoption populations go through successive stages of adoption, finally reaching some type equilibrium (full penetration). New product or process innovations can disturb the equilibrium, which may lead to successive diffusion curves. The S-shaped form of the curve can also divide the diffusion curve into distinct stages (Gurbaxani and Mendelson, 1990; Nolan, 1973, 1979). Diffusion pattern analysis using such concepts would thus focus on specific features of EDI technology such as its triability, communicability and originality for the adopting individuals within a diffusion pattern (Bouchard, 1993; Premkumar et al., 1994; Tornatzky and Klein, 1982); and/or the structure of the social system used to communicate such features.

## 3.4. Meso level analysis

The meso level (from mesos (Greek), middle) analyses concentrate—using strategy analysis and power dependency analysis—on networks of interacting agents within each diffusion pattern. Such networks are expected to shape the diffusion trajectory (Delhaye and Lobet-Maris, 1995; Kambil and Short, 1994; Kumar and van Dissel, 1996; Porter, 1985; Webster, 1995). These types of relationships are depicted by solid connecting lines in Fig. 1. This level is necessary to cater for the inter-organizational nature of EDI, its dependency on infrastructure, and on third party operators. The level is valuable in understanding how organizational power dependencies shape and are being shaped by the diffusion process. Hence accounts of diffusion on this level are wider in scope and appreciate the impact of long term industry factors.

Meso level accounts of EDI diffusion patterns help narrate stories about interactions between adopting organizations and external institutions, their mutual dependencies and power relationships. Transaction specific investments (such as proprietary EDI standards), information asymmetries, loss of resource control, or buying power (Cox and Ghoneim, 1996; Kumar and van Dissel, 1996; Short and Venkatraman, 1992) may create such relationships. Each agent is regarded as actively seeking to achieve some selfish ends by influencing the other agents and thereby the diffusion process (Attewell, 1992; Boch, 1987; Webster, 1995). Examples of agents considered powerful are: supply-chain intermediaries;

trade and industry associations; multinational corporations and telecommunication service providers (King et al., 1994). Many of these agents are active in the technology selection process, standard setting, and they conduct pilot studies to lubricate the diffusion process (Boch, 1987; Damsgaard, 1996; Sako, 1996).

## 3.5. Macro level analysis

The macro level draws boundaries for the diffusion arena by recognizing regulatory regimes as focal points that can constrain or enable the diffusion process. The dependency of EDI on advanced infrastructures, standards and its abstract and innovative nature legitimate the need for the macro level analysis.

The institutional regimes create necessary standards, value orientations and policies that favor or constrain the diffusion process (such as incentives, symbolic value given to technologies, etc.). The diffusion process mobilizes and enacts several regulatory regimes that can intersect with the technological innovation. This can range from national and intergovernmental regimes (such as the EU), to industry specific or vendor specific (such as Microsoft’s de-facto regime of operating system standards for PCs). Such regimes can be identified and analyzed using notions adopted from institutional theory (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 1996; King et al., 1994). The role of regulatory regimes in the diffusion process is currently not well understood. Consequently, regulatory mechanisms have received scant attention in the study of diffusion (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 1996).

## 4. Field study

## 4.1. Research design and method

The framework helped identify a set of questions about EDI diffusion patterns that guided the formulation of the field study. These questions informed the collection of data and the development of explanatory accounts of the observed phenomena. Using these questions we compiled a semi-structured questionnaire. Parts of the questionnaire dealing with the micro level were obtained from an European field study of EDI diffusion (Krcmar et al., 1995). We used value chain analysis and power dependency analysis (Porter, 1985) to capture events and behaviors on the meso level, and finally we applied institutional theory to identify macro level factors (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 1996; King et al., 1994). Because we wanted to observe several and different types of diffusion patterns we organized the study as a multiple case study (Yin, 1989) where each diffusion pattern was regarded as one case. However, these cases were not fixed beforehand but emerged from the analysis of our field study data. Each case lead to a discovery of separate theoretical accounts that explained the evolution of that specific identified diffusion pattern. Thereafter using comparative analysis and induction we sought to generalize these diffusion patterns into types of diffusion patterns which were used to develop analytical generalizations (Yin, 1989).

We used a flexible data gathering strategy with a desire to find a representative and unbiased set of data (Benbasat et al., 1987). The data was mostly unstructured and non-quantitative. Therefore the primary data collection method was semi-structured interviews. In collecting interview data we contacted industries, different organizations (if possible), and people in different roles that had participated in the analyzed diffusion processes. Accordingly, the set of informants used in the study covered: government agencies; semi-public organizations; trade associations; EDI service suppliers; and individual organizations.

## 4.2. Research process

The interview sample included nine organizations from three industries: transportation; retail; and paper and pulp. These three industries were chosen because of their importance for Finland’s economy and their advanced use of EDI technologies. In addition, our study included representatives from Customs, an association of EDI users, a sales association of paper and pulp mills (paper and pulp), two EDI service suppliers, three retailers (retail), and a national rail cargo company (transportation). The data-gathering lasted 2 months, October through November 1994.

The interviews were scheduled to last approximately 2 hours, to allow a thorough examination. The interviews were carried out using a set of open questions that were derived from the questionnaire. If more interesting items were observed during the study we could digress from the interview structure. Interviews also covered normal background information about the size and type of business and about the affiliation, and education of the interviewee. The interviews were tape-recorded and transcribed. In addition, various types of written documents were collected from all the participating organizations of their EDI use and adoption, and additional notes were taken during the course of the interview. The printed material included information leaflets, annual reports and other printed material.

All transcripts and notes, along with obtained printed and archival material, were analyzed for each data point (organization) and a case story was written for each interview. The case descriptions were shared with the interviewees to check for possible errors and omissions and to evaluate the validity of our interpretation of their ‘‘story’’. All errors and omissions were corrected and some stories were modified to reflect the true opinions and perceptions of the interviewees.

The resulting case stories and their descriptions of diffusion behavior were classified into a number of themes using the study framework such as the type of diffusion pattern examined, its macro level diffusion barriers, observed business and competitive value of the solutions (meso level), and benefits for the interviewee’s organization (micro level). These themes were used to develop a set of separate diffusion patterns on the basis of industry policies and the similarity of diffusion behaviors. In this task we used content analysis (Patton, 1990), and analytical generalization (Yin, 1989).

Altogether five typical patterns were identified through a number of sorts and investigations of the data sets. Some diffusion pattern types had several instances. In the following each diffusion pattern will be discussed in terms of the content and outcomes of the diffusion process and how it took shape over time.

## 5. A spectrum of EDI diffusion in Finland

We were able to condense five typical diffusion patterns in Finland. We will go through them using the scope of the diffusion pattern as an ordering mechanism. We start with analyzing local dyadic relationships, which embraced between one organization and its immediate partners. Thereafter we turn to industry networks that covered an entire sector or industry. Finally we focus on nationwide initiatives which embodied the overall diffusion space (see also Fig. 1). The three types of patterns also correlate with the complexity of the diffusion pattern and thereby the number of the factors from three levels that have to be taken into account when seeking to understand the success or failure of that adoption process.

## 5.1. Local dyadic diffusion patterns

In this section we analyze a set of local EDI adoption processes. Such processes seek normally to achieve relatively narrow integration effects (both horizontally and vertically). The adoption decisions result thus in arrangements that Yates and Benjamin call ‘‘virtual vertical integration’’ (Yates and Benjamin, 1991), i.e. where the control over trading partners in the supply-chain is achieved by creating electronic linkages (see also O’Callaghan, 1995). Some researchers have tried to develop a framework of possible dyadic diffusion constellations. For a good literature review and one such taxonomy see Choudhury (1997). We observed one such case in our sample and it had passed the implementation phase and reached institutionalized use mode. The relatively low number of diffusion patterns of this sort does not exclude the possibility that other similar or different dyadic patterns existed at the local level in our sample—because our data set was is in no way exhaustive (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 1997).

## 5.1.1. Pattern 1. Creating a win–win deal

This dyadic pattern was an outcome of joint approach to develop a mutually beneficiary EDI service and forms nearly an opposite case in relation to experiences reported in (Nygaard-Andersen and Bjørn-Andersen, 1994; Webster, 1995). In these cases hubs in the retail sector are described as pursuing selfish ends and not allowing suppliers to grow with their experience in regard to EDI and to adopt at their own pace. This case, instead, involved a dyadic relationship between a supplier and a customer engaged in a mutual beneficiary EDI integration.

The two companies studied are dominant in the retail sector. One is a dominant wholesale company (pseudonym the A-group), and the other is a dominant chocolate and biscuit manufacturer. The business relationship has been a long term (more than a decade). Currently, they are moving towards an increasingly complex use of EDI. This is reflected in the ordering and delivery process. The process is becoming increasingly complicated and built around highly integrated business processes in ordering, invoicing, replenishment and production circles. This way both companies enjoy a high degree of virtual vertical integration, similar to O’Callaghan’s description of Alcatel Bell Telephone in Belgium (O’Callaghan, 1995). There are of course huge operational benefits to be gained from automating the ordering process. Furthermore the automated order circle allows the supplier to optimize inventories and realize JIT (Just-In-Time) production.

In extending the virtual integration the EDI systems manager explained that A-group is sending Point Of Sale (POS) data that will allow the supplier to implement EDI even further into the operation and production procedures. The idea is to ‘‘sell’’ the concept of EDI to the supplier (Ciborra, 1995), so that the supplier through EDI has the information necessary to provide the customer with the right products at the appropriate location (JIT production and delivery, Clark, 1995; Clark and Hammond, 1995; Clark and McKenny, 1994). At the time of interview the supplier did not have a system that could take an advantage of such detailed POS information. However the incentive to build the system was obvious, since it would enable a close monitoring of the market and competitors, and examination of the effects of marketing efforts.

The A-group’s goal is to automate an increasingly larger portion of its business transactions, whereby overall operating costs are reduced. One tangible benefit has been reduced human resources from handling invoices and processing orders manually. In assessing the benefits of EDI the A-group’s chief analyst explained:

I think that one data flow gives only benefit to one. But if EDI is used in a rich way then there are benefits for everybody. This is not necessarily the case for every single message. It may be so that the partner gets the benefit from one message, but the EDI goes further and further, and in the end we also get a benefit. For many years we had no benefits. Actually in many years we lost money doing EDI, but we looked into the future. Now we are starting to get the money back. EDI was so important to us, we saw the future possibilities, but somebody had to begin. We were big enough to bear the cost and now we can start to benefit from EDI. We have not taken any money from our partners from doing the kind of services, which benefit them. In the same way we do not pay them when we benefit. In the end everybody benefits. If a partner suggests us to send him some kind of information, we will, since it is easy for us.

The A-group has successfully sold the idea of EDI to its partners. In 1993 EDI messages were exchanged with 200 partners. In late 1994 41% of all outgoing orders and 91% of the invoicing volume, and all outgoing payments were handled through EDI.

## 5.2. Industry-wide diffusion patterns

In this section we analyze industry wide EDI diffusion processes. Such processes span a whole industry sector and seek to transform a larger portion of its value-adding network. Thus the diffusion pattern embraces all organizations in the same line of business, and their associated institutions. In our study sample long term business relationships and tradition of cooperation in the retail sector and the paper and pulp sector have leveraged the EDI diffusion significantly. This is similar to Swatman’s observation that one characteristic of EDI is its cooperative nature (Swatman, 1994). In the transportation industry the initiatives were launched by concessioned companies as an attempt to achieve long term operational advantages by transforming the operations in the value system.

Companies can be active players both in local dyadic adoptions and in broader industry wide adoptions. This reflects companies’ dilemma of on the one hand competing for the same limited resources and on the other hand working together to pursue common interests (Boch, 1987). This often takes place though joint activities in some trade and industry association or semi government body (see also King et al., 1994; O’Callaghan and Eistert, 1995; Sako, 1996).

## 5.2.1. Pattern 2. Wholesales gentle push

The grocery sector in Finland is centralized when compared to many countries (e.g. Italy and Spain, Ciborra, 1995; O’Callaghan and Eistert, 1995). There are essentially three wholesale companies that account for 80% of the grocery market. The largest, the A-group (from the previous pattern), has a market share of 40%, followed by the B-Group and C-group (these are also pseudonyms), that have a market share of 18% and 21%, respectively. There has been a long tradition of information sharing and exchanging data in the grocery sector in Finland. EDI-like systems started in the mid 1970s with the exchange of magnetic tapes in a standard format between large wholesale companies and the large suppliers (e.g. a dairy). This was limited to invoicing only. The benefits of introducing a larger variety of messages and electronic networks was recognized in the Finnish grocery sector in the mid 1980s. Distributing and coordinating the flow of goods in a low population density area like Finland was costly, and therefore the companies primarily sought cost reductions when assessing the business value of EDI.

The A-group was the first mover, and because of its superior size it has set the rules by which EDI is adopted. When A-group considered EDI there were no international standards. Therefore the necessary standards had to be developed on a national scale. The chief systems analyst explained that the company decided not to use the standards as a competitive weapon as follows:

We feel that it is not right to compete in standards. It is like competing by using a different telephone system, it is foolish. It will become very non-profitable in the future. This concept is widely recognized in Finland.

The other two wholesale companies initially left the initiative with the A-group. It soon became evident, however, that a neutral forum for EDI coordination and standardization was needed (Boch, 1987). Therefore an association for the coordination and facilitation of EDI in Finland was established. The Finnish Data Communication Association became responsible for the development of a national Finnish standard, and it also functions as a library for implementation guidelines. A representative from the association explained:

…we have agreed that the first who implements a new message will also write and publish an implementation guide in Finnish. This is necessary since the standards are so complicated. If two partners independently implement a new standard, they will implement them differently. The implementation guide is a very good tradition. When the international standards arrived in the late 1980s, people said ‘‘let’s throw away the national standards and use the international standards’’, but we soon learned that it was also impossible to implement the international standards separately.

The national standard became an instant success, because it was developed as a joint effort and was made to fit local business practices. When the international standards were established, the Finns confronted with a new question: to keep the original Finnish standard or to adopt the new international standard? The success of the national standard in this way became a barrier to an early adoption of EDI related international standards. Furthermore, the international standard kept changing at a rapid pace as more functionality was build into it and new versions released. Today most of the original first adopters still use the original standard, whereas later adopters are using the international standard (UN/EDIFACT). The same argument explains why the grocery sector companies have chosen to adopt the general EDIFACT standard instead of the EANCOM<sup>2</sup> subset of EDIFACT.

In persuading suppliers to join the network the A-group has chosen a strategy of a gentle push (see also MacKay, 1993). The chief analyst explained:

Our policy has been to encourage but not force partners to do EDI. If we had forced them they would implement EDI in one function for our communication only. They would be reluctant to implement EDI for other purposes. The best method is to leave it to the partners to figure EDI out themselves.

When we asked of barriers to diffusion, two specific ones were mentioned in particular: old standards and old applications. Like in Ciborra’s study (Ciborra, 1995) interviewees indicated that building the interface to existing system was not trivial, but formed a major barrier. Some of the old systems had multiple entries for the same suppliers, data was often redundant and it was distributed in several systems, and gaining access to the core of the system was difficult, since nobody knew what was going on in the old systems kernel.

## 5.2.2. Pattern 3. Long tradition of cooperation

This pattern evolved within the pulp and paper industry that accounts for a significant share of the Finnish manufacturing output (Mills, 1988; Reponen and Copeland, 1985). The Finnish paper and board mills have a long tradition of combining their forces for marketing and selling their products on the international market. This is done through two sales associations offering complementary products (paper and board). Together they represent 45 mills. At the time of interviews a total of 90% of Finnish paper and pulp sales went through these associations that are represented world-wide with 100 sales offices in 50 countries . The revenue of the organization was annually around 20 Billion - FIMs. During the time of the interviews, the sales association was doing EDI with 175–200 partners around the world.

In regard to EDI the initial partners were US printing houses. They were chosen because US printing houses represent big volume and they also procure a large variety of products. The distribution chain from Finland to the US is very long and impeded, with a number of middle hands. Therefore the first message, which was implemented, describes the delivery from the warehouse to the customer. The customer is eager to obtain this information. Providing this service therefore gives a competitive advantage to the sales association. At the time of the interviews the sales association did not receive anything from the US customers, but messages for ordering and payment were under development.

When addressing the value of EDI, everybody concurred about the competitive value of introducing EDI systems to improve business relationships with customers.

EDI is the invisible part of our business or products. We see it the way that it is more or less part of the customer approach. This means in terms of technology that in addition to the actual products we are able to give associated information about the products. It is customer service…. The relationship with the US printing houses have improved and the communication is deeper. Now not only the sales people and purchase people communicate, but also other parts of both organizations are in contact. That is a good development.

## 5.2.3. Pattern 4. Concessioned companies showing the way

This pattern evolved in relatively broad diffusion arena, but was still confined into one industry sector. In fact we did not find many cross-sectoral EDI linkages. It seems that here the Finns are still left with a major challenge. The pattern is supported by three illustrations, which exhibit that the pattern is quite well established in Finland. The pattern evolves around large government or semi-government owned enterprises (Similar findings reported in Singpore (Neo et al., 1993, 1994). Common for these companies are that they are privileged in a way that limits or removes competition from their functional areas. Another representative trait is the desire to improve their image as a modern and advanced user of IT. Exemplary is also that they have been first movers and have started EDI early in their functional areas.

## 5.2.4. Illustration A. Transportation: putting the transportation industry on the right track

The cargo part of the Finnish state-owned railways handles all transportation of goods on rails in Finland (See also (Bjørn-Andersen and Nygaard-Andersen, 1995) for a review of a similar Danish EDI initiative). It started doing EDI in 1991 and it is exchanging EDIFACT messages with 10 partners. The messages account for 20–25% of the total transportation volume.

The main barrier for further diffusion is the low percentage of transport companies that have adopted EDI. One of the reasons for introducing the system has been a desire to move the whole transportation sector towards an increasingly sophisticated use of IT. The fast and committed move into EDI has also been to improve the corporate image of the company. The value of the technology has mainly been reductions in operating costs. The issue was clarified to us this way:

It is obvious that we have a competitive advantage by using EDI. It is also obvious that it is harder for our customers to switch to another transportation company because they do EDI with us, this is also because we are the most advanced in EDI within the Finnish transportation sector. Nevertheless the best value, to us, is undoubtedly cost reduction.

The benefits of EDI have mainly been reduced document handling and reduced human resource dedicated to red tape. Like in Jelassi’s study (Jelassi, 1995) the human resource surplus had not been laid off, but rather assigned to other tasks. The interviewee emphasized the situation.

We did not apply EDI to save human resource. The employees have been relieved for what they are here for, i.e. to sell transportation and not to struggle with paper. Nobody has been laid off because of EDI.

## 5.2.5. Illustration B. Transportation: piloting EDI to port

The Finnish ports, which are municipality owned, have been involved in a similar initiative. The new PortNet is mainly an administrative system. The basic idea is that the shipping agent will give pre-arrival notice with an estimate of the arrival of a ship. The system will then generate the necessary information to the appropriate authorities that will prepare the needed documents for clearing. Closer to arrival the shipping agent will send another EDI message with more detailed information, what services are required for the ship, and whether it needs a pilot. The Finnish Customs is connected to the system and receives information about cargo for control and statistics purposes. For the small shipping agencies a PC-based solution has been developed. What is unusual for the system is that it is a national system that covers all major ports in Finland (Finland has a coastal line of over 1500 km plus some inland ports in the lakes. Overall there are ca. 10 major hubs). The system is mainly intended to serve the authorities, but the Government, the ports and the shipping agents developed it as a joint effort. In Singapore government authorities have set up TradeNet, which is a similar system (King and Konsynski, 1990a,b). One difference, however, is that using the Singapore system is mandatory (Neo et al., 1994).

As many small shipping agents do not possess sufficient knowledge to implement an EDI system, their lack of knowledge forms a significant barrier. The problem was addressed by developing a PC-based version of the system, which was low cost and easy to use. A similar problem and response is reported in Singapore (Neo et al., 1993). Because the system was developed through joint efforts the main value from the system is expected to be operational rather than competitive. The major benefits are related to reductions in handling paper documents. Another benefit has been improved business relations between the authorities on the one hand and the shipping agents on the other hand. This was achieved by developing the system together.

## 5.2.6. Illustration C. Trade: opening borders for EDI

By the end of 1991 Finnish Customs introduced its EDI system. One of the goals of introducing the EDI system for custom clearance has been to improve and promote the use of EDI in the trading. The system allows traders to declare imported goods. By 1995 over 50% of all import declarations were received in electronic form.

In the analysis of barriers we found that the relative large number of SMEs forms the most significant barrier since they neither have the resources nor the skills to implement and operate EDI. The EDI project have improved Customs’ relations with the trading community and the image of Customs as a slow and inefficient government unit has shifted towards an image of a modern organization. For Customs the benefits of EDI has been a reduction of document handling and it has also enabled Customs to provide better service to its customers.

## 5.3. Macro level diffusion patterns

## 5.3.1. Pattern 5. EDI the Finnish way

Here we synthesize observations of the macro level initiatives by making some claims of how EDI diffusion overall is addressed in Finland. Some countries have national EDI plans (for example Denmark (Ministry of Research and Information Technology, 1996) and Singapore (King and Konsynski, 1990a,b; Neo et al., 1993), while others like Finland have chosen to support EDI diffusion indirectly.

Finland as a highly developed industrial country with an advanced infrastructure, a well-educated workforce, a sophisticated industrial base and excellent telecommunications infrastructure meets all necessary conditions for the rapid uptake of EDI. The Finnish practice to achieve this has been to promote grassroots initiatives and escalating technology trials, which have lead to rapid and wide uptake of EDI in different industrial sectors. This has happened in nearly all situations where the companies and the industry sectors have recognized promising to exchange information in electronic form<sup>4</sup>. Here intermediary industry organizations and professional organizations have played a prominent role as coordinators and educators. There are several reasons for this mode of policy. Some are related to the fact that Finland is a Nordic welfare state. Other explanations are needed however, because the Finnish experience and diffusion patterns are quite different from, for example, the Danish strategies, while Denmark has the same prerequisites for the fast uptake (Damsgaard, 1997).

One reason is that Finns have been able to develop tight social networks across industries and government organizations that help mobilize fast necessary resources and create bias for EDI uptake. Another is the consensus-oriented culture in Finland. For example, Finland is the only country in Europe where the conservatives, communists and the greens are sitting in the same cabinet (this is called the RAINBOW coalition Finland). Accordingly, all industry initiatives followed a consensus oriented strategy successfully—a phenomenon unheard in countries like Hong Kong (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 1997) or Denmark (Damsgaard, 1997). This consensus-seeking attitude is to a large extent an outcome of the harsh history of the Finnish nation—especially during the Second World War when Finns were twice left alone to fight against the Red Army. Most Finns feel that they only can survive by being united and they cannot depend on external help. The huge geographical size combined with a low population and oligopolistic market structures (transportation, retail, and paper and pulp) lead early on to realize the benefits of EDI to reduce operation costs. EDI was in the late 1970s still in its infancy thus creating the need to develop and implement joint technical standards, which demanded considerable investments and coordination. Therefore a common approach to EDI was chosen, which was driven by the need to create a technology push. The need for technology coordination created also institutional regimes that governed the evolution and distribution of standards and also had a significant role in knowledge dissemination and deployment. One of the

interviewees explained:

In Finland we have put a lot of work into doing EDI in common. Finland is very small, so EDI is not profitable if we use different standards….

This tradition of collaboration continues today and it is most likely to prevail also in the future where Finland as member of the European Union faces increased competition in an open European market. One of the interviewees clarified the matter this way:

EDI is a competitive necessity, when taking into account Finland’s geographical situation. When we are importing goods or exporting goods our major trading partners are located in Central Europe. So it is a competitive necessity to Finland to have our foreign trade practices and procedures as smooth and effective as possible.

## 6. Discussion and conclusions

Our study disclosed five EDI diffusion patterns in Finland. None of these patterns are uniquely new, as all of them have been in one form or another discussed in the previous literature. What is new is our starting point to use them as the units of analysis for diffusion theory. Based on this and our earlier studies we already know a great deal of the factors that affect the success of each diffusion pattern. What is unique in our analysis, is to show how these patterns are interwoven and take place simultaneously within a specific diffusion unit like Finland. Moreover, different diffusion processes cannot be understood without the interference of the other processes. Diffusion patterns criss-cross one another and may change over time from one pattern type to another through changes in the service scope, underlying technology, or institutional arrangements.

Our analysis suggests that a more complete understanding of EDI diffusion rates and patterns requires both breadth and depth in the analysis—not only at the organizational level, or within the industry sector, or using institutional analysis. We need all of them simultaneously in the study of EDI diffusion and accordingly we should focus on the complex mixture of different elements located at different levels of the analysis.

In the past most of the initiatives have been launched within local dyadic constellations or in narrow industry segments. Many of these have been implemented with considerable success. Similar to Bouchard’s study (Bouchard, 1993) we observed that the main reason for adopting EDI in dyadic cases has been the ‘‘herd’’ effect and not the direct benefits or other intangibles accrued through EDI use (See also Nygaard-Andersen and Bjørn-Andersen, 1994). The key question in singular adoptions was: are important business partners using EDI or are these partners ‘‘encouraging’’ its use? In this pattern it is large companies (i.e. hubs) that launch EDI. They can do so either in conflict mode or using a common good approach, or they might even shift between them (Kumar and van Dissel, 1996). Some hubs with sufficient bargaining power demand business partners to adopt EDI regardless of their IT capabilities or needs, as it is so commonly referenced in the automotive industry (Reekers and Smithson, 1994). As such the hubs participate in a technology race (Perez and Soete, 1988) to dominate the choice of standards, increase market share, and preserve their power (for typical examples see (Nygaard-Andersen and

Bjørn-Andersen, 1994; Webster, 1995). This most frequently happens were several hubs are engaged in fierce competition and rivalry, or some sector is experiencing decreasing profit margins (e.g. international transportation). Other hubs elude this EDI or DIE strategy and instead look to establish an electronic infrastructure, where its widespread adoption will take costs out of the value chain through more efficient customer response (Clark, 1995; Clark and Hammond, 1995) and lower transaction costs (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 1997). Here the goal is to decrease the overall costs and thereby secure all parties increased benefits. This strategy is successful where there are high transaction and transportation costs (as in Finland), or a consensus among hubs to build up a common basic infrastructure (following the ethos of ‘‘cooperate in morning, compete in the afternoon’’ strategy (Keen, 1991). The latter approach seems to be the most common in Finland.

With industry-wide networks the logic of adoption decisions was more complicated. Organizations did not decide only on adopting the EDI service, they were also opting for a specific strategic choice and seeking for a business opportunity through a strategic alliance. In the case of the retail industry the largest player took the challenge, and played a significant role in clearing a cooperative arena for the EDI diffusion. In other industries, concessioned companies have taken the lead and implemented EDI for the common benefit for the whole sector. This exemplifies that EDI is not a stand-alone technical component that can be implemented independently or in isolation from other companies working in the same line of business (Kumar and van Dissel, 1996), i.e. it exhibits path dependencies and has large negative or positive network externalities (Oliva, 1994). Its networked nature and dependence on standards necessitates some kind of collaboration and common infrastructure. Network externalities and associated ‘‘prisoners dilemma’ make it profitable to postpone the adoption of EDI until after the standard has been set and a majority of business partners have already adopted EDI. This is actually the converse of what Rogers (1983) would suppose. In Rogers’ analysis it is the first adopters that reap the benefits of new innovations. Therefore the meso level analysis, that focuses on these aspects, is a necessary ingredient to appreciate the diffusion of EDI in industry networks.

Within the scope of national initiatives the Finnish Government has limited its role to providing a supporting infrastructure and by providing some funding for the Finnish Data Communication Association. This organization has played a leading role in developing standards, sharing experiences and increasing the awareness about EDI. In addition, several public organizations have launched EDI trials in their domains to arm Finland with a public EDI infrastructure. They have done so, also, to consolidate their business positions and to improve their image. Some times they followed this due to strategic necessities: they felt responsibility to introduce EDI for their domain. The fact that the Finnish Government has not directly intervened in the EDI diffusion does not seem to have impeded the diffusion of EDI. This is in contrast to what Farhoomand and Boyer suggest for developing countries of the Far East (Farhoomand and Boyer, 1994). Though the question of fostering and sustaining a national system of innovation has recently received a growing attention in the literature (Andersen, 1998; Cats-Baril and Jelassi, 1994), it is unclear to what degree and through which means governments could or should intervene (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 1996). In Finland the government has adopted a coordinating role (as a neutral partner). As a promoter of EDI it has sought mainly to introduce bias through training and information distribution services and also actively supported organizations, which support the establishment of EDI infrastructure. The international turmoil about EDI and EDI standards has not played a major role in the diffusion of EDI. This is due to the fact that Finland has until recently been a relatively protected market with oligopolistic structures in most industries which has resulted in high internal coupling of the markets. Another reason is that Finland has been 5–6 years ahead of the international community in the adoption of EDI, which has created the need to develop own technical standards. In the future a more open economy and advances in Web based technologies may alter this tradition of national collaboration about EDI towards a more loose coupled network with extensive linkages to other countries.

To summarize, the dyadic pattern was initiated by a desire to achieve virtual vertical integration. The industry-wide patterns by the oligopolistic structure and cooperative strategy, and the national initiatives were driven by a desire to develop a supporting electronic infrastructure and realizing a vision of an electronic society. When we juxtapose the main conjectures of DOI (diffusion of innovation) research with our empirical findings, we do not believe that any specific ‘‘diffusion’’ theory with push and pull factors can explain these observations. In particular, we suspect the capability of the Rogers’ type innovation and diffusion theory (Rogers, 1983), which solely focus on individual adopters and on static features of technology. The fundamental premises of the DOI model can be summarized in the following six conjectures (Mahajan et al., 1990; Mahajan and Peterson, 1985; Premkumar et al., 1994; Prescott and Conger, 1995; Rogers, 1983; Tornatzky and Klein, 1982): (1) technologies form a set of separate, distinguishable and objective features, which are easily recognizable; (2) the technology moves in a discrete package from a separate innovator to the adopter through a constant social ‘‘ether’’; (3) the user’s decision to adopt is based on her rational choice based on technological characteristics and other information available through the information channels; (4) her choice to adopt is seen as an isolated decision and is shaped by the push and pull factors; (5) the diffusion process moves through distinct stages, which are determined by the push and pull forces; and finally (6) the process has neither feedback nor any notion of history.

Very few of these factors are of primary importance in explaining the rate and success of the EDI diffusion in Finland. Instead the factors seem to change radically when the scope of the diffusion expands and the number of stakeholders grows. We can follow a ‘‘Popperian’’ approach and demonstrate for each conjecture a counterexample or sets of counterexamples, which refute each conjecture of adopting complex and networked technologies like EDI (see, for example, Lee, 1989; Popper, 1968). In short the major keys to understand the success of EDI diffusion in Finland are not solely technological, nor economic, nor rational. Instead they cover a blend of institutional, technological, socioeconomic and cultural factors including the following.

• Because many Finnish companies adopted EDI before any international standards existed, the technical problems associated with developing EDI systems have been a major barrier. Consequently competitors had to collaborate to design and implement EDI (refutes conjecture number 1).

• One of the main reasons for adopting EDI has been the ‘‘herd’’ effect and not the direct benefits or other intangibles accrued through EDI use (refutes conjecture 4).

• Cooperation and win–win situations are the most common (refutes conjecture number 5).

• The first EDI implementations were initiated between existing business partners exchanging proprietary EDI messages on magnetic tapes (refutes conjectures number 4 and 6).

• Cooperation was necessary to establish national Finnish standards and the tradition of cooperation prevails (refutes conjecture number 2).

The success of the Finnish standard over time became a barrier to the diffusion of EDI based on the international EDIFACT standard (refutes conjecture number 2, 4 and 6).

• In many Finnish industries there has been a long tradition of collaboration (refutes conjecture number 6).

• A trade and industry association was set up to coordinate EDI standardization and implementation (refutes conjecture number 2).

• Government and semi-government authorities have been active in setting up a public EDI infrastructure (refutes conjectures number 2 and 5).

• The SMEs’ lack of knowledge and skills to adopt EDI is a major hurdle to diffusion (refutes conjecture number 3).

We therefore believe that to fully comprehend the diffusion of networked and complex technologies it is necessary to approach the diffusion process using a multiple units of analysis and mutiple levels of theory, as depicted in Fig. 1. The study demonstrates that each of the three levels brings a new contour when painting the whole landscape of diffusion. We therefore suggest that diffusion researchers should trade generalizability against accuracy and simplicity in their theoretical accounts of diffusion. In short, knowing better and deeper is more important than knowing broader.

Several issues beg for further clarification. First, do these patterns prevail in other settings, and how do they interact? What factors affect their birth and mortality? Therefore we are conducting similar field studies else where to shed light over these questions. Finally a longitudinal study could describe how actors play various roles at different phases of the diffusion.
