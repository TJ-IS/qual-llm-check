# Problems, knowledge, solutions: solving complex problems – a response to Enid Mumford’s paper ICIS 1998, Helsinki, Finland

R.D. Galliers<sup>1</sup>

Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, UK

In reflecting on Enid’s paper, I have been thinking of two things, one serious, the other somewhat flippant. First, the more serious comment. What I have always respected about Enid’s work is the fact that she makes us think, and brings an ethical dimension to our discourse, whether it is the need for an emanicipatory, inclusive dimension to systems design, improving the working environment of secretaries or, in this case, drawing our attention to complex, pervasive and worrying problems against which societies, across the globe, appear defenceless. My purpose in this response, is to reflect on and reinforce Enid’s messages, and then attempt to draw some initial, tentative conclusions as to what these messages mean for the Information Systems community.

The more flippant thought is a quote from Somerset Maugham: ‘‘It is bad enough to know the past – intolerable to know the future.’’ But that attitude, while entirely understandable, is inappropriate given the threatening nature and devastating impacts of the problems to which Enid draws our attention. And not only do we need to recognise those problems to which she refers, we need also to anticipate them, and the ‘‘ability to analyse the present and forecast the future [is] always a difficult task,’’ as Enid notes.

Her article also reflects, I think, a growing awareness amongst us in the Information Systems community that our subject domain is broader than we had first thought. While it is undoubtedly true that we (well, many of us at any rate!), unlike our computer science colleagues, tended to focus our attention from the technology outwards, we often drew the boundaries to our problem space very narrowly. All (sic.) we were interested in was the design of the computer system: organisational or process design was the domain of the OD or OM specialist. Change management? Well, what had that got to do with us? We simply designed the computer system, surely? But problems do not come nicely packaged any more – as if they ever did – the complex problems of today, made more difficult given the speed with which change takes place, together with the ‘reach’ of the impacts of some influence somewhere in ‘the system’, require a transdisciplinary approach – what Gibbons (1995) calls Mode 2 thinking. This is where and how necessary new knowledge – and solutions – will emerge.

Enid reminds us of the early work of the distinguished systems scientist C. West Churchman in raising such questions as:

<sub>•</sub> which aspects of the problem (or problematique, as the French more accurately and succinctly describe the nexus of problems to which he and Enid refer) should be given most attention;

<sub>•</sub> how can we be sure that the information on which we base our judgements is ‘‘correct or distorted’’?

how and when do we know whether the problem (sic.) has been solved (sic.)? and what if it is an on-going one, continually re-emerging and/or reforming itself?

how can the problem solver ever be seen as separate from the problem (sic.)? are not they an integral part of the problem space? (Churchman, 1971).

She also reminds us that no single solution is likely to work. Referring to Ashby’s Law of Requisite Variety (Ashby, 1956), she notes that ‘‘only variety can control variety’’. Contrast that message with the siren call of many a supplier, peddling the drug of a ‘‘simple’’ or ‘‘total’’ solution! A quote of HL Mencken taken from Chris Sauer’s book on systems failures (Sauer, 1993; p. 315) is apposite in this regard: To every problem there is a simple solution – and it is always wrong. And she reminds us that problems look different from different perspectives: funnily enough, different people have different views about the same issue. As a result, a stakeholder perspective is required – something for which Checkland (1981) actively argues. We all have our biases, and that is why the phenomenologists such as Husserl remind us that our research should always include an account of the lens through which we investigate any particular phenomenon. And what of the ‘bias’ of time? Enid rightly reminds us that in this day and age, time seems to march on with astounding rapidity. A phenomenon we may study at time t.1, will undoubtedly have changed by time t.2.

So, all this suggests that we require sound problem formulation methods as well as sound problem solving methods, and the issue of implementation-an old favourite of the IS community!-raises its head once again. Indeed, Kwon and Zmud would also include innovation in this rubric (Kwon and Zmud, 1987). The work of Rosenhead and colleagues is relevant in relation to problem formulation and problem solving techniques (Rosenhead, 1989), and we would do well to note the work arising from research in the general field of change management (refs.) so that our ‘solutions’ may stand a better chance of success! In addition, Enid calls for the development of sound theories to aid our interventions and our learning-the kind of ‘double-loop’ learning advocated by Argyris and Scho¨n [1978], and the kind of reflective learning that emerges from the kind of applied (or action) research projects to be found in Master’s and Doctoral programmes-and please forgive my parochialism here - at Lancaster, the LSE and Warwick in the UK. Who was it who said: ‘‘There is nothing so practical as a good theory’’?

What does this all mean for the Information Systems community? And what are the implications for our research agendas and for curriculum design? The key capabilities that Enid lists are: problem formulation and solving skills; learning and sharing knowledge; time management and interpersonal skills, including team working and ‘‘stickability’’;

such organisational skills as strategic thinking, resource allocation and an understanding of the bigger picture; innovation and an ability to think creatively, and last, but by no means least, acting ethically. While we see these attributes being covered in leading MBA programmes, they are noticeable by their absence in many IS programmes unfortunately. Successful, forward thinking CIOs, such as Justin Yaros of 20th Century Fox (Infoworld, 1998), emphasise these kinds of attributes, however, while not minimising the importance of technical knowledge and the identification of opportunities arising from new technological developments. Teamworking and networking is particularly important in this context (Swan and Galliers, 1997) as, increasingly, we require different viewpoints and expertise in dealing with the complexity which we face. Increasingly, too, such networks will span organisational boundaries and the incidence of global, virtual teams in now commonplace. While face-to-face contact and communication is important, as has been noted by Enid and other researchers in the area such as Leidner (1999), the issue of trust, coordination and cooperation takes on a new significance in this context. We should, however, be aware that indiscriminant networking can be as wasteful of time and energy as indiscriminant web surfing. Continuing problems such as ‘information overload’ (sic) come to mind in this context too.

From this analysis it would appear that our research agendas require perhaps a broader outlook: team working in virtual contexts; a reevaluation of our theories of strategy alignment and implementation in the context of inter-organisational systems; security issues; the diffusion of innovations – all these appear ripe for further in-depth study as do the sort of concerns Enid highlights.

A word of warning, however: when we come to look at current research agendas and the issues upon which CIOs are constantly focusing, these themes tend not to appear as visibly as one might have thought or wished. It is to Enid’s credit that she has drawn these issues to our attention and we would do well to hear her message and consider the implications for our future endeavours. A final comment: we are currently overly conservative and parochial in our editorial policies. Few editors would wish to run the risk of publishing such wide-ranging and thought provoking material. If our field is to advance to meet head on the challenges that confront us, however, it is my view that such risks should more readily be taken. We should take on board this wider perspective rather than view the world through the myopic lenses we have used up till now. Let us hope we can rise to Enid’s challenge.
