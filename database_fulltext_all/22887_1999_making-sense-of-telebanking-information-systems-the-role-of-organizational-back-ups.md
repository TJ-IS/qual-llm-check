# Making sense of telebanking information systems: the role of organizational back ups

M. Faia-Correia<sup>\*</sup>, G. Patriotta, M. Brigham, J.M. Corbett

Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, UK

Received 24 September 1998; revised paper accepted for publication by Professor R.D. Galliers 15 May 1999

## Abstract

A case study of a telebanking information system located within a state-owned European bank is described and analysed. Findings indicate that while the information system supports the operators’ work practice in routine situations, it fails to do so when the work flow is disrupted. The case demonstrates how a pervasive pattern of stability is achieved through a variety of back-up mechanisms enacted and improvised in the work setting. The paper concludes with the implications of these findings for the theory and practice of information systems design. q 1999 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Telebanking unformation system; Back-up mechanisms; Information systems design

## 1. Introduction

Traditionally, the introduction of new information systems in the work setting has been informed by a rational vision of design, which treats technology as a neutral, unproblematic entity. Technology is assumed to embody authority, objectivity and technical necessity (Scarbrough and Corbett, 1992), and hence can be introduced in a top–down manner consistent with an engineering logic of design and use. Within this technology paradigm, designing an objective technical system means designing functional requirements and specifications in terms of the visible patterns of activities in the work organization, namely routines, operations, work flows, economic transactions, etc. The epitome of a rational design is the notion of automation, whereby work is translated into specific items of machinery or technical systems in order to streamline processes and increase organizational efficiency. The procedure adopted when automating a task is first to break the task down into its smallest components. The information from this analysis makes it possible to rationalize events and processes to the extent that human agency can be translated into a computer program.

In the last decade, a new body of literature has emerged which has challenged the dominant rational vision of design. In her groundbreaking work on ‘smart machines’, Zuboff (1988) detected two faces of intelligent technology which bear crucial implications for any computer-mediated design activity. One refers to its power of automating operations; the second concerns the capability of IT to translate the production process into a visible form and to ‘textualize’ work. As a consequence of their capability to ‘informate’, smart machines not only enable the organization to process information, but also produce information. In a similar vein, the study of situated work practices (after Suchman, 1987) has shown how work may be conceptualized as a situated human activity involving a continual (re-)interpretation of resources and structures, and the maintenance of identity when confronted by both routine and breakdown events (see, e.g. Weick, 1990; Brown and Duguid, 1991; Ciborra et al., 1995; Orr, 1996). Employing such a perspective, Orlikowski (1992) emphasizes the need for placing technology in context and analysing the linkages between action and structures. Based on Giddens’ structuration theory (1984), Orlikowski (1992) recognizes the fundamental duality of any technology: technology shapes and the shaping by the organizational context hosting it. Finally, Ciborra and Patriotta (1996) have looked at the encounter between technology and organization as an ‘open match’, where socio-technical boundaries are brittle and subject to a dynamic process of negotiation between human and non-human actors.

The fundamental difference between the two perspectives presented above becomes visible in the conceptualization of technology as a platform. The term platform has at least two meanings: a platform is a neutral, standard infrastructure that can be introduced in a work setting in a top–down manner. This definition is consistent with what we have called the rational vision of design, which seems to take for granted the neutrality of the technological ‘black box’. On the other hand, when we look at organizational practice, it becomes apparent that systems are never adopted as they are—as neutral platforms— but rather they become a point of departure that is continuously redefined in the light of existing organizational values and practices, power relationships and conventions. Because of this process of reconfiguration and redefinition, every information system mirrors—to a certain extent—the organization hosting it: technology embodies, and is an instantiation of, some of the rules and resources constituting the structure of the organization (Orlikowski, 1992). Moreover, further re-definitions are performed by the users, as part of the process of appropriation of technology.

In this paper, we focus on two important aspects of the organizational practices surrounding the introduction and embedding of a new technology into a work setting. The first emphasizes the need to look at any design activity as a holistic process. As Winograd and Flores (1986) have pointed out, any technological artefact is situated within a heterogeneous network that includes other equipment, materials, institutional arrangements, practices and conventions. Accordingly, the effective use of a new technology implies an understanding of its physical, organizational and social features. The second important aspect concerns the ambiguity that the technology expresses in relation to practice. Weick (1990) has argued that the complex nature of new technologies requires specific cognitive models and sense-making capabilities that enable the user to represent and understand events associated with them. New technologies can be seen as equivoque because they admit several interpretations and therefore can be subject to misunderstandings. As a consequence, although technologies are partially self-controlled, individuals have to handle the unexpected and provide back-up controls when automatic control systems fail. This second aspect of our study highlights the importance of improvisation.

The paper attempts to understand the above processes by deliberately blurring any distinction between the technical and the social in an examination of the patterns whereby organizational knowledge and practices are reproduced and re-informed through means of inscription in computer-based information systems. Our argument is illustrated by a case study conducted at a telebanking (TB) centre of a large public European bank. Based on rich ethnographic data, the paper focuses on the technology in action by describing some typical scenes observed in relation to the use of the new TB system. The scenes portray the TB operators and their ongoing relationship with the TB information system. At first sight, these would appear to be, respectively, the knowledge seekers and the information provider. However, such a conceptualization does not account (or allow) for the multiple practices of organizing or the dynamic nature of knowledge requirements inherent in a system comprising customers, operators, technicians, managers, supervisors and the TB information system.

## 2. Research method

The research took the form of an in-depth case study, utilizing an interpretative method (Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991; Walsham, 1993). This stemmed from an epistemological belief that in order to understand the ordering and stabilizing processes of work, it is necessary to understand and undercover how everyday working interactions—what Porac et al. (1991) term ‘micro-momentary actions’—stabilize and reproduce organizational work relationships. The first named author spent over four weeks observing life in the TB, analysing documents and memoranda, as well as interviewing key personnel. A total of 14 in-depth interviews were conducted (two TB managers, three supervisors, two technicians and seven operators) in an effort to uncover the day-to-day sense-making activities associated with work in a TB. Interview transcripts were translated into English by the first author.

The research site was chosen on the strength of the quality of research analysis it offered. The company was able to offer high quality access, which as Streeck (1986) has pointed out, is an indispensable, but rarely granted, requirement for qualitative research. In addition, the company’s TB represented an ideal case study through which to explore the ways in which the implementation of a complex information and communication system may affect, and be affected by, organizational sense-making activities. As we shall see in Section 3, the company’s Board of Directors attempted to establish a TB which would be structured, managed and operated in the same manner as all of its other (non-telebanking) branches. Hence, our case study offered an opportunity to assess the neutrality (and thus the controllability) of a particular TB platform from the viewpoint of operators, support staff and managers.

Our case analysis highlights a pervasive pattern of stability, based on a variety of back up mechanisms, embedded in the design of the work setting. This leads us to describe the interplay between information systems and TB employees as a set of interconnected spirals, where practices of each actor (human and non-human) are backed up by other actors. This spiral maintains stability to the whole socio-technical system.

## 3. MCF telebank

## 3.1. Company background

MCF—a pseudonym—is a large, long-established state-owned European credit bank. The bank employs around 9000 people and has over 500 branches nationwide. Interviews revealed a fairly consistent picture of MCF as a centralized, bureaucratic and traditionally minded bank. In a typical branch of MCF, bank clerks were given little discretion or autonomy. Any non-routine transactions were required to be dealt with by supervisors or, in some circumstance, by the branch manager. Some interviewees talked, somewhat disparagingly, of the new company headquarters, likening it to a ‘huge dinosaur’. Few employees seemed aware of the major restructuring that had been undertaken in recent years—indicative of the one-way upward flow of information commonly associated with a hierarchical bureaucratic organizational structure. Interviewees perceived the company to be a very solid, risk-averse and trustworthy institution. For example, the TB manager described MCF as “a moral bastion, a serious institution that does not cheat people”. A similar sentiment was expressed by an operator who suggested that “three ‘S’s describe MCF. It is secure, serious and solid”.

The rationale offered by the MCF management for a strategic investment in TB reflects this conservatism. As is so common in many business sectors throughout the US and Europe, technology investment decisions at MCF were primarily reactive in nature (Scarbrough and Corbett, 1992). The MCF Board noted with unease that a number of its competitors were investing in TB, and feared the consequences for the company if they failed to compete. Branch managers, on the other hand, were not fully convinced that they needed a TB, not only because the bank had the biggest branch network in the country, but also because they perceived their customers as not being ideal types for ‘tele-mediated services.

It was clear from the company documentation and memoranda that the Board of Directors agreed to make a substantial investment in TB, on the understanding that the operation of the TB would mirror that carried out in all of the 520 other branches. Essentially, they regarded the TB simply as a ‘bank without a counter’. In this way, it was believed that customers would get the benefit of TB facilities without losing any of the benefits associated with a more traditional banking. In short, the TB system should support and augment corporate culture and the well-tried and tested practices of bank–customer relations. In this way, MCF was attempting to assimilate TB and all the technology that it involves under previous patterns of practice and interpretation in order to reduce the possibility of a conflict between new technology and MCF’s conservative culture.

## 3.2. Telebank technology and MCF

Telemediated banking services involve the integration of telecommunications and computer technology to provide personal financial services remotely from a ‘branchless’ retail bank. A TB can be defined as a ‘dedicated’ operation in which computer-utilizing agents receive inbound or make outbound telephone calls, with calls processed by an automated call distribution system (ACD). TB involves ‘space shrinking’ information and communication technologies, and extends these technologies towards the customer such that a variety of services supplying consumer markets, production and consumption no longer need to occur in the same place (Richardson and Marshall, 1996).

At the heart of TB is the ACD. This sends incoming calls wherever they need to go. As a telephone system handling large call volume, it automatically offers the next call to the operator who has been waiting for the longest period. These devices are very sophisticated and can be configured within a variety of parameters in order to give priorities to different callers and send certain types of calls to specific groups or individuals, based on previous calls as well as different services, priorities and skills.

Calls arrive at the ACD. The ACD can recognize and differentiate calls from the number the caller has dialled, and can therefore handle these differently. It needs computer telephony integration (CTI) to recognize the caller’s telephone number. The system also provides real-time statistics on call flow and operator availability, thus allowing real-time reconfiguration of staff resources. The operators, wearing headsets, are seated in front of a terminal screen connected to a Customer Information System. This system breaks transactions into their components and takes callers through the transaction via menu-driven software. ACDs are, then, used to speed up work, but, crucially, they also control and monitor workflow. For example, they monitor ‘wrap-up’ time (i.e. the time it takes for an operator to carry out post-call data-input and to take the next call).

Earlier ACDs were not linked to a TB database and the operator had to ask the caller his or her name and then access their records from the computer. However, most ACDs are now linked to host computers and are able to forward information on existing customers’ detailed history automatically onto the computer screen. In this way, the technology used in TB facilitates the standardization and routinization of parts of retail banking. Customer service can be subdivided into a number of discrete stages, thus producing a more clearly defined output. In turn, automation makes possible improvements in labour productivity, closer monitoring by management of outputs, changes in employment structure and corporate hierarchy, shifts in retail banking location, as well as the continual updating of customer records.

SimplePhone (a pseudonym) is an integrated TB management system (based on client/ server architecture) for inbound and outbound applications. The system provides a wealth of reports on calls, transactions and operators’ performance. All calls are recorded and there is a tracking device to enable the supervisor or the technician to find records quickly and easily. This is a compulsory practice for the security of both the customer and the operator, and it is also used for coaching purposes.

SimpleWatch is the supervisor’s real-time monitoring tool, allowing covert monitoring of all calls. It gives instant graphic display of pending calls, waiting customers and overall activity, and actually runs in the manager’s personal computer. It includes off-line batch statistics, covering both telephony and business data, and incorporates a tool for creation and modification of operators’ scripts and local database.

At MCF, the SimplePhone system is operated 24 hours a day in six four-hour shifts. Each shift comprises eight operators, one supervisor, one clerical worker and two technicians (one internal to the TB and one dedicated to the interface with MCF databases). Only one technician is available ‘on call’ during the night shifts. The system has been running for approximately two years in its present configuration.

## 4. Technology in action

The MCF TB system, SimplePhone, is an ‘off-the-shelf’ platform used by many bank offering TB services. However, only by looking at the system in action, can we get an insight into the interaction between technology and organization within a tele-mediated environment. The three scenes described below portray the on-going relationship between the TB operators and the information system, and provide the empirical material for the analysis of the case study.

## 4.1. The standard call

SimplePhone operators wear headsets and sit in front of a terminal screen. They can have access to customer records via computer, and can check on the caller’s credit card status, the details of their mortgage, and their business history. When a customer call arrives at the ACD, it is routed either to a Voice Response System (VRS) or to an operator. If all operators are busy, the call will ring out (ring time). After 45 s, a delay announcement cuts in, the ACD answers the call, and offers a recorded announcement suggesting the automatic service. The call is held in this queue (queue time) until an operator is free or the caller rings off. A large, centrally located, wallchart shows how many callers are waiting in the queue.

With the VRS, the customer may perform a limited number of operations and optionally ask for human assistance. If there is a free operator, the ACD routes the call to that operator and his or her telephone turret will display where the call has come from. When the operator answers a call and the customer has been identified by the VRS, the SimpleScript module starts with the relevant information about the customer and a predefined dialogue sequence which is used to ‘process the customer’. If the customer did not identify himself/herself through the VRS, the telephone of that operator switches and the script’s first screen initiates customer identification via the account number and a PIN.

SimpleScript, a Windows-based software, permits the creation, upkeep and storage of scripts. Script files contain the definition of the dialogue sequence (questions and answers that can be processed by computer), ‘help’, customers’ possible emotional states and operators’ possible reactions and other facilities. These scripts guide the operator in the course of conversation and let them save the data that have been collected in the form of a set of attributes and events. Scripts are based on a decision tree that automatically prompts the operator with new information depending on the previous answers. SimpleSystem reads all the required information about the customer on the main system and fills up the spaces in the script. Operators are not allowed to work outside SimpleScript.

In line with MCF’s long-standing rules and regulations governing ‘front line’ clerical working in traditional branches, the operator is required to write down all the details of the customer transaction before signalling that they are ready to deal with another customer. This procedure is carried out in the TB despite the fact that SimpleSystem automatically records these details. At the end of each shift, the supervisors verify the operators’ work, by cross-checking these hand written details with the information that SimpleSystem provides. A clerical worker then checks this with the information held in the bank’s own database before directing the different requests to the relevant specialist area of the central back office. Although, SimpleSystem could carry out this tasks automatically, the TB Manager insists—to the great annoyance of the TB technicians—that the procedures are conducted in exactly the same (manual-intensive) way as they are in other branches.

When an operator lacks the necessary knowledge or authority to deal with a customer request s/he keeps the customer on hold and gets help from the supervisor off-line. If the problem cannot be solved quickly, the customer call is terminated with the promise that the operator will return their call (call back) as soon as possible. In this case, the supervisor may need to contact departmental experts in a particular area and then instruct the operator, who, in turn, will call back the customer.

To sum up, the system can be conceptualized as standardizing and routinizing the transaction between the customer and the bank. It is possible to detect at least three different levels of transactional complexity, which require increasing levels of knowledge in order to fulfil the requests of the customer:

1. Customer interacting with the system using the key pad;

2. Customer interacting with the operator. In this case, the operator is relying on the information provided by the scripts (conditional statements: if-then clauses);

3. Information provided by the scripts is not exhaustive. In this case, the transaction has to be completed by going off the line (putting the customer on hold, call back, consulting the main system, etc.). The transaction becomes increasingly less automatic since organizational back ups are brought into play.

## 4.2. Coping with breakdown

The call back mechanism also applies to breakdown situations, when the telephony, the computer system or the link between the two is disrupted. If the system fails to provide the necessary informational–communicational support, the transaction is accomplished offline by drawing on the traditional functional–hierarchical division of labour. Front line mechanisms, both humans (operators) and non-humans (SimpleSystem) are supported or replaced by administrative ones (technicians, supervisors). In turn, supervisors and technicians may back up each other according to the type of expertise required.

The TB technicians’ tasks include system monitoring, programming, SimpleScript dialogue design, system development support, and the building of ‘quick checklists’ to help supervisor solve the more common breakdowns. This checklist tends to become a new routine and works as a back-up tool for both TB technician and supervisor. Nevertheless, supervisors have considerable difficulty in supporting and managing operators, as they regularly and continually become involved in the day-to-day maintenance of the systems hardware and software. Interviewees cited three reasons for this. Firstly, supervisors share the very same physical space as the technician; secondly one technician is not enough to keep the technology going; and thirdly, the technician does not possess sufficient declarative or procedural knowledge. TB technicians’ work is often improvised and lacks formal institutional support. This, in turn, leaves room for slippage to occur between institutional job descriptions and the practice of everyday work.

This is consistent with the view taken by Barley (1986) who argues that by the time new technologies are introduced, attention shifts from institutional context to social practices around the use of technology, deploying behavior and cognitions, which in turn can be a raw material from which interaction orders emerge. There is thus an emerging and ‘evolving community-of-practice’ (Brown and Duguid, 1991) and supervisors’ support skills repeatedly reconfigure around the maintenance of technical equipment. As one operator stated succinctly:

‘Supervisors have become SimplePhone technicians’.

The lack of physical differentiation between supervisors and technicians clearly plays an important part in determining the supervisor’s role, although this is only one element in complex processes of mutual interdependence and interaction. According to the TB project manager:

‘We were not able to give the supervisors the commercial perspective they actually need to do their job. We believed that SimplePhone would be fully automatic, and wouldn’t need a permanent technician. We were wrong.

## The TB manager shared this view:

‘The evolution of supervision into a technical function came about as a result of the belief that SimpleSystem was an autonomous and automatic application. But it hasn’t turned out that way. When things go wrong the person with the most free time has to deal with it. The operators are busy taking telephone calls, so the supervisor has to sort out the technical problems. The problem is that the supervision area is located in the heart of the TB—everything is concentrated in 150 m<sup>2</sup>: the operation, the development and the supervision. The technician should be the ‘ironmonger’, but it is the supervisor who is usually repairing the workstations, or installing new software, so he has become a technician by default.

As technicians try to keep the technology going, they develop a system of interaction that permits them to solve an increasing number of problems. However, our research suggests that while the technician and supervisors improvise solutions in the face of crisis, no one individual fully grasps how the system is working. This view was confirmed by a TB technician who opined:

‘In most cases, we know how to operate them [the TB system modules] but we don’t know how they actually work. We have not been trained to know. Instead we are taught recipes for the most common mistakes or breakdowns. We should at least have been provided with sufficient information to be able to intervene proactively in modifying the system to avoid breakdown’.

Declarative knowledge is important for making sense out of new situations, for deriving meaning from unstructured situations. It implies substantial search time. The situations of equivocality (such as a breakdown) bring to the surface the conservative, bureaucratic organization of work at MCF. So it is that the unpredictability of SimpleSystem necessitates improvisation and the development of emergent routines to ensure maximum uptime. During the time of our research, many of the emergent routines developed by supervisors and technicians became standardized, even though they did not strictly conform to formal MCF procedure and practice. Furthermore, with the supervisor’s being increasingly absorbed with technical matters, the operators remained unsupervised for long periods. This enabled a small degree of informality to encroach into everyday work practice. Most noticeably, operators began experimenting with SimpleScript—improvising scripts when dealing with particularly ‘awkward’ customers. The TB manager, under pressure to reduce the system downtime, realized that a few of these improvisations added a certain robustness to the system and, in due course, the technician made minor modifications to Simple-Script:

“The anticipation of all possible scenarios and scripts is a never-ending process. There is always a way to do things better and more quickly. As operators gain more experience with the system, they introduce new variables, new ideas for scripts. They develop these ideas themselves and this has really raised team spirit. It is now clear that we need to pay constant attention to improving scripts.”

Indeed, operators did appear to benefit from this unanticipated opportunity to improvise. One remarked:

‘SimpleSystem has been improved progressively and more often than not our suggestions for changing scripts are taken seriously and implemented. It has become like a son to us.

## 4.3. The back office

The back office represents the terminal section of the workflow within TB. Only one clerical worker is assigned to the back office, where the bulk of administrative work is carried out. This includes sending letters to customers, signing documents, doing minor tasks that operators cannot do on-line and sorting out and scrutinizing the work that will go to specialized different departments or to the central back office. The back office is the centre of the MCF bureaucracy. It can be understood as a physical or manual database. All the transactions completed by the operator have to be saved in the system, written down on paper and printed.

At the end of each shift the supervisor verifies the operators’ work, by comparing their hand written papers with the information that Simple provides, both in the system and on the print outs. Therefore, the supervisor is backing up both the operator and the system, while the back office acts a storage device, where different information can be retrieved in various information supports (System or paper). It backs up all the work done inside the TB. The print outs can be seen as a redundancy because the SimpleSystem generates a working queue that goes straight to the TB back office and could be sent electronically to the central back office.

The dynamics inside the TB change when there is a breakdown, but also when the call traffic is low. When this occurs or when the system breaks down, operators switch to the back office, backing up their own work and supporting the clerical worker. Interestingly, the back office itself is caught up in a back-up dynamic: it mirrors the operator’s work and is in turn backed up by the central back office. The back office is a command and control centre that works as a gatekeeper for information from the TB to the remaining areas of MCF.

## 5. Switching organizational gears: the interplay between on-line and off-line

On the face of it, the system seems to have been implemented with the operational objectives of streamlining processes and making transactions with the customers more efficient. This rationale is apparent in our observation of how the system performs in routine situations. Transactions with the customer are standardized to an extent that the customer is translated into a PIN number. A simple four-digits code triggers a complex search in the host databases allowing either the VRS or operators to track down customer records and retrieve a variety of detailed information. Queues and call allocations are handled automatically by the system. Dialogue boxes prompted by SimpleScript are designed to carry out communication in a stereotypical form and mediate conversations on-line. More generally, routine around the TB system can be conceptualized as an attempt at providing a kind of encyclopedia of the future by predicting the content of customers’ calls. Interaction is therefore circumscribed by sets of a priori established questions and conditionally based responses evoked through either the conversion of information into voice synthesized responses or SimpleScript dialogues conveyed by operators.

The smooth functioning of the system in routine situations was nonetheless challenged by the occurrence of unexpected events—such as unanswerable calls or technical breakdowns—which eventually unveiled a dynamic interaction between front stage and back stage dynamics. In such situations, on-line, computer-mediated work activity is interrupted (e.g. ‘hold’ and ‘call-back’) and reconfigured around off-line social interaction. Similarly, during periods of technical breakdown, work is accomplished through the traditional functional division of labour within MCF. Off-line activity is possible because MCF has a pervasive culture of duplicating and backing up its administrative apparatus (which is mirrored in the system architecture). Supervisors, technicians and other parts of MCF are regularly called upon to support, back up and sustain SimpleSystem in order to maintain the stability of the workflow.

From our observations it seems that a clear separation is being maintained between front line operators and experts acting in the back lines (supervisors and technicians). Operators are not supposed to handle exceptions, nor are they allowed to transfer ‘difficult’ calls to experts. Rather, they simply act as a filter between customers and supervisors/technicians, just another (human) interface between the customer and the organization, which remains in the background. Indeed, operators do not remain idle when the system is down. Instead, they switch to different tasks, such as helping the clerical worker in the back office. In line with the description provided in Section 4, the back office appears to be the locus of bureaucracy—the institutional repository where information is cross-checked and collated.

Switching between on-line and off-line is most visible when something unexpected occurs. However, interdependency between technical apparatus and social organization at MCF is a continual and an ever-present feature for the preservation of the work setting at MCF. This is well illustrated by the way in which operators are persuaded to work in a particular way through a combination of SimpleScript and the monitoring and observation of operator activity by supervisors through SimpleWatch. The collation of performance figures for the TB and their distribution throughout MCF continues the complex interplay of social and technical relations. The pervasive nature of socio-technical backing up throughout MCF suggests that social and technical relations, while continually reconfiguring and shifting, simultaneously rely on each other for their constitution and definition.

The dynamic interplay between front stage and back stage dynamics, on-line and offline, and the spirals of back ups associated with it emphasized some unusual and perhaps unique features of the work organization at MCF. For instance, unlike similar case studies of hot lines support systems (e.g. Pentland, 1992) where the functional/hierarchical division of labour is enacted electronically while keeping the customer on-line (e.g. calls transferred to the appropriate experts), at the MCF TB centre the same dynamics occur through some sort of socio-technical switching. The technology goes ‘off-the-line’, telephone calls are transferred manually by having operators physically going to talk to supervisors, and customers are called back when a solution is available. On the other hand, the way the system is designed contributes to call queues and bottlenecks, since such direct call transfers are not allowed. This, in turn, diminishes the quality of the service provided (e.g. approximately one in five MCF TB customers cancel their call if they are put on hold).

A special case concerned the relationship between supervisors and technicians, since the role of the latter was not institutionally defined. Here, organizational actors could deliberately play on role ambiguity to bring in additional (back up) resources for the resolution of problematic situations (supervisors becoming technicians). In responding to breakdowns, both technicians and supervisors often have to act in ways that go beyond automatic, pre-existing routines (Crossan and Sorrenti, 1998), thereby fostering the development of new routines through their on-going interactions (Barley, 1986). During crisis there is a creation of a system of collective improvisation in which “each episode of system repair is built on shared knowledge of earlier success and failures” (Orr, 1996: 5).

Changes in scripts as a result of unexpected and unanswerable customer questions are an opportunity for both individual and organizational learning. To be sure, scripts can be seen as cognitive, causal maps designed according to explicit ‘if-then’ clauses in order to anticipate as many typical conversations as possible. At MCF, such occasions are generally interpreted as a further situation requiring cataloguing and classifying by supervisors and technicians, while the involvement of operators in system updates is regulated by the simple administrative expedient of requiring formal written requests for technical help. The way learning is institutionalized through ‘system upgrades’ reinforces the functional hierarchical division of labour. This tightly-bounded and static view of organizational and individual learning is well illustrated at MCF with the on-going updating of SimpleScript, while simultaneously denying operators access to MCF’s main information and data system. This means that operators never acquire a comprehensive and sophisticated understanding of MCF’s activities, despite the fact that it is they who call back customers with an appropriate answer to their question.

Finally, only the automating effects of IT are taken into account, while the enormous amount of information generated by the system—its informating effect—is not exploited. More importantly, the use of information does not facilitate learning. Information is not analysed or interpreted with a view towards improved organizational performance; it is just produced in massive quantities, filed and put aside ‘for the record’.

## 6. Conclusion

The introduction of SimpleSystem was an attempt to provide a stand-alone and independently organized TB unit at MCF. Our analysis of the workflow at MCF has emphasized that while a substantial proportion of the company’s banking activities can be internally standardized and routinized through automation, this process needs to be sustained by organizational back ups. More specifically, the case study has highlighted the emergence of collaborative work patterns based on mutual support and back up in the face of potentially disruptive situations (cf. Hutchins, 1991). In order to preserve the stability of the work setting, operators, supervisors and technicians are required to work laterally and deductively through various off-line organizational pathways which entail shifting and modifying their efforts within the formally defined division of labour. As a consequence, communicative and sense-making activity across divisional and professional boundaries is a constant and on-going part of stabilizing MCF’s TB operation and is instrumental in ‘keeping things running smoothly’.

The case study depicts the company’s attempt to stabilize organizational activity by harnessing the technology to preserve existing values and organizational identity. This rational vision of design and implementation was challenged by events and the Board of Directors’ strategic objectives met with very limited success. The dynamics observed in the case study suggest the utility of a more situated approach to system design and use. The impossibility of transferring difficult calls automatically and the necessity for a constant switching between technology and organization, have highlighted some important dysfunction in the system, namely the creation of call queues and bottlenecks. Accordingly, an obvious re-design suggestion would be to reconfigure the system in order to allow the automatic transfer of ‘difficult’ calls. SimpleSystem is probably flexible enough to incorporate this. However, even such an incremental change has significant organizational implications. Bringing in additional electronic links implies re-designing the traditional division of labour within the TB centre: by recognizing and formalizing teamworking practices; by empowering front line operators and increasing their level of discretion in the face of disruptive situations.

Recognizing that improvisation and sense-making activity are an inevitable and potentially creative part of an everyday organizational operation and evolution would be a substantial step towards rethinking the nature and the role of information and communication systems at MCF. Our case study restates the need for reconfiguring technological platforms around socio-organizational changes. Technology not only replaces or substitutes a substantial portion of human activities in organizations; it also challenges the organizational status quo and calls for a corresponding reconfiguration of the way people think about work. As Latour (1996) argues:

“It is no longer clear if a computer is a limited form of organization or if an organization is an expanded form of computer system. Not because, as in the engineering dreams and sociologist’s nightmares, complete rationalization would have taken place, but because, on the opposite, two monstrous hybrids are now coextensive” (p. 302).

Along with Latour, we would argue that to understand the complexities of the interactions within ‘monstrous hybrids’ such as the MCF TB, we need to move beyond simple notions of the general ‘impact’ of information systems on organizational work. In particular instances, organizational relations will shape information systems, or information systems shape organizational relations, but this is a complex empirical question. The research findings described in this paper suggest that the interpretative case study method, informed by a theory of situated action, may be particularly helpful in the development of a clearer understanding of the complexity of information system-organization relations.
