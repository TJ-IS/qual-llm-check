# Metaphors of virtuality: shaping an emergent reality

Ulrike Schultze <sup>a,\*</sup>, Wanda J. Orlikowski <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Cox School of Business, Southern Methodist University, P.O. Box 0333, Dallas TX 75275-0333, USA <sup>b</sup> Sloan School of Management, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, 50 Memorial Drive, E53-325, Cambridge MA 02142-1347, USA

## Abstract

In this paper, we explore the contemporary discourse associated with the new phenomenon of virtual organizing, and identify a number of metaphors used in this discourse to characterize various aspects of virtuality. We believe paying attention to such metaphors is important because in the absence of experiences (direct or vicarious) to guide practice, the images and ideals promoted in the discourse will shape people’s views of and actions towards this new way of organizing. As such, metaphors play a powerful role in structuring the reality of virtuality. To understand the kind of reality being imagined and incited, we examine the various metaphors being proposed in the practitioner-directed literature on virtual organizing. We find that this discourse contains a multiplicity of different metaphors, each highlighting and hiding distinct aspects of virtual organizing. We identify five overarching metaphors in this discourse. These metaphors (re)present virtual organizing as a platform, as existing in space, as composed of bits, as operating as a community, and as engaging in a network of relationships. We analyze these metaphors in terms of their assumptions and presumptions about how to organize work, as well as their affordances and challenges. We conclude by considering what the consequences might be for people acting on the basis of such different, and often contradictory, metaphors in practice.  2001 Elsevier Science Ltd. ## 1. Introduction

In recent years, there has been a growing interest among practitioners and academics in new ways of organizing. These new — or what have come to be known as “virtual” — organizations have been posited as radical departures from the traditional, established modes of organizing that have dominated the last few decades of the twentieth century. These traditional organizational characteristics are seen to be bureaucratic, hierarchic, integrated, co-located, stable, adversarial, and emphasizing financial capital, mechanization, automation, economies of scale, and fixed employment. In contrast, virtual ways of organizing have been posited to be: dynamic; networked; outsourced; distributed; digital; flexible; collaborative; and emphasizing intellectual capital, innovation, knowledge, learning, and temporary contracts. While a number of organizations are attempting to operate virtually (Hardwick & Bolton, 1997; Kraut, Steinfield, Chan, Butler & Hoag, 1998; Weiner, Nohria, Hickman & Smith, 1997), our interest in this paper is primarily in the discourse, rather than the practice, of virtual organizing.

The discourse concerning virtuality and virtual ways of organizing is diverse and ambiguous. Davidow and Malone (1992) describe virtual corporations in terms of project-based, strategic alliances among corporations. Nohria and Berkley (1994) associate virtuality with simulation. Mowshowitz (1997: 32) proposes virtual organizing as “a set of principles for metamanaging goal-oriented activity”. Faucheux (1997: 55) regards virtual organizing as “a reflective tool for critical self-examination,” which will lead to a “higher sense of human conduct, through development of human consciousness and conscience”. Kraut et al. (1998) define a virtual organization in terms of its organizational structure, which they see as fluid, flexible, and adaptive to its dynamic environment. And Davenport and Pearlson (1998) see the essence of a virtual organization as based not on the sharing of workspace, but on the sharing of information. In these discussions, information technology is portrayed as a key facilitator of the alliances, simulation, meta-management, reflection, adaptive structures, and information sharing characterizing virtual organizing. The reality of the virtual organization is seen to rest not on bricks and mortar, but on an information and technology infrastructure (Wiesenfeld, Raghuram & Garud, 1998).

People use symbolic language to make sense of situations that are new, problematic, ambiguous, or unsettled (Frost & Morgan, 1983). Metaphors are particularly useful for defining and understanding new phenomena because they serve as carriers of meaning from a situation that is well understood to one that is not. For instance, the expression “an organization’s chain of command” takes the phenomenon of a chain and people’s everyday experiences with chains and applies it to the realm of social authority, a phenomenon that is more abstract and complex, and consequently more difficult to comprehend. Because virtual forms of organizing are new, complex, and poorly understood, metaphors play a particularly important role in shaping the definitions, descriptions, and strategies used to engage with virtual ways of organizing. That is, metaphors help to construct the reality of virtual organizations by provoking vivid images that make future action more tangible (Sackman, 1989) and familiar (Stutman & Putnam, 1994).

Given the importance of metaphor in the social construction of phenomena, examining the different metaphors used to describe virtual organizations should offer insights into the variety of ways commentators are making sense of and interacting with this phenomenon. Through an examination of the different images of virtual organizations proffered by the literature, we can come to a deeper understanding of the discourse on virtuality, and how it both highlights and hides aspects of the virtual phenomenon. Because all metaphors are partial and partisan, their use will enable and constrain particular ways of seeing and being in the world. As Morgan (1986: 17) notes, metaphorical analysis can enhance “our capacity for creative yet disciplined thought, in a way that allows us to grasp and deal with the many-sided character of organizational life”.

We thus set ourselves the task of first identifying the imagery and rhetoric used to describe virtuality in texts selected from well-known journals directed at practitioners (e.g., Harvard Business Review). We then sought to analyze the content of these metaphors, asking, in particular, two questions: what can we learn about the discourse of virtual organizations by examining the metaphors used to describe different aspects of virtuality?; and what does the discourse reveal and obscure by a particular choice of metaphor? Our approach to performing such an analysis follows Morgan’s (1986: 322) two-step strategy of diagnostic reading to identify and interpret the different metaphors used to describe specific aspects of a phenomenon, followed by a critical examination of the different interpretations produced by such a reading of the different metaphors.

Before describing our research findings and interpretations in terms of these two steps, we first outline the theories of metaphor that we drew on for our analysis and offer some examples from the empirical literature to describe how the theory has been used in practice. We also briefly discuss the methodology we used to analyze the texts on virtual organizing. Then we turn to a diagnostic reading of the metaphors we found in the literature, following the first of Morgan’s analytic steps. We interpret the meanings, origins, affordances and challenges of each of the metaphors we identified, and offer illustrations of their usage from the texts. We then critically examine such a reading of the metaphors following the second of Morgan’s analytic steps. We conclude by discussing some implications — for research and practice — of taking seriously the multiple different metaphors we identified in the discourse on virtuality.

## 2. Theory of metaphor

In its most literal sense, metaphor is a trope, i.e., a figure of speech and thought that twists the conventional meaning of words (Gibbs, 1993; Manning, 1979). As a trope, metaphor serves to embellish text by applying concepts, ideas, and terms that belong in one domain to another domain.

Departing from this more literal interpretation, Lakoff and Johnson (1980) have popularized the notion that metaphor is more than a figure of speech. They suggest that it is a “seeing as,” a mode of cognition in which “understanding and experiencing [of] one kind of thing [is done] in terms of another” (1980: 5). They contend that metaphor is so ingrained in everyday language and so intertwined with everyday life that its use is unavoidable and thus fundamental to thought and language use. As a result, metaphors cannot simply be picked up and used like tools; rather, as a part of language, they make up the medium that constitutes our mode of being in the world (Boland, 1989). Since metaphors are so much a part of everyday life, this figurative nature of language is frequently overlooked. For instance, moving in an upward direction, as in “upwardly mobile” or “climbing the career ladder”, has a positive connotation, whereas a downward motion, as in “stepping down” or “falling from grace”, has a negative connotation.

While metaphors are fundamental to our way of thinking and acting in the world, they are particularly useful means to think with when considering novel, complex, and poorly understood phenomena (Klagge, 1997). This is because of their generative quality as carriers of meaning across conceptual realms. One theory of metaphor suggests that metaphors transfer ideas and meanings from one domain, “by analogical extension, [to] some domain to which those ideas do not immediately apply” (Black, 1962: 241). As Lakoff and Johnson (1980: 193) note:

Metaphor is one of the most important tools for trying to comprehend partially what cannot be comprehended totally: our feelings, aesthetic experiences, moral practices, and spiritual awareness. These endeavors of the imagination are not devoid of rationality; since they use metaphor they employ an imaginative rationality.

The use of metaphors to transfer and construct meanings across realms has been extended by Black (1993). His interaction theory of metaphor proposes that there is no simple one-way transfer of meaning from the realm that is well understood [i.e., the source domain (Tsoukas, 1991: 568)] to the realm that is not [i.e., the target domain (Tsoukas, 1991)]; rather, he argues that the meaning systems in both realms are changed when metaphor is used to bring them together. As Turner (1974: 25) puts it, metaphors are “in fact, metamorphic, transformative”. For instance, the metaphor “Richard is a lion” not only makes us think of Richard in a new, more animalisitic way, that is, as someone powerful, brave and majestic, but it also makes us see lions as having human traits, that is, drawing on our knowledge of what it means to be king. Black’s interactionist theory of metaphor views the construction of metaphors as a two-way and interactive sensemaking activity, in which our understandings of both the source and the target domains are brought into play and changed.

Metaphors are also constitutive of reality. By shaping how an unknown and unfamiliar phenomenon is understood, metaphorical expressions shape what actions are taken with respect to it. In this way, as Pondy (1983: 157) citing Geertz (1973) puts it, “metaphors serve both as models of the situation and models for the situation”. Black (1993: 38) contends that “some metaphors enable us to see aspects of reality that the metaphor’s production helps to constitute”. This constitutive role of language can be seen most effectively through Weick’s (1979: 135) notion of enactment, that is, that people make sense of things by seeing a world upon which they have already imposed what they believe. “We don’t describe the world we see; we see the world we can describe”. Stutman and Putnam (1994: 285) note that the “primary function of metaphor, then, is to structure reality (our perceptions and beliefs) and behavior”.

A substantial amount of work in organizational studies has relied on metaphors as an analytical lens as well as a scheme of classification and categorization. Putnam, Phillips and Chapman (1996), for example, argue that analysis of metaphors is important in organization studies because it facilitates conceptual development. Much use of metaphor relies on an oppositional or dichotomous construction of meaning. The use of oppositions within a text is a particularly powerful rhetorical device because it involves one of the fundamental ways in which we construct meaning in the world — through the use of contrasts. That is, we make sense of things or concepts by comparing them to different things or concepts we already know something about, for example, hot/cold, black/white, in/out, good/bad, and so on.

Numerous examples of the use of opposition in metaphor can be found in the organizational literature. For instance, Keeley (1980) compares two opposing models of organization: the organismic model, which depends on both organic and mechanistic metaphors, and the social contract model, which includes market and game metaphors. Stutman and Putnam (1994) use the opposing metaphors of litigation and negotiation to characterize the human resource practices in two firms. Boland and Greenberg (1992) prime the subjects of their research with two archetypal metaphors: the organization as machine and the organization as organism. Mason (1991) contrasts the use of the economic and the intelligent organization metaphor in organizational strategy. Jones (1994) finds that both engineering and religious metaphors are used as rhetorical devices in texts on Business Process Redesign. On the one hand, the voice of hard rationality and planned change presents reengineering as a logical, disciplined activity, while on the other hand, images of faith, vision, inspiration and transformation present it as a sacred experience.

Examining rhetorical oppositions can reveal important tensions and ambiguities inherent in a discourse (Beath & Orlikowski, 1994). While two opposing elements may be seen to simply reflect divergent or dichotomous views of a phenomenon, more careful consideration often indicates that the opposition or dualism is not as mutually exclusive as first seems apparent. That is, far from being opposing, the elements may reflect a relationship of dependence, a duality that suggests their inherent inseparability. Oppositions, while they are often rhetorically powerful and serve as a conceptual shorthand, may also instigate an inappropriate polarization of concepts, thus obscuring rather than clarifying meaning. In such a case, oppositional simplicity becomes simplistic and rhetorical power is merely reductionist.

As will become evident in the discussion of our findings, the use of metaphorical opposition is quite pronounced in the literature on virtual organizations. The metaphors we identified in this literature both reflect and go beyond the organic/mechanic and hard/soft distinctions so pervasive in the organizational literature. In particular, we found that the metaphors of virtual organizing could be characterized by contrasts between surrounding and supporting structures, between place and space, between the material and the ideational, between control and cooperation, and between stability and change.

## 3. Method of analysis

In order to explore the construction of virtual organizations through metaphoric rhetoric, we selected relatively recent publications that are directed at practitioners and that have engaged the topic of virtual organizing and virtual organizations. We chose three well-known and widely-read journals targeted at practitioners in both the management and computer fields: Communications of the ACM<sup>1</sup>, Harvard Business Review and Sloan Management Review. We decided to limit our sampling to practitioner-directed publications because:

1. they are more likely to contain symbolic expression than scientific publications (Pinder & Bourgeois, 1982; Tsoukas, 1991); and

2. the constitutive influence of metaphors is likely to be more direct as practitioners — influenced by the metaphors they read about — put them into action.

Since virtual organizing is a topic that has emerged quite recently, we did not search the literature prior to 1992, which is the year that the first extended treatment of this topic appeared in the form of Davidow and Malone’s book The Virtual Corporation. Because most discussions of virtual organizing refer to this book, we treat its publication as an important landmark in the discourse on virtual organizations.

Using our selection criteria, we examined all the articles that address issues of virtual organizing, virtual organizations, virtuality, and virtual work in the three journals during the period 1992–1999. Seventeen relevant articles were identified (please refer to Table 1). It is interesting to note that even as these articles are published in journals targeting management and computer practitioners, most are written by academics or consultants with academic affiliations. To the extent that such academic associations confer some legitimacy on the images of virtuality presented (at least in the eyes of the readers), these texts will be especially influential elements of social construction. It is also important to note that the journals we examined are directed at senior practitioners in the fields of management and computing, and not the broader public or practitioner communities comprised of non-managerial or nontechnical members.

Having identified the articles that made up our data set, both of us independently analyzed each of these articles for their metaphoric content. During these first readings, we attempted to identify all of the metaphoric expressions contained in the articles, whether they appeared to be directly relevant to virtuality or not. Each instance of a metaphoric expression was captured in a list together with the page number on which the trope was located and a short comment explaining the meaning or significance that the researcher had attributed to it at the time of reading. Having made our lists, we analyzed them in terms of such notions as metaphor type, source domain, entailments, and affordances. Lakoff and Johnson (1980) differentiate between different types of metaphors including: orientational or spatial metaphors, which capture a direction or orientation that is typically associated with positive and negative attributions; ontological metaphors, which create an ontological status such as a substance, container or object for more ephemeral experiences like states, events, and emotions; metonymy metaphors, which capture the whole in terms of a constitutive part; personification metaphors, which lend human traits to inanimate objects; and structural metaphors, which pose puzzles of matching structural requirements of the phenomenon with those of the metaphor.

The source domain of a metaphor is the context or conceptual realm in which the term has a literal meaning and is well understood. It is from this realm that the meaning of the concept is transferred to a different realm — the target domain — which is poorly understood (Tsoukas, 1991 citing Ortony, 1975). The target realm is the one in which the term becomes a metaphor, that is, where it takes on a figurative rather than a literal meaning.

Lakoff and Johnson (1980: 87–96) use the concept of entailment to capture the connections between source and target domains that necessarily follow for metaphorical coherence to be achieved. For example, Lakoff and Johnson (1980: 9) show that the metaphor, time is money, “entails that time is a limited resource, which entails that time is a valuable commodity”. Through the examination of such entailments, Lakoff and Johnson (1980, 1980) argue that we can identify the logical consequence of or the meaning that follows from the use of a given metaphor.

We adapt the notion of affordance, which is frequently applied to the design of physical objects, to conceptual objects such as metaphors. Norman (1988: 9), relying on Gibson’s work (1977), defines affordance as “the perceived and actual properties of things, primarily those fundamental properties that determine just how the thing could possibly be used”. Our view of affordance includes the socially constructed nature of an object’s features and the culturally and historically contingent ways in which people use them (see also Giddens, 1984: 9). In seeking a metaphor’s affordances in the context of virtuality, we will be looking for the particular meanings and uses of a metaphor that are evident in the contemporary discourse on virtuality.

After our initial data analysis, which took place between October 1998 and February 1999, we compared our readings by discussing each of the articles and deciding jointly on the appropriateness of our individual interpretations. Together, we first eliminated from our initial lists those metaphoric expressions that we agreed were unrelated to virtual organizing. We then focused our attention on the strategy that we had both found in the texts — where authors contrast the “real” organization with the “virtual” one in order to construct a definition of virtuality. We had both identified a number of different oppositions between real and virtual in our readings of the various texts. We set aside those oppositions where we had no agreement, and concentrated on those where we had some shared agreement. After discussing our interpretations, and resolving differences in interpretation, we ended up with five distinct oppositions that we jointly agreed were apparent in the texts:

1. liquid/fluid/continuous change versus solid/stable/equilibrium;

2. space versus place;

3. active/responsive to customers versus passive/unresponsive;

4. community versus competition; and

5. trust versus control.

Each of these categories represents a specific metaphoric opposition that was strongly evident, whether implicitly or explicitly, in the articles.

The oppositions we identified in this inductive way of hermeneutic reading and re-reading served as a useful starting point. In March 1999, we returned to the more academic literature on virtual organizations, and then compared our inductivelyderived oppositions with more theory-based representations of virtual organizations. We found a substantial overlap between the meanings of our oppositions and Grabowski and Roberts’ (1998) categories of organizational structuring, communication, trust, and culture, Wiesenfeld et al.’s (1998) concept of organizational identification, and Kraut et al.’s (1998) notions of coordination and ownership of resources. We next searched for conceptual linkages between the deductive categories that addressed various aspects of organizing and our inductively-derived oppositions. Seeking similarities among these two sources of data, we arrived at five organizational aspects of virtual organizing — architecture, location, resources, identity, and governance — which were addressed by the overarching metaphors of platform, space, bits, network, and community, respectively.

With our framework in place, we proceeded with another re-reading of the complete source texts to refine and elaborate our overarching metaphors. We did this by asking the following questions:

O What aspect of organizing is highlighted with this metaphor?

O What is the “opposite” (i.e., what aspect of organizing is hidden by this metaphor)?

O What are the meanings of this metaphor?

O What are the source domains of this metaphor?

O How is the metaphor expressed in the texts on virtual organizations?

O What affordances does this metaphor grant organizations and their members?

O What challenges for virtual organizing are suggested by this metaphor?

In the following section, we begin with an overview of the five overarching metaphors and then proceed to a more detailed discussion of each.

## 4. Metaphors of virtuality

## 4.1. Diagnostic reading

Our analysis of the examined practitioner-directed texts reveals that many authors use a strategy of contrasting traditional with virtual organizations. Virtual organizations are portrayed as platforms<sup>2</sup> while traditional organizations are characterized in terms of buildings or containers. This metaphoric opposition highlights the architectural aspects of the organization. With respect to location, virtual organizations are seen as operating in space, literally everywhere and nowhere, while traditional organizations are grounded in a place where people go to work. Characterizing the virtual organizations as bits, digitized information and capabilities, as opposed to the more tangible atoms representing a traditional organization’s material goods and bodies, highlights the differences in resources between these two types of organizations. The metaphor of community characterizes the governance of the virtual organization and this stands in contrast to the traditional organization’s governance mode of command-and-control. With respect to organizational identity<sup>3</sup>, the metaphor used for the virtual organization is the inter-organizational network, whereas that used for the traditional organization is integrated organizational culture (Martin, 1992). The identity of a traditional organization is seen to be cohesive and relatively stable. Furthermore, it is regarded as deriving from the founding values of its members or the portfolio of products it produces. In contrast, the identity of a virtual organization is seen to be dynamic, differentiated and fragmented as it is constructed and reconstructed from a shifting “portfolio of relationships” among multiple business partners.

We now examine, in turn, the images and interpretations used to describe each of these overarching metaphors of platform, space, bits, community, and network. The key elements of this discussion are presented in Table 2.

## 4.2. Virtual organization is platform

This metaphor highlights the architectural aspects of virtual organizations. In the texts we analyzed, the architecture of the traditional organization is represented as a building, and the virtual organization’s architecture is viewed as a platform (please refer to Appendix A for examples of the use of platform imagery in the literature we examined). A traditional organization is identified by a sign on a building that people walk into and out of. A building suggests that the organization is physically contained within and surrounded by an external structure that is both enabling and constraining. The container protects the various activities of the organization and holds them together, but it also holds them in, isolating them from the world. In the context of virtual organizations where people are no longer co-located, the continued use of the container metaphor which results in images of a hollow corporation or empty shell, are being dismissed as “impossibilities” (Venkatraman & Henderson, 1998: 47) and as a danger to a virtual organization’s continued existence (Chesbrough & Teece, 1996: 70). The trend in the literature has been to adopt the metaphor of platform, portraying the architecture of virtual organizations as open and exposed.

The meaning of a platform is that of a flat, raised surface on which a performance is staged by various people-actors, politicians, even academics. A platform may also indicate a set of guiding principles or ideas, as in the platform of a political party or candidate. The term platform is also used in the computer field to refer to hardware or software architectures. For example, the x86 chip used in personal computers is an example of a hardware platform, while the Windows operating system is an example of a software platform. Even though these meanings of platform originate from different source domains and operate on different levels of abstraction, they nevertheless build on one another and thereby form a generalized concept of architecture. For instance, the notion of computer platform embraces both the physical (staging area) and the political (guiding principles) connotations of platform. Interestingly, many of the metaphoric expressions in the texts were related most closely to and accessible primarily through the computer science meaning of platform. We thus begin our discussion with the platform metaphor as informed by the computer science domain.

Where the platforms of two computers are not common, data transfer between them becomes cumbersome because interfaces are required that can translate information across the seams and edges that form between platforms. In recent years, increasing attention is being paid to the creation of open architectures and open communications standards to support a more fluid exchange of information across platforms. This is where “the rigid formulation of traditional electronic data interchange will give way to a world of greater fluidity” (Upton & McAfee, 1996: 133). The Internet with its TCP/IP communication standard is one such example, allowing people to jump from one machine to another, and to patch machines together into a mosaic that supports the “interoperable” (Venkatraman & Henderson, 1998: 35) activities of virtual organizations. Open computer standards resemble the guiding principles that derive from the meaning of the political party platform. In the virtual organization, an open platform creates opportunities for building coalitions with other organizations and addressing potential mutinies that are one of the hazards of openness.

Taking the more physical meaning of platform, the platform architecture provides a base or basis that gives the virtual organization the capacity to take on multiple shapes and to dynamically reform itself by assembling assets and capabilities. The platform metaphor affords a view of organizing as supported by a foundation that underpins or underlies organizational activities. As suggested by authors of the texts we reviewed, this implies an underlying infrastructure that does not impose a specific structure, but affords a freer, edgeless organization<sup>4</sup>. Presented as an open architecture, the platform does not offer a complete blueprint specifying what the organizational structure ought to be, but rather provides “a framework for the conduct of life” (Venkatraman & Henderson, 1998: 33).

In the absence of a container-like structure surrounding the virtual organization and holding it in place, the challenge for virtual organizations is to find a way to gel, or bind, or pull different people and activities of the virtual organization together, back into the fold as it were, in order to create a “holistic” structure (Rayport & Sviokla, 1995: 79). The assets and activities that are assembled on the platform are not stable, permanent, or secure. Coalescing, collating, or holding elements together as a whole becomes an important task for an organization that cannot rely on the containing capacity of a corporate building. In this literature, an edgeless, seamless, common and unifying platform is seen as central to putting the pieces together and information is regarded as the glue “that binds virtual workers to their fellow employees and the company as a whole” (Davenport & Pearlson, 1998: 59).

## 4.3. Virtual organization is in space

This metaphor highlights the locational aspect of virtual organizations. In the literature on virtual organizations, the notion that work is an activity is contrasted with the notion that work is a place, i.e., an understanding of work relevant to traditional ways of organizing. In the virtual organization, “work is what you do, not where you go” (Handy, 1995: 42), and so work can be accomplished in any place. The notion that work can be executed anywhere and anytime is pervasive in the texts on virtuality (please refer to Appendix B). For instance, (Handy, 1995: 42) maintains that “[virtual organizations] exist as activities not as buildings; their only visible sign is an E-mail address”. Thus, organizational members no longer go into an organization’s buildings, but access its activities instead. Rayport and Sviokla (1995: 79) observe that in “the virtual world” organizations can “transcend the limitations of time and space that characterize management in the physical world”. This capacity to disassociate tasks from place (and time) implies that the virtual organization operates both everywhere and nowhere, that is, in space. This, the texts argue, is accomplished through “telepresence” (Upton & McAfee, 1996: 126) and the creation of global, electronic workspaces or information spaces, which together make up the unseen and sprawling empires of virtual organizations.

Place and space refer to different, socially constructed configurations of timespace. They are abstract concepts dealt with in disciplines like philosophy, physics, sociology, and geography. The time-space configuration of place is associated with traditional societies. It connotes boundedness and cyclical time, that is, events recurring in accordance with tradition such as the growing seasons and cycles of birth and death (Harvey, 1989: 204). Place further implies that people and things are copresent and engaged in face-to-face relations (Casey, 1993; Giddens, 1991). Being, rather than becoming, is prevalent in place (Harvey, 1989), suggesting that people and events are situated in the here and now, and that time and space are not separate concepts, but so tightly interwoven that they are distinguishable only analytically.

Modernity, through the Enlightenment and industrialization, breaks from the locality, tradition, and cyclical time associated with place (Sack, 1992), and fosters a configuration of space (Casey, 1993) that abstracts and divorces space from time through the lifting of social life out of the here and now, and through dissolving the ties that hold local practices in their place. Giddens (1990: 18) writes that the “advent of modernity increasingly tears space away from place by fostering relations between ‘absent’ others, locationally distant from any given situation of face-to-face interaction” (Giddens, 1990: 18). In contrast to the boundedness and uniqueness of place, the notion of space projects an image of uniform and infinite expanse across which people and ideas can move freely (Casey, 1997: 141). It thus promises generalizability of knowledge, globalization of consumer tastes, freedom of movement, social independence, unlimited growth, and a focus on the future (Tuan, 1977).

Even though virtual organizations are seen to operate out of no common place, they may still have physical offices. In this case, activities rather than people and their status will affect the allocation of available space. “An office in the future…will be like a clubhouse: a place for meeting, eating and greeting, with rooms reserved for activities, not for particular people” (Handy, 1995: 42). The virtual organization will engage in such practices as hot-desking and hoteling where workers check into “hotel rooms (which are more like cubicles)” in the morning rather than at night, like a normal hotel. Giving people “a room of [their] own, or at least a desk of [their] own [which] has been the executive security blanket” (Handy, 1995: 42), is a strategy associated with the traditional image of organizations as buildings and places. With the shift to space, workers in virtual organizations are dis-located and dis-placed. As these spatial metaphors suggest, workers will be “out of sight, but not, one hopes, out of touch or, for that matter, out of line” (Handy, 1995: 42).

Virtual organizations are likely to operate in both the marketplace and the marketspace. The marketspace represents a world of information, which acts as a mirror that reflects the world of physical goods. According to Rayport and Sviokla (1995), the virtual realm or information mirror presents organizations with the capability to “see their operations more effectively” (p. 78). This visibility granted by the reflective vantage point of the mirror world, allows organizations to shift — without any apparent friction — activities from “the place” to “the space”. In the world of information, a home page on the World Wide Web represents the virtual organization’s showroom, store, and mini-storefront.

The affordance of the space metaphor with respect to organizational location relates, in large part, to the elasticity and extendibility it promises. Venkatraman and Henderson (1998: 33) propose a strategy for virtual organizing comprised of three vectors — “customer interaction, asset sourcing, and knowledge leverage”. The image of vectors suggests a set of trajectories into the unknown and unbounded future afforded by the space metaphor.

An important challenge of working in space is reconciling the performance of work in specific historical contexts with the idea of activities abstracted out of a localized place. Even though the world of information promises to grant virtual organizations greater visual powers through the mirroring of the conditions in the marketplace, a greater ability to see and reflect on operations does not guarantee improvements in either the local place or the abstracted space. Furthermore, finding ways to sustain connection and coherence among the distributed, and perhaps fragmented, places that comprise the space of a virtual organization will be important. Motivating “out-of-sight” employees, retaining the most valuable contributors, and generating and sustaining trust and loyalty among the dispersed members of the organization, are all challenges in the space-like world of digitized resources.

## 4.4. Virtual organization is bits

The bits metaphor highlights the resource aspect of organizations. The fluidity and flexibility of the virtual organization — so evident in the platform and space metaphors — is seen here to be based on the digitized nature of its resources (please refer to Appendix C). In the traditional economy, exchange of goods takes place through physical channels, whereas the virtual economy is characterized by the exchange of bits over a network or along a virtual value chain in an electronic environment. Unlike the traditional organization, which relies primarily on atoms that make up its inventory of tangible goods, the virtual organization is seen to rely on bits in the form of knowledge and capabilities, replacing corporal muscle with computing muscle. Faucheux (1997: 53) notes that computers provide “electronic prostheses that increase memory capacity, speed of retrieval”. The raw material of virtual organizations is digital code, which — like such intellectual assets as information and knowledge — is not consumed or used up like atoms but reused and reharvested.

The source domain of bits is computer science. A bit is a binary digit, that is, either a 1 or a 0. All contemporary computer-based operations and representations, from simple text to complex animated graphics, rely on bits. The metaphor of resources as bits affords simplicity and flexibility to organizational representation, operation, and change. Advantages of digitized resources include their simplicity, malleability and the electronic speed at which they can be pooled, tapped (like liquids), and assembled (like solids). This means that the virtual organization can be quickly revised and the resources that are required to complete a task can be cutand-pasted as needed. Bits are presented as the power source behind the virtual organization. As the drivers of the information economy, these bolts of information replace the oil and electricity that fueled previous eras. When aggregated, they may even displace physical violence: “bytes become more important than bullets” (Davidow & Malone, 1992: 51).

The virtual organization, which feeds on information, ideas, and intelligence is primarily interested in the worker’s mind — the bright light of brain work — from which value can be extracted. In the virtual organization, all resources — even the workers — can be converted to bits. Thus, like text, workers can be digitized, and then added and deleted as needed. This metaphor of resources as bits affords organizations the possibility of endless substitutability, malleability, and reusability of resources, and particularly in the case of a previously less than tractable resourcelabor. Workers are not viewed holistically, as having both a body and a mind, but as representing receptacles and generators of information, having expertise that must be harnessed, and vested with intellectual assets, which in turn are to be milked as needed for the sake of the organization.

The challenge in such a digitized world is ensuring that the reusable resources remain valuable over time. According to this literature, reusable workers are those that are capable of self-renewal. Ensuring that such renewal occurs, and in a way and on a time schedule that is useful to the organization, represents a significant challenge to virtual forms of organizing. Furthermore, given the multiple, fragmented selves of digitized workers<sup>5</sup>, some means of integrating these many selves, or at least managing among them, may be required.

## 4.5. Virtual organization is a community

The aspect of organizing addressed with the community metaphor is organizational governance. As work moves away from being contained within a stable structure, as it is no longer associated with a particular place and time, and as it involves workers who are out of sight and — to some extent — out of mind, the traditional command-and-control mode of governance is rendered inadequate in a virtual environment. In response, texts on virtual organizing invoke the metaphor of community, which is seen to provide a high touch mode of governance, one with soft not hard connections (please refer to Appendix D).

The notion of community has its origin in the idea of mutual exchange: the group that is bound together for mutual service is the community, from the Latin “communis” — common or general, and “munia” — service. The community of a virtual organization, whether composed of suppliers and customers, of organizational members, or of professional experts, is seen to depend on a more open and friendly mode of governance. This is needed to turn the virtual organization into an extended family composed of members that have both rights and responsibilities. Indeed, for some authors, the virtual context is one where “employees become co-equals with their managers” (Davidow & Malone, 1992: 178).

The community-based form of governance prescribed for virtual organizations relies on “trust to make it work” (Handy, 1995: 44). Trust is the heart of the matter. Trust is not an impersonal commodity; it demands learning, bonding, touch, mutual destiny and reciprocal loyalty. Trust is not blind; it can be ruthless and tough as needed. When the virtual organization is built upon a plateau of trust, some workers become “trusties” (Handy, 1995: 46). These “trusties” form the core of the organization; the rest makes up the periphery. Trust, cooperation, and community are presented as the cure for the ills of the traditional mode of governance, where the obsession with control and audits has infected organizations with a virus, and where “checkers check checkers” on an almost continual basis (Handy, 1995: 44).

An affordance of the community metaphor is that it outlines an alternative, soft form of governance. The trust and cooperation of mutual service in the community supplies the edgeless, digitized, and dislocated organization with heart and soul. It also provides a refuge, as Davidow and Malone (1992: 264) write: “In the future, competition will be so fierce and the need for trust and teamwork within the virtual corporation so vital that … under the threat of annihilation, we will have to cling to one another for safety”.

The challenge to managing virtual organizations as communities is treating individuals as members to be trusted rather than tethered workers, free to wander around, yet committed to the company. Another challenge is establishing cooperation, trust, and respect among individuals and groups who may have little knowledge of or history with one another, who may have few opportunities to interact frequently or face to face, and who may be participating in multiple, overlapping — even competing — networks and relationships. A further challenge here is the shifting of managerial mindsets, honed over the years in the discourse and practice of commandand-control, competition, and strategy as war, now needing to move towards a discourse and practice of community, cooperation, and trust.

## 4.6. Virtual organization is a network

Organizational identity is the aspect of organizing highlighted by the network metaphor. In traditional organizations, identity is crafted from members’ shared beliefs about the organization’s central enduring and distinctive values, in other words, its culture (Wiesenfeld et al., 1998). In a virtual environment, the organization’s identity is a dynamic collage, emerging from its interactions with the organizations who are its allies and partners. Whereas the identity of the traditional organization is seen as the organization’s coherent and relatively stable culture, what Martin (1992) refers to as the integrated organizational culture, the identity of the virtual organization is constructed from its “portfolio of relationships” with other organizations (Venkatraman & Henderson, 1998: 40). This implies a more differentiated, distributed and fragmented organizational culture and identity. The metaphor invoked for the virtual organization’s identity is the network (please refer to Appendix E). More particularly, the inter-organizational network is seen as critical to virtual organizations, which are given little chance of surviving as loners outside of a network. Those “that delay…the process of becoming a virtual corporation will be swept away, their remnants seized, reorganized properly, and absorbed by fast-moving modern competition” (Davidow & Malone, 1992: 11). From this vantage point, “virtuality looks like a very precarious state” (Handy, 1995: 49).

Network is a concept that is used extensively in the domains of engineering and computer science. An earlier source is the domain of natural sciences where the notion of network was used to characterize the interwoven nature of — among other things — veins, rivers, and spiders’ webs. During the industrial era, the network concept was used to depict such support infrastructures as transportation, electricity, and communication, for example, networks of roads, rail, electricity, and telephony. The concepts of circuits and switching are closely associated with networks. A circuit represents a circular path through the area defined by a network of nodes and relationships between them. Switching is the act of establishing new or breaking existing circuits or paths. For instance, a switch in the railroad domain is a movable section of the railroad track by which a train is changed from one set of rails to another. Switching thus results in a transfer or shift from one circuit (or set of rails) to another.

One affordance of the network metaphor is a recognition of the densely knit nature of the external relationships encompassing the virtual organization. That is, the virtual organization is part of a “cluster of activities in the midst of a vast fabric of relationships” (Davidow & Malone, 1992). It is from these apparently tightly woven threads of relationships that the virtual organization derives its identity and it seems, its destiny. Another affordance of the network metaphor suggests that the identity of a virtual organization is both multiple and dynamic, arising as it does out of the multitude of external relationships orchestrated by the virtual organization with its customers and suppliers. These relationships are established through dynamic linking and switching. The virtual organization is involved with a variety of partners simultaneously. At any point in time, a virtual organization can be dating one partner, engaged to a second, and married to a third. Thus, a virtual organization engages in a form of polygamy, an open marriage with multiple partners including suppliers, customers, and competitors. This suggests that the identity of a virtual organization shifts continuously as the organization engages in a variety of different relationships — both at the same time, when it interacts with different partners, and over time, when it changes its partners as deemed appropriate.

The switching, shuffling, and shifting between partners, which is facilitated by this openness, “militates against long-term relationships” (Mowshowitz, 1997). This allows a virtual organization to drop, prune, slash or cut suppliers from its rolls of eligible partners based on circumstances in the competitive environment. Even though the possibility for short-term relationships has positive implications for the virtual organization’s ties with its suppliers, such relationships may present a challenge when it comes to the virtual organization’s relationships with its customers. According to Davidow and Malone (1992: 222), the goal of the virtual organization is to “maximize the binding energy between themselves and their customers”. It attempts to enlist customers into a relationship of “mutual” destiny or “co-destiny” (Davidow & Malone, 1992: 142), which is the price of a ticket to the business world of the future. The objective is to embrace, capture, bind, and hold customers for life. However, whether this life-long relationship that is envisaged between the firm and its customers is one of mutual partnership, one of enlisted citizens, or one of captured and bound prisoners, remains ambiguous.

The challenge for organizations juggling multiple, variable, and temporary partners is to maintain a balanced “portfolio of relationships”, making them neither too cutthroat and opportunistic, nor too entrenched and invested. Another challenge is managing the organization’s position within the network of allies and partners. It is not sufficient to be merely a part of the network; having influence and being able to call the shots requires a central position in the midst of the inter-organizational network.

## 5. Metaphors of virtuality

## 5.1. Critical examination

Having provided a diagnostic reading of the metaphorical language used in the practitioner-directed literature on virtual organizing, we now turn to a more critical examination of this contemporary discourse. As we have discussed, metaphors offer both partial and partisan pictures of the phenomena they describe. The five metaphors that we highlight in this paper each describes a different aspect of organizing with images that — when taken together — may be seen to be both convergent and divergent. That is, when we examine the relationships among the five metaphors, we find that while some are relatively compatible and complementary, contributing to a coherent view of virtuality, many undermine the possibility of coherence by furnishing incongruent and contradictory views of virtuality.

Consider first the congruent images. On the surface, the notion of the open platform that supports any assemblage of activities such that the virtual organization can operate seamlessly in space seems to portray a coherent view of a virtual organization as free and fluid and yet grounded on a solid foundation that simultaneously facilitates a connection to many different places. Despite this lack of connection to any one place, membership in a virtual organization is seen to “replace the sense of belonging to a place with a sense of belonging to a community” (Handy, 1995: 48). Furthermore, the common, unifying platform makes up for the common place of work that is lost when an organization competes in the marketspace. This platform also provides the base for the vectors of the virtual organization’s strategy within the marketspace. Information bits provide the glue that holds these activities together, as does the community governance and network identity that are based on coalitions of trust and multiple, dynamic partnerships.

However, there is also considerable incongruence among the images of virtual organizing. For example, we find the following: organizations establishing homepages in cyberspace; married partners who nevertheless have to bid for work with each other, who have short-term relationships, and who can be married to multiple partners at the same time; members of virtual organizations who are seen to be on the periphery; networks that are open and friendly but that pose a threat to the loner; networks that enlist and capture customers while also attempting to embrace them within relationships of mutual destiny; communities of trust that demand reciprocal loyalty but which are also tough and ruthless, keeping some of their members tethered and milking their assets; self-organizing teams that need a perfect formula; virtual organizations that are both tight-knit communities as well switching machines or a box of contracts.

That the discourse on virtuality is largely suffused with diverse and divergent images should come as no surprise. As new and emerging as well as complex phenomena, virtual organizations will appear to be many different things at the same time-multi-dimensional, dynamic, and inherently ambiguous. It is unclear whether these metaphors will coalesce over time as more instances of virtual organizations appear along with a richer empirical basis for developing a vocabulary for virtuality. Given the multiple domains from which individual metaphors are appropriated, there is little reason to believe that either the metaphors of virtual organizations or their meanings will stabilize and become unequivocal. For example, consider the multiplicity of meanings found in the platform metaphor. Our discussion showed how it can be understood in terms of its physical presence as a raised surface, its political meaning as a set of guidelines, and its manifestation as a hardware or software architecture. Because such ambiguity is built in, it is unlikely to be displaced, even over time.

In addition to revealing the multiplicity and instability of convergent and divergent images in the discourse on virtuality, a critical examination highlights that much of this discourse displays more than a little metaphoric ideology. To some extent, this is inevitable. As Tinker (1986: 378) notes: “Metaphors are never neutral representations of social affairs”. That is, metaphors always both enable and constrain ways of seeing, understanding, and acting in the world. What bears examining, though, is which images are being enabled and which disabled by the metaphors used in this practitioner-directed literature. For example, in many of the texts we reviewed, the role of information is highlighted, and even glorified. Information is portrayed as particularly enabling, and assigned to many (central) roles in virtual organizations, such as: a way of seeing the physical organization better [e.g., in the mirror world imagery of Rayport & Sviokla (1995)]; an opportunity for partners to learn about each other [e.g., in the romantic relationships described by Upton & McAfee (1996)]; a means of constant self-renewal (Faucheux, 1997); a source of innovation and new value creation (Rayport & Sviokla, 1995); and as the glue that binds the members of the virtual organization together (Davenport & Pearlson, 1998). Unexamined in these texts are the constraining aspects of information, for example, the problems, errors, and conflicts that may arise when information is ambiguous, obscure, obsolete, incorrect, incomplete, incoherent, misleading, misrepresented, misinterpreted, misused, mismanaged, and misplaced.

Similarly, when Davidow and Malone (1992: 216) write about the shift to virtual organizing as “crossing over to the Promised Land”, we might ask what meanings are silenced or not articulated. Some that come to mind include: structural conflicts, means of control, economic isolation, social alienation, labor inequities, politics and power plays, the psychic and societal costs of exploitation and extraction, dispersion and displacement, disruption and disparity, as well as the large number of probable unintended consequences. By and large, the texts on virtual organizing paint an optimistic picture of life in the virtual world. Unexamined, with the exception of Davenport and Pearlson (1998) and Handy (1995), are the personal, professional, and cultural dislocations that are likely to accompany the physical, structural, temporal, and boundary dislocations resulting from what are anticipated to be profound shifts in modes of governing, norms of community, rules of engagement, and forms of identity.

## 6. Implications

We began this paper by suggesting that the use of metaphor may be a powerful means of understanding and structuring reality. Particularly in the case of new and complex phenomena such as virtual organizing, the images, ideas, and ideals espoused by the practitioner-directed discourse on virtuality can play a significant role in constituting that reality. That is, in the absence of experiences (direct or vicarious) to guide action, people will draw on the prevailing rhetoric to enact particular instantiations of virtual phenomena.

In the case of virtual organizing, the role of metaphorical analysis is particularly difficult because we are trying to understand how metaphors are being used to describe an emergent phenomenon. What we find when we do a metaphorical analysis is that the discourse of virtuality is suffused with multiple, complex, and paradoxical images of virtual organizing. This finding raises important questions for researchers and practitioners attempting to use one or more of the metaphors to engage with virtual forms of organizing.

We have found that the use of metaphorical opposition is quite pronounced in the literature on virtual organizations. The metaphorical oppositions we identified — between bounded building and edgeless platform, between grounded place and abstract space, between physical atoms and electronic bits, between hierarchical control and trusted community, and between stable culture and shifting network — can be seen to both echo and extend the dominant metaphorical oppositions of organic vs mechanistic and hard vs soft evident in much of the organizational literature. A number of the metaphors of virtuality are derived from the domain of computer science, including the metaphors of platform, bits, and network, whereas the metaphors of community and space derive from the human and physical sciences. The representation of metaphors deriving meaning from both the domains of computer science and the human and physical sciences suggests that a balance between technical and social, mechanistic and organic, and hard and soft aspects of organizing may be achieved in the representations and realizations of virtual organizations.

However, as we have noted above, a strategy of opposition is often reductionist and may result in oversimplification. Furthermore, such a strategy is likely to set up false dichotomies that need to be overcome in order to increase the opportunities for thinking about and enacting new phenomena. For instance, in the discourse on virtual organizing that we analyzed, we can find authors who are attempting to develop new vocabulary to resolve some of the constraining dualisms created by the strategy of opposition. For example, Venkatraman and Henderson (1998) propose the term “co-opetition” to capture the blurred distinction between co-operative and competitive strategies that need to be considered concurrently in virtual ways of organizing. Similarly, Davenport and Pearlson (1998) use metonymy to bridge the opposing views of work as an activity and work as a place by creating concepts like “home office” and “home work”, and “hotel cubicle”. However, these new terms are ambiguous. For instance, “co-opetition” evokes ideas of cooptation, while the term “home work” conjures up images of school and domestic housekeeping. This raises questions about their appropriateness and their success in creating a vocabulary that embraces opposition.

It also raises questions about the possibility of creating a vocabulary that bridges the divide between bipolar opposites. Other examples of new vocabulary in the texts are less problematic, as in Handy’s (1995) “trusties” — the trusted members of virtual organizations, and Davidow and Malone’s (1992) coining of “co-equals” to talk about the new relationship between workers and managers. However, such new vocabulary does not attempt to bridge metaphoric oppositions, and thus may be less powerful rhetorically, unable to capture complexity and diversity in meanings and experiences of virtuality. Before adopting any invented or reinvented terms, we should carefully consider the intended and unintended meanings that may be associated with such terms.

It is also important to note that the ambiguities embedded in new vocabulary of virtuality have a ripple effect on the work practices prescribed for virtual organizations. For instance, Davenport and Pearlson (1998) recommend that virtual workers engaging in “home work” establish clear boundaries between home and work. They are encouraged to teach their families that they are “at work” when they are in their “home office”. Thus, families are required to place telephone calls to their family members’ “home offices” on the home phone rather than interrupt them face to face. There is considerable irony associated with this “at work” rhetoric in that it still implies that virtual work is a place one goes to, despite the extensive talk about work being an activity.

Such ironies and ambiguities may confuse practitioners in terms of how they think about and act towards virtual organizations. In their confusion, practitioners may find justification for any course of action they may choose to adopt. For example, practitioners could be compelled to create community and trust forms of governance, but they might also be moved to treat their employees as texts that can be revised and cut-and-pasted at will.

As an alternative, practitioners may learn to accommodate (Boudreau & Robey, 1996) the multiple and conflicting images of virtuality, directing their energies towards a constructive engagement of the paradoxes and ambiguities inherent in the discourse, and using these as a catalyst for flexibility and creativity (Handy, 1994). For instance, even as virtual organizations create and operate in the abstracted space of cybermalls, their employees and customers will need to act out of some grounded place. The comprehension and engagement of space can only occur from the vantage point of particular places (Tuan, 1977). Such consideration of dualities rather than dualisms suggests the importance for both research and practice of embracing rather than discarding the multiplicity of complementary and conflicting images of virtual organizing generated by the discourse on virtuality.

For researchers, our metaphorical analysis suggests that in addition to extensive empirical investigation, research on virtuality requires careful conceptual work. The practitioner-directed discourse suggests that no single, coherent view of virtual organizations exists and none is likely to emerge. To the extent that this discourse is shaping action, practitioners and researchers will be enacting forms of virtuality that embody multiple, diverse, and conflicting elements. Making sense of these complex and divergent enactments will require researchers to not only pay attention to the language used within organizations, but to also adopt conceptual frameworks that entail diversity, multiplicity, and contradiction. Robey and Boudreau (1999: 181) observe that “Theories with a logic of opposition offer an alternative explanation of the ways in which information technologies interact with organizations to produce social consequences”. Such theories embrace paradox, contradiction, and duality and in doing so provide fresh insights into organizational phenomena. The same benefits may accrue to researchers who use such paradoxical logic to help explain the empirical reality of virtual organizing.

In conducting empirical research into the practice of virtual organizing, researchers should investigate the extent to which practitioners are enacting the images and ideas propounded in the contemporary literature on this topic. In addition, empirical research might usefully examine the consequences, intended and unintended, of the various virtual ways of organizing suggested in the literature — such as platform architectures, placeless work, digitized resources, community forms of governance, and networked identities. Researchers might also examine the extent to which the incongruencies among the metaphors that we have identified are associated with problems and opportunities in the realization of virtual organizing.

In this paper, we have suggested that metaphors are powerful tools of social constructions, and that the five overarching metaphors of virtual organizing we have identified here are likely to significantly shape the reality of virtuality. Consequently, consideration of these metaphors should raise researchers’ and practitioners’ awareness of the critical implications associated with conceiving of a virtual organization as a platform, operating in space, composed of bits, governed as a community, and identifying with its network. It should also increase researchers’ and practitioners’ understanding of the unintended consequences of using these metaphors uncritically.
