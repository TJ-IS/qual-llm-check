# A computational model of communication for reducing linguistic opacity based on the language-action perspective

Monica Divitini <sup>a,\*</sup>, Carla Simone <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> IDI, Norwegian University of Science and Technology, Trondheim, Norway <sup>b</sup> DI, University of Torino, Corso Svizzera, 185-10149 Torino, Italy

## Abstract

This paper presents a communication model based on the language-action perspective aiming at reducing what is called linguistic opacity. In particular, the focus is on mechanisms supporting the maintenance of the contexts against which the interpretation of conversations occurs. The mechanisms are based on a communication model where two types of conversations are considered: conversations for action and conversations for possibility. They are used for negotiating commitments that shape the work arrangements in relation to the actions to be performed (the operational context) and the organizational space of possibility for future actions (the organizational context). The proposed computational model takes into account the dynamics of knowledge creation and sharing by describing how conversations modify the operational and organizational context as well as the partial views of them shaped by different actors.  2001 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Communication; Pragmatics; Cooperation contexts; Conversation; Speech act; Linguistic opacity

## 1. Modeling communication: for what purpose?

Communication is one of the main means, though not the only one, through which people working together coordinate their distributed activities and manage the complexity of their work. Given the central role of communication in articulating cooperative work, any technology aiming at supporting cooperation must take into account communication and the way the communicative process is influenced by the intrinsic complexity of cooperative settings. In these settings communication can be problematic for many reasons. For example, actors belong to different or distributed organizations; they posses different roles, skills, and competencies; they communicate over long periods and/or with varying frequencies. Not only actors are different, but also the membership to a cooperative ensemble is often highly dynamic and new actors continuously join and leave the group temporarily (e.g., because of a delegation) or permanently (e.g., because of turnover). As a result of all these different factors, it is often difficult for the interlocutors to keep consistent and updated the framework in which communication happens and obtains its meaning. We metaphorically denote this difficulty linguistic opacity. Linguistic opacity can be defined as the unease people have in ‘moving in a linguistic framework’ which is perceived unfamiliar and then ‘opaque’, obscure. This opacity arises mainly from the discrepancy between actor’s knowledge ‘ready-at-hand’ and the knowledge needed in the interpretation of the messages.

The focus of this paper is to identify mechanisms that can alleviate linguistic opacity. A promising framework from which to base this inquiry is the languageaction perspective (Winograd & Flores, 1986). In fact, the assemblage of theoretical foundations, methods and technologies grouped under this label proposes an important paradigm shift in the way communication is studied, because it allows one to conceive communication as a social activity. In this perspective, communication is not seen as an exchange of information, but as establishing relationships among speakers in terms of mutual commitments and thus modifying the world in which communication takes place. More specifically, the work presented here is influenced by the Coordinator, a seminal piece of technology based on this approach. However, as reported in Simone and Divitini (1999, p. 241): “% we realized that the Coordinator, while original and provocative, disregarded two aspects that, however, were covered by the underlying conceptualization. First of all, the fact that the negotiation happens within and modifies the organizational setting in which it occurs, and secondly, the fact that the negotiation takes its meaning from, and creates new meaning for, the people belonging to the organization in which the negotiation happens%”. Hence, our effort is to develop a model for organizing mechanisms that reduce linguistic opacity in a coherent way. This model is intended to be ‘in alignment’ with features belonging to the language-action conceptualization that were often implicit or ‘forgotten’ in Winograd and Flores (1986), but more explicitly treated in Habermas (1991), although without any reference to a technological context.

It is important to observe that our goal is to develop a model that provides a set of concepts on which a technological artifact supporting communication and cooperation can be constructed (hereafter, a computational model). The proposed model does not pretend to describe communication completely, or analyze how people understand communication and cooperate through it (analytical model). A clear distinction between these two types of models is important because the underlying goals define quite different requirements for modeling, both in terms of content and of validation principles.

Analytical models must be as comprehensive as possible in order to take into account the multifacetal nature of human communication. That is, various modes, means and forms in which human beings use language(s) must be accounted for. Developing a model for designing a computational artifact leads one to formulate models that are, by definition, partial: they must take into account intrinsic limited capabilities of the technology. Hence, the phenomenology of communications can cover a limited portion of the phenomena possible to observe.

The validation principles are distinct in the two cases too: in the first case, the best model is the one that captures the richest set of features characterizing communicative behavior. According to the second goal, the best model is the one that identifies the richest set of mechanisms that can be reasonably implemented in an artifact supporting human communication and cooperation in a user acceptable way.

It is likely that to the extent that analytical models are good in their explanatory capabilities their richness cannot be captured in an artifact (and vice versa). The lack of a clear distinction between these two goals often leads to models that do not serve anyone in an appropriate way. In fact, there is a risk that human behavior is explained in terms of the capability of the technology, or the technology is expected to show sophisticated human capabilities.

The above distinction is fundamental because the two types of models are both useful in the design of a supportive technology: in fact, “The most successful designs are not those that try to fully model the domain in which they operate, but those that are ‘in alignment’ with the fundamental structure of that domain %. As observers (and programmers), we want to understand to the best of our ability just what the relevant domain of action is. This understanding guides our design % but need not (and in fact cannot) be embodied in the form of mechanism” (Winograd & Flores, 1986, p. 53). In this context, making explicit the computational model incorporated in the artifact is not just useful but strongly recommended. First, it allows for the design of a coherent set of functionalities that will improve the quality of the engineered artifact. Due to the above mentioned complexity of cooperative settings, the supporting artifacts must be easy to change, in order to adapt to recurrent and local changes (malleability), and easy to extend, in order to deal with different aspects of cooperative work (extendibility).

Second, the visibility of the model incorporated in the artifact allows the users to understand the scope of the provided functionality, to exploit its capabilities at the best of their current needs, and finally, to evaluate its degree of acceptability (that is, how it is ‘aligned’ to their current understanding of communication and cooperation, or at least to a model they can potentially envisage to adopt). The fundamental requirement of the visibility has been highlighted by several studies of the use of cooperation technologies (Schmidt & Simone, 1996). In particular, visibility is required if the artifact is designed so as to allow users themselves to modify its functionality.

The language-action tradition proposes several modeling approaches: primarily, SAMPO (Lyytinen & Lehtinen, 1984) DEMO (van Reijswoud, Mulder, & Dietz, 1999), BAT (Goldkuhl, 1996) together with their specializations and usages in the analysis of organizations, their information systems and communication structures. They belong to the class of computational models as their overall goal is toward the design of a technology supporting business process and information system definition, analysis and reengineering. The present paper goes in the same direction as it presents a computational model that we conceived to help design tools that alleviate linguistic opacity. The model has the same basic focus on commitments and role in business communications as the above-mentioned models. However, it focuses on aspects that the other approaches leave ‘in the background’. In this respect, we do not consider it as an alternative, but rather as a complementary proposal, in that the existing models presuppose a context where communication takes place (irrespective of the different terms they use to denote it). In general, they do not consider how communication modifies this context and how the modifications, together with the dynamics of the organization in which the interlocutors live, generate linguistic opacity that makes communication difficult. The model proposed here deals with these aspects.

The proposed model has been used in the development of a prototype supporting the asynchronous, message-based communication in cooperative activities. A complete description of the prototype, called CHAOS (conversation handling active office system) can be found in De Cindio, De Michelis, Simone, Vassallo, and Zanaboni (1986), Divitini and Simone (1994) and Simone and Divitini (1999).

The remainder of the paper is organized as follow. Section 2 considers in more detail the concept of linguistic opacity. Section 3 delimits the scope of the communication model, while Section 4 describes its components from a static point of view. Section 5 describes how the model accounts for the partial views that users have of contexts where they act. Section 6 analyzes the dynamics of the model, showing how communication events change people’s partial views. Section 7 sketches how the computational model can serve as a basis for the design of functionalities that help people reduce linguistic opacity. The concluding section highlights possible developments of the current proposal.

## 2. About linguistic opacity

Linguistic opacity is pervasive in communication. It relates to the mismatches between the knowledge that it is needed to interpret communications and the interlocutors’ partial knowledge of the world in which they interact. This connotation shows how the term shares part of its meaning with what has been called opacity in philosophy and linguistics (see e.g., Allwood, Andersson, & Dahl, 1977, for the genesis of this term), but it has, however, a different focus. Similarities refer to the need to consider different worlds to define the meaning of a sentence when substituting co-referring terms. Differences are in the focus on mutual understanding in communication rather than in the assignment of truth values to sentences. In communication what plays an essential role is the history of past interactions among interlocutors, which determines the set of worlds and time relationships that are relevant for the current interaction. In this view, linguistic opacity can be defined as the outcome of what cybernetics calls a negative feedback cycle. When people exchange messages to manage commitments (i.e., to define, modify, cancel, conclude them) it is necessary that they have a certain knowledge about the entities they are referencing. This does not imply that those people share the same knowledge about the (implicitly) referenced entities and their interrelations. Indeed, knowledge sharing is a product of communications. In order to make the negotiation effective, people try to reach a minimal consensus on what the commitment is about, so that its fulfilment can start. In Maturana’s words (see Winograd & Flores, 1986, p. 50), “the basic function of language as a system of orienting behavior % [is] the creation of a consensual domain of behavior between linguistically interacting systems through the development of a cooperative domain of interactions”. Accordingly, Winograd and Flores (1986) emphasize the role of the social background in interpretation by asserting that “Meaning is fundamentally social and cannot be reduced to the meaning-giving activity of individual subjects” (p. 33). In the same vein, Habermas (1991) refers to a common background (‘life world’) which contains the knowledge the interlocutors share (e.g., about facts, institutions and competencies), and which makes the communication possible.

What closes the negative feedback cycle is that the generated consensual domain constitutes a source of linguistic opacity, as it reduces the speaker’s capability to orient the listener in his or her cognitive domain. In fact, consciousness of sharing a common set of references leads people to simplify communications by exchanging ambiguous and incomplete messages whose interpretation becomes difficult in the presence of different sets of references owned by individuals or subgroups with different communication experiences. This is common, in particular when communications involve many people, or are conducted over a prolonged period of time, or in any form of asynchronous communication.

The above considerations impose important requirements on the computational model. The model must embed mechanisms to represent both harmonized ‘knowledge’ and contradictory knowledge, to account for their evolution through linguistic interactions, and finally to make this knowledge usable to the interlocutors in order to improve their future or present communications.

As pointed out by several authors, different sorts of knowledge coexist in a group (e.g., Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Davenport & Prusak, 1998). All of them are relevant in knowledge creation and diffusion within the group. However, not all of them can be easily captured and handled in a computational model without making it unrealistic, or untractable. For this reason, the proposed computational model does not consider implicit/tacit knowledge as its articulation is open-ended (Winograd & Flores, 1986), nor knowledge conveyed through sophisticated means (like, histories, metaphors, tales and the like). We will therefore use the term knowledge in a restricted meaning. Knowledge is interpreted as the corpus of references to entities, actions, facts and their mutual relationships that characterize a work setting in which people cooperate. From the concept of linguistic opacity it follows that each actor owns a different and dynamic view of knowledge, or in other terms, of the consensual domain constructed through language actions. The computational model considers only knowledge (i.e., the sets of references) explicitly mentioned in conversations that negotiate commitments. This is a strong limitation. However, our every day experience tells us that linguistic opacity arises even in relation to this circumscribed portion of knowledge.

Moreover, it is necessary to define the extent to which the model allows for the identification of automatic reasoning capabilities. When considering this aspect it is important to recall that “a language exists among a community of individuals, and is continuously regenerated through their linguistic activity” (Winograd & Flores, 1986, p. 49). To our knowledge, no logical system is at the moment able to capture this complexity, with inferences based on rules that are reasonably acceptable. We therefore decided to focus our attention on the characterization of pieces of knowledge that are contextualized in the communications that generated them, and to introduce in the model minor inferential capabilities. The actual task of drawing conclusions is left to humans. Thus, the only role the technology can play in this game is to act as a ‘memory’ of different contexts in which conversations or conversation networks flow. To fulfil this role the memory cannot be just a dumb container. Rather it must become an active component that co-evolves with linguistic behaviors that at the same time construct and use it.

## 3. The boundaries of the communication model

While alleviating linguistic opacity our focus is on technology based mechanisms that can support the maintenance of dynamic contexts against which the interpretation of language acts occur. Recall that we use the term context to denote what others call background, or life world, and which is explicitly represented in the model.

As discussed by Winograd and Flores (1986), design requires us to represent ‘the recurrent pattern of breakdown’ characterizing the target reality - in our case, the contexts and their changes — as a “systematic domain, for which definitions and rules are articulated”. In this effort, a first step is to delimit the scope of the computational model to incorporate the pertinent definitions and rules, and its ‘interface with what remains outside it. This issue is the content of the current section. How the model is internally articulated is described in the next sections.

First of all, the model considers a group of people that belongs to an organization, possibly organized in sub-groups. The group can be a project team, a department, a faculty, the teachers and fellows of a school, and so on. This means that all members of the group are governed by a coherent set of organizational rules and conventions. Obviously, this does not imply that the group is a ‘monad’ and closed to any influence coming from outside. On the contrary, each individual member belongs to other groups: the latter influence the processes generating linguistic opacity too. In fact, linguistic opacity is not only generated by different communication experiences within the group, but it is also amplified by the different communication experiences each individual member acquires or did acquire as a member of other groups. Hence, the communication model has to take into account this unavoidable external interference, as well as the impossibility of considering the external communication processes generating differences and modifications in the group members’ knowledge. In fact, by definition, a computational model is finite and therefore cannot incorporate this phenomenology which becomes recursive in each group, and thus in principle open-ended. Then, how can the model take into account these modifications? We avoid adopting an approach of ‘closing the system’ by a simplified representation of an environment since this solution is impractical. In fact, any representation of the environment in order to be feasible is unduly fictitious and arbitrary. Instead, we prefer a strictly open approach which considers the group as a (finite) open system interacting with its environment through its members (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Wegner, 1998). Therefore, the proposed model does not incorporate the communication events occurring outside the group, and it accounts for the knowledge these events generate only when the group’s members bring the related knowledge inside the group.

Obviously, when the group is formed or enlarged, its members own a certain amount of mutual knowledge which reflects the characteristics of each individual. This knowledge can have at least two main connotations: it is owned by all of them as a common background, or can be seen as an outcome of specific events of each individual’s history in the family, school, work, hobbies and so on. The proposed computational model explicitly recognizes these two types of knowledge that are defined as follows:

 Public domain knowledge (PDK), i.e., the knowledge that all group members are supposed to possess, irrespective of their own characteristics, since they live in a common social, political, economical environment. Entities like ‘customer’, ‘law’, ‘price’ and actions like ‘send’, ‘read’, ‘vote’ typically belong to this type of knowledge.

 Stereotypical knowledge (StK<sub>i</sub>), i.e., the knowledge that member i of the group is supposed to have in consequence of some events characterizing his/her own history. For example, if a member has a degree in software engineering entities like ‘programs’, ‘operating systems’, ‘design methodology’ and actions like ‘specify’, ‘implement’, ‘document’ belong to the knowledge characterizing the stereotype ‘software engineer’. Notice that a single member can belong to different stereotypes focused on independent experiences (related, e.g., to family, education, work environments). So, for example, a ‘software engineer’ can be a ‘tennis player’.

Both types of knowledge have been widely considered, for example, in computational linguistics where related conceptual and computational complexity has been emphasized. For example, the use of stereotypes, common to different application domains (Rich, 1989), is proposed in combination with more dynamical techniques, in order to avoid starting knowledge representation ‘from scratch’.

In the proposed model, the role of the two above types of knowledge is limited to the identification of some default knowledge characterizing the group’s member and is thereby used to initialize the description of their past linguistic experience at the moment when they join the group. This description plays the role of a background for the initial communication. This is what happens in real situations, e.g., when a group receives a newcomer. For example, when a new member joins a university department her origin (within or outside Europe) and her education (in computer science or in psychology) define, by default, different pieces of knowledge that the other members can believe she owns and can be used in communicating with her with a low probability of misunderstanding.

Since we search for computational acceptability, we are not necessarily presupposing accurate public domain or stereotypical knowledge. On the contrary, given an initial representation of both of them, we account for the dynamics of their evolution in relation to the communication experience generated within the group. These modifications are perceived by the group members when they interact among them in the course of their cooperation. In other words, the mechanisms updating the sharing of the public domain and stereotypical knowledge are the same as the ones dealing with the knowledge about the group itself, its organization and activities, which is generated within the group. In relation to the previous examples, the newcomer can acquire experiences of the European culture by conversing with people outside the group and use it in conversing with the group members exactly as she acquires and uses the knowledge pertinent to the interaction implied by her activities within the group.

In this way, the proposed model is both finite and open to its environment. This solution mimics what happens in real situations: upon their arrival, newcomers are introduced and their characteristics are presented to other members of the group. Subsequent discrepancies and modifications have to be ‘discovered’ through the interactions they have with the group members in the course of their cooperation. These aspects will be illustrated in Sections 5 and 6, where the public domain and stereotypical knowledge are put to work.

## 4. The basic elements of the communication model

The inherently dynamic nature of the social world suggests we should define the model in terms of an ‘engine’ which recognizes events inducing modifications in the contexts, and activates mechanisms that make changes in the entities involved in these events (Fig. 1). In the spirit of the language-action perspective, the engine is based on a conversation manager which recognizes communication events that are part of conversations. In addition, since a computational model can just incorporate a part of the world, the model is characterized by the linguistic entities it takes into account. Two main kinds of entities are considered: operational entities, i.e., entities which are related to the execution of actions fulfilling the commitments negotiated within conversations, and organizational entities, i.e., entities which are related to the organizational environment in which those actions occur. These entities and their interrelationships define the contexts we are considering: the operational and the organizational contexts. Moreover, the model has to take into account that actors hold partial views of linguistic entities. To capture the dynamics of the social world, the engine incorporates mechanisms linking communication events to modifications of entities and partial views (as described in Section 6).

The operational and organizational contexts are two logically separated portions of the computational model. They are linked by conversations. At the highest level of abstraction, their relationships are illustrated by the metamodel in Fig. 2. An actor plays a role in the organizational context. The role constrains the space of possibilities in the conversations that the actor is involved in. Conversations (re)define both the organizational and the operational contexts. Operationally, this process is controlled by the conversation manager which handles the space of possibilities of each interlocutor and triggers the modification mechanisms in accordance with the evolution of the conversation (as illustrated in Fig. 1). In the following the conversation manager and the two contexts are explained in detail.

## 4.1. The conversation manager

According to the language-action tradition, conversations denote recurrent patterns of communicative acts. These patterns define which communication events are recognized during the instantiation and execution of the conversations.

In order to have a comprehensive view of the organizational setting in which a group cooperates, we will have two kinds of conversation (Winograd & Flores, 1986): conversations for action (CfA), and conversations for possibility (CfP). They negotiate commitments that contribute to the definition of the work arrangements: the former in relation to actions to be performed (commitments to do) the latter in relation to ‘new possibilities for the future’ (commitments to be). The structures of the two types of conversation are shown in Fig. 3 as state transition diagrams. These diagrams represent the successive states an involved commitment goes through during its life cycle. Each conversation is supposed to deal with a single commitment. This is just for the sake of simplicity, as an extension to handle more than one commitment is not difficult in so far as it is possible to recognize which part of the conversation refers to which one of the involved commitments. In any case, each commitment follows its own life cycle.

The diagram of a CfA is somewhat different both from the original one proposed in Winograd and Flores (1986), and from DEMO and the transaction process model (TPM) (Dietz, 1994b,a; van Reijswoud, 1996). First of all, the nodes in our model are interpreted as commitment states, not as states of the conversation as in other models. This allows for a unified model of conversations starting with either a request, or an offer and, by consequence, showing different roles of the two interlocutors who perform successive language acts. In other words, here, we are not accounting for a detailed representation of the conversation in terms of a ‘dance’ between the interlocutors. This abstraction is motivated by the goal of capturing pragmatic effects of language acts on commitment states. It provides sufficient information to account for the modifications of the context. The consequent disembodiment of the conversation from the conversing people, however, creates a problem of how to connect language acts to the state changes of the related commitments.<sup>1</sup> This is the role of what we call communicative events, which is discussed below.

Fig. 3. Commitment life cycle. Circles represent commitment states, horizontal bars communicative events. An arrow connecting two states represents a state transition triggered by the communicative events that appears in the label of the horizontal bar. In both diagrams, the state under discussion is the initial one.

The diagram of a CfP has no counterpart in preceding studies. It has been derived from an informal, although very inspiring, description provided by Winograd and Flores (1986): “% we also need to incorporate the most essential responsibilities of managers: to be open, to listen, and to be the authority regarding what activities and commitments the network will deal with. This can be characterized as participation in ‘conversations for possibilities’ that opens new backgrounds for the conversations for actions”. The new backgrounds are generated by declarations about some aspects of the organizational setting where commitments to do will be fulfilled. Hence, the structure of a CfP mimics the one for a CfA as far as the initial negotiation is concerned, and ends with a declaration stating a new organizational fact, or with failure. Being oriented to the definition of a new background for future action, the following phases of the ‘transaction’ are not needed. The agreed upon commitment to be is fulfilled through the language acts it makes possible to execute in the future. This issue will be further discussed in Section 4.3.

As shown in Fig. 1, actors interact with the communication model (or in more realistic terms, with the application implementing it) through ‘language actions’. The language-action literature proposes different taxonomies of these actions, e.g., those of Searle (1975) or Habermas (1991). In any case it presupposes that actors explicitly declare their ‘intentions’. The literature criticizing this approach (Bowers, Churcher, & Roberts, 1988; Bannon & Kuutti, 1996), and more specifically the Coordinator as the reference technology adopting it, argues how the explicit declaration can become problematic. Since the mechanisms proposed by our computational model are based on commitment states (and not on the conversation states) we introduce the notion of communicative event to denote sets of language actions irrespective of the adopted taxonomy. Two language actions belong to the same set if they induce the same state transformation on the involved commitment. Hence, in order to define these sets, the previous state, the language action and the resulting state have to be considered. So, for example, a counter-offer can belong to three sets as it can take place when the involved commitment is in the state under-discussion, taken and done, although in all cases the resulting state is under-discussion. In other words, the only ‘intention’ the computational model is interested in is about negotiations and deletions (in the various situations) as well as approval and declaration.

If, for any reasons a ‘finer intention’ is desired (e.g., one believes that the users can feel more comfortable with it, or that some additional functionality of the system can use it in some way) then this issue is left to the implementation of the user interface. The latter can adopt different techniques to solve the problem: from explicit language actions, through more user-oriented cues, up to the use of some implicit group conventions. In any case, the duty of recognizing the ‘used intentions’ from the ‘real ones’ is part of the engine that has to be customized accordingly. For example, in the CHAOS prototype, these intentions coincide: that is, its user interface shows cues capturing exactly the mentioned communicative events.

## 4.2. The operational context

The propositional content of CfA describes the commitments to do under negotiation. Hence, the propositional content is constituted of pieces of knowledge related to the involved actions, actors and resources together with time attributes and constraints. Relevant information also involves causal relations among the actions (possibly in relation to the time constraints), the competition over resources, and the like. As far as actions are concerned, their representation in the proposed computational model follows a traditional representation approach based on pairs: attribute–value-. Resources can be modeled similarly. How actors are modeled will be described in Section 4.3. We focus here on some aspects of action and resource modeling which is specific to our approach. A more detailed description of actions and resources is beyond our scope since we are not aiming at systematic taxonomies.

As commitments to do contain references to actions that have to be performed in order to fulfil them, and since actions create and modify the work setting, it is useful to characterize actions on the basis of how their execution modifies the properties of the involved entities. The idea to explicitly describe these changes is also present in SAMPO (Lyytinen & Lehtinen, 1984) where a frame-based representation is used. Since the goals of SAMPO are broader than ours, its way of modeling actions and involved entities is much more articulated and formalized than in our approach. However, since both modeling approaches are conceptually compatible, they could be integrated if required by the needs of the modeling effort. For example, this is the case when communication and information management applications have to be deeply integrated.

![](/api/attachments/CWYU8HER/fulltext/images/d83b72ac7d766624ac566ca43af55ec158ac865b9566d97b6ccc002c78742f05.jpg)  
Fig. 4. The main concepts handled by the conversation manager and their relationships.

In our approach, the characterization of actions is based on the roles entities play like:

 input, i.e., the entity(s) on which the action is performed (e.g. remove a file);

 output, i.e., the entity(s) resulting from the execution of the action (e.g. write a letter);

 resources, i.e., the entity(s) used to perform the action (e.g. write a letter with a blue pen).

Hence, the life cycle of an entity can be described through states describing the different degree of existence of the entity. This notion (Hirst, 1989) allows the model to represent any entity that can become an object of a commitment, irrespective of its physical existence: in fact, the existence we are interested in is just existence in the language. Accordingly, the following five states aptly describe how entities are ‘created’ through language actions:

 E1 denotes an entity that exists in a world ‘under negotiation’. Entities in this state do not exist in a physical world but they have some characteristics that are recognized by one or more members of the group.

 E2 denotes an entity that could become actual in the future. This means that one or more members of the group have accepted a commitment that implicates the creation of the entity. Though the entity has no ‘actuality’, it is expected that it will reach it.

 E3 denotes an object that exists in the present real world. This means that the entity has reached a physical or institutional existence.

 E4 denotes an object that is expected to no longer exist in the future. This means that one or more members of the group have accepted a commitment that implies the destruction of the entity.

 E5 denotes entities existing in a past world. For example, if a file has been canceled, it no longer exists in the world, but as it existed in the past, people can still refer to it.

Actions are grouped in classes whose elements share the way in which they modify the state of the input and the output entities. For example:

 The class creation collects the actions whose execution changes the output from E2 (existence in the future) to E3 (existence in the real world), for instance write a paper.

 The class destruction collects the actions whose input passes from E3 to E5 (existence in the past), for instance cancel a file.

 Another class (called usage) collects the actions whose input is in E3 both before and after the action execution, for instance drive a car.

 The class modification contains actions that ‘destroy’ the object which they are performed on to reproduce a new version of it. They can be defined as actions having the input which passes from E3 to E5 (as in the destruction class) and the output from E2 to E3 (as in the creation one). An example of action belonging to this class is correct a program.

 The class production contains actions whose input is not destroyed: it is at the E3 level both before and after the action execution. As above, the output passes from E2 to E3. An example of action belonging to this class is document a program (producing a software document as output), as illustrated in Fig. 5.

In all action classes, entities acting as resources are characterized, among other obvious attributes, also in terms of a state transition associated with them: E3 E3 (reusable) and E3 E5 (consumable). In the example of Fig. 5, for instance, a documentation standard is used as a resource. The transition E3 E3 is associated with this resource: this means that the resource is not affected (for what is concerning its existence) by the action. Resources must be at level E3 before the execution of the action.

Fig. 6 summarizes the main concepts connected to the operational context and their relationships. Operational entities are characterized by their existence state. The existence state is transformed by action performed on the entities according to the transformation of the existence state characteristic of the particular action’s class.

## 4.3. The organizational context

The propositional content of CfP describes the commitments to be under negotiation. Unlike CfA, the situation with CfP is problematic since there is a substantial lack of theoretical investigation of these conversations, their possible propositional contents, and what characterizes the related commitments. All of them are about the ‘creation’ of entities referring to the organizational setting, where commitments to do are fulfilled. The literature on organizations proposes different ways to represent organizational entities. To our knowledge, however, the ways in which organizations are described in this literature is difficult to combine with the language-action approach. DEMO is an exception, but adopts a perspective that is restricted to CfA (van Reijswoud et al., 1999). In other words, the DEMO approach treats as ‘in the background’ what here is called organizational entities and their transformations. We tried to overcome the lack of results by the following strategy. First of all, we define a minimal lexicon to speak about an organization. Minimality was sought in order to avoid becoming biased in pre-understanding what an organization is. Secondly, we derived a tentative classification of the modifications of the organizational context and defined the structure of the propositional content expressing them. The most interesting case is when the commitment is about the role that an actor commits to play in the future. By consequence, the last step is focused on the concept of role and its definition. That is, a role is created through language and is played within language.

## 4.3.1. A minimal lexicon

The minimal lexicon contains a few basic concepts: area, activity, task and role. An area is an organizational unit provided with a basic role, called manager, which has the responsibility for structuring the area into sub-areas, or activities. An activity is a unit of work linked to one or more areas, where one or more people are employed (employee is the second basic role) to perform pertinent tasks<sup>2</sup>. Following this lexicon, the project leader of X is the manager of the area called Project X. This area can encompass different sub-areas, for example associated with different tracks of the project. A manager is assigned to each sub-area. Sub-areas can be structured into activities such as design, testing etc. to which different people (employees) are allocated. The above definitions propose an oversimplified view of an organization: for example, matrix or team based organizations would require an adaptation of the above lexicon. The present simplification is motivated by the fact that the attempt to read an organization, and changes in its structure, in the light of the languageaction perspective, is new and challenging. Once the approach is consolidated in the simplified lexicon, extensions can be introduced with greater awareness of the implications which they can have on the overall coherence of the model.

The lexicon has to be related to conversations. First of all, in any conversation (for possibility and for action) the interlocutors converse by playing a (set of) roles. This is not to say that actors cannot perform ‘free’ conversations. The point is that their pragmatic effect depends on the role of the involved interlocutors. Typical is the example of a declaration: ‘You are married’, which requires to be spoken by an appropriate authority in a certain setting in order to be effective.

Moreover, the lexicon has to be placed in relation to the commitments to do. When an actor (who is potentially) committed to executing an action converses within a CfA as an employee of a particular activity, then the action related to the negotiated commitment belongs to a task in that activity. For example, the activity ‘prepare budget’ is associated with the area ‘administration’ whose manager is the ‘secretary’: the latter can ask an employee of the ‘administration’ to perform the task ‘collect foreseen expenses’ from all organization areas. This task can involve different actions to which different actors can commit in CfA.

The use of these concepts allows one to represent a well-known triple: what (activity), how (task and action), and the why constituting — from the organizational perspective — the fulfilment of commitments. Different granularity of tasks and actions allows one to take into account the notion of a (predefined) flow of work. To simplify the argument, we assume in the following that each task is associated with a single activity, and the latter, in turn, with a single area.

## 4.3.2. About commitments ‘to be’

The life cycle of a commitment to be shown in Fig. 3 requires some additional considerations. Recall that a commitment to be concerns the manipulation of organizational entities. Which way can this be accomplished? Since at the present stage it is difficult to give an exhaustive answer, we will try to restrict the scope of the question to a framework that was identified by the above organizational lexicon.

In this framework a new organizational context can be constructed by actions that:

 create/modify/cancel an area/sub-area, an activity/task, and finally a role;

 authorize the execution of a (language) action.

These actions shape the propositional content of CfP. In these conversations the final agreement is basically a declaration that the action at hand is ‘done’ and its effects are immediate, as explained below.

In the case of manipulating an area/sub-area, or an activity/task, the authority expressing the declarations is expected to be the area manager related to the new entity. How this area is specified depends on the specific organization. However, more importantly, in these two cases the commitment life cycle can collapse into a single, final declaration. The authorized manager can fulfil the commitment to be associated to his/her role just by doing the declaration, without any negotiation. The manager can ‘listen’ and ‘be open’ to the other actors by making the declaration after a request from others or after his/her offer to others of this new possibility. This is a special way to look at the negotiation phase.

The authorization of a (language) action is another type of CfP as it allows, or forbids, the execution of future actions: it starts with a request to approve the pertinent action and ends either by a declaration, or a cancellation. No further action is expected. For example, the declarations considered in the previous point can require an approval which therefore becomes part of the related conversation.

The last way to create a new background is exactly what is needed to express this constraint. In fact, the necessity to obtain an authorization is an additional specification of the basic role manager, that can be desirable if the role is assumed by a specific actor and may not be necessary in the case of a different actor. Hence, in this case the problem to be solved is the specification of how to define a role.

Before doing this, it is worth noting that organizational entities (areas, activities and roles) may have a different degree of existence as in the case of operational entities involved in commitments to do. Since the fulfilment of a commitment to be does not imply any action to be approved within the conversation negotiating it, the set of possible states reduces to E1, E3 and E5.

## 4.3.3. The definition of a role

A quick overview of the notion of role in different frameworks (Conrath, De Antonellis, & Simone, 1988; COSMOS, 1989; Pankoke-Babatz, 1989; Prinz, 1993), shows that a role is generally represented as a property to which a list of actors are associated. This association allows one to check if an actor can be assigned to tasks that prescribe this role as a constraint to their execution. In frameworks more oriented to the management of non-human resources, roles are used to specify sophisticated access rights for their manipulation: e.g., Shen and Dewan (1992). In both cases, roles are viewed as stereotypes specifying a set of rights and duties. We think that this approach is a too strong simplification, as it basically denies the fact that roles, as much as actions, are a matter of negotiation among the involved actors. What a stereotype can specify (for example, manager, secretary, senior/junior analyst, student, professor) is, in the practice of an organization, tuned to the characteristics (for example, experience, reliability, accuracy) of the actor candidate to play this role. Since the latter change in the course of action, the tuning process can be iterated.

In line with Habermas’ argumentation we like to see role definition as an outcome of a negotiation: that is, as a contract between two subjects that is tailored to them and can be modified (again through negotiation) at any moment. These contracts can contain recurrent parts, that we call patterns, which serve just to simplify the communication, or to serve as starting points for a negotiation. The proposed notion of the manager and employee are patterns in the above sense. The default rules associated with them specify that a manager can make declarations whose scope of effectiveness is delimited by the area, while an employee cannot refuse a request of doing something in relation to this activity. For example, the manager of the area Project X can create a sub-area, say ‘Research Track X’, together with its manager. As for the second case, a professor Y, who can be seen as an employee in the activity called Teaching Course W cannot decline a request from a student (regularly subscribing to this course) to have the related examination. The professor can just negotiate or impose the date and the time but s/he cannot say: ‘forget’, or ‘ask professor Z’ unless the latter agrees (or is committed by his/her specific role) to help Y in accomplishing his/her duties. The above examples give a flavor of what we are looking for in our effort to improve the concept of role.

The proposed computational model allows one to define an unlimited collection of roles according to the nature of the context, and the culture in which the organization (or part of it) and its members are embedded by expressing the rules that specialize the two basic patterns. Hence, our effort is devoted to the definition of a grammar to express these rules and therefore to formalize the propositional content of CfP. The grammar is described below in Backus-Naur form where + denotes ‘at least one occurrence of’, \* denotes ‘any number of occurrences of’ and / denotes alternative generation paths. Non-terminals are enclosed in  - brackets. A disclaimer is in order: the following grammar is an example of a possible way to define roles in the spirit of the language-action approach. It is an initial attempt whose main value is to pinpoint a possible way to address the problem. Yet, a deeper investigation is required, both at the theoretical and empirical levels, to reach an adequate and comprehensive formulation.

In our model, an actor is defined by his/her personal data and the roles s/he is assigned to:

<div class="mineru-algorithm" style="white-space: pre-wrap; font-family:monospace;">
$\langle$ actor $\rangle$ ::= $\langle$ personal data $\rangle$; $\langle$ role $\rangle^{+}$
</div>

A role is defined by its scope and the related set of rules (policy) that apply to the pertinent type of conversations (terminal symbols are indicated by using a different font):

```txt
<role>::=<role name>:<specification>
<specification>::=manager of <unit>=<policy-CfP>/
employee in <activity>=<policy-CfA>
<unit>::=<area>/sub-area of <area>
```

As roles are played within the language, their specification is given in terms of constraints on the space of possibilities within CfA and CfP s/he is involved in. These constraints can form a neat reduction of possibility (cannot), or require an additional behavior (ask, notify). Constraints can be strengthened or weakened by making explicit the consequences of their violation: the compulsory mode expresses that a sanction can eventually occur, the recommended mode expresses that a reproof can eventually occur. If the constraint is about asking for an authorization, then the interlocutors can just be people playing the role of manager. Here everybody can be the target of a notification.

```txt
<policy-CfP>::={<rule-CfP>*, cannot <declaration-type>*}
<rule-CfP>::=<declaration-type> <mode> <constraint-CfP>
<declaration-type>::=declare <action-CfP> <unit>/declare <action-CfP> <activity>
/authorize <communicative-events>
<action-CfP>::=create/modify/cancel
<communicative-events>::="any communicative event of a CfA or CfP"
<mode>::=compulsory/recommended
<constraint-CfP>::=ask for authorization <man-group>/notify <group>
<man-group>::=[manager of <unit)]+
<group>::=<any-role>+
```

Let us suppose that the manager of the area ‘Project X’ wants to appoint actor GGG as manager of the sub-area ‘Research Track H’. When assigning the role, s/he can associate to the role policies that, for instance, take into consideration the degree of experience of GGG and his/her reliability. Later on, these policies can be relaxed or made stricter according to the evolution of the situation. Examples of rules<sup>3</sup> that could apply in this case are:

You cannot declare a sub-area.

When you cancel a sub-area, you must ask for authorization from the manager of Project X and send an acknowledgement to XY and to the manager of area Z.

With the first rule, the manager of Project X limits the freedom of GGG when restructuring the area s/he is managing. With the second rule, the manager wants to keep a strict control over some crucial actions, thus giving visibility of actions also to other people. The application of the rule is compulsory (must).

Later on, when GGG has acquired more experience, the rules could be relaxed as follows:

When you declare a sub-area you must ask for authorization from the manager of Project X.

When you cancel a sub-area, you must send an acknowledgement to the manager of Project X, to XY and to the manager of area Z.

Policies for CfA are defined in a similar way, with two differences. The first is about the specification of the communicative events to which the constraint applies. In this case, one can tailor the specification in relation to the interlocutors and the task the involved action (possibly) belongs to. The second difference is about who can authorize the communicative event under concern: in this case, both managers and employees can be involved.

```txt
<policy-CfA>::={<rule-CfA>}*, cannot <comm.events-CfA>*}
<rule-CfA>::=<comm.events-CfA> <mode> <constraint-CfA>+
<comm.events-CfA>::=negotiation of <action> [<task>] <interlocutors>/ ...
    'see diagram of Fig. 3'
<interlocutors>::=[employee of <activity>]+
<constraint-CfA>::=ask for authorization <group>/notify <group>
```

For example, GGG can have among the rules that apply to his/her role of manager of the sub-area ‘Research Track H’, some rules indicating his/her expected behaviour in various conversations.

When you negotiate an action ‘write a report’ of task ‘Annual Documentation with an employee employed outside the activities belonging to your subarea, you must ask for authorization from the manager of Area XX.

When you approve an action ‘document a program’ with an employee employed in one of the activities belonging to your sub-area, you should send an acknowledgement to the manager of Project X and to XY.

You cannot negotiate an action ‘document a program’ with an employee employed outside Project X.

After the detailed description of the operational and organizational contexts, the next section is devoted to the description of how the computational model accounts for the partial, possibly contradictory, views that actors have of these contexts.

## 5. Modeling partial views of the world

Linguistic opacity is due to actors’ different linguistic experiences within a group. It generates partial and contradictory views of the ‘world’ in which communication happens.

In order to maintain the knowledge about the context, the model proposes a sort of memory where the entities selected according to Section 4 can be recorded. The reference to a memory, and to its dynamic maintenance, is not casual as the notion of context is related to the issue of knowledge management in terms of organizational memory and learning. Of course, our framework is a sub-case of this much broader topic as it specifically concerns the case where the interaction among people is achieved through conversations mentioning explicit knowledge. Nevertheless, it shares almost the same problems, and specifically the basic requirement put in evidence by Bannon and Kuutti (1996) to avoid reducing the memory to a bare repository and to conceive it as a support for ‘remembering’, for re-constructing meanings in a ‘world’.

The above requirement can be partially met by considering that linguistic action has two main consequences: first of all, it modifies the world; secondly, it affects the sharing of knowledge about this world among the group members. Since the modified sharing is part of the world’s modifications too, we start by describing how the different degrees of knowledge sharing are represented in the proposed memory. This is the first way to answer the above requirement. The second way is to define how the modifications of the world are conveyed into the memory: this is the subject of the next section.

The memory is defined as the set of member’s partial views of the background world. To this aim we use the concept of user model (UM) (Kobsa & Wahlster, 1989). Recently, user modeling has become a central issue in the development of applications that overcome the rigidity of stereotypes in the design of the interaction between human and computer. In our approach, this notion is used to record the individual knowledge actors acquire interacting with each other through a technological artifact.

For all actors, their individual UM contains the following types of knowledge:

 Private knowledge (PK ), i.e., the knowledge a user i acquires by acting within the group without any communication with other members. For example, let us suppose that Ann (A) took on the responsibility of writing a report on a project and that she has done it. As long as she does not communicate to anybody that she has written the report, she will be the only person aware of the report’s physical existence and final characteristics. Hence, this fact belongs to her private knowledge $( \mathrm { P K } _ { \mathrm { A } } )$

Interactions with other actors can transform private knowledge into a shared one in two ways: consciously or unconsciously.

Consciously shared knowledge $( \mathrm { C S K } _ { i j } )$ , i.e., the knowledge actors i and j agree upon during conversations. For example, if Ann tells Bob (B) the location of a particular document, then this information enters in their consciously shared knowledge $( \mathrm { C S K } _ { \mathrm { A B } } )$ .The symmetry of $\mathrm { C S K } _ { i j }$ with respect to actors i and j can be broken when they acquire (within conversations between one or both of them and other actors) a piece of knowledge $\mathbf { X ^ { \prime } }$ that is inconsistent with some knowledge X belonging to $\mathrm { C S K } _ { i j }$ . In this case X is marked in the $\mathrm { C S K } _ { i j }$ to denote that i and j can use $\mathbf { X }$ in an asymmetric way. Referring to the previous example, if the location of the object has been changed and Ann acquires this information conversing with Carl (C), Ann will share with Bob and with Carl two different values for an attribute of the same object. The information about the location is marked in the $\mathrm { C S K } _ { \mathrm { A B } } .$ , reflecting that it will probably be considered false by Ann from now on, while it is still true for Bob. The unmarked knowledge is the current knowledge of the involved actors, though in the group more relevant knowledge might exist.

 Shared knowledge $( \mathrm { S K } _ { i j } )$ , i.e., the knowledge an actor i shares with a user j without being aware of sharing. For example, in a conversation with Bob, Ann can acquire the same knowledge K that Carl acquired by conversing with Bob. K is in the $\mathrm { C S K } _ { \mathrm { A B } }$ and $\mathrm { C S K } _ { \mathrm { C B } }$ but not in $\mathrm { C S K } _ { \mathrm { A C } }$ , though they both possess the same knowledge. A user i may share with different individuals different, possibly contradictory views of the same object. Then, the more recent view of i and j is considered when the $\mathrm { S K } _ { i j }$ is computed, since this view is supposed to be the more reliable one. $\mathrm { S K } _ { i j }$ is initialized by the public domain knowledge mentioned in Section 3. In addition, the stereotypical knowledge is assumed as shared between any two interlocutors belonging to the same stereotype. If only one user i belongs to a given stereotype, then the associated knowledge is recorded as $\mathrm { P K } _ { i }$

We call individual knowledge (IK ) the collection of all types of knowledge expressing the partial view an actor i has of the world.

We conclude this section by emphasizing that the proposed types of knowledge are not aimed at explaining how people organize their ‘memory’. Rather they aim at supporting the identification of relationships between communicative events and modifications of the memory itself in view of the design of a supportive technology. In this respect, the above categorization has a very circumscribed role. It is not unnatural to think, however, that in trying to interpret ambiguous messages people refer to what they actually share, or presume to share, consciously or unconsciously, with their interlocutors. In our opinion, technology helping meta-information to be remembered and reconstructed can provide communicating actors with support that makes sense to them.

## 6. Linking communication events to world modifications

The last feature of the communication model captures the basic idea of the language-action approach: ‘how to do things with words’ (Austin, 1962), i.e., how communication events modify the world and affect actors’ partial views of it.

Communication events are generated by the intertwined execution of conversations for action and for possibility. These two types of conversations involve different linguistic entities in relation to the commitments they negotiate but at the same time show some states of the commitments, which can be considered to have a similar pragmatic effect. For this reason, the ‘rules’ expressing the link between events and modifications are presented in a unified way. Unless otherwise specified, in the following a commitment refers to both commitments to do and to be. In the same way, an entity refers to any linguistic entity, i.e., to both operational and organizational entities. Since within conversations entity instances are mentioned, the term ‘entity’ actually refers to the instance. The rules are based on the communicative events and their connections to the state transitions of commitments (see Fig. 3). The terms doer and asker are used to denote who performs the action and who will eventually approve it, respectively.

When a conversation starts, the related commitment is in the state under discussion. When a commitment is under discussion the involved entities refer to the individual knowledge of the two interlocutors. In particular, each entity has a state of existence that can be different in the individual knowledge as well as the values of some of their attributes. During the discussion, different standpoints can be clarified which increases sharing of knowledge. For example, if the action characterizing the commitment to do under discussion presupposes an input and the ‘hearer’ does not know about its existence, she can become aware of it — possibly after an exchange of utterances. The information acquired in this way is recorded in the knowledge consciously shared by the two interlocutors. The negotiation does not change the level of existence of the objects, but new objects can be mentioned (consider, for example, when the commitment is about writing a new paper). In this case, the new objects (at state E1) are stored locally to the conversation.

A failure moves a commitment from the state under discussion to the state canceled. Canceling a commitment does not alter the sharing of information achieved during negotiation nor the state of the involved entities. So, for example, if two interlocutors have acquired knowledge about an available resource (e.g., a portable to write a paper) while negotiating a commitment, then they will continue to share this knowledge independently of the failure of the commitment, and they can use it in other conversations.

An agreement makes a commitment to do reach the state taken. The changes entities pass are determined not only by the commitment negotiation steps, but also by the kind of action. Consider the action classes mentioned in Section 4.2. If the commitment is about to create one or more entities being in state E1, when the commitment is taken the entities reach state E2. Moreover, information about all the involved entities that has been recorded during the negotiation locally shifts to the consciously shared knowledge. The actions belonging to the class ‘usage’ do not modify the state of the entities on which they have to be performed, in contrast, for actions belonging to the classes ‘modification’ and ‘production’ it is necessary to record in $\mathrm { C S K } _ { \mathrm { A D } }$ a new (output) object in state E2. In the case of ‘modification’ a new entity reflects changes that will occur in the input, while in the case of ‘production’ it represents the entity resulting from the action. Finally, an action of the class ‘destruction’ modifies the state from E3 to E4. For example, if the commitment is about writing a paper (class creation), a new object (recording the characteristics of the paper as discussed during the negotiation) is recorded in the knowledge that is consciously shared by the interlocutors. This object is at level E2, implying that a commitment for its creation has been taken.

An agreement makes a commitment to be reach the state declared. The mentioned organizational entities come immediately to existence: the new area, the new task, the new role and so on reach state E3. The new entities also modify the organizational context because their existence affects the behavior of other entities, e.g., in the case of a role assigned to an actor. The rules defining it immediately become effective. For example, as soon as an agreement is reached for the definition of a new area, actors can be employed in the area and can commit themselves to the related activities.

The agreement negotiation of a CfA has the effect of bringing back the commitment to the initial state under discussion: hence, the above considerations about this state apply again.

The next transitions concern the last part of the ‘transaction’ according to the state transition diagram of CfA.

A deletion moves a commitment to do from the state taken to the state canceled. The entities that have been taken to the state E2 are backtracked to E1 (e.g., the above-mentioned paper is recorded as something people have been talking about, but is not expected to appear in the future). If the action to perform was a modification or a production, the entity that has been recorded as ‘appearing in the future’ (E2) is canceled. Canceling the commitment to do does not destroy the objects that have possibly been produced in the meanwhile, and the sharing of information concerning its conditions of satisfaction (e.g., about the resources, as mentioned above).

Performing action makes a commitment to do reach the state done. The state transformations characterizing the action are applied and recorded in the private knowledge of the doer. For example, if the action was about to destroy an entity, e.g., the canceling of a file, the entity reaches state E5. The resulting information stays there until the doer decides to use it in communicating with someone else. A typical case is when the doer asks for the approval of the asker. Notice that the approval is just a request to acknowledge whether the action implies an unrecoverable destruction.

An approval makes a commitment to do move from the state done to the state concluded. The involved knowledge moves from the private knowledge of the doer $\mathrm { ( P K _ { D } ) }$ to the one shared with the asker $( \mathrm { C S K _ { A D } ) }$ , together with a link to the commitment from which it has arisen. If the asker does not approve, the commitment is rediscussed: approval negotiation makes the commitment to do again reach the state under discussion. Objects that have been created during the conversation are recorded locally to the conversation itself.

The downstream of information represented in Fig. 1 is now fully described. The goal of a computational model is to support the design of a technology providing communicating actors with functionalities that allow them to communicate more effectively. This is the only way to verify its validity. With this aim the model has been adopted in CHAOS, a prototype that aims to demonstrate the feasibility of the concept.

## 7. Reducing linguistic opacity

In CHAOS information on the organizational and operational contexts provides functionality that reduces linguistic opacity. This functionality supports CHAOS users in determining the most appropriate reference context in which to formulate and interpret language acts. Let us consider the following scenario. A user realizes that she has a pending commitment and that it is time to handle it. A first necessity is to reconstruct the genesis of the commitment with the information sensible at the time of its definition. By accessing this knowledge, the user gets the impression that some conditions have changed, for example someone has left the organization, or is playing a new role. Before starting a new stage of negotiation she would need to have more updated information on the current context. The proposed mechanisms support her in different ways according to her particular situation. If she wants to have a view of what she shares with her interlocutors (e.g., during the definition of a commitment), she can access knowledge consciously shared (CSK) with the specific user she wants to interact with. Alternatively, she could have a view of her cognitive state, and how it has evolved through communicative processes and working activities. To this end she can look at her individual knowledge where CHAOS systematically maintains links between the stored information and the communicative events that generated it.

Finally, the user could obtain information about the context that is not directly under her organizational responsibility, or no longer updated in her individual knowledge because it was not involved in her recent conversations. In this case, CHAOS allows her to look at the knowledge that is held by the person responsible for that specific information (e.g., about resources, actors, and so on) in order to obtain an ‘official’ picture of the context. This is done by a combined use of information contained in the individual knowledge (IK) and information about role assignments in the organizational context.

In addition, CHAOS uses information contained in their partial views in order to implement notification mechanisms that will increase users’ awareness of changes in the organizational and operational contexts. These mechanisms are activated during the course of conversations when the system guesses that some information could be of interest for some people not directly involved in the specific conversation. Again, this guess is based on the role of the group members, on the knowledge of the conversations they are or have been recently involved in, and on the contents of the commitments these conversations are about. In doing so, special attention is paid to make these notifications as little intrusive as possible. This is achieved by making the information visible just when it is really needed. For example, this happens when a user mentions an entity in a conversation of which s/he has an obsolete view (Simone & Divitini, 1999).

In summary, CHAOS increases knowledge sharing among its users both by actively making them aware of possible contradictions, and by making available updated knowledge. This helps users in aligning their partial and contradictory views of the contexts when they are conversing, since it reduces the linguistic opacity generated by their possibly contradictory communication experience. These aspects incorporate part of what is called ‘success of speech-acts and negotiating commitments’ in Auramaki and Lyytinen (1996), when the source of the failure originates from mutual misunderstanding.

Technical feasibility does not imply that the mechanisms are suitable to all users. Although there is no experience of using the CHAOS prototype, its development was conducted by taking the development team as a real example of an ‘open system’ which the prototype could support. In fact, since the project lasted over several years, the team changed its composition, was open to receive the individual experience its members gained outside the team, and had strong requirements in keeping different views of the operational and organizational contexts according to the various classes of project members together with their different roles. The concepts of the model were used as a lexicon by which the development activities and the related organization structure were ‘spoken about’. From this continuous exercise, examples of ‘linguistic opacity’ were recognized during the direct communication among people and they served as a source of inspiration for the definition of the above functionalities. The prototype was not used as the communication support since it was always too unstable. However, it reached a maturity that allowed us to build demos to be presented for users. Specifically, CHAOS was selected by the Italian national authority that funded the project as one of the prototypes to be demonstrated to people coming from public administration. This class of users was positively impressed by the way in which the organization structure was modeled. Specifically, they appreciated the capability of the approach to account for fine-grained definition of roles in contrast to the more bureaucratic definitions they were used to. They saw themselves able to negotiate the actual content of formal roles. More generally, they liked the idea that a technology could monitor their communication not for the sake of control, but for the sake of helping people to feel more comfortable with the communication and improve it. In this respect, they appreciated the support given to cooperating actors by situating their actions at different points in time during the commitment life cycle. This happened in the most appropriate reference context on the basis of the identified classes of knowledge.

## 8. Summary and conclusions

We have presented a (computational) communication model based on the language-action approach that can reduce linguistic opacity. In particular, the focus is on mechanisms that support the maintenance of contexts against which interpretations of conversations take place. In the proposed model two types of conversations are recognized: conversation for action and conversation for possibility. These are used for negotiating commitments that shape work arrangements in relation to actions to be performed (the operational context), and in relation to an organizational space of possibility for future actions (the organizational context). The main entities related to these contexts were analyzed with an emphasis on their evolution. In order to take into account partial views that different members have of these contexts, the notion of a user model was introduced. A user model reflects the fact that different people are involved in different conversations, and participate in groups with varying degrees of involvement. Each person’s knowledge is therefore partial and not always ‘aligned’ to other members’ knowledge. Alignments and contradictions are essential as they allow people to place their communication and action in a context where communication receives meaning and possible misunderstandings can be prevented, or solved. The proposed computational model takes into account the dynamics of knowledge creation and sharing. It describes how conversations can modify the operational and organizational context as well as change the partial views of different actors.

We believe that the proposed model can play a positive role in supporting communications, and also in the development of the organizational memory that can be constructed from them. In accordance with Bannon and Kuutti (1996) we believe that an organizational memory cannot be considered as a passive repository of information. Passive recording of information will result in an informational overload that is of no use for the organization. In order to have something that can be effectively used it is essential to better understand processes that led to the creation of different pieces of information, and the ways information is spread. The proposed model makes a small step in this direction.

The approach recognizes limited capabilities of technology, which cannot fully incorporate ways in which human beings learn, and maintain their memory. Technology can, however, provide support for learning by recording facts within the context in which they were produced, and by allowing a flexible reading of what has been recorded so far to support continuous rediscovery. Moreover, technology can trigger learning processes by increasing awareness of relevant events. Within a group it is not necessary (or desirable) that all the people share the same knowledge. It is enough to reach a ‘minimal’ consensus. The knowledge that each participant must share depends deeply on her role, her responsibility, and her pre-understanding. Communication tends to increase the knowledge sharing. Moreover, communications can be activated as reactions to misunderstandings, ambiguities, and breakdowns. In these cases, people engage in communications in order to solve them, and to make possible future action. From this perspective, organizational memory provides a means to reduce communicative overhead. The computational model provides conceptual tools for developing an organizational memory that helps place information in appropriate contexts for cooperating people. The most useful knowledge supporting cooperation is not always the knowledge reflecting the most updated knowledge of the organization. A communication process involving intertwined conversations can properly contextualize a new linguistic action, if the contexts in which the process was suspended are available together with the changes which occurred in the meantime (Simone & Divitini, 1999). The same holds when past experience about a communication process has to be retrieved and reinterpreted in the current situation. This is mainly what organizational memory is about. Moreover, the evolution of the individual knowledge of each actor can be used to organize and present information at different levels of abstraction, so that it is tailored to the current situation and the actor’s needs.

This points to one of the possible evolutions in the current model, namely the design of a more effective and usable user interface. This aspect becomes even more relevant if another limit is to be overcome. The organizational memory is made of pieces of information describing levels of knowledge sharing. This is a very primitive step toward a more comprehensive solution that takes into account groups and subgroups as owners of shared knowledge. The real difficulty of this generalization is the identification of the mechanisms that take into account the evolution of this more aggregated form of sharing. Conversations as understood by the current languageaction perspective are too weak. Multi-party conversations can be a small generalization. More generally, what is needed is a deeper understanding of the interplay between communications and the generation of a group model, a natural generalization of the user model proposed in the paper. This generalization is a preliminary step toward a smooth integration of communication support with other tools supporting actor cooperation, i.e., shared information spaces and distributed workflows.
