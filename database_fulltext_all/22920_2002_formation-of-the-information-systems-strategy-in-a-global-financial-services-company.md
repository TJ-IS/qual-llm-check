# Formation of the information systems strategy in a global financial services company

S.C.A. Peters <sup>a,\*</sup>, M.S.H. Heng <sup>b</sup>, R. Vet <sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Faculty of Economics, Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, De Boelelaan 1105, 1051 HV Amsterdam, The Netherlands

<sup>b</sup> School of Accounting and Information Systems, Faculty of Business and Enterprise, University of South Australia City West Campus, Adelaide, Australia

## Abstract

Over the past few decades, the potential of information systems (IS) to support organizational activities and help them gain competitive advantage has been widely recognized. At the level of strategy, there is a genre of organizational activities dedicated to realizing this potential. Methods known broadly as strategic information planning have been constructed to support such activities in organizations but these methods have not lived up their promises. Meanwhile, the use of evolutionary approaches in the application of IS in organizations have also received some attention. This paper describes and analyzes a case study of the evolution of the IS strategy within LeaseHold NV, a Dutch global leasing company. The study reveals that no IS planning was used. Instead, concepts of organizational evolution are found to be useful in analyzing the development of the IS strategy at LeaseHold.  2002 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information systems strategy; Organizational change; Evolution; Information systems planning methods; Case study research; Longitudinal research

## 1. Background

Over the past few decades, the potential of information systems (IS) in supporting organizational activities and helping them gain competitive advantage has been widely recognized by researchers and the business community. Competencies in the area of IS are becoming increasingly important (Quinn & Paquette, 1990) in business organizations especially those involved in financial services.

However, recognizing the potential of IS is one thing and using it to serve organizational goals is another. It has been claimed that strategic IS planning can help an organization visualize the potential contribution of IS (Lederer & Gardiner, 1992). In the Eighties, the general agreement among the IS business world and researchers seemed to be that an IS strategy should be derived from the business strategy (Beats, 1992; Huff & Chan, 1992; Kovacevic & Majluf, 1993; Powell, 1993; Scott Morton, 1991). The search for this link between business and IS strategy has produced methods known generally as strategic information planning methods. More recently, incremental and evolutionary approaches in the application of IS in organizations have also received some attention (Ciborra & Jelassi, 1994; Galliers, 1993). These approaches are similar to the incremental formation of organizational strategies advocated by Lindblom (1959), Mintzberg (1989) and Quinn and Paquette (1990).

So far, very little research has been conducted to investigate the general validity of these theories and models in the real world of business (Philip, Mawalkar, & Gopalakrishnan, 1995). However, research in organizations where the use of IS has resulted in a competitive edge indicated that the practical use of IS planning methods is minimal (Beats, 1992; Galliers, 1993; Yetton, Johnston, & Craig, 1994). According to Mintzberg (1989), planning models are useful in complementing existing strategies but are not useful for creating new strategies.

## 2. Introduction

The lack of empirical research on strategic IS planning and the idea that planning methods might not work with IS development in organizations suggest the need for more investigation. This paper is an attempt in that direction. It investigates the formation of the IS strategy within a Dutch global lease company, LeaseHold (a fictitious name). Research was conducted in this company for ten years from 1987 to 1996. This longitudinal field study investigated the evolution of the IS strategy within LeaseHold, covering major IS activities in the organization from 1963 to 1996. By conducting interviews, examining organizational documents related to IS strategy and witnessing the development of the organization’s IS in practice, we were able to get a picture of how the IS strategy evolved in the company. This approach, proposed by Pettigrew (1990), aimed to make a thorough study of linkages between changes in business environment, business strategy and IS strategy. This case study examined the influence of the following on the organization as its IS strategy emerged:

 the history of Information Technology (IT) usage in the organization;

 the state of technology available;

 the changes in the competitive environment; and

 the organization’s general approach to business and its operating practices such as its culture, plans and vision.

In this paper, we describe how the IS strategy evolved at LeaseHold. The case study revealed some important phenomena. Firstly, the IS strategy was developed without the use of any IS planning method. Secondly, concepts of evolutionary dynamics of organizations were useful in describing the development of LeaseHold’s IS strategy. Thirdly, we find some support for the notion of co-evolution of the organization and its IS. Fourthly, the study reveals that the variation–selection–retention cycle can be applied in relation to the co-evolution between the organization and its IS. Finally, it shows that management’s vision played an important role in the development of the organization.

This study shows that the approach adopted by LeaseHold might be the success factor in its IS development. However, another possible success factor to consider is the specific entrepreneurial culture of the company.

This paper is organized as follows: the next part — Section 3 — presents an overview of the literature on the use of IS strategy in organizations and the theory of evolutionary dynamics of organizations as well as that of their IS. Section 4 presents the case study of the evolution of the IS strategy at LeaseHold. Based on this study, the match between theory and business practice is discussed in Section 5. The findings and recommendations for further research on this subject is summarized in Section 6.

## 3. Use of IS in organizations

Over the past decade, there had been an evident focal shift in academic literature from the purely administrative or operational role of IS in organizations to a more competitive role (Henderson, Thomas, & Venkatraman, 1992; Luftman, 1996; Venkatraman, 1994). The administrative role relates to the automation of specific administrative functions, the aim being to enhance efficiency. The operational role is an extension of the administrative role and focuses on the capability to automate business processes as opposed to administrative activities alone. During the Eighties, both the research and the business communities began to recognize the potential of IS to improve the competitiveness of organization. This new role for IS identifies it as a source of competitive advantage (Galliers, 1993; Henderson et al., 1992). Cases were reported where the use of IS delivered sustainable competitive advantage. However, this “mythology of the success of IS has begun to pale in the cold light of the Nineties” (Galliers, 1993) and the competitive role of IS in organizations must be viewed with caution.

## 3.1. Strategic IS planning methods

From the late 1970s to the early 1990s, strategic IS planning models that introduce IS into organizations to increase competitive edge, received much attention in academic literature. Beats (1992), van der Poel (1991) and Powell (1993) have described top down strategic planning models as methods to apply IS within organizations. Closely linked to these methods is the Critical Success Factor approach (Rockart,

1984). The theories mentioned above do not consider the influence of the environment on IS strategy. To correct this inadequacy, Henderson et al. (1992), Luftman (1996) and Venkatraman (1994) have developed the Strategic Alignment method to use IS in organizations. This method does recognize the influence of the environment on IS.

While all these theories and studies make the application of strategic IS planning models seem rational and advantageous there is no track record of their effectiveness in application. Galliers (1993) questions the claim that strategic IS planning gives organizations a competitive advantage. Although these gains more or less arise, there are also losses. Firstly, IS applications can be easily duplicated by rival companies who can then gain the same advantages. Secondly, since the IS environment changes very rapidly, whatever advantages gained will be lost quickly. Galliers points out that sustainable competitive advantage is possible only if additional features, desired by customers or created by new IS possibilities, can arise continuously.

Inkpen and Choudhury (1995) argue that in a number of organizations no business strategy can be identified at all and this casts doubt on the usefulness of strategic IS planning approaches, which mostly assume the existence of a business strategy. Pruijm (1990) observes that in almost all of the European case stories where competitive advantage was established through the application of IS, the successful system was an incremental evolution of an existing application. These observations reflect traditional views on evolving business strategies (Lindblom, 1959; Mintzberg, 1989; Quinn & Paquette, 1990). They suggest that the strategic application of IS is more the result of a process of continuous evolution of IS in organizations (Senn, 1997). This evolution is a response to changes in the organization’s environment. To explore this perspective, we will discuss briefly below, current evolutionary theories on organizations and attempts to apply them to IS in organizations.

## 3.2. Evolution theory and organizational development

Several authors (amongst them Dosi, Freeman, Silverberg, & Soethe, 1988; Fombrun, 1993; Ginsberg & Baum, 1993; Nelson & Winter, 1982; Miner, 1993) have attempted to explain organizational development and behavior by applying the evolutionary theory. Basic evolutionary characteristics, drawn from biology and sociology, were used to analyze the development process of organizations. In general, an organization is compared to a ‘species’ in biology, that is, an entity that follows a path of evolution. By applying insights from the evolution theory to organizational studies, certain features of organizational evolution can be discerned.

## 3.3. Path dependency

An organization develops via a path of evolution in order to survive and this path is constructed through a process of continuous change. Every organization has a specific evolutionary path in order to survive. This process can be compared to the phenomenon of natural selection in biology (Dawkins, 1988). One main feature of the paths of evolution is that their past cannot be reversed and they influence, to a certain degree, the future path of the evolution of the organization (Dawkins, 1988; Ginsberg & Baum, 1993; March, 1994; Mintzberg, 1989; Nelson & Winter, 1982). An organization’s past evolutionary path helps it to build a set of procedures, routines and organizational characteristics that form its current context. In this way we can say that past changes shape the current context of organizations (Ayres, 1994). The context determines the types of opportunities, threats and solutions that can be applied within the organization. The current context (Fombrun, 1993) and past changes and characteristics act as guides for the organization in analyzing and responding to changing conditions (Ginsberg & Baum, 1993; Nelson & Winter, 1982). Adaptations that had been successful in the past will be repeated while new solutions are shunned because stakeholders usually fear the unknown. Organizations learn from past experience, from processes such as learning-by-doing as well as learning-by using. All these become important influences in the evolution of organizations (Dosi et al., 1988).

## 3.4. The role of the external environment

Changes within the organization are brought about by the imperative to survive or to exploit the changing external conditions (Dawkins, 1988). These changes eventually lead to organizational evolution. Nelson and Winter (1982) argue that the ability to cope with this imperative determines the relative position of an organization in relation to its competitors. The wish to exploit new possibilities or to survive leads to changes and evolution. Unfortunately, changes do not necessarily lead to a higher success rate since competitors also evolve at the same time. Moreover, organizational evolution does not necessarily mean improvement. In turbulent environments the changes or adaptations of species, and hence also the path of evolution of these species, are said to be accidental, jerky, non-lineal and are aimed at short-term improvement (Ayres, 1994; Dawkins, 1988). Hence, according to the evolutionary theory, the use of a formal plan for organizational change is not feasible. One factor that determines how organizations perceive and respond to external forces is the strategic vision of individuals who play a key role in the evolution of organizations (Ayres, 1994).

## 3.5. The variation–selection–retention cycle

We are interested to know how organizations detect changing conditions or possibilities as they change to survive and how these are translated into organizational actions. One possible answer to these questions is given by the ‘variation–selection– retention’ cycle (Ginsberg & Baum, 1993; Meyer, 1993; Miner, 1993; Nelson & Winter, 1982). Variation is defined as the offer of different possibilities for changing the way people do things within organizations. Variations can be brought about by almost anything but technological innovation is considered the main cause. The variation stage yields several alternatives for running a business. From this set of variations, an action is chosen in the selection process. Nelson and Winter (1982) argue that the most common selection criterion is profit generation. Evolutionary theorists stress that the main criteria for selection are survival and duplication of past successes. Retention is the last phase where the alternative path selected by the central organization is implemented according to the rules it set up. If proved successful, these are then encoded into organizational norms and procedures and would, in future, be used by the organization as formulae for success.

## 3.6. Implications for organizations

March (1994) succinctly points out several characteristics of the evolutionary process described above.

 The course of organizational evolution is a result of a sequence of particular historical branches realized in the past development path (path dependency), with the nodes of each branch representing turning points in the path.

 The external environment is the source of opportunities as well as threats; this environment is rapidly changing and unpredictable. Since evolution is a path of changes representing responses to the environment, it is possible to intervene in an organization’s evolution.

 By making the right interventions at break points along the evolutionary path, desired organizational change can be realized. The aim of these interventions is to improve organizational performance or to construct a better match between the organization and the external environment. The results of the interventions are hard to forecast and interventions do not necessarily lead to improvements.

March (1994) suggests two evolution characteristics that can be applied to organizational development. The first is to strengthen the processes of evolution. One way to do this is for the organization to find the right balance between the possibility to seek out or generate new options (exploration) and the improvement of their capabilities for implementing options that are proven to be effective (exploitation). The second is to have a vision that acts as a guide for evolutionary intervention. However, March also points out that both characteristics do not necessarily bring about desirable positive changes.

## 3.7. Co-evolution and IT champions

Some attention has been given to the idea that organizations and the role of technology in organizations co-evolve (Soe & Markus, 1995). Instead of perceiving technological change as the driving force behind innovation, the co-evolution approach stresses the inter-dependent dynamics between technological change and organizational change. Another factor is the contribution of IT champions in the process of IS development within organizations. According to Beath (1991), IT champions are middle or senior management figures in the organization who display leadership qualities and who project vigorously their vision for using IT. They support and push for specific IS projects despite hurdles encountered in the process. Due to path dependency, the evolution of IS strategy is strongly influenced by the IT champion’s vision. In addition his/her vision also plays a big part in organizational change. This is because the IT champion, who is normally a member of the management team, is also usually the person who leads the IT project. His/her strategic vision is also part of the management vision which is an important factor in organizational change (Ayres, 1994). In this way, the vision of the IT champion is also important to the co-evolution of the IS and the organization.

## 3.8. Evolutionary theory applied to IS strategy

In some case studies where technology is involved in organizational change, the importance of evolving technology as a source of innovation for organizations is emphasized (Dosi et al., 1988). For such cases, technological change is looked upon as the driving force behind the change. Nevertheless, it is still predominantly the features of organizational evolution that help to transform these changes into organizational actions. An attempt to apply the theory of evolution to IS was also made by Rosenberg (1994) who examined path dependency in the evolution of telecommunications.

Leasing companies share some common characteristics with telecommunication companies. Both are heavily dependent on IT and the costs of switching from a chosen technology to a new one are, for both, extremely high. As with telecommunications, it is impossible to run an international leasing company without IT-based IS.

## 3.9. Summary

To sum up, there are in general two different approaches as to how IS strategy is developed in an organization. One is based on the assumption that an IS strategy can be planned and is often closely associated with the business strategy. The other more organic approach says that an IS strategy develops as an evolutionary process triggered by changes in the environment. There is no explicit planning during this process. Our case study suggests that an IS strategy can indeed evolve over the years. In the context of this paper, the organization approach of Earl (1993) may be interpreted to exhibit evolutionary features.

The characteristics of the evolutionary process are:

 Path dependency i.e. decisions from the past influence the path for the future.

 Bounded rationality where changes from the past influence management’s decisions of on current issues.

 Change occurring as a co-evolution process, i.e. the IS strategy evolving together with the organization.

 The influence of the decision-maker’s vision on the path selection.

 The external environment as the most important factor for the change.

 The variation–selection–retention cycle which brings technological innovation (as part of the external environment) into the organization, although strongly biased by the vision of the IT champion or decision-maker.

 The need for balance between the exploration and exploitation processes of IS.

To illustrate these characteristics, we will describe, in Section 4, the development of the IS strategy in our case study.

## 4. Brief history of leasehold and its IS strategy

The emergence of the IS strategy in LeaseHold can be divided into four periods. Each represents changes in its IS strategy as well as changes to its organizational structure and development.

## 4.1. Period I: from 1963 till 1980

## 4.1.1. No formal business strategy

LeaseHold was founded in 1963 as LeaseNed with the goal of introducing equipment leasing in the Netherlands. By 1971 it had 15 employees and was operating only in the Netherlands. In that same year a new product, the full-service operational lease for cars was introduced. This is now the company’s core product. During this period, due to the international oil crises, the organization grew very slowly at less than 10% per year. In spite of this, LeaseNed started subsidiaries in Belgium, France, Germany and England, known as the ‘Big Five’. This was not an explicit business strategy but was merely a recognition of the high potential of the car-leasing.

## 4.1.2. No IS strategy

There was no implicit or explicit IS strategy for the organization. IS strategy was generally not known and even if there was one, the subsidiaries were organized as independent units and a general strategy would not have been possible at that time. Each subsidiary developed its own IS adapted to its own local environment. This led to much flexibility but made future standardization almost impossible.

## 4.2. Period II: the beginning of the Eighties

## 4.2.1. No overall business strategy, local IS

At the beginning of this period, in 1981, the five subsidiaries were placed under the control of a holding company, LeaseHold. They were organized as profit centers and the only control mechanism from the holding company was that each had to earn at least 15% profit on equity.

Such flexibility led to the rapid growth of LeaseHold but at the same time, products sharing the same name evolved differently because they were adapted to local needs. LeaseHold still did not formulate an overall business strategy during this period. This was not necessary for two reasons. Each subsidiary grew rapidly, more than 20% a year. There appeared to be no need for interference from the holding company. At the same time the management of the holding company wanted to promote an entrepreneurial culture. As a result, at the beginning of this period each subsidiary continued to develop its own IS, customized to the local needs of clients and local management.

## 4.2.2. Development leading to implicit IS strategy

Between 1982 and 1985, the Belgian subsidiary created a lease administration system (the New Operational Leasing System, NOLS). A standard version based on NOLS was developed and the holding company gave it to new subsidiaries so that they could be operational immediately. Nevertheless, existing subsidiaries like LeaseNed persisted with their own systems. LeaseHold at this time did not have any corporate IS personnel. The only coordination effort was conducted by the IS manager of LeaseNed, who traveled to the various subsidiaries providing support. In this way, although there was no corporate IS department and no explicit IS strategy, LeaseHold was carrying out an implicit policy of coordinating IS development within the whole organization. There was no corporate IS department yet and no explicit IS strategy.

## 4.3. Period III: the end of the Eighties/beginning of the Nineties

## 4.3.1. Business developments and outside competition

During this period, the organization as a whole grew rapidly. One new subsidiary was set up every year and it had a net profit growth of more than 20% per year. Nevertheless, competition from outside was increasing. The business strategy of the corporate management in this period was ambiguous. On the one hand the Board of Directors stressed the importance of an entrepreneurial approach for local management. This had been the basis for fast growth in the past. On the other hand, they wanted to gain more control over the subsidiaries because of increasing competition from outside. LeaseHold began to realize that the joint development of products and exchange of knowledge among the subsidiaries could be the solution to keep ahead of competitors.

## 4.3.2. Changes in IS development

Gradually, as the organization grew, the support work carried out by the IT manager of LeaseNed was not enough. In 1987, LeaseHold established an IS department whose formal task was to support the subsidiaries’ IS activities in the implementation, maintenance and development of NOLS. However, the subsidiaries still retained their own IS staff. There was still no explicit IS strategy but the Board of Directors saw the danger of the development of completely separate IS strategy for each individual subsidiary. They also realized that cooperation on IS areas could mean considerable cost reduction. Hence, in 1987, a Director of Automation was also appointed at LeaseHold.

## 4.3.3. The emergence of an IS strategy

The primary task of the director was to carry out more coordination of IS activities for the entire organization. The director began to develop a more organization-wide vision for the application of IS. This development was based on the ideas of the Board of Directors and also on a vision he had himself developed. An important feature of this vision was the goal to transform each subsidiary into an open network organization. The vision could be implemented in different ways. One was to define a standard way of working supported by a standard system in each subsidiary. Another would be to install a standard system centrally, which would have to be used by all subsidiaries. Such a vision would not only make an impact on the IS strategy but also on the organization itself. At the end of the Eighties, this vision and the wishes of the Board of Directors were moulded into an explicit IS strategy with the following general aims:

 To enhance cooperation on IS development among the subsidiaries.

 To broaden and exchange knowledge on IS matters within the whole organization.

 To change the role of IS from being just a supportive tool into the basis for the operation of the organization.

Specifically, the strategy aims at providing:

 An IS infrastructure that support the departments individually, helping them to connect electronically to clients and suppliers.

 A shared development environment helping innovation to spread quickly throughout the whole organization.

## 4.3.4. Implementation of the IS strategy

The formation of a network organization made up of all the subsidiaries could be seen as a huge organizational transformation. Considering the past development path of LeaseHold, a ‘big bang’ approach for this transformation was not feasible. This transformation was started at the end of the Eighties and is still proceeding even after more than 10 years. Under the guidance of the Director of Automation, an infrastructure for such open network organization was introduced in LeaseHold supporting the explicit IS strategy. Examples of this infrastructure are a common hardware platform (the AS400) for the entire organization, a Wide Area Network (WAN) and a Local Area Network (LAN).

## 4.3.5. Problems

The implementation of the WAN and the LAN did not run into big problems because it did not interfere very much with the autonomy of the subsidiaries. However, the implementation of the system NOLS on the AS400 in each subsidiary was more problematic. To implement this organization-wide, the holding company asserted their formal authority over the subsidiaries for the first time. This generated strong opposition from the subsidiaries. The implementation took a long time and involved much political haggling. In the end all subsidiaries had the same hardware platform, except LeaseNed. To solve the conflict with LeaseNed, subtle changes in the IS strategy were made. A compromise was reached with LeaseNed which agreed to start a project to migrate to the common hardware platform on condition that they could, at the same time, develop a new software system instead of using NOLS to replace their own outdated system. LeaseNed had found NOLS to be wanting and the intention was that the new system, developed by LeaseNed, could ultimately replace NOLS. A project, called INFO’90, was started in LeaseNed to effect this change.

## 4.3.6. Failure of INFO’90 lead to further revision of the strategy

Due to mismanagement and the enormous complexity of creating one IS for an entire organization, after more than four years and 12 million dollars, INFO’90 failed and was terminated. As a result, several top managers were dismissed.

## 4.3.7. External influence

IS implementations were also guided by changes in the external — and internal — environment in this period. For instance, the decision for LeaseNed to develop a Dealer Network system (electronic link between LeaseNed and car garages) and a Knowledge System for the maintenance department arose due to the need to compete with LeaseNed’s external competitors in the Netherlands. LeaseNed was market leader in the Netherlands and saw the danger of competitors encroaching on its market share using cost reduction measures. The combination of the Dealer Network and the Knowledge System allowed the Dutch organization for the first time to communicate electronically with more than 3000 small suppliers and to have an edge over its competitor. This external influence was a big push towards the creation of a network of similar departments within the organization in accordance with the general IS strategy.

## 4.4. Period IV: the mid 1990s

## 4.4.1. Business developments

At the start of the Nineties, LeaseHold controlled subsidiaries in 20 countries. The coordination role of LeaseHold increased. Some of the smaller subsidiaries started to ask for the formulation of a business strategy that could indicate the direction of the development of the company as a whole. This occurred because subsidiaries in some countries experienced a setback in their lease markets which was caused by increased competition and the change of attitude towards the use of leased cars in most European countries. This change in attitude is a result of the legislation backed government encouragement of car leasing. The increasing competition also had a negative effect on the profits of LeaseHold.

## 4.4.2. Need for explicit business strategy

During this period, the organization as a whole was still very profitable, able to show double-digit growth in profit annually. This was the result of setting up new subsidiaries in economically fast growing regions worldwide. Another source of growth was the successful acquisition of other leasing companies. The implicit business strategy was to strive continuously to enter new markets to secure future growth. However a formal, explicit business strategy would help the whole organization to grow and survive. Although the subsidiaries resented the growing control of the holding company, they too saw this need. The formulation of such a strategy gave rise to internal conflicts. There were heated arguments and discussions within the

Board of Directors between the older managers and those more recently appointed. The former had been responsible for the founding of the company and the fast growth of LeaseHold because of their entrepreneurial skills. The latter were more schooled in strategic thinking and recognized the need for a clear-cut strategy for the whole company. The first formal strategic outlook was produced in 1994. This was very general and did not influence the already developed IS strategy. In fact it was still country-based and was strongly related to the product market.

## 4.4.3. External business changes

An important development of the mid-Nineties was the demand by multinationals for complete leasing solutions for their entire fleets worldwide. This development required international contracts for the entire car fleet that would override the local contracts. Since the lease products had evolved very differently in the individual countries, this posed a big problem. LeaseHold recognized this and initiated discussions to arrive at a common view on product management. An international approach was necessary towards business dealings with multinationals. As a result, a new corporate (international) sales department, the LeaseHold International (LHI), was established. The coordination and support function of this department was made possible by the introduction of uniform IS into the subsidiaries.

## 4.4.4. The explicit IS strategy

LeaseHold continued the IS strategy that was formulated in the previous period. This strategy proved to be an effective response to external developments. This was reflected when WAN was introduced electronically connecting all the subsidiaries. In addition, the introduction of the International Client System made possible the management of international client portfolios. The coordination job of IS development at LeaseHold was growing. The Director of Automation had the role of scanning and approving IS proposals and budgets before passing them on to the Board of Directors. He had the opportunity to make sure that proposals were in accordance with the IS strategy. However, individual subsidiaries were also allowed to bypass the Director and present their ideas directly to the Board of Directors. If the Board wanted to encourage the development of that particular system, it could overrule the advice of the Director. If a local system worked well, the holding company would require other subsidiaries to adopt it too. The local approach towards IS development is still considered important when serving local markets. At the same time, if promising new IS technologies were available and increased client satisfaction or realized cost benefits, resources would be released for the implementation of such technologies. One can thus say that LeaseHold had always striven to be an innovative organization.

A change of attitude towards organization-wide IS development had been evident in this period. Not only was the acceptance of organization-wide IS development growing in the subsidiaries, LeaseHold no longer tolerated any resistance to this. IS developments initiated by the holding company must be implemented.

## 5. Discussion

This section discusses the evolution of the IS strategy within LeaseHold from 1963 to 1986. By studying the evolution pattern of the IS strategy and the factors that influence it, we hope to gain better insights on the successful application of IS in organizations. The evolution pattern will be analyzed using theories discussed in Section 3. We use the framework of organizational approach with evolutionary development while taking the following factors into account:

 The role of the environment including the role of available technology, as perpetuators of changes.

 The role of the management especially from the viewpoint of path dependency.

 The existence of plans or models.

 The evolution of the IS technology and the organization.

## 5.1. Evolution pattern of LeaseHold organizational development

In the early days of LeaseHold, there was no explicit business strategy only an implicit one. Above all, there was no explicit — or implicit — IS strategy. Characteristics of the organization formed during this period under this chosen path of ‘no strategies’ were:

 An entrepreneurial style of operation.

 The independence of subsidiaries which responded to their own environments and conducted business as they saw fit.

Specifically, the effects of this chosen path are:

 An independent style of work and autonomous organization culture.

 Role of central organization as coordinator, not controller.

 Ad-hoc, non-lineal and accidental responses to external factors.

This path allowed local variations instead of fixed prescriptions for the local subsidiaries from the holding company. The evolution of the organization in this period can thus be compared to the evolution of species. The changes were initiated by the desire to improve performance and growth, which were seen as necessary for survival. Each subsidiary strove to grow and bring about organizational change by exploring the variations offered by the local environment. Since they were independent, selection and retention of alternatives (from the variations) were also done locally and this did not cause many problems. These organizational actions, which resulted from the entrepreneurial approach, proved successful for a while. There was no reason to make changes and the whole organization continued along this path up until the end of the Seventies. Hence, we can see here that past successful actions influence both organizational changes as well as the current organization behavior.

## 5.2. Same path but basis for IS strategy was being laid

At the beginning of the Eighties, the organization underwent some changes. It expanded quickly and adopted new technology. These could be regarded as the groundwork for the birth of an IS strategy at LeaseHold. Nevertheless, before the IS strategy became explicit, the organization continued evolving in the same style as before.

## 5.3. Change of path

Up to the end of the Eighties, local IS developments in the business units were encouraged because the local approach towards lease markets was seen as the key to success. However by the end of the Eighties, important external factors made the holding company change its implicit IS strategy. These changes were:

 increased international competition; and

 changing external market conditions especially the outsourcing of fleet management by the bigger international companies.

To secure and improve future organizational performance, those challenges had to be met. LeaseHold realized that synergy, joint development of products and exchange of knowledge in the IS area were required. Changes that occurred were:

 Structural changes: the management appointed a Director of Automation and later on set up a department to oversee the coordination of IS. The Director’s primary task was to increase coordination in IS for the entire organization.

 IT champion: the Director’s role evolved to become that of an IT champion, facilitating the success of an organization-wide approach towards IS development.

 Vision of key player: the Director had a vision for the IS activities in the organization. He combined this vision with the management’s ideas on IS, and eventually formulated an explicit IS strategy.

Hence the IS strategy was formulated not just from external factors but also from the vision of a key player. This vision encompassed mainly the advocacy for an open network system for the whole organization. This strategy initiated the organization-wide IS implementations. It also provided a framework which made it possible for IS innovations to be used by all subsidiaries within the whole organization.

Having an explicit strategy does not mean having a planned and methodological way of making IS decisions and carrying out implementations. IS implementation at LeaseHold at the end of the Eighties was still driven by short-term responses to the environment but by then this was done within the framework of a long-term vision. Hence the organization and the IS function were co-evolving and influencing each other but the path of IS was somewhat changed when compared with the past.

## 5.4. Variation–selection–retention process

The application of the variation–selection–retention approach can be found within LeaseHold towards the end of the Eighties. LeaseHold wanted local IS variations to be shared with the other subsidiaries as far as possible. Hence when one subsidiary made an IS decision that was seen as successful, others were asked to take the same path. This was based on the view that the organization as a whole should evolve together within the constraints set by the implicit business strategy. In addition, by exploiting IS more centrally, retention was made possible. When IS strategies were implemented successfully, the procedures and rules of the whole process would be encoded into organizational norms and standard procedures. They could then on be used by the whole organization. Retention in this way helped the organization to reduce the costs of IS as a whole.

## 5.5. Path dependency

At the end of the Eighties, although the IS strategy had evolved from an implicit to an explicit well-formulated strategy, the business strategy still remained implicit. The past path taken by the holding company made IS implementations in this new period very difficult to realize. In the past, subsidiaries had autonomy and had individually developed their own IS. This made the implementation of a common, unique hardware platform laborious. An example of this path dependency in IS development is the failed INFO’90 project in LeaseNed. The fate of this project reflected the difficulty of achieving the new IS strategy of having a common IS infrastructure and platform for the whole organization. Another example is the Dealer network and the expert system that were only developed for LeaseNed. These were not shared applications and actually did not fall within ambit of the new IS strategy. All these highlighted the fact that a truly organization-wide approach and an overall strategy towards IS development were still not yet possible at the time. The subsidiaries still operated within their independent organizational structure and, most importantly, within their own embedded cultural and psychological make-up. To implement the strategy, gradual organizational changes were necessary and they had to evolve together with the changing environment. Following are some suggestions for these changes:

 Change in organizational culture — subsidiaries have to get used to the organization-wide approach of IS development and the holding company has to create an environment in the entire organization in which the IS strategy can grow and be accepted.

 Change in organizational political structure to reduce resistance from the subsidiaries to more control from the holding company, as required by the IS strategy.

The above two factors highlighted the path dependency of the organization. The past of the organization still played a big role.

## 5.6. Business strategy still lagging behind explicit IS strategy

It was only in the Nineties that a business strategy was explicitly formulated at LeaseHold. This strategy was, however, too general to have a direct influence on the IS strategy formulated earlier on. Following the footsteps of its past, no methods were used at LeaseHold to define the basis for the business strategy nor were there any plans for its implementation. It was somewhat different from its IS strategy. IS implementations were guided by the IS strategy, taken up by the Director of Automation and worked on by the corporate IT Department. The evolution of the IS function at LeaseHold could thus be characterized as both deliberate and emergent (Mintzberg, 1989). The IS strategy guided implementations (deliberate), although ad hoc responses to changing environmental conditions were also encouraged (emergent). IS development was used at LeaseHold as the champion of organizational change and some evidence is thus found for the notion of co-evolution.

## 5.7. Analysis of success factors

LeaseHold formulated its IS strategy at the end of the Eighties because their past development path had resulted in an organization that was incapable of competing successfully in the changing external environment. This strategy was formulated with the goal of consolidating and rationalizing the IS function. This exhibited similarities with the strengthening of evolutionary processes, as suggested by March (1994). The IS strategy was influenced by the vision of a key player, the Director of Automation.

As mentioned in Section 3, March discusses two possibilities for realizing improvement: strengthen the process of evolution or create a vision to guide evolutionary intervention. However, March is not explicit on whether these two possibilities are mutually exclusive or inclusive and he does not discuss the conditions for each of them. In our opinion, the two possibilities should be integrated in a successful organization. When the organization tries only to find the right balance between the exploration and exploitation, the organization acts as a follower, reacting to a changing environment without clear direction. Selection and retention processes will then be ad-hoc, resulting in either a continuous process of adaptation to environmental changes or in a continuous attempt to maximize the result of the previous change. On the other hand, when the organization ignores changes in the environment (if that is possible) and only uses its own vision to guide the process of evolution, it acts more like a research and development company.

In this case study we find evidence for the two possibilities to co-exist in an organization with an entrepreneurial culture. We see a clear vision coming from not only the Director of Automation but implicitly also from the LeaseHold management. At the same time there is a continuous process of balancing exploration and exploitation. By allowing business units to explore new possibilities but immediately exporting these to the other units, LeaseHold was maximizing the results of changes in the organization and the IS environment.

## 5.8. No IS strategy models or formal plans

Mintzberg (1989) argues that planning can be used to program already developed strategies. In this case study we found no support for the use of IS planning in that way. IS in line with the IS strategy were implemented in a rather ad hoc manner, at the appropriate time. This approach was necessary because the organization’s past development path had resulted in a situation where the holding company had to first create an environment in which an organization-wide strategy would be accepted. Hence the past was taken into account at the point where the IS development plan/strategy was approved.

## 5.9. Continuous, ad-hoc decision on planning

From our case study it appears that the organizational approach described by Earl (1993) can be used to describe the development of the IS strategy at LeaseHold. The different phases in the company’s development may look similar to the Nolan development curve but the similarity is deceptive since the Nolan curve model is prescriptive and ours is descriptive. The lack of planning and modeling had no impact on the development of the company but the existence of a clear vision by a member of the management team had played an important role.

## 6. Conclusions and suggestions for further research

The application of evolutionary theory to IS development in organizations has given us some important insights.

1. The examination of the evolution of the LeaseHold IS strategy showed that IS planning and modeling was not used in this case. Instead, an evolutionary approach was adopted. This has more descriptive and explanatory potential for IS strategy and development. However, this approach is still very young. Explorative theoretical elaboration grounded on empirical study is needed to enhance its application in the real world. Although the case study does not support strategic IS planning methods, our study does not rule out the possibility of using them successfully. The entrepreneurial culture of LeaseHold is not conducive to the use of strategic IS planning methods. Perhaps in a less entrepreneurial culture, an organization with a strong IS leadership could use such methods to its advantage.

2. The co-evolution of IS and organizational development may be a sound concept for explaining (and perhaps guiding) the evolution of IS functions in organizations. However, this concept needs more elaboration, both practically and theoretically. At LeaseHold, the evolution of IS strategy was driven by the desire to improve organizational performance in a changing environment. With the increasing pace of technology, IS development was increasingly used to change the way of work in this organization in response to the environment. The development of the IS function at LeaseHold is both a result of deliberate choices to achieve desired directions for the future, as well as responses to emerging possibilities and threats in the external environment (ad hoc IS implementations).

3. The evolution of the IS function was heavily influenced by the vision of one key person in the management team. This IT champion facilitated the change to a more organization-wide approach towards IS development at LeaseHold and advanced an IS strategy that guided IS implementations. The IS strategy was formulated in an attempt to rationalize and consolidate the organization’s IS function which had evolved in the past.

4. The past development path of an organization is shown to be important both for the formulation as well as the realization of an IS strategy. Much effort was made at LeaseHold to recognize the context and create a climate in which the IS strategy can grow. Through this we can see the importance of such a vision.

5. More research on the development of IS strategy and the organizational response to changing internal and external environment is needed, especially in the area of global companies where variation and retention play an important role. Apart from that, any investigation should also incorporate the impact of organizational culture (e.g. entrepreneurial, autocratic) on the development of IS strategies in companies.
